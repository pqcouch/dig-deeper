## The Greek Text — Critical and Majority

The brief was that the overview should rest first on the original text, "what is considered to be the majority text", and not on any English version. Two things are meant by that phrase, and the overview honours both.

**The substrate is the Greek, read whole.** Every observation below was made by reading Luke through in the SBLGNT before any English was opened, and every count is made by lemma against the MorphGNT index of that edition. The NASB95 is quoted as the English reference only. `[T]`

**The Majority Text has been set beside it throughout.** The corpus held no Byzantine text, so the Robinson–Pierpont 2018 edition of Luke (public domain) was added at `_texts/source/byzantine-rp2018/` and collated against the SBLGNT verse by verse, with accents, case and movable ν normalised away. The NA28 apparatus siglum 𝔪 marks the same witness group, and was used to cross-check. The results:

- The two texts differ somewhere in **728 of the Majority Text's 1,150 verses**. The great majority are spelling (Μωϋσέως against Μωσέως), word order, the article, or a conjunction. `[T]`
- **65 verses** carry a plus or minus of three words or more; some of these are word-order moves that the comparison reads as a deletion and an insertion. `[T]`
- **Verse count.** The SBLGNT has 1,149 verses (no 17:36, no 23:17); the Majority Text has 1,150 (it has 23:17 and lacks 17:36); the NASB95 prints 1,151, bracketing both. `[T]`
- **Where the two agree against NA28's doubts.** NA28 double-brackets 22:43–44 (the angel and the sweat like blood) and 23:34a ("Father, forgive them"). The SBLGNT prints both without brackets; so does the Majority Text; so does the NASB95. The so-called "Western non-interpolations" at 24:12, 24:40 and 24:51–52 stand in all three. **On the passages most often doubted in Luke, the critical text used here and the Majority Text agree.** `[T]`

## Canonical Position

The NT equivalent of the Tanak block — position in the collection, what it presupposes, what it hands on.

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Section** | The Gospels; third of four in the received order (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John). The order is stable at this point: Luke is third in the Western order too (Matthew, John, Luke, Mark) `[S]`. It is the **first volume of a two-part work** whose second volume, Acts, opens by naming "the first account" to the same Theophilus (Acts 1:1) `[T]` — and the canon separates the two volumes by placing John between them. |
| **Reading implication** | Read among the Gospels, Luke is one portrait of Jesus among four. Read as volume one, it is a story that is **deliberately left open**: the risen Jesus tells the disciples to "stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high" (24:49), and the book ends with them waiting in the temple (24:53). **Prefer to hold both**, but let the second govern how the ending is heard: 24:44–53 is a handover, not a finale. `[I]` |
| **Presupposes** | (1) **The Scriptures as a tripartite whole** — "the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" (24:44), and "Moses and all the prophets … all the Scriptures" (24:27). Luke is the only NT book to name three divisions (checked across the SBLGNT), and the third is headed by the book that heads the Ketuvim in the BHS order `[T]`; the phrase ἐν βίβλῳ ψαλμῶν ("in the book of Psalms") also belongs to Luke–Acts alone (Luke 20:42; Acts 1:20) `[T]`; that ψαλμοί ("Psalms") stands for the whole of the Writings is `[I]`, moderate. (2) **The Tanak's span, end to end** — "from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah, who perished between the altar and the house" (11:51): Gen 4:8–10 to 2 Chr 24:20–22, checked against the WLC and Swete `[T]`. That this means *first book to last book* depends on Chronicles closing the canon, which is the BHS and Talmudic order; **Codex Leningradensis itself places Chronicles first among the Writings** (`_texts/README.md`), so the "whole canon" reading of 11:51 rests on a sequence, not on L `[I]`, moderate. (3) **Prior written accounts** — "many have undertaken to compile an account" (1:1) `[T]`; that Mark is among them is the two-source hypothesis `[S]`. (4) A reader who already knows the Greek Scriptures: the Isaiah Luke quotes is the Greek Isaiah (3:6, "all flesh shall see the salvation of God", has no counterpart in the Hebrew of Isa 40:5; 4:18, "recovery of sight to the blind", renders LXX Isa 61:1 where the Hebrew has פְּקַח־קוֹחַ ("opening [of the prison]")) `[T]`. |
| **Handoff** | Directly to Acts, which re-narrates the ascension (Acts 1:2, 11, 22, ἀνελήμφθη ("he was taken up"), answering Luke 9:51's ἀνάλημψις ("taking up")), fulfils "the promise of my Father" (24:49 → Acts 1:4; 2:33), and closes the two volumes as Luke opened them: Isa 6:9 is quoted in part at Luke 8:10 and in full at Acts 28:26–27, and τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ θεοῦ ("the salvation of God") stands at Luke 3:6 and Acts 28:28 and nowhere else in the NT `[T]`, verified in the SBLGNT index. |
| **Neighbours** | Mark before, John after. Luke follows much of Mark's narrative sequence `[S]` — checkable mechanically with `_texts/tools/synoptic_diff.py`, not run in this Draft; the adjacency to John does no demonstrable work, and its only effect is to separate Luke from its own sequel. |

## Presenting Situation

**Author / narrator.** The prologue speaks in the first person: κἀμοί ("to me also", 1:3), with a masculine participle, παρηκολουθηκότι ("having followed", 1:3) `[T]`. The writer places himself among those to whom the tradition was *handed down* (παρέδοσαν ἡμῖν ("they delivered to us"), 1:2), distinct from the αὐτόπται ("eyewitnesses") `[T]`; he does not claim to be one. After 1:4 the "I" never returns, and an unobtrusive third-person narrator takes over `[T]`. The traditional attribution to Luke the physician and companion of Paul (Col 4:14; Phlm 24; 2 Tim 4:11; the "we" of Acts) is `[S]`, early and well attested (Irenaeus, the Muratorian fragment), and not stated anywhere in the book.

**Audience.** Named: Theophilus, addressed as κράτιστε ("most excellent", 1:3) `[T]`. The same honorific is used in Acts for the Roman governors Felix and Festus (Acts 23:26; 24:3; 26:25) `[T]`, which makes a man of standing likely `[I]`. He has already been instructed: περὶ ὧν κατηχήθης λόγων ("the things you have been taught", 1:4) `[T]`. The book glosses things a Jewish reader would not need — "the Feast of Unleavened Bread, which is called Passover" (22:1), "Arimathea, a city of the Jews" (23:51) `[T]` — so the compositional audience includes readers outside Judaea, probably Gentiles `[I]`, moderate–high. The canonical audience is every later reader of the two volumes.

**Occasion.** Not stated beyond the prologue. Date and place are `[S]` and contested: a date in the 60s (Acts ends with Paul still alive) against the 80s (reading 19:43–44 and 21:20–24 as written after AD 70). The text itself does not settle it, and the overview does not depend on it.

**Pressure.** The prologue names one: ἀσφάλεια ("certainty", NASB95 "the exact truth", 1:4) about "the things accomplished among us" (τῶν πεπληροφορημένων … πραγμάτων, 1:1) `[T]`. The book then shows where certainty is under strain. The disciples cannot understand the passion predictions (9:45; 18:34) `[T]`; the Emmaus pair "were hoping that it was He who was going to redeem Israel" (24:21) `[T]`; the leaders of Israel reject Jesus while "the people" hang on his words (19:47–48; 23:35) `[T]`. The dominant tension: **how can a crucified Messiah, refused by Jerusalem's rulers, whose salvation goes out to the nations, be the faithful fulfilment of God's oath to Abraham and his promise to David?** `[I]`, high. The book's answer is its most repeated word, δεῖ ("it is necessary", 18 times: 2:49 to 24:44) — the story went this way because it had to, and it had to because it was written (24:26–27, 44–46) `[T]`.

**Purpose.** To give a catechised reader certainty that what happened in Jesus is God's visitation of his people, promised in the Scriptures and accomplished exactly as written, so that repentance and the release of sins may be proclaimed in his name to all the nations. `[I]`

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|--------|----------|----------|
| 1:1–4 | Prologue | One periodic sentence in literary Greek; names the purpose — ἀσφάλεια ("certainty") |
| 1:5–2:52 | The Visitation Announced: two births | John and Jesus in paired panels (annunciation, birth, naming, song, growth); opens and closes in the temple (1:9; 2:46); announces visitation, redemption, Saviour, light to the nations |
| 3:1–4:13 | Preparation | A second, dated beginning (3:1–2); John's call to repentance for the ἄφεσις ("release") of sins; the Son declared; the genealogy run back to Adam; the Son tested in the wilderness |
| 4:14–9:50 | Galilee: Who Is This? | The Spirit-anointed ministry opened by the Nazareth reading of Isaiah (4:16–30); the identity question asked four times (5:21; 7:49; 8:25; 9:9) and answered by Peter (9:20); the first δεῖ of the passion (9:22); the Transfiguration speaks of his ἔξοδος ("departure", 9:31) |
| 9:51–19:27 | The Journey to Jerusalem | "He set his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51), with reminders at 9:53; 13:22, 33; 17:11; 18:31; 19:11; teaching on discipleship, possessions, prayer; the lost sought and found (ch. 15; 19:10); ends with the parable that reframes the entry (19:11–27) |
| 19:28–21:38 | Jerusalem: The King and the Temple | The royal entry, the lament over the city that did not know "the time of your visitation" (19:44), daily teaching in the temple (19:47 → 21:37–38), the controversies, the discourse on the temple's fall and the Son of Man's coming |
| 22:1–24:53 | The Exodus Accomplished | Passover and new covenant; arrest, trials, crucifixion, burial; the empty tomb; the Scriptures opened; the commission to the nations; the blessing and the parting; the disciples back in the temple |

**Device.** A **linear journey with temple bookends**. The book begins in the ναός ("sanctuary") with a priest who cannot bless the waiting people because he has been struck dumb (1:21–22), and ends with Jesus lifting his hands to bless the disciples, who return to the temple "blessing God" (24:50–53) `[T]`. The geography is the structure: Galilee → Jerusalem, stated by the accusers themselves, "starting from Galilee even as far as this place" (23:5), and then reversed outward, "beginning from Jerusalem" (24:47) `[T]`. Ἰερουσαλήμ and Ἱεροσόλυμα ("Jerusalem") together occur 31 times in 30 verses `[T]`.

**The hinge is 9:51**, with 9:18–36 as the pivot of identity: confession, first passion prediction, Transfiguration. 9:51 is marked by a solemn formula — ἐν τῷ συμπληροῦσθαι τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ἀναλήμψεως αὐτοῦ ("when the days of his taking up were being fulfilled") — whose noun ἀνάλημψις ("taking up") occurs only here in the NT `[T]`, and which points past the cross to the ascension (24:51; Acts 1:2, 11) `[I]`, high.

**Contested seams.** The close of the journey section is disputed — 19:27, 19:28, 19:44 and 19:48 all have defenders `[S]`; the 19:11–27 dig reached the same judgement. This overview takes 19:27/28, because 19:28 ("he was going on ahead, going up to Jerusalem") is the last journey notice and 19:29 is the approach itself, but the arc does not depend on it. Chiastic schemes proposed for 9:51–19:27 (Bailey, Goulder, others) are `[S]`, were not tested in this run, and are **not adopted**.

## Microscript

*So that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he was taught: in Jesus — Spirit-anointed Son, David's heir, the Saviour born "today" — the God of Israel has visited his people. He must go up to Jerusalem to be rejected, to suffer and to be raised, and so "today" salvation reaches the lost, and repentance for the release of sins is proclaimed from Jerusalem to all the nations, exactly as Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms had written.*

**Keynotes, verified by lemma in the SBLGNT:**

- δεῖ ("it is necessary") — **18 verses**: 2:49; 4:43; 9:22; 11:42; 12:12; 13:14, 16, 33; 15:32; 17:25; 18:1; 19:5; 21:9; 22:7, 37; 24:7, 26, 44. MorphGNT lemmatises δεῖ under δέω, which also carries δέομαι ("to ask", 5:12; 8:38; 9:40; 10:2; 22:32) and "to bind" (13:16; 19:30); separated by parse code. `[T]`
- σήμερον ("today") — **11 verses**: 2:11; 4:21; 5:26; 12:28; 13:32, 33; 19:5, 9; 22:34, 61; 23:43. `[T]`
- The visitation group — ἐπισκέπτομαι ("to visit") at 1:68, 78; 7:16, and ἐπισκοπή ("visitation") at 19:44. `[T]`
- ἄφεσις ("release, forgiveness") — 1:77; 3:3; 4:18 (twice); 24:47. `[T]`

**Test against the movements.** The infancy panels announce the visitation (1:68, 78), the Saviour "today" (2:11), the first δεῖ (2:49) and the light to the nations (2:32). The preparation preaches ἄφεσις (3:3) for "all flesh" (3:6). Galilee opens with "today" (4:21) and ἄφεσις (4:18), confesses the visitation (7:16), and turns on the δεῖ of the passion (9:22). The journey is driven by δεῖ (13:33) and climaxes in "today salvation has come to this house" (19:9–10). Jerusalem fails to know its visitation (19:44). The passion fulfils what δεῖ τελεσθῆναι ("must be fulfilled", 22:37), promises paradise "today" (23:43), and ends in the Scriptures opened and ἄφεσις for the nations (24:44–47). Every movement serves the microscript.

## Intertextual Map

Luke is **citation-rich and allusion-dense**, which is the opposite of Philippians. Explicit quotations (γέγραπται ("it is written"), εἴρηται ("it is said"), "David says in the book of Psalms") cluster in three places — the infancy and preparation (chs. 2–4), the Jerusalem controversies (chs. 19–20) and the passion (22:37) — while the infancy canticles are woven from Scripture without a single formula `[T]`. The book states its own hermeneutic twice (24:27, 44), so reading the Old Testament towards Christ is here `[T]`, not an imported method.

Sources are listed in **Tanak order** (BHS). Locations were found by a systematic 4-gram sweep of the whole of Luke against the whole of Swete, supplemented by a deliberate hunt for name, number and type-scene allusions that carry no verbal overlap. Every row's verbal overlap was then checked verse against verse in Swete (accent-stripped), and the Hebrew was read in the WLC wherever the Greek and Hebrew diverge. Swete versification is used where it differs and is flagged. Confidence: *high* = quotation or distinctive shared wording; *moderate* = shared wording plus conceptual fit; *uncertain* = conceptual only.

## Christological Trajectory

Luke is a Gospel, so Christ is the subject, not a trajectory to be found. What the overview must name is **how Luke presents him**, and here the book is explicit about its own method: the risen Jesus interprets "in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (24:27), and says that everything written about him "in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (24:44). `[T]`

**Ground — who he is and what he has done.**

- **The Son**: "Son of the Most High" (1:32), "Son of God" (1:35), the beloved Son at the Jordan (3:22), "son of Adam, son of God" (3:38), the Son who knows the Father (10:22), the Son before the council (22:70). `[T]`
- **David's heir and King**: throne, house and kingdom without end (1:32–33); born in David's city (2:4, 11); "Son of David, have mercy" (18:38–39); hailed as ὁ βασιλεύς ("the king", 19:38); mocked as king of the Jews (23:37–38); the kingdom promised to a dying criminal (23:42–43). `[T]`
- **Saviour and Lord**: σωτὴρ ὅς ἐστιν χριστὸς κύριος ("a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord", 2:11). The word σωτήρ ("Saviour") occurs twice in Luke — for God (1:47) and for Jesus (2:11) `[T]`. The narrator calls Jesus ὁ κύριος ("the Lord") from 7:13 onwards (7:13, 19; 10:1, 41; 11:39; 12:42; 13:15; 17:5–6; 18:6; 19:8; 22:61), and the Eleven's Easter confession is ὄντως ἠγέρθη ὁ κύριος ("the Lord has really risen", 24:34) `[T]`, verified by lemma, and "the way of the Lord" that John prepares (3:4, Isa 40:3) is Jesus' way `[I]`, high — in the one who comes, **Israel's God visits** (1:68, 78; 7:16; 19:44) `[I]`, high.
- **The prophet like Moses and greater than Elijah**: a prophet not accepted in his own country (4:24); "a great prophet has arisen" (7:16); Moses and Elijah on the mountain, and the voice's "listen to him" (9:30–35; Deut 18:15); "a prophet mighty in deed and word" (24:19). Luke corrects the Elijah-reading at every turn — Jesus is taken up as Elijah was, but he rebukes Elijah's fire (9:54–55) and does not allow Elisha's farewell (9:61–62). `[T]` for the data; `[I]` for the correction, high.
- **The Servant**: numbered with transgressors (22:37; Isa 53:12), the chosen one (23:35; Isa 42:1), the righteous one (23:47; Isa 53:11), a light to the nations (2:32; Isa 49:6). `[T]` for 22:37; `[I]`, moderate–high, for the rest.
- **The Son of Man** — 24 occurrences, from authority on earth to forgive sins (5:24) to "must be delivered … and rise" (24:7), with the Danielic coming (21:27) and session (22:69). `[T]`, verified by lemma sequence.

**Pattern — what he makes of those who follow.** Luke also sets Jesus before disciples as a pattern: in prayer at every hinge (3:21; 6:12; 9:18, 28–29; 22:41–44; 23:34, 46) `[T]`, in bearing the cross daily (9:23), in serving at table (22:27), and in forgiving enemies (23:34, with 6:27–28). The first martyr in Acts dies echoing two of Jesus' sayings from the cross (Acts 7:59–60; Luke 23:34, 46) `[T]`.

**Moralism check.** Luke is the easiest Gospel to preach as ethics: the Good Samaritan as "be a good neighbour", the Prodigal as "come home", Zacchaeus as "give generously", Jesus at prayer as "pray more". The book blocks each of these. The verb σπλαγχνίζομαι ("to be moved with compassion") occurs three times in Luke — of the Lord at Nain (7:13), the Samaritan (10:33), and the father (15:20) — and the book has already located that compassion in God: διὰ σπλάγχνα ἐλέους θεοῦ ἡμῶν ("because of the tender mercy of our God", 1:78) `[T]`. The compassion Luke commends is first the compassion by which God visits. Zacchaeus' restitution follows Jesus' "today I must stay at your house" (19:5) and is answered by "today salvation has come" (19:9) — the order is visitation, then response `[T]`. **Christ is the one who seeks and saves (19:10) before he is the one to be imitated.**

## Preaching Traps

1. **Luke as a social manifesto — the poor without the ἄφεσις ("release").** The poverty and wealth language is real and material: πτωχός ("poor") occurs 10 times and πλούσιος ("rich") 11 times, and neither word occurs anywhere in Acts `[T]`. But the Nazareth programme joins good news to the poor with ἄφεσις ("release") sounded twice (4:18), and the book's final charge is ἄφεσις ἁμαρτιῶν ("forgiveness of sins", 24:47). A sermon that makes Jesus a reformer of economics alone has cut the release off from the sin it releases. *Bites at:* 4:16–30; 6:20–26; 12:13–34; 16:19–31; 18:18–30. *Corrective:* hold the material and the spiritual together as Luke does — Zacchaeus is saved and his purse opens (19:8–10), in that order.

2. **The parables as moral tales.** The Good Samaritan (10:25–37) is told to a lawyer "wishing to justify himself" (10:29) and reverses his question, so that the despised Samaritan becomes the one who shows mercy. The "Prodigal" parable (15:11–32) is told to grumbling Pharisees (15:2), and it ends with the elder brother outside. *Bites at:* chs. 10, 15, 16, 18. *Corrective:* preach each parable to the audience Luke names for it — Luke almost always names one (10:29; 15:2; 18:1, 9; 19:11).

3. **Supersession — "the Jews rejected him, so God turned to the Gentiles".** The book opens and closes in the temple among devout Israelites (1:6; 2:25, 37; 24:53); salvation is "the consolation of Israel" (2:25) as well as "a light to the nations" (2:32). The book distinguishes the rulers from the λαός ("people"): at the cross "the people stood by, looking on; even the rulers were sneering" (23:35); earlier "all the people were hanging on to every word he said" (19:48) `[T]`. The judgement oracles over Jerusalem are laments, spoken in tears (19:41). *Bites at:* 4:16–30; 13:31–35; 19:41–44; 20:9–19; 21:20–24; 23:13–25. *Corrective:* preach the city's refusal as a prophet's grief over his own people, not as a verdict on a race.

4. **Christmas sentiment.** The birth narrative is read aloud every December and usually preached as idyll. Its songs overturn thrones (1:51–53), its sign is a baby in a feeding trough (2:12), and its blessing ends in a sword and "a sign to be opposed" (2:34–35). *Bites at:* 1:26–2:40. *Corrective:* let Simeon's second oracle (2:34–35) stand beside the first (2:29–32).

5. **"The kingdom of God is within you."** ἐντὸς ὑμῶν (17:21) has been read as inner spirituality. It is addressed to Pharisees (17:20), and the NASB95's "in your midst" follows the setting: the kingdom is present in Jesus among them. *Bites at:* 17:20–37. *Corrective:* read it with 11:20 — "the kingdom of God has come upon you".

6. **Reading Luke without Acts — or reading Acts back into Luke.** One error ends the story at the ascension, as though 24:53 were a finale. The other preaches Pentecost in the Gospel. But the Spirit is promised and *withheld* (3:16; 11:13; 24:49), and the disciples are told to wait. *Bites at:* 3:15–22; 11:1–13; 24:36–53. *Corrective:* preach the ending as a handover — the blessing is given, the promise is pending, and the mission begins "from Jerusalem" (24:47).

**Addressee differentiation.** The infancy narrative sets two households side by side, John's and Jesus', and two kinds of waiting Israelite — priests (Zechariah, Elizabeth) and prophets (Simeon, Anna). Both kinds are resolved at the close. The priestly thread ends at 24:50–53 in a blessing and a temple, and the prophetic thread ends at 23:50–51 in Joseph's waiting and at 24:44 in "the Prophets" fulfilled. Hold chs. 1–2 open against chs. 23–24: **the book ends by doing what it opened by promising.** `[I]`

**Dig in hand — Luke 19:11–27 (12 July 2026).** It was written before the `_texts/` corpus existed, from the ESV recalled, and so is `[S]` here. Its findings were reconciled against the text-first draft as follows:

- *The close of the journey section is disputed (19:27/28/44/48).* **Agrees**; this Draft takes 19:27/28 without depending on it.

- *Dan 7:13–14 behind the nobleman's investiture — moderate–high.* **Kept at that level.** The overview's Daniel rows rest on 21:27 and 22:69, where the allusion is verbal, not on the parable.
- *"Purpose-prefaces are characteristic of Luke" (15:3; 18:1, 9; 19:11).* **Agrees** — see Preaching Trap 2.

**Commentaries.** None was opened in this run.
