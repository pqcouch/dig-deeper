Dig Deeper: 2 Corinthians 2:14–4:6

Primary texts: SBLGNT (Greek NT, with its word index); NA28 text and apparatus (Logos export) for every reading that is cited; WLC Hebrew; Swete LXX for observation and Rahlfs LXX (Logos export) for citation, for Exod 4:10; 31:18; 32:15–16; 33:18–23; 34:1, 10–35; Deut 9:9; 29:3; Jer 17:1; 31:31–34 (LXX 38:31–34); Ezek 11:19–20; 20:34, 41; 36:25–27; Gen 1:3–4; Isa 9:1 (LXX 9:2); Prov 3:3; 7:3 Study text: NASB95 Pulpit text: None — no sermon in view (study run) Date: 13 October 2026 Book-overview context: None. It is the first of three pieces of further work named at the end of Torah/tripartite-law-paper.md (§7). The open question it was asked to test: in 3:7, 11, 13 and 14, what is “being set aside” — the covenant’s administration, or its content? The paper’s own reading (§5.3) and the Exodus 32–34 dig were held back until the checking stage (Phase 5.5).

Extent. 2:14 to 4:6, set at the letter’s own seams. 2:14 breaks off the travel narrative mid-sentence; 4:7 opens the “treasure in clay jars” unit, and the veil and glory vocabulary does not return after 4:6. The material from 3:1 to 3:18 alone would have cut away two things. One is the “sufficiency” question of 2:16, which 3:5–6 answers. The other is the veil of 4:3–4 and the face of 4:6, which close the argument of chapter 3.

Headline Findings

  1. 3:3 fuses two Old Testament images into one antithesis, and both sources say that God’s commands continue under the new covenant: what changes is the surface they are written on and who writes them. [T] for the wording; [I] for the synthesis. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Original Languages. πλαξὶν λιθίναις (“tablets of stone”) is the Greek Old Testament’s phrase for the Decalogue tablets. It occurs in eight verses (Exod 31:18; 32:15; 34:1, 4; Deut 4:13; 10:1, 3; 3 Reigns 8:9, Swete). καρδίαις σαρκίναις (“hearts of flesh”) is Ezekiel’s phrase: in the whole Greek Old Testament the heart is called σάρκινος only at Ezek 11:19 and 36:26, where God removes the heart of stone. Paul takes Ezekiel’s stone and moves it from the heart to the tablets. Each source then says what the new heart is for. Ezekiel: “that they may walk in my statutes and keep my ordinances and do them” (Ezek 11:20; 36:27: in 11:20, בְּחֻקֹּתַי יֵלֵכוּ וְאֶת־מִשְׁפָּטַי יִשְׁמְרוּ). Jeremiah: “I will put my torah within them, and on their heart I will write it” (31:33: נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם וְעַל־לִבָּם אֶכְתֲּבֶנָּה). The old and new are contrasted by material (ink and stone against Spirit and heart) and by writer, not by what is written. High confidence on the wording; moderate–high on the synthesis.

  2. On the open question, the grammar names three different things as “being set aside”, and the one thing never named is “the law”. [T] for the grammar; [I] for the conclusion. Surfaced by: Tool 4, Tool 7, Tool 8, Tool 10, Original Languages, Textual Variants.

    • At 3:7, τὴν καταργουμένην is feminine and agrees with δόξαν: what is being set aside is the glory of Moses’ face.
    • At 3:11, τὸ καταργούμενον is a neuter substantive set against τὸ μένον, “what remains”. It summarises the comparison of 3:7–10, so it is the old ministry (διακονία) as a whole.
    • At 3:14, the subject of καταργεῖται is genuinely disputed. It is either the veil or the old covenant, and the two editions punctuate the verse differently.
    • The noun νόμος (“law”) occurs nowhere in 2 Corinthians (SBLGNT, by lemma; the same search returns its occurrences in Romans).

    The thing set aside is described as a ministry (διακονία ×5 in the passage), and it is the ministry “carved in letters on stones” (3:7). So the passage sets aside the stone-tablet administration itself, the Decalogue as administered on stone included. Meanwhile “Moses” goes on being read, and in Christ he is read with the veil removed (3:14–16). On the passage’s own terms the answer is the administration, not the content. One qualification matters: Paul gives that administration no reprieve on the ground that its words were good. He calls it “the ministry of death” and “of condemnation”. Moderate–high confidence.

  3. Paul’s argument reads the Greek of Exodus 34 and depends on it at three points. It also adds three things neither the Hebrew nor the Greek of Exodus says. [T] Surfaced by: Tool 8 (Hebrew/Greek triage), Tool 11 Move 1, Original Languages.

    • What Paul takes from the Greek.
      • The Hebrew says the skin of Moses’ face קָרַן (“sent out rays”, a verb used in this sense only in Exod 34:29, 30, 35). The Greek turns this into δεδόξασται, “had been glorified”. Paul’s δόξα vocabulary (δόξα and δοξάζω together 15× in 3:7–4:6) and his δεδόξασται at 3:10 come from that rendering.
      • περιαιρεῖται τὸ κάλυμμα (3:16) is Exod 34:34’s verb.
      • ἐπιστρέψῃ (“turns”, 3:16) is the verb the Greek uses at 34:31, where Aaron and the leaders turn to Moses (Hebrew וַיָּשֻׁבוּ).
    • What Paul adds. Neither text says:
      • that the glory was being set aside (καταργουμένην);
      • that Israel “could not gaze” (Exodus says they were afraid to come near, וַיִּירְאוּ מִגֶּשֶׁת אֵלָיו);
      • what the veil was for (3:13 πρὸς τὸ μή, “so that … not”).

    Each of these is Paul’s inference from the text, and the report treats them as interpretation, not quotation. High confidence.

  4. The Moses comparison begins at 2:16, not 3:7. [T] for the wording; [I] for the allusion. Surfaced by: Tool 4, Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11. τίς ἱκανός; (“who is sufficient?”, 2:16) is answered at 3:5–6 by ἱκανοί… ἱκανότης… ἱκάνωσεν. These are the only uses of ἱκανότης and the only use of ἱκανόω in 2 Corinthians (SBLGNT). In the whole Greek Old Testament the phrase ἱκανός εἰμι occurs once: Moses’ objection at the burning bush, οὐχ ἱκανός εἰμι, “I am not sufficient” (Exod 4:10, Swete). Paul presents his ministry as one made sufficient by God in Moses’ position, before he ever names Moses. Moderate confidence that the echo is intended.

  5. The passage ends with new creation, and with the glory moved from Moses’ face to Christ’s. [T] Surfaced by: Tool 3, Tool 10, Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Textual Variants. In 4:6, ὁ εἰπών· Ἐκ σκότους φῶς λάμψει combines Gen 1:3–4 (God speaks, light, separated from darkness) with the wording of Isa 9:1 in Greek (MT 9:1 = Swete 9:2: φῶς λάμψει ἐφ᾽ ὑμᾶς, “light will shine on you”), which matches exactly. πρόσωπον (“face”) occurs five times in the passage: Moses’ face twice (3:7), Moses’ veiled face (3:13), the believers’ unveiled face (3:18), and Christ’s face (4:6). The glory that the sons of Israel could not look at on Moses’ face is now seen “in the face of Christ”. High confidence.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level [I] (no overview exists; inferred from the letter itself). Paul writes to a church that has questioned his reliability:

  • his change of travel plans (1:15–2:4) has been read as fickleness (“yes and no”, 1:17);
  • his letters have been weighed against his presence (1:13; 10:10);
  • by chapters 10–13, rivals who “commend themselves” (10:12) and claim Jewish credentials (11:22: “Are they Hebrews? So am I”) are drawing the church away.

The letter’s purpose is to re-establish the Corinthians’ confidence in the gospel by re-establishing the character of the ministry that brought it to them. That ministry is weak in appearance and glorious in reality.

Passage-level [T]. 2:14–4:6 answers the question the passage itself asks: “who is sufficient for these things?” (2:16). The answer comes in three steps:

  1. Paul’s credentials are the Corinthians themselves, written by the Spirit (3:1–3).
  2. His sufficiency is God’s gift, as minister of a new covenant (3:4–6).
  3. That covenant’s ministry outshines Moses’ in glory, in permanence and in openness (3:7–4:6).

The passage serves the book’s purpose by grounding Paul’s boldness (3:12, παρρησία) in the nature of the covenant he serves, not in his own adequacy.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 1:1–2:13 sets up four things.

  • The question of Paul’s sincerity (εἰλικρινείᾳ, 1:12).
  • His letters. He insists he writes nothing beyond what they can read and understand (1:13), and he refers repeatedly to the painful letter (ἔγραψα, 2:3, 4, 9).
  • The Spirit “in our hearts”. God has given “the Spirit in our hearts as a pledge” (1:22: τὸν ἀρραβῶνα τοῦ πνεύματος ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις ἡμῶν).
  • Satan’s schemes. “We are not ignorant of his designs” (2:11: αὐτοῦ τὰ νοήματα).

Then the travel narrative breaks off mid-journey. At Troas Paul “had no rest” (2:13, οὐκ ἔσχηκα ἄνεσιν) and left for Macedonia. The narrative resumes at 7:5 with the same noun: “when we came into Macedonia our flesh had no rest” (οὐδεμίαν ἔσχηκεν ἄνεσιν). ἄνεσις occurs only at 2:13, 7:5 and 8:13 in the letter (SBLGNT). So 2:14–7:4 is set between the two halves of one journey [T].

Necessity answer. The passage exists here because the preceding movement has raised two questions that now need answering [I]:

  • Is Paul sufficient? His conduct and his letters have been questioned.
  • What written thing authorises a ministry? His opponents carry letters (3:1).

Paul answers at the point where his narrative shows him at his weakest (“no rest”). He turns at once to thanksgiving for God’s triumph (2:14), and the contrast is the argument: the sufficiency is “from God” (3:5), not from the restless man on the road to Macedonia. The letters theme of 1:13 and 2:3–9 leads directly into “you are our letter” (3:2). The Spirit-in-hearts pledge of 1:22 is unfolded at 3:3, written “with the Spirit of the living God … on tablets of hearts of flesh”. Satan’s “designs” (2:11) return as the blinding of “minds” at 4:4 (τὰ νοήματα again).

Implication for this passage. 2:14–4:6 is not a free-standing essay on the two covenants that Paul happened to insert. It is a defence of a ministry, and it brings in the covenant contrast to explain why his ministry needs no letter of recommendation and why it can be bold. That changes how the open question should be put. Paul’s subject is two ministries (διακονία ×5 in the passage), not two bodies of law. The covenant contrast is the ground of the ministry contrast. Moderate–high confidence.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 finds no use of Exodus, Jeremiah or Ezekiel earlier in the letter. All three return later (Ezekiel and the new-covenant formula at 6:16–17; Exodus at 8:15). So Paul introduces these sources here, at the passage that defines his ministry, and returns to them when he applies it. This confirms the positional answer: this is the letter’s foundational statement of what the ministry is.

General context. Immediately before: Troas and Macedonia (2:12–13). Immediately after: the treasure in clay jars (4:7–15). It keeps the vocabulary of glory, light and ministry and turns from the glory of the ministry to the weakness of the minister. Section: the ministry defence of 2:14–7:4.

Structural devices [T]:

  • Bookends.
    • “Commend ourselves”: ἑαυτοὺς συνιστάνειν (3:1) and συνιστάνοντες ἑαυτούς (4:2).
    • “The perishing”: ἐν τοῖς ἀπολλυμένοις (2:15) and ἐν τοῖς ἀπολλυμένοις (4:3). These are the only two uses of ἀπόλλυμι in the passage, and they open and close it.
    • Glory, Christ and knowledge: “the knowledge of him” (2:14, γνώσεως) and “the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Christ” (4:6, γνώσεως).
  • Hinge. 3:12, ἔχοντες οὖν, “having therefore”: the comparison (3:7–11) turns into conduct (3:12–4:6).
  • Recurring image. Writing runs through it: letter, written, ink, tablets, letter (γράμμα), “engraved in letters”. So does seeing: gaze, face, veil, unveiled, mirror, image, blinded, light.

  • 3:7 opens with an evidential “if”: the premise is granted, not hypothetical. ὥστε (“so that”) introduces the result. τὴν καταργουμένην agrees with τὴν δόξαν.

  • 3:9 is a “for” clause grounding 3:8, with its own main clause (“much more…”).
  • 3:10 is a “for” clause explaining the “much more”.
  • 3:11 is the third ground, with the third main clause.

Load-bearing clauses. There are three assertions, all statements of truth, not commands:

  1. The ministry of the Spirit will be in glory (3:8).
  2. The ministry of righteousness abounds in glory (3:9).
  3. What remains is in glory (3:11).

Each concedes the old ministry’s glory in a granted “if” and then exceeds it. The three “if” clauses never deny the old ministry its glory [T]. 3:10 is the only verse that says it “has not been glorified”, and it explains at once: “in this respect, because of the glory that surpasses it”.

4. Linking Words

  • 2:14 δέ (“but”) turns from “no rest” (2:13) to thanksgiving. It is a contrast, not a continuation [T].
  • 2:15 ὅτι (“for”) grounds the aroma image.
  • 2:16 καὶ πρὸς ταῦτα τίς ἱκανός; (“and for these things, who is sufficient?”). The question governs everything to 3:6.
  • 2:17 γάρ (“for”) gives the implied answer: we are, because we do not peddle the word.
  • 3:5 οὐχ ὅτι … ἀλλ᾽ (“not that … but”) corrects at once: not from ourselves, but from God.
  • 3:6 γάρ (“for the letter kills”) is the reason the ministry is “not of letter but of Spirit”.
  • 3:7, 9, 11 εἰ … πῶς οὐχὶ μᾶλλον / πολλῷ μᾶλλον (“if … how much more”). These are three evidential “if” clauses, true premises and not hypotheses, each followed by a lesser-to-greater argument. Paul grants the premise every time [T].
  • 3:9, 10, 11 γάρ ×3 chains the reasons.
  • 3:12 οὖν (“therefore”) draws the conduct from the comparison.
  • 3:14 ἀλλά (“but”) turns from Moses’ act to Israel’s hardening. Paul puts the fault in the “minds”, not in Moses.
  • 3:14 γάρ explains the hardening by the continuing veil.
  • 3:14 ὅτι is either causal (“because in Christ it is set aside”) or content (“[it not being revealed] that in Christ it is set aside”). The two editions differ (see Tool 8 and Textual Variants).
  • 3:16 δέ (“but”) is the reversal: the turn to the Lord.
  • 3:17 δέ (“now”) explains: the Lord of the Exodus quotation is the Spirit.
  • 3:18 δέ (“but we all”) contrasts us with them.
  • 4:1 διὰ τοῦτο (“therefore”).
  • 4:3 εἰ δὲ καί (“and even if”) concedes that the gospel is veiled to some.
  • 4:5 γάρ, 4:6 ὅτι ground it twice: we preach Christ, because the Creator has shone in our hearts.

5. Parallels

The passage is not Hebrew poetry, but it is built from sustained antithetical pairs. They are listed because they are the argument, and each pair’s second member interprets its first [T]:

Old New Verse
ink Spirit of the living God 3:3
tablets of stone tablets that are hearts of flesh 3:3
letter (γράμμα) Spirit 3:6
kills gives life 3:6
ministry of death ministry of the Spirit 3:7–8
ministry of condemnation ministry of righteousness 3:9
what is being set aside what remains 3:11
veiled face (Moses) unveiled face (we all) 3:13, 18
minds hardened being transformed 3:14, 18

The pairs show what is being contrasted [I]. On the “old” side Paul never lists contents (commandments, statutes, sacrifices). He lists a medium (ink, stone, letter), an effect (death, condemnation) and a duration (being set aside). On the new side he lists a writer (the Spirit), a surface (hearts), an effect (life, righteousness) and a duration (remaining). This is a parallel between two administrations of God’s word. It is not a parallel between two sets of commandments.

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A — epistle, not narrative. The closest equivalent is the gloss at 3:17, “Now the Lord is the Spirit”. Paul steps out of his reading of Exod 34:34 to say who “the Lord” is. It works like a narrator’s decoder (compare John 2:21) and governs how 3:16 is to be read [T].

7. Vocabulary

καταργέω (“render inoperative, set aside, abolish”): 3:7, 11, 13, 14 [T]

  • Distribution. Four occurrences in 2 Corinthians, all in these eight verses (SBLGNT, by lemma). The verb occurs 27 times in the New Testament, and all but two (Luke 13:7; Heb 2:14) are in the Pauline letters.
  • Range in Paul. Paul uses it of things that are evil and destroyed: death, “the last enemy” (1 Cor 15:26); “every rule and authority” (15:24); “the body of sin” (Rom 6:6). He also uses it of things that are good but provisional, set aside when something greater arrives:
    • prophecy and knowledge “will be set aside” (1 Cor 13:8);
    • “when the perfect comes, the partial will be set aside” (13:10);
    • “I set aside childish things” (13:11).
  • “Fade”. In none of its Pauline occurrences does it mean “fade” (see Tool 8 on the NASB95).
  • In the Greek Old Testament it occurs only in the Aramaic chapters of Ezra (Swete 2 Esdras 4:21, 23; 5:5; 6:8), of halting the temple rebuilding. So the verb is not drawn from Exodus: it is Paul’s own word, laid over the Exodus story.
  • In this passage, the Corinthians’ earlier letter (1 Cor 13:8–13) supplies the closest analogy. A good thing, given by God, is set aside when the greater, permanent thing comes. 1 Cor 13 even sets the same verb against μένει (“remains”, 13:13), as 2 Cor 3:11 does. High confidence on the lexical data; moderate–high on the 1 Cor 13 analogy.

διακονία / διάκονος (“ministry, service / minister”): 3:3 (participle), 3:6, 7, 8, 9 (×2), 4:1 [T] The noun occurs five times in the passage (3:7, 8, 9 ×2; 4:1) and twelve times in the letter (SBLGNT). The chain continues to “the ministry of reconciliation” (5:18). The noun Paul repeats is ministry, not covenant: διαθήκη occurs only twice (3:6, 14) and νόμος not at all. The comparison is between the ministry that administers each covenant. High confidence.

γράμμα (“letter, written thing”): 3:6 (×2), 3:7 [T] Paul uses the word seven times (Rom 2:27, 29; 7:6; 2 Cor 3:6 ×2, 7; Gal 6:11, SBLGNT). Three times it stands directly opposite πνεῦμα:

  • “circumcision of the heart, by the Spirit, not by the letter” (Rom 2:29);
  • “serve in newness of the Spirit, not in oldness of the letter” (Rom 7:6);
  • “not of letter but of Spirit” (2 Cor 3:6).

In each of those three, γράμμα is the Mosaic code as written and external, set against the Spirit’s inward work. In 3:7 the plural γράμμασιν (“letters”) is concrete: the engraved letters on the stones. High confidence.

πλάξ (“tablet”) and λίθινος (“of stone”): 3:3 [T] πλάξ occurs three times in the New Testament: twice here, and once at Heb 9:4, αἱ πλάκες τῆς διαθήκης (“the tablets of the covenant”). See Tool 11 for the eight Greek Old Testament verses that contain πλάκες λίθιναι, all of them about the Decalogue tablets. High confidence.

σάρκινος (“of flesh”): 3:3 [T] Paul does not mean “carnal” here: elsewhere he does use σάρκινος negatively, of the “fleshly” person (1 Cor 3:1; Rom 7:14). This use is positive, and the reason is Ezekiel, where “flesh” is the living heart God gives in place of stone. See Tool 8 on how the English loses this. High confidence.

ἱκανός / ἱκανότης / ἱκανόω (“sufficient / sufficiency / make sufficient”): 2:16; 3:5 (×2); 3:6 [T] The word family forms a chain that frames 2:16–3:6. ἱκανότης occurs only here in the New Testament; ἱκανόω occurs only here and at Col 1:12. See Tool 11 for Exod 4:10. High confidence on the chain.

κάλυμμα / ἀνακαλύπτω / καλύπτω (“veil / unveil / veil”): 3:13–16, 18; 4:3 [T] κάλυμμα occurs four times, at 3:13, 14, 15 and 16, and nowhere else in the New Testament. ἀνακαλύπτω occurs at 3:14 and 3:18 only. καλύπτω occurs twice in 4:3. The image moves from Moses’ face (3:13), to the reading of the old covenant (3:14), to the hearers’ heart (3:15), to its removal (3:16), to the believers’ unveiled face (3:18), and finally to the gospel veiled to the perishing (4:3). The veil moves: it starts on Moses’ face and ends on the hearers’ hearts. High confidence.

νόημα (“mind, design, thought”): 3:14; 4:4 [T] Five of its six New Testament occurrences are in 2 Corinthians: 2:11 (Satan’s designs); 3:14 (their minds hardened); 4:4 (the god of this age has blinded the minds); 10:5 (every thought taken captive); 11:3 (your minds corrupted, as the serpent deceived Eve). See Tool 11, Move 4. High confidence.

μεταμορφόομαι (“be transformed”): 3:18 [T] It occurs four times in the New Testament. Twice it describes the Transfiguration (Matt 17:2; Mark 9:2): in Matthew, Jesus’ “face shone like the sun” and Moses appears with him. It also occurs at Rom 12:2 (“be transformed by the renewing of your mind”) and here. Positive control: the lemma is filed under the middle form μεταμορφόομαι; a search on μεταμορφόω returned nothing, and the corrected search was confirmed against 2 Cor 3:18 itself before the count was taken. High confidence on the count; the Transfiguration resonance is canonical reflection, not a claim about Paul’s intention.

Proper-noun inventory. Christ (×9), God (×14), Moses (3:7, 13, 15), the sons of Israel (3:7, 13), the Lord (3:16–18; 4:5), Jesus (4:5). The canonical freight sits on two of them:

  • Moses, as the mediator of Sinai, whose face shone. Freight is high; see Tool 11.
  • “The sons of Israel”, which is Exodus’s own designation for the people at Sinai (Exod 34:30, 32, 34, 35 in the Hebrew). Freight is routine here; Paul is simply quoting the story’s own term.

8. Translations

There is no pulpit text, so the NASB95 (study text) is compared with the ESV, and the KJV is brought in where it is the historic witness.

3:7, 11, 13 — καταργέω: “fading” (NASB95) against “being brought to an end” (ESV). This divergence bears directly on the open question.

  • The NASB95’s “fading as it was … that which fades away … the end of what was fading away” makes the glory a light that dims over time. The KJV’s “which glory was to be done away” and the ESV’s “being brought to an end” keep the sense of abolition.
  • The Greek supports the ESV. Paul never uses καταργέω for fading (Tool 7), and Exodus never says the shine faded (Tool 11).
  • “Fade” turns Paul’s theological verdict (set aside, by God, because something greater has come) into a description of a physical process. A reader of the NASB95 will hear that the old covenant was dimming on its own. Paul says it was being set aside.
  • On this verb the study text is less accurate than the ESV. High confidence.

3:3 — καρδίαις σαρκίναις: “human hearts” (NASB95 and ESV) against “fleshy tables of the heart” (KJV).

  • Both modern versions level σαρκίναις to “human”. That removes the word that links the verse to Ezekiel’s “heart of flesh” in place of “heart of stone”. The KJV keeps “fleshy”.
  • A preacher working from either modern version will not see that 3:3 contrasts stone with flesh.
  • This is a choice that obscures an allusion (Tool 8’s checklist) [T]. High confidence.

3:14 — “because it is removed in Christ” (NASB95) against “because only through Christ is it taken away” (ESV).

  • Both take the veil as the subject of καταργεῖται. The ESV adds “only”, which is not in the Greek.
  • The two Greek editions punctuate differently:
    • The SBLGNT puts a comma after ἀνακαλυπτόμενον: “the veil remains unlifted, because in Christ it is set aside”.
    • NA28 puts a comma after μένει: “the veil remains, it not being revealed that in Christ it is being set aside”. That allows the old covenant to be the subject.
  • Early manuscripts carry no such punctuation, so the decision is editorial. A reader of either English version meets one option presented as the only one. See Textual Variants and Difficult Verses. High confidence that the subject is contested; moderate on which reading is right.

4:6 — “Light shall shine out of darkness” (NASB95) against “Let light shine out of darkness” (ESV).

  • The NASB95 keeps the future λάμψει, “shall shine”, which is the wording of Isa 9:1 in Greek.
  • The ESV renders it as a command, which points the reader to Gen 1:3 alone.
  • The KJV’s “commanded the light to shine” follows the Byzantine infinitive λάμψαι.
  • The NASB95 is the more accurate here, and it is the only one of the three that lets the reader hear Isaiah. High confidence.

2:16; 3:5–6 — ἱκανός: “adequate / adequacy / made us adequate” (NASB95) against “sufficient / sufficiency / made us sufficient” (ESV). Both keep the repeated root, so neither breaks the chain. The ESV’s “sufficient” sits closer to the English of Exod 4:10 in older versions, but nothing turns on the difference.

Ancient versions check.

  • The Vulgate at 3:13 (ut non intenderent filii Israhel in faciem eius quod evacuatur, “so that the sons of Israel would not gaze at the face of what was being done away”) reads “face” for τέλος (“end”), together with Codex Alexandrinus (NA28: πρόσωπον A b f* vg). This is a Translation-tradition split that starts as a textual variant (see Textual Variants). The Vulgate tradition reads 3:13 as Israel being prevented from seeing the face. The Greek text reads it as their being prevented from seeing the end (or goal, τέλος) of what was being set aside. The τέλος reading carries Paul’s point: the old glory had an end, and Israel’s veiled minds did not see it. Moderate confidence on how to weigh the Vulgate’s evidence; high that the split exists.
  • The Vulgate at 3:7 renders καταργουμένην as quae evacuatur, “which is being emptied out, made void”. That agrees with abolition, not fading. Moderate confidence (recalled).

9. Tone and Feel

Confident, combative and exultant, with grief at the centre.

  • Thanksgiving. The passage opens on a triumphal procession (2:14). The image is one of victory, but Paul is in it as a captive led along: θριαμβεύοντι ἡμᾶς, God “leading us in triumph” (see Historical Background).
  • Combat. It turns sharp at 2:17 (“not like the many, peddling the word”) and 3:1 (“as some”). These are rivals, not abstractions.
  • Exultation. The three “how much more” comparisons (3:7–11) build like a crescendo; δόξα and δοξάζω sound 15 times in 3:7–4:6.
  • Grief. At 3:14–15 the register drops: “but their minds were hardened … to this day … a veil lies over their heart”. Paul is speaking of his own people (11:22; compare Rom 9:1–3), and the doubled “to this day” (ἄχρι τῆς σήμερον ἡμέρας … ἕως σήμερον) is lament, not triumph.
  • Wonder. It rises again into 3:18 and ends in creation-light (4:6).

Soundtrack. A brass fanfare that falls to a solo cello at 3:14–15, then a full string swell into dawn. Do not preach 3:14–15 triumphantly: the text grieves there [I].

Exodus 34:27–35 (with 31:18; 32:15–16; 34:1) → 2 Cor 3:3, 7–18 (high confidence: sustained verbal allusion with an adapted quotation at 3:16) [T]

Source context.

  • The setting. Exodus 34 is the covenant remade after the covenant broken. The first tablets were “the work of God”, the writing “the writing of God, engraved on the tablets” (32:16: מִכְתַּב אֱלֹהִים הוּא חָרוּת עַל־הַלֻּחֹת). They were “written by the finger of God” (31:18). Moses smashed them at the calf (32:19; 34:1, “which you broke”). In ch. 33 Moses intercedes and asks “show me your glory” (33:18), and he is shown God’s back, not his face (33:20–23).
  • The remade covenant. In ch. 34 YHWH proclaims his name (34:6–7), makes a covenant (34:10) and gives its terms (34:11–26):
    • no covenant with the land’s inhabitants;
    • YHWH alone (“you shall not bow to another god”, 34:14);
    • no molten gods (34:17);
    • Unleavened Bread (34:18);
    • the firstborn;
    • the Sabbath (“six days you shall work, on the seventh you shall rest, even in ploughing and harvest”, 34:21);
    • Weeks and Ingathering;
    • three pilgrim appearances;
    • Passover;
    • firstfruits;
    • and no boiling a kid in its mother’s milk (34:26).
    Then: “Write these words, for according to these words I have made a covenant with you and with Israel” (34:27). Moses is forty days on the mountain, and “he wrote on the tablets the words of the covenant, the ten words” (34:28).
  • The face. Moses comes down not knowing that his face shines. Aaron and the people are afraid to come near (34:29–30). He calls them and speaks; afterwards he veils. Whenever he goes in before YHWH he removes the veil; he comes out and speaks the commandments unveiled; then he veils again (34:31–35).
  • The register is awe after grace. A covenant broken has been remade, and the mediator bears the reflected glory of the God who forgave.
  • What the cited verses do in their own passage. 34:29–35 is the narrative seal on the renewal. The mediator’s face authenticates the renewed covenant and its words. The veil manages the people’s fear; it is not concealment. The text gives it no stated purpose at all.

A finding from Move 1 that bears on the open question [T]. The “words” by which Exodus says the covenant was made (34:27) are, in their own context, the list of 34:11–26. That list is dominated by worship, festival, firstborn and Sabbath law. Exodus 34 thus presents the covenant renewal Paul alludes to as constituted chiefly by commandments a later taxonomy would call “ceremonial”, with “the ten words” written on the tablets alongside them (34:28). A neat division between a moral Decalogue and a ceremonial remainder is foreign to the source passage Paul is reading. High confidence on the source content; the inference for the tripartite debate is [I].

Book usage. There is no earlier use of Exodus in 2 Corinthians. Exodus returns once, at 8:15 (Exod 16:18, the manna). In the Corinthian correspondence, 1 Cor 10:1–11 has already read the wilderness generation “typically” (τυπικῶς), ending “written for our instruction, on whom the ends (τὰ τέλη) of the ages have come” (10:11). Paul is therefore following an established Corinthian habit of reading Exodus as written for the church. The noun τέλος appears in both passages: the “ends of the ages” have come (1 Cor 10:11), and Israel did not see “the end” (τὸ τέλος) of what was being set aside (2 Cor 3:13). [T] for the shared noun; [I] for any intended link. Moderate confidence.

OT-to-OT. Exodus 34 already stands in canonical conversation with both of the other sources. With Jeremiah 31:

  • Jeremiah’s new covenant is “not like the covenant I made with their fathers in the day I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt, my covenant which they broke” (31:32: אֲשֶׁר־הֵמָּה הֵפֵרוּ אֶת־בְּרִיתִי). The broken Sinai covenant is the one Exod 32–34 narrates: broken at the calf, remade in ch. 34.
  • Jeremiah’s remedy uses Exodus’s own verb: God will write (כָּתַב) his torah, as he wrote on the tablets (Exod 31:18; 32:15–16; 34:1, 28). But he will write on hearts.
  • Jeremiah himself has already joined tablet and heart: “the sin of Judah is written with an iron pen … engraved on the tablet of their heart” (17:1: חֲרוּשָׁה עַל־לוּחַ לִבָּם). The engraving verb differs from Exodus’s (Jeremiah’s חָרַשׁ against Exodus’s חָרַת at 32:16, which is a different root), but the image is the same. The tablet-and-heart pairing occurs elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible only in Prov 3:3 and 7:3 (WLC, by lemma: three verses in all). Jer 17:1–4 is absent from the Greek, so this link exists only in the Hebrew.
  • So within Jeremiah the heart-tablet first carries engraved sin (17:1) and then God’s written torah (31:33).

With Ezekiel, see the Ezekiel entry below: Ezekiel’s “stone” is the heart, Exodus’s stone is the tablet, and Paul joins them.

What it adds.

  • The Exod 34 frame explains why Paul’s foil is a ministry and a face, not a code. The glory in Exodus 34 is on the mediator’s face, and Moses’ role is to stand between God and a fearful people. Paul’s comparison is between mediators: Moses and himself as “minister of a new covenant” (3:6).
  • It explains the boldness (παρρησία, 3:12). Paul ministers without needing a veil for the people’s fear, because the glory now shines “in our hearts” (4:6).
  • It makes Paul’s additions visible as additions. Exodus does not say the glory was being set aside, that the people could not look, or why the veil was worn. A preacher should present these as Paul’s apostolic reading of Exodus, not as Exodus’s plain sense.

Jeremiah 31:31–34 (LXX 38:31–34) → 2 Cor 3:3, 6, 14 (high confidence: distinctive phrase plus conceptual match) [T]

Source context.

  • The setting. Jeremiah 30–33 (the “book of consolation”) follows long chapters of judgement and exile. Here, uniquely in the Hebrew Bible, God promises “a new covenant” (31:31: בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה). The co-occurrence of covenant and new is confined to this verse in the WLC; in the Greek Old Testament, διαθήκη καινή occurs only at Jer 38:31 (Swete).
  • What the covenant is set against. It is defined against the Exodus covenant “which they broke” (31:32).
  • What it contains:
    • “I will put my torah within them and on their heart I will write it” (31:33);
    • the covenant formula, “I will be their God and they shall be my people” (31:33);
    • universal knowledge of YHWH (31:34);
    • forgiveness: “I will forgive their iniquity, and their sin I will remember no more” (31:34).
  • The register is tender restoration after catastrophe.
  • What 31:33 does in its own passage. It is the hinge. The new covenant differs from the old not in having a different torah (“my torah”, the same God’s instruction) but in where it is written and in its result (they will know me) and ground (forgiveness).

Book usage.

  • No earlier use of Jeremiah in 2 Corinthians. Jer 9:23 [EVV 9:24] is cited later (10:17), on boasting, as it was at 1 Cor 1:31.
  • In the Corinthian correspondence, “the new covenant” first reached the church in the Supper tradition: “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (1 Cor 11:25: ἡ καινὴ διαθήκη). The Corinthians already knew the phrase as a liturgical reality bound to Christ’s death. In 2 Cor 3:6 Paul makes it the definition of his ministry.
  • The covenant formula returns at 6:16. “I will be their God and they shall be my people” (2 Cor 6:16) is the Jeremiah and Ezekiel formula (Jer 31:33; Ezek 11:20; 36:28; 37:27). It is cited in the form closest to Lev 26:12 and Ezek 37:27. Paul is following the new-covenant promise from its definition here (3:6) to its application in the church as temple (6:16) [T] for the shared formula. Moderate–high confidence.

OT-to-OT. See the Exodus entry above: Jeremiah 31 is already a reading of the broken Exodus covenant. Jeremiah and Ezekiel also echo each other:

  • the heart (לֵב) as the place of renewal (Jer 31:33; Ezek 11:19; 36:26);
  • the covenant formula (Jer 31:33; Ezek 11:20; 36:28);
  • God himself acting inwardly (Jer: “I will write”; Ezek: “I will give … I will remove … I will put my Spirit … I will cause you to walk”).

Ezekiel adds what Jeremiah does not name: the Spirit (36:27, רוּחִי, “my Spirit”). Paul’s “Spirit of the living God” (3:3) and “the Spirit gives life” (3:6) come from Ezekiel’s side of the conversation. His “new covenant” comes from Jeremiah’s. So Paul is not combining two unrelated texts: he is relying on a conversation already running between two exilic prophets.

What it adds.

  • 3:6’s καινῆς διαθήκης is Jeremiah’s phrase and nobody else’s. That makes Jeremiah’s definition of the new covenant the controlling one, and Jeremiah defines it as the same God’s torah written on the heart. On the open question, the prophetic source Paul names says the content is kept and the medium changes.
  • A divergence worth knowing about (category 3 for Hebrews, not for Paul). The Greek renders “my torah” (singular) as νόμους μου, “my laws” (plural), and Heb 8:10 and 10:16 quote that Greek. Paul does not cite the clause, and he never uses νόμος in this letter. So nothing in 2 Cor 3 turns on the singular or plural.

Ezekiel 11:19–20; 36:26–27 → 2 Cor 3:3, 6 (high confidence: distinctive word, σάρκινος of the heart, plus Spirit and stone) [T]

Source context.

  • Ezek 11:14–21 answers the Jerusalemites’ claim that the exiles are far from YHWH and have lost the land. God promises to be a sanctuary to the scattered, to gather them, and then: “I will give them one heart, and a new spirit I will put within them; I will remove the heart of stone from their flesh and give them a heart of flesh, so that they may walk in my statutes and keep my ordinances and do them; and they shall be my people and I will be their God” (11:19–20).
  • Ezek 36:16–38 answers the profaning of God’s name among the nations. God will act “for my holy name’s sake” (36:22). He will gather and cleanse (“I will sprinkle clean water on you … from all your idols I will cleanse you”, 36:25). Then: “a new heart I will give you and a new spirit I will put within you; I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh. And my Spirit I will put within you, and I will cause you to walk in my statutes and keep my ordinances and do them” (36:26–27).
  • The register. In the first oracle, consolation to the despised. In the second, God’s zeal for his name.
  • What the cited verses do. In both oracles they are the climax. The heart-change is the means; walking in “my statutes and my ordinances” (חֻקֹּתַי … מִשְׁפָּטַי) is the stated end.

Book usage.

  • No earlier use of Ezekiel in 2 Corinthians. Ezekiel returns at 6:16–17: the covenant formula of Ezek 37:27 at 6:16, and “I will welcome you” of Ezek 20:34 at 6:17 (Greek εἰσδέξομαι, which matches Ezek 20:34 in Swete).
  • That same Ezekiel 20 joins “I will welcome you” to “with a pleasing aroma I will accept you” (20:41: ἐν ὀσμῇ εὐωδίας προσδέξομαι ὑμᾶς). The aroma language of 2:14–16 may therefore belong to the same Ezekiel cluster (see the moderate allusions below).
  • Paul thus uses Ezekiel’s restoration oracles at the definition of his ministry (3:3) and again at its application (6:16–17).

OT-to-OT.

  • With Exodus. Ezekiel’s “heart of stone” (לֵב הָאֶבֶן) and Exodus’s “tablets of stone” (31:18: לֻחֹת אֶבֶן; 34:1: לֻחֹת אֲבָנִים) share the word for stone but not the object. In the Hebrew Bible, stone (אֶבֶן) and heart (לֵב) stand together in four verses only (WLC, by lemma): Ezek 11:19; 36:26; 1 Sam 25:37 (Nabal’s heart “became as stone”); Job 41:16 [EVV 41:24] (Leviathan’s heart “hard as stone”). So the stony heart is distinctively Ezekiel’s image of Israel. Paul’s antithesis puts stone tablets opposite hearts of flesh, joining Exodus’s stone to Ezekiel’s flesh. The implied link: the stone tablets were given to a people with stone hearts, and the new covenant changes the heart [I].
  • With Jeremiah. See the Jeremiah entry above.

What it adds.

  • The Spirit as writer. It explains why “Spirit” stands opposite “ink” and “letter”.
  • The heart as surface. It explains why “hearts of flesh” stands opposite “tablets of stone”.
  • The result: obedience to God’s statutes and ordinances. This bears most directly on the open question. Ezekiel’s new covenant ends in the people walking in God’s statutes. The content of God’s commands is not abolished; it is done, by people whose hearts have been changed.
  • A Hebrew/Greek divergence at 11:19 (category 2, not load-bearing). The Hebrew לֵב אֶחָד (“one heart”) stands against the Greek καρδίαν ἑτέραν (“another heart”). BHS’s apparatus notes that the Greek reflects אַחֵר (“other”), and it suggests reading חָדָשׁ (“new”) with a few manuscripts, the Syriac and the Targum, comparing 18:31 and 36:26. Nothing in 2 Cor 3 turns on it. Moderate confidence.

Genesis 1:3–4 with Isaiah 9:1 (LXX 9:2) → 2 Cor 4:6 (high confidence: introduced by ὁ εἰπών, “the one who said”, a citation formula) [T]

Source context.

  • Genesis 1:3–4. God’s first creative word, “Let there be light”, and the separation of light from darkness. It is the first act of the first creation.
  • Isaiah 9:1 [EVV 9:2]. In Isaiah’s own setting, after the darkness of 8:21–22 (distress, darkness, the gloom of anguish), “the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light; those who dwelt in the land of the shadow of death, light has shone upon them”. It is the light of the coming child-king (9:5–6 [EVV 9:6–7]) and Davidic peace.
  • The register. In Genesis, sovereign creation. In Isaiah, rescue after judgement.
  • The Greek of Isaiah reads φῶς λάμψει ἐφ᾽ ὑμᾶς, “light will shine on you” (future). It matches 2 Cor 4:6’s φῶς λάμψει exactly. In the whole Greek Old Testament the two words stand side by side only at Isa 9:2 (Swete numbering). Tobit 13:11 has φῶς λαμπρὸν λάμψει (“a bright light will shine”), but with no darkness in view. The other verses that turn up in the search have compounds (ἐκλάμψει, ἀναλάμψει, ἐπιλάμψει).

Book usage. No earlier citation of Genesis or Isaiah in the letter. Both return:

  • 5:17’s “new creation” (καινὴ κτίσις) takes up the creation theme;
  • 6:2 cites Isa 49:8;
  • 6:17 cites Isa 52:11.

So 4:6 opens a creation and new-exodus thread that reaches its statement in 5:17 (“if anyone is in Christ, new creation”) [T] for the references; [I] for the thread. Moderate–high confidence.

OT-to-OT. Isaiah 9 already draws on creation imagery: darkness into light, God acting where no one could. Isaiah’s light is the dawn of a new reign, the creation word spoken again over a people in exile-darkness. Paul’s conflation therefore follows a link already present in the Old Testament.

What it adds. It closes the passage’s argument in the right register. The glory on Moses’ face was the reflected light of the Creator’s presence; now the Creator himself has shone in our hearts, and the glory is seen “in the face of Christ”. The new ministry’s glory is the light of new creation, not a brighter version of Moses’ shine. The Byzantine infinitive λάμψαι (“who commanded light to shine”) reduces the citation to Genesis alone; the future λάμψει (NA28 and SBLGNT, with 𝔓⁴⁶ א* A B D*) keeps Isaiah (see Textual Variants).

Moderate-confidence allusions (Move 1 only)

  • Exodus 4:10 → 2 Cor 2:16; 3:5–6 (moderate). At the burning bush Moses objects: “I am not a man of words” (Hebrew לֹא אִישׁ דְּבָרִים), rendered in Greek οὐχ ἱκανός εἰμι, “I am not sufficient”. It is the only place in the Greek Old Testament where ἱκανός εἰμι occurs (Swete, accent-insensitive). God answers that he made the mouth and will be with it (4:11–12). What it brings. Paul’s “who is sufficient?” and “our sufficiency is from God” stand in Moses’ position at his commissioning, and they answer it the way God answered Moses. The echo depends on the Greek; the Hebrew idiom differs (category 3). [T] for the wording; [I] for the intention.

  • Deuteronomy 29:3 (Swete 29:4) → 2 Cor 3:14–15 (moderate). “The LORD has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, ears to hear, until this day” (עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה; ἕως τῆς ἡμέρας ταύτης). Moses speaks this on the plains of Moab, renewing the covenant. Paul’s ἄχρι τῆς σήμερον ἡμέρας … ἕως σήμερον echoes the phrase. It names the same diagnosis: Israel’s hardness as the condition under which the covenant is heard. In Romans Paul cites the verse itself (Rom 11:8). Here it is an echo, not a quotation. [I].

  • The sacrificial “pleasing aroma” → 2 Cor 2:14–16 (moderate). ὀσμή (“aroma”) and εὐωδία (“fragrance”) stand together in 49 verses of the Greek Old Testament (Swete), almost all of them sacrificial (“a pleasing aroma to the LORD”, e.g. Gen 8:21; Exod 29:18; Lev 1:9). Ezek 20:41 applies the phrase to the regathered people themselves: “with a pleasing aroma I will accept you”. Paul’s “we are the fragrance of Christ to God” makes the ministers the offering. That matches Ezekiel’s restored people. [I].

  • Proverbs 3:3; 7:3 → 2 Cor 3:3 (uncertain). “Write them on the tablet of your heart” (כָּתְבֵם עַל־לוּחַ לִבֶּךָ). The Greek has it at 7:3 (ἐπίγραψον δὲ ἐπὶ τὸ πλάτος τῆς καρδίας σου). Swete’s 3:3 lacks the clause. Wisdom’s image of the heart as a writing surface is part of the background; it is not a specific source.

Pauline parallels outside the letter (the Corinthian correspondence and Romans; reported here because Move 2 concerns the book, and these concern the author and his addressees):

  • 1 Corinthians 13:8–13 [T] vocabulary; [I] link. Moderate–high. It shares a cluster of vocabulary with 2 Cor 3: καταργέω ×4 (13:8 ×2, 10, 11), μένει (“remains”, 13:13), δι᾽ ἐσόπτρου (“through a mirror”, 13:12; compare κατοπτριζόμενοι, “beholding as in a mirror”, 3:18), and πρόσωπον πρὸς πρόσωπον (“face to face”, 13:12). The Corinthians have already been taught that God’s good gifts (prophecy, knowledge) are “set aside” when the perfect comes, and that what “remains” is greater. That is the closest analogy anywhere in Paul for καταργέω applied to something glorious, and it was given to the same church.
  • Romans 2:29; 7:6; 8:4 [T]. High. These are the letter/Spirit pairs. Rom 7:6 says “we have been released from the law (κατηργήθημεν ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου) … so that we serve in newness of Spirit and not in oldness of letter”. The very next verse quotes the tenth commandment (7:7). And 8:4 says the purpose of God’s sending his Son is “that the righteous requirement of the law (τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ νόμου) might be fulfilled in us who walk not according to the flesh but according to the Spirit”. Romans therefore states in one argument both halves of what 2 Cor 3 implies:
    • release from the letter, the Decalogue included (Rom 7:6–7);
    • the law’s righteous requirement fulfilled by the Spirit (Rom 8:4).
    Cross-letter evidence; it informs, it does not decide, the reading of 2 Cor 3.

  • Internal: answers §2:11 (high). Satan’s “designs” (νοήματα) become “the god of this age has blinded the minds (νοήματα)” (4:4).
  • Internal: planted → §11:3 (high). “Not walking in craftiness” (πανουργίᾳ, 4:2) and the blinded “minds” (4:4) return at 11:3: “as the serpent deceived Eve by his craftiness (πανουργίᾳ), your minds (νοήματα) may be corrupted”. πανουργία occurs only at 4:2 and 11:3 in the letter (SBLGNT; πανοῦργος at 12:16). The god of this age (4:4) is the serpent of Eden (11:3).
  • Internal: planted → §11:13–15 (high). Satan “disguises himself (μετασχηματίζεται) as an angel of light”, and so do “his ministers … as ministers of righteousness (διάκονοι δικαιοσύνης), whose end (τὸ τέλος) will be according to their works” (11:14–15). Against this stand:

  • Internal: planted → §5:12 (high). “Those who boast in face (ἐν προσώπῳ) and not in heart (καρδίᾳ)”. The face/heart pair of this passage (3:7–4:6) becomes the test of the rivals.

  • Internal: planted → §10:12, 18; 12:11 (high). “Commend ourselves” (συνίστημι). It occurs nine times in the letter, opening and closing this passage (3:1; 4:2), and it becomes the theme of 10:12–18.

12. Genre

Pauline epistle; within it, apostolic self-defence (apologia) built on scriptural argument. The central section (3:7–18) is an argument from the lesser to the greater (qal wa-ḥomer), followed by an exposition of Exod 34:29–35 that reads the text figuratively: the veil moves from Moses’ face to Israel’s heart. Reading rule: Paul’s statements about Moses are apostolic interpretation of Exodus, made inside an argument about ministry. They are not a separate narrative about Sinai, and they do not claim that Exodus says what Paul infers [I].

13. Copycat

N/A for narrative imitation — this is an epistle. Two applications check:

  • Moses’ veiling is not a model or an anti-model of personal conduct. Paul does not accuse Moses of deception; he puts the fault in Israel’s hardened minds (3:14, ἀλλά).
  • “We use great boldness” (3:12) is the stance of the apostolic ministry, grounded in the new covenant. It applies to Christian ministry generally only as far as the ground (the Spirit’s ministry) is shared. It is not a licence for any preacher’s assertiveness.

14. Bible Timeline

Paul writes this side of the cross and of Pentecost, with the Spirit given (1:22; 3:3, 17–18). The passage is itself about the Bible’s timeline: it reads Sinai (Exod 34) from the far side of the new covenant (Jer 31; Ezek 36) and sees one ministry “being set aside” and another “remaining” (3:11). Its present tense matters: Israel’s veil remains “to this day” (3:14–15), at the reading of Moses. A timeline caution for the open question: “set aside” describes a ministry superseded in history, not a text discarded. Moses is still read (3:15), and the remedy is turning to the Lord, not ceasing to read [T].

15. Who Am I?

  • “We all” (3:18) is everyone who has turned to the Lord. The pronoun widens from Paul’s apostolic “we” (2:14–3:6) to all believers. Who am I? — one of the “we all”, being transformed.
  • Moses is the mediator of the old covenant. He is a foil to Paul, not a villain and not a model for us.
  • The sons of Israel are the hearers of Moses whose minds were hardened. They are not a target for contempt; Paul grieves for them, and the veil lifts “whenever anyone turns” (3:16).
  • The Corinthians are the letter (3:2–3): the Spirit’s handwriting. They are the evidence, not the authors.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. Confidence in Paul’s gospel ministry as Spirit-given and glorious. The Corinthians should not need, or be impressed by, letters of recommendation or a ministry that “commends itself”. They should receive the gospel boldly and unveiled.

Stage 2.

  • Worldview. Glory now lies in the Spirit’s inward work, not in impressive outward credentials; the new covenant’s ministry exceeds Sinai’s. The Old Testament is read rightly only “in Christ”: the veil lifts on turning to the Lord.
  • Stop:
    • judging ministry by “letters” and appearance (5:12: “in face, not in heart”);
    • reading Moses with a veiled heart, as a ministry of death in its own right, without Christ.
  • Start:
    • beholding the Lord’s glory in the gospel, expecting transformation “from glory to glory”;
    • praying for those to whom the gospel is veiled, knowing whose work the blindness is (4:4);
    • seeing believers as the Spirit’s living letters.
  • Motive. The ministry’s sufficiency and glory are “from God” (3:5), not from us. That frees ministers to be bold, and hearers to be unafraid.

Four audiences.

  • For me: do I read the Old Testament with the veil lifted, as a text whose glory points to Christ’s face?
  • For a Christian friend: the believer who feels inadequate for any service hears that adequacy is “from God”.
  • For the church: the congregation is the letter of recommendation, read by all people (3:2).
  • For an unbeliever: a veil can lie over the heart, and it lifts on turning to the Lord.

Prayer. Lord, who said “Light shall shine out of darkness”, shine in our hearts. Lift the veil when we read Moses, and let us see your glory in the face of Christ, until we are changed into the same image, from glory to glory.

Grammatical features.

  • 3:7, τὴν καταργουμένην. Feminine accusative, agreeing with τὴν δόξαν, not with διακονία. Strictly it is the glory of Moses’ face that is described as being set aside. High.
  • 3:11, τὸ καταργούμενον / τὸ μένον. Neuter substantives. Paul generalises from the glory of 3:7 to “what is being set aside” as a whole, in contrast with “what remains”. The neuter is broad enough to cover the old ministry, and it does not specify a code. High on the grammar; moderate on the referent.
  • 3:14, μὴ ἀνακαλυπτόμενον ὅτι ἐν Χριστῷ καταργεῖται. The participle is neuter and may agree with κάλυμμα (“the veil remains, not being lifted”). Or it may stand absolutely, “it not being revealed that …”. If ὅτι is causal, the subject of καταργεῖται is the veil; if it introduces content, the subject is “it”, most naturally the old covenant. Each of the three other uses of καταργέω in the chapter has the old glory or ministry as subject (3:7, 11, 13). The veil is removed by a different verb, περιαιρεῖται (3:16, from Exod 34:34). That favours the old covenant as the subject at 3:14 too [I]. Moderate.
  • 3:17, ὁ δὲ κύριος τὸ πνεῦμά ἐστιν. The article with κύριος points back to the κύριον of 3:16, the Lord of the Exodus quotation. Paul is interpreting, not making a statement of identity between persons. Moderate–high.
  • 3:18, τὴν αὐτὴν εἰκόνα μεταμορφούμεθα. An accusative of respect or result: “transformed into the same image”. 4:4 identifies the image: Christ, “who is the image of God”. High.

Wordplay and sound.

  • γράμμα / γράμμασιν / ἐγγεγραμμένη / ἐπιστολή is a writing cluster.
  • κάλυμμα / ἀνακαλύπτω / καλύπτω builds the veil.
  • δόξα / δεδόξασται / δεδοξασμένον in 3:10 is deliberately cumbersome, and it echoes the Greek of Exodus.

Textual Variants

3:14 — the punctuation, not a manuscript reading. NA28 punctuates μένει, μὴ ἀνακαλυπτόμενον ὅτι ἐν Χριστῷ καταργεῖται. The SBLGNT punctuates μένει μὴ ἀνακαλυπτόμενον, ὅτι ἐν Χριστῷ καταργεῖται, and its marker at ὅτι records that the editions it compares differ there. In unpunctuated script the letters could equally be divided ὅ τι (“which”). The uncials carry neither punctuation nor word division, so this is an editorial decision.

  • The issue: is it the veil that is set aside in Christ, or the old covenant?
  • Most likely: see Original Languages. The chapter’s use of the verb favours the old covenant (moderate).
  • Preaching implication: do not build the answer to the open question on 3:14 alone. 3:7, 11 and 13 carry it more securely.
  • Confidence: moderate.

3:13 — τέλος (“end”) against πρόσωπον (“face”).

  • The issue: Codex Alexandrinus, Old Latin b and f* and the Vulgate (NA28) read “face” in place of “end”.
  • Manuscript evidence: the rest of the tradition, including 𝔓⁴⁶ א B, reads τέλος.
  • Most likely original: τέλος (high). The reading πρόσωπον is easily explained as assimilation to the “face” earlier in the verse.
  • Preaching implication: “the end of what was being set aside” is Paul’s point. Israel did not see that the old ministry had an end, or goal; the “face” reading loses this.

3:7 — ἐν γράμμασιν against γράμματι. B, D·ᶜ, F, G and the Peshitta read the singular “letter”. The plural (𝔓⁴⁶ א A C and others) matches the concrete “letters engraved on stones”. Nothing turns on it. High confidence for the plural.*

3:3 — καρδίαις σαρκίναις. Some witnesses read καρδίας (“tablets of the heart of flesh”: F Ψ and the Latin tradition, Irenaeus in Latin, Eusebius). Others omit καρδίαις (0243 630 1739). The text’s apposition, “tablets that are hearts of flesh”, is the harder reading. High.

4:6 — λάμψει (future indicative) against λάμψαι (aorist infinitive).

  • Manuscript evidence: λάμψει is read by 𝔓⁴⁶ א* A B D* 0243 6 1739, Clement and Epiphanius. λάμψαι is read by א² C D² F G H K L P Ψ and the majority text, with the Latin.
  • Most likely original: λάμψει (high).
  • Preaching implication: the future keeps the exact wording of Isa 9:1 in Greek (Tool 11); the infinitive (KJV) leaves only Genesis. Preach “Light shall shine”.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting.

  • Time period: mid-50s AD, after the “painful letter” (2:3–4), written from Macedonia (7:5; 8:1) [S], standard.
  • Location: Corinth, a Roman colony, refounded in 44 BC, with a Jewish community and synagogue (Acts 18:4) [S].
  • Political context: Roman civic honour culture.

What the original audience knew.

  • The triumph (θριαμβεύω, 2:14) was the Roman victory procession. The conquering general rode through the city; captives and spoils were led in his train, and incense and spices were burned along the route. Paul’s image puts himself among the captives led by God the victor, not among the soldiers. The aroma of 2:14–16 fits the procession’s incense. For the captives at the end of the procession, it was the smell of death [S], standard. High confidence on the institution; moderate on how far Paul presses the captive image.
  • Letters of recommendation (συστατικαὶ ἐπιστολαί, 3:1) were a normal institution. Travelling teachers and traders carried them, and a stranger without one was suspect [S], standard.
  • Writing on stone was how a Greek city made law permanent and public: decrees and laws were engraved on stelae. Ink on papyrus was for letters. The Corinthians would hear in “ink” and “stone” the two ordinary kinds of writing, private and public [S].
  • Synagogue reading of Moses (3:14–15: “whenever Moses is read”) was a weekly practice. Acts 15:21 says the same: “Moses has been read in the synagogues every Sabbath” [T] for Acts; [S] for practice.
  • Mirrors (κατοπτρίζομαι, 3:18) were polished bronze, and Corinthian bronze was famous. They gave a real but imperfect image (compare 1 Cor 13:12, “dimly”) [S]. Moderate.

What this changes about how we read.

  • The triumph image keeps 2:14 from being heard as a boast. Paul is the captive whose presence displays God’s victory, and that is the thread chapters 4 and 10–13 pick up.
  • The ink-and-stone pairing tells a Greek audience that Paul is contrasting kinds of document, which supports reading the old/new contrast as one of administration and medium.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. A New Testament epistle, read by a church that has Paul’s earlier letter (1 Corinthians) and some knowledge of the Old Testament in Greek. The canonical reader knows Exodus, Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and knows the Supper’s “new covenant in my blood”.

The first hearers.

  • Who they were: a mixed congregation, mostly Gentile with some Jews, in a status-conscious Roman colony.
  • Their situation: shaken confidence in their founder, and rival teachers who arrived with letters and Jewish credentials (11:22).
  • What they brought: an expectation that a true minister would be impressive, recommended and eloquent (10:10). Possibly also a sense, from the rivals, that Moses and the law carried a glory Paul’s gospel lacked [I].

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.

  • Shocking: the “ministry of death” and “of condemnation” (3:7, 9) said of Sinai, by a Jew, and immediately after he concedes its glory.
  • Surprising:
    • the veil is not on Moses’ face now but on hearts, and it lifts whenever anyone turns to the Lord;
    • “the Lord is the Spirit”;
    • believers, Gentiles included, behold with unveiled face what Israel could not look at.
  • Comforting:
    • “you are our letter”, the Corinthians themselves as the Spirit’s credentials;
    • transformation “from glory to glory”.
  • Disturbing: the gospel is veiled to the perishing, and the god of this age blinds minds (4:3–4). The Corinthians are being warned about the rivals’ hold over them.

What we bring that they didn’t. A post-Reformation law/gospel framework, and the three-part taxonomy itself. Both can lead us to hear “letter” as “the law’s demands” and “Spirit” as “grace”, or to look in 2 Cor 3 for a division between moral and ceremonial law. The passage never uses νόμος and never sorts commandments.

Biblical-Theological Themes

Covenant (old and new).

  • Where this passage sits: the inauguration of the new covenant promised in Jer 31 and Ezek 36, read against the Sinai covenant of Exod 34.
  • Before: Sinai ratified (Exod 24), broken (Exod 32) and renewed (Exod 34); the prophetic promise of a new covenant written on hearts, with a new Spirit and a new heart (Jer 31; Ezek 11; 36).
  • After: the Supper (1 Cor 11:25); Hebrews’ sustained exposition (Heb 8–10, citing Jer 31 at length).
  • Contribution: the covenant contrast is administered through ministries, and the new covenant fulfils the prophets’ own terms. God’s torah is written on the heart by the Spirit.
  • High confidence.

Glory and the face of God.

  • Before: Moses asks to see the glory and is denied the face (Exod 33:18–23); his face shines from speaking with God (34:29–35).
  • Here: the glory seen “in the face of Christ” (4:6), and believers behold it with unveiled face (3:18).
  • After: “they will see his face” (Rev 22:4).
  • Contribution: what was denied to Moses (33:20) and reflected on his face is now seen directly in Christ.
  • High.

New creation.

  • Before: Gen 1:3; Isaiah’s light on those in darkness (9:1).
  • Here: 4:6.
  • After: 5:17.
  • Contribution: the new covenant’s ministry is creative, not merely regulatory.
  • Moderate–high.

Relation between the themes: climactic convergence. Covenant, glory and creation meet in one reality, “the glory of God in the face of Christ” shining in hearts (4:6). That sentence is the new covenant (written on hearts), the fulfilled glory (the face), and the new creation (light out of darkness).

Christological Reading

  • Type of connection. For the New Testament, typology in view: Moses as the mediator of glory (type), Christ as the face in which God’s glory is seen (antitype).

    • New Testament precedent: passes; this passage is the precedent.

    • Authorial pattern: passes. Exodus itself raises the unresolved desire to see God’s face (33:18–23).
    • High confidence.
  • How the passage points to Christ.
    • The glory of the old ministry was reflected and passing.
    • The glory “in the face of Christ” is the Creator’s own light.
    • Christ is “the image of God” (4:4) into which believers are changed (3:18).
    • The veil is set aside “in Christ” (3:14).
    • The Lord to whom one turns (3:16) is identified with the Spirit (3:17), so the Christ-centred reading is also Spirit-centred.

Transformation is passive (μεταμορφούμεθα), and sufficiency is “from God”. The gospel ground: God has shone in our hearts (4:6).

Difficult / Contested Verses

3:7–11, 14 — what is “being set aside”? (the open question).

  • Category: doctrinal (law and covenant). Contested between Reformed, Lutheran, progressive covenantal and New Covenant readings.
  • The difficulty: whether Paul declares the abolition of the Mosaic covenant as an administration, of its moral content, or of the glory of Moses’ face only.
  • Common mishandlings:
    • reading “letter” as the law’s moral content and “Spirit” as freedom from obligation;
    • conversely, reading “set aside” as applying only to the ceremonial parts;
    • reading “fading” (NASB95) as a natural decline.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the comparisons ground Paul’s boldness (3:12) and his claim to a ministry needing no letter. They are not a treatise on which commandments still bind.
  • The main interpretive options:
    1. Only the glory on Moses’ face (strict grammar of 3:7).
    2. The old ministry or administration as a whole (3:11’s neuter; 3:14 on the old-covenant reading).
    3. The Mosaic law as such, content included.
    4. The law only as misused (legalism).
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces:
    • Option 1 is too narrow for 3:11 and 3:13.
    • Option 3 is not supported by the passage’s own vocabulary: no νόμος, and Moses is still read and is unveiled in Christ. Nor is it supported by its sources: Jeremiah keeps “my torah”, and Ezekiel keeps “my statutes”.
    • Option 4 finds no anchor in the text. Paul blames hardened minds, not misuse, and the ministry is “of death” in its own right.
    • Option 2 fits the evidence best: what is set aside is the stone-engraved ministry, the Decalogue tablets included, as an administration of death and condemnation. It is replaced by the Spirit’s ministry, which writes God’s torah on hearts.
    • Moderate–high confidence. Note that this cuts against both a tripartite claim (that the Decalogue as written stands outside what is set aside) and a strong discontinuity claim (that the content of God’s commands is retired).
  • Suggested handling: address it directly if the passage is preached in the setting of the law debate. Otherwise acknowledge the issue in passing, and keep the sermon on ministry and glory.

3:13 — did Moses deceive Israel?

  • Category: ethical and apologetic.
  • The difficulty: “so that the sons of Israel would not gaze at the end” can sound like Moses hiding the glory’s passing.
  • Rhetorical function: a foil to Paul’s openness (3:12). The next word is ἀλλά, “but their minds were hardened”.
  • Honest exegesis: Paul puts the fault in Israel’s hardness, not Moses’ guile. Exodus gives the veil no purpose and records the people’s fear. Moderate.
  • Handling: address briefly.

3:14–15 — Israel’s hardened minds.

  • Category: pastoral, and liable to misuse in relations with Jewish people.
  • Honest exegesis: Paul speaks with grief of his own people (Tool 9). The veil lifts for anyone who turns. The same blindness is attributed to “the god of this age” over all the perishing (4:4), Gentiles included.
  • Handling: preach it with grief, never with contempt.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: “The letter kills, the Spirit gives life” as literalism against spirit-of-the-text

  • What it looks like: “Don’t be a letter-of-the-law Christian; follow the spirit of the thing.”
  • Why it’s wrong: γράμμα here is the Mosaic code written on stone (3:7), and πνεῦμα is the Holy Spirit (3:3, “Spirit of the living God”; 3:17–18). It is not a hermeneutical maxim about literal and figurative reading.
  • The corrective: “letter” means the covenant administered as external writing; “Spirit” means God himself writing on hearts.

Pitfall: “The old covenant was fading away” (from the NASB95)

  • What it looks like: “Moses’ glory was fading, so he hid it — the old covenant was running down.”
  • Why it’s wrong: καταργέω means “set aside, render inoperative”, not “fade” (Tool 7). Exodus says nothing of fading, and Paul’s verb expresses God’s verdict in the history of salvation, not a decline.
  • The corrective: “what was being set aside”, because something greater has come (compare 1 Cor 13:10).

Pitfall: Making 2 Cor 3 a proof-text for either side of the law debate

  • What it looks like (one side): “Paul says the moral law remains; only the ceremonies were set aside.”
  • What it looks like (the other side): “Paul says the whole law is abolished; Christians have no commandments.”
  • Why it’s wrong:
    • The passage never mentions νόμος or sorts commandments.
    • What it sets aside is a ministry, and that ministry includes the stone-engraved tablets.
    • Its sources (Jer 31; Ezek 36) keep God’s torah, now written on hearts.
  • The corrective: preach what the text contrasts: two ministries, two media, two effects. Let the law question be answered from the passage’s own terms.

Pitfall: Moses as a deceiver

  • What it looks like: “Moses hid the truth from Israel.”
  • Why it’s wrong: Paul’s next word is “but their minds were hardened” (3:14).
  • The corrective: Moses’ veil is the foil for Paul’s boldness, and the fault lies in hearts that do not turn.

Pitfall: Preaching the veil (3:14–15) with triumph or contempt

  • What it looks like: a confident “they just can’t see it”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the register is lament, and the same blindness covers all the perishing (4:3–4).
  • The corrective: grief, and the open door of 3:16, “whenever anyone turns”.
