# Book Overview: Ruth

## Presenting Situation

**Narrator.** Anonymous, omniscient, retrospective, and outside the story. Three things on the page fix the retrospection: the closing genealogy runs to David (4:22), the aside at 4:7 explains a redemption custom as belonging *lə-p̄ānîm bə-Yiśrāʾēl*, "formerly in Israel", to readers who no longer practise it, and 1:1 dates the action *bîmê šəp̄ōṭ haššōp̄əṭîm*, "in the days when the judges judged", from a standpoint after that era. `[T]`

**Authorship and date.** The book names no author and gives no date of composition. It must post-date David (4:22); nothing on the page settles more than that. Talmudic attribution to Samuel, and the various post-exilic datings, are reconstructions from outside the text and should be carried as `[S]`, not smuggled in as background fact. Downstream digs should treat the compositional date as genuinely open. `[S]` *(confidence: low for any specific date)*

**Narrative-world setting.** The judges era — the period the book of Judges closes by describing as one in which there was no king and each man did what was right in his own eyes. Ruth reports that same period and finds in it not chaos but decorum: a landowner who greets his reapers with a blessing, a foreign widow who is not molested, elders who convene at the gate and do business properly. The contrast is the point. `[T]` (the dating formula) / `[I]` (the contrast)

**Audiences, distinguished.**

- *Narrative-world:* Bethlehemites in the judges period.
- *Compositional:* readers who know David, know the sandal custom is obsolete, and need the Moabite question addressed. `[I]`
- *Canonical:* here the two texts supplied embody the two answers. In **BHS** (Leningrad) Ruth stands in the Writings, immediately **after Proverbs** — so the canonical reader meets *ʾēšet ḥayil* at Ruth 3:11 with Proverbs 31:10 still in the ear. In the **LXX** (and so in the ESV) Ruth stands **after Judges and before Samuel**, easing the reader from "no king" to David. Both placements aim the book at the Davidic line; they differ over whether Ruth is primarily *a portrait of the worthy woman* or *a bridge to the monarchy*. Do not choose silently — name the placement a sermon is assuming. `[T]` (the two orders as given) / `[S]` (their interpretative weight)

**Pressure.** Two tensions, braided. Domestically: a household emptied by famine and three deaths, and a widow who reads that emptiness as the hand of YHWH gone out against her (1:13, 1:20–21) — *is God at work when life is bitter, and is he for me or against me?* Nationally and covenantally: how is the line that reaches David preserved through the judges' disorder, and preserved **through a Moabitess** — a woman from the nation Deuteronomy 23:4 [Eng. 23:3] bars from the assembly of YHWH to the tenth generation? `[T]` / `[I]`

**Purpose.** To show YHWH securing the line to David through the ordinary *ḥesed* of unremarkable people and the costly action of a redeemer — turning empty to full, and bringing an outsider all the way in. `[I]`

**Note on the ESV.** It is a careful translation, but it flattens four of this book's load-bearing repetitions: *śādeh* (rendered "country" of Moab but "field" of Boaz), *kānāp̄* (plural for both 2:12 and 3:9), *šip̄ḥāh / ʾāmāh* (both "servant"), and *yeled / bēn* (both "sons"/"child"). Every one of those flattenings costs a link the Hebrew makes deliberately. Any sermon whose structure depends on a repeated English word must be checked against BHS first. `[T]`

## Leading Words (*Leitwörter*)

*This book is carried by internal repetition far more than by quotation. These are the threads, and they are the primary fuel for dig-deeper's Move 4.*

**1. שׁוּב *šûb*, "return / turn back / restore" — the dominant word, some twelve times in chapter 1 alone** (1:6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 15 ×2, 16, 21, 22 ×2), then 2:6, 4:3, and finally 4:15. Chapter 1 is an argument about who will return where. Naomi's charge is that YHWH *hĕšîbanî rêqām*, "**brought me back** empty" (1:21). The women's verdict on the child is that he will be *lə-mēšîb nep̄eš*, "**a restorer** of life" (4:15) — the same hiphil of the same root. **The word Naomi uses to accuse God is the word the book uses to answer her.** `[T]` *(confidence: high — the single most important verbal observation in the book, and invisible in English)*

**2. שֵׁם *šēm*, "name" — twelve times**, front-loaded at 1:2 (three names in one verse: the man, his wife, his two sons) and massed in chapter 4 (4:5, 10 ×2, 11, 14, 17 ×2). The book opens by naming the dead and closes by naming the living heir. Set against this: the nearer redeemer at 4:1 is *pəlōnî ʾalmōnî*, "so-and-so" — **the man who refuses to raise up a dead man's name is the one man in the book whose own name is withheld.** `[T]` / `[I]` *(high)*

**3. חֶסֶד *ḥesed* — three times, one per major party** (1:8, Naomi of YHWH toward the daughters-in-law; 2:20, of YHWH — or possibly of Boaz, since the Hebrew antecedent is genuinely ambiguous and the ESV decides it; 3:10, Boaz of Ruth). Three uses, and each turns on whether divine and human *ḥesed* can be told apart. That ambiguity at 2:20 is a **theological feature, not a defect**. `[T]` *(high)*

**4. כָּנָף *kānāp̄*, "wing / skirt-corner" — twice, and the number matters.** 2:12: Boaz blesses Ruth for taking refuge *taḥat kənāp̄āyw*, under YHWH's **wings** (plural). 3:9: Ruth asks Boaz to spread *kənāp̄ekā*, his **skirt** (singular — the garment-corner), over her. **She asks him to become the answer to his own prayer**, and the Hebrew marks the move from metaphor to garment by number. The LXX preserves the distinction (πτέρυγας / πτερύγιον); the ESV does not. `[T]` *(high)*

**5. דָּבַק *dābaq*, "cling" — 1:14, 2:8, 2:21, 2:23.** Ruth *clings* to Naomi in the word Genesis 2:24 uses of a man and his wife; Boaz then twice tells her to *cling* to his young women, and 2:23 reports that she did. The verb migrates from covenant loyalty to daily employment and back. `[T]` *(high)*

**6. חַיִל *ḥayil* — 2:1, 3:11, 4:11.** Boaz is *gibbôr ḥayil*; Ruth is *ʾēšet ḥayil*; the elders bless *ḥayil* in Ephrathah. Three uses bracket the couple and the blessing. The LXX keeps the link (δυνατὸς ἰσχύι / γυνὴ δυνάμεως / ἐποίησαν δύναμιν). `[T]` *(high)*

**7. שָׂדֶה *śādeh*, "field".** Naomi's family goes to *the fields of Moab* (1:1, 2, 6, 22; 2:6; 4:3) and Ruth's rescue happens in *the field* of Boaz (2:2, 3, 8, 9, 17, 22). **One Hebrew word; the ESV uses two English ones.** The field of exile becomes the field of provision. `[T]` *(high — a gain available only from the Hebrew)*

**8. יֶלֶד *yeled*, "child".** At the point of maximal loss the narrator drops "Naomi" and drops "sons": *wattiššāʾēr hāʾiššāh miššənê yəlādêhā*, "**the woman** was left from her two **children**" (1:5) — depersonalised, and with the tenderer word. At 4:16, *wattiqqaḥ Noʿŏmî ʾet-hayyeled*, "Naomi took **the child**". The woman left without her *yəlādîm* receives a *yeled* in her lap. Both LXX and ESV flatten this to "sons" / "child". `[T]` *(high — BHS-only)*

**9. רֵיקָם *rêqām*, "empty-handed" — twice, 1:21 and 3:17.** Naomi: YHWH brought me back *empty*. Boaz: "you must not go back **empty-handed** to your mother-in-law." The redeemer answers the accusation in its own vocabulary, with barley, before he answers it with a son. `[T]` *(high)*

**10. גָּאַל *gāʾal*, "redeem / act as kinsman" — sparse until 2:20, then saturating.** First occurrence 2:20 (the hinge of the book); then 3:9, 12, 13 ×3; and in 4:1–14 more than twenty times in fourteen verses. The plot's legal engine. **Note the LXX's choice:** it renders throughout with ἀγχιστεύω / ἀγχιστεύς, "act as next-of-kin", **not** λυτρόω / λυτρωτής. The Greek foregrounds *kinship*; the ransom-price note that English "redeemer" carries is muted. A sermon that runs straight from Boaz to Christ *the Redeemer* is leaning on a vocabulary the LXX declined. `[T]` *(high)*

**11. Ruth's ladder of self-designation.** Six rungs, all on the page: *nokrîyāh* "foreigner" (2:10, the strongest outsider word) → *šip̄ḥāh* "maidservant" (2:13, the lower menial term) → *ʾāmāh* "handmaid" (3:9, the term for a woman eligible for marriage) → *ʾēšet ḥayil* "worthy woman" (3:11, Boaz's verdict) → *ʾiššāh* "wife" (4:13) → "better to you than seven sons" (4:15). The ESV renders rungs two and three identically as "servant"; the LXX flattens 2:10 to ξένη, "stranger". **This ladder is entirely text-derivable and is the cleanest single map of the book's movement.** `[T]` *(high)*

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|:------|:-----------------|:-----------------------------------------|
| 1:1–5 | **Prologue: the emptying** | Narration only, no dialogue. Famine in the house of bread; sojourn; three deaths; *the woman* left without her *yəlādîm*. |
| 1:6–22 | **The road: return and bitterness** | *šûb* twelve times; Orpah turns back, Ruth clings; the oath (1:16–17); "call me Mara"; arrival at barley harvest. |
| 2:1–23 | **The field: refuge and notice** | *ḥēn* and *ḥesed*; the deadpan "her chance chanced" (2:3); Boaz's blessing under YHWH's wings (2:12); **first *gōʾēl* (2:20) — the hinge of the book**. |
| 3:1–18 | **The threshing floor: the appeal** | Naomi seeks *mānôaḥ* for Ruth; the singular *kānāp̄* (3:9); the second *ḥesed* (3:10); "not empty-handed" (3:17). |
| 4:1–12 | **The gate: the name secured** | *gāʾal* saturates; the man with no name declines; the sandal; the elders' blessing invokes Rachel, Leah, Tamar and Perez. |
| 4:13–17 | **Epilogue: the filling** | Narration returns (the only other place YHWH is the subject of a narrated verb); Naomi receives *the child*; "a son has been born to Naomi". |
| 4:18–22 | **Coda: the *tôlədôt* of Perez** | Ten names, Perez to David. Marked off in BHS by a *petuḥah* after 4:17. |

**The device is a symmetrical panel structure with a coda.** Two narration-only panels (1:1–5 and 4:13–17) frame four dialogue scenes, and the frames answer each other exactly: three deaths against one birth, *the woman* deprived of her *yəlādîm* against Naomi taking *the yeled* to her breast, a household name facing extinction against a name called in Israel. `[T]`

Inside that frame the four scenes alternate location and initiative: road (Ruth's initiative), field (Boaz's), floor (Ruth's, on Naomi's counsel), gate (Boaz's). `[T]`

**Two things to hold as genuinely open, not settled:**

- **Where the centre falls.** 2:20 has the strongest claim on internal grounds — the first *gōʾēl*, and the first time Naomi speaks well of YHWH. That is a real judgement, not a certainty. `[I]` *(moderate)*
- **Whether 4:18–22 is climax or appendix.** The *petuḥah* in BHS marks it as a distinct unit, and its formula (*wəʾēlleh tôlədôt*) belongs to Genesis rather than to this narrator's voice. But 4:17 has already reached David, so the coda repeats rather than completes the destination. Best current reading: 4:17 is the narrative climax and 4:18–22 the canonical anchoring that ties Ruth into the Genesis *tôlədôt* chain. **Flag this for the chapter 4 dig; it is the structural claim most likely to be corrected.** `[T]` (the *petuḥah*) / `[I]` (the reading, *moderate*)

## Microscript

> *In the lawless days of the judges, YHWH is the stated subject of a narrated verb only twice — he gives bread (1:6) and he gives a child (4:13) — and between those two gifts the* ḥesed *of ordinary people carries a bitter widow from empty to full and a barred Moabite from the fields of Moab to the field of Boaz, until the God she accused of testifying against her turns out to have been restoring her all along.*

Keyword: **שׁוּב** *šûb* (return / restore). Structural pulse: **empty → full**. `[T]`

*The two-verb observation is the spine, and it is checkable: search the narration — not the speeches, blessings, oaths or jussives — for YHWH as grammatical subject. It yields 1:6 and 4:13 and nothing else: the two gifts that open and close the book's providence.* `[T]` *(high confidence; worth re-verifying at the first dig)*

Test against the movements: emptying (1:1–5) → the contested return (1:6–22) → refuge in the field (2) → the appeal (3) → the name secured at the gate (4:1–12) → the filling (4:13–17) → the line to David (4:18–22). Every movement serves the spine; the coda extends it beyond the story.

## Christological Trajectory

**1. Typology — the *gōʾēl*.** The strongest and most controlled line. It runs through an established biblical category (kinsman-redemption, Lev 25), and the text itself sets out the three conditions a redeemer must meet: he must be **kin** (2:20; 3:12), he must be **able** (the nearer man is kin but cannot act without ruining his own inheritance, 4:6), and he must be **willing** (4:9–10). Boaz is all three, and redeems at cost. The escalation is clean: a field and a widow → a people. *Confidence: high (typology, not allegory).* `[I]`

*Discipline:* the LXX's ἀγχιστεύς (next-of-kin) rather than λυτρωτής (ransomer) is a caution. Preach the **kinship** first — the redeemer must belong to the family in order to act for it — and let the price follow from 4:9–10, where the text supplies it, rather than importing it from the English word "redeemer". `[T]`

**2. Trajectory — raising up the name of the dead.** The transaction's stated purpose, twice, is *ləhāqîm šēm-hammēt ʿal-naḥălātô*, "to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance", so that the name of the dead "may not be cut off" (4:5, 10). Redemption here is defined as the reversal of a death's erasure, secured through a birth. The vocabulary is not resurrection and should not be over-claimed — but the shape is the one the gospel completes. *Confidence: moderate–high; state the category honestly.* `[I]`

**3. Trajectory — the outsider brought in.** The excluded Moabitess crosses from *nokrîyāh* to a place among Rachel and Leah, and thence into David's line — the Abrahamic promise of blessing to the nations working itself out in one household. It lands explicitly in Matthew 1:5. *Confidence: high.* `[T]` (the ladder, the genealogy) / `[S]` (Matt 1:5)

**4. Trajectory — the Davidic line.** The coda is the point of the book's placement: providence preserves the line to David, and past David to David's greater son. *Confidence: high.* `[T]`

**Moralism check.** The temptation is obvious and strong, because the narrative genuinely commends its people: "be loyal like Ruth", "be a man of integrity like Boaz", "be a woman of noble character". A sermon can reach any of those without lying about the text — which is exactly why the trap is dangerous. Two guards, both textual:

- **The narrated-verb test.** YHWH is the subject of a narrated verb twice (1:6, 4:13), and the gifts are bread and a child. Everything the human characters achieve happens *between* those two gifts and depends on them. The story's agent is God; its exemplars are recipients.
- **The *ḥesed* ambiguity at 2:20.** The Hebrew will not let you decide cleanly whether it is YHWH's kindness or Boaz's that has not forsaken the living and the dead — because in this book divine *ḥesed* arrives as human *ḥesed*. That is the answer to moralism, not its licence: the call to *ḥesed* flows *from* the God whose *ḥesed* it is.

Preach Boaz as pointing to Christ, and Naomi and Ruth as recipients of *ḥesed* as we are — then the call to imitate lands after grace rather than instead of it. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **Moralising the leads.** "Be like Ruth / be like Boaz" as the sermon's main point. *Bites at:* everywhere, hardest at 1:16–17 and 2:11–12. *Corrective:* the narrated-verb test and the 2:20 ambiguity above. Exemplarism flows from grace, never toward it.

2. **Preaching Naomi's theology as the book's.** 1:13 ("the hand of the LORD has gone out against me") and 1:20–21 ("the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me") are **characterisation in direct speech**, not the narrator's verdict — and the narrator never endorses them. Worse, 1:21 rests on a contested root (*testified against* / *humbled*). *Bites at:* 1:19–22. *Corrective:* let Naomi's protest stand as honest lament that the book will answer, and never build a doctrine of providence on a disputed rendering. Name the crux from the pulpit if you use the verse.

3. **Romanticising or sanitising the threshing floor.** Either a love scene or an embarrassed skirt-past. *Bites at:* 3:1–14. *Corrective:* preach it in its own categories — a *legal* appeal in a *betrothal* idiom (Ezek 16:8), deliberately staged at the exact point where Genesis 19 says a Moabite story goes wrong, and deliberately not going there. The danger and the restraint are both the point.

4. **Reading Ruth as a love story.** *Bites at:* the whole book, and it quietly reduces chapter 4 to paperwork. *Corrective:* the book is about a *name* (twelve occurrences) and an *inheritance*. The marriage serves the line.

5. **Smoothing the Moabite scandal.** *Bites at:* 1:22 and 4:5–10. *Corrective:* the narrator restates "the Moabitess" five times, twice inside the legal proceedings themselves. The offence is deliberate. Preach Deuteronomy 23 alongside it, and let the tension stand until the genealogy resolves it.

6. **Building on an English repetition the Hebrew does not make.** *Bites at:* 2:12 / 3:9 (the ESV's "wings" for both, which hides the actual move), 2:13 / 3:9 ("servant" for two different words), 1:1 / 2:2 ("country" / "field" for one word). *Corrective:* check every word-repetition point against BHS before it becomes a sermon structure. Conversely, three of the book's best structural gifts — *šûb*, *yeled*, *śādeh* — are invisible in English and have to be given to the congregation explicitly.

7. **Treating 4:18–22 as an appendix to be skipped.** *Bites at:* the final sermon of any series. *Corrective:* the ten names are the payoff — and possibly, deliberately, the tenth generation.

Notes for series planning:

- **The seams are not the chapter divisions in one place.** 4:1–12 and 4:13–22 are separate movements (legal transaction / birth and coda) and preach better apart; conversely 1:1–5 and 1:6–22 are distinct panels but too short to separate.
- A **four-week series** merges 4:1–12 into 4:13–22 — workable, but the sermon then carries both the gate scene and the genealogy, and usually loses the coda.
- A **two-week treatment** (1–2 / 3–4) is possible but forfeits the *gōʾēl* build, which is the book's engine.
- If preaching one sermon only, **2:1–23** carries the most of the book's theology per verse; **1:1–22** carries the most of its pastoral weight.
