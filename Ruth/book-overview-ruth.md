## Canonical Position

| Field | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Section** | Ketuvim, fourth in the sequence BHS prints `[T]`. English Bibles and the Greek tradition set Ruth between Judges and 1 Samuel `[S]`. Codex Leningradensis itself puts Chronicles first among the Writings `[S]`. |
| **Reading implication** | In the Hebrew order Ruth is read as a Writing; in the English and Greek order as the hinge between Judges and Samuel. Neither placement is the book's own claim `[I]`. **The Proverbs-adjacency argument is withdrawn as evidence of design, and that withdrawal is compositional, not hermeneutical** (v1.4, Logos L1; ***v2.3 — restated after the Lau–Goswell audit***): *Baba Batra* 14b places Ruth **first** among the Writings, Campbell reports near-consensus that this position **antedates** the move into the Megillot, and Campbell suggests the Leningrad placement may itself have been prompted by the אֵשֶׁת חַיִל link — making the lexical link the *cause* of the adjacency rather than evidence of design `[S]`. ***(v2.3)*** **Goswell, the position's best advocate, concedes the same point in terms** — the juxtaposition is "post-authorial" and the link "must be viewed as postcompositional" `[S: audit]`. **So the withdrawal is over-determined and may be stated with confidence.** What is *not* withdrawn is the adjacency as a later **reading frame**: that is legitimate, and what it yields is a wisdom reading the book will bear. **But on this project's own dating — the move into the Megillot falling between the sixth and ninth centuries AD — the readers who supplied the sequence are medieval, not ancient**, and must not be called ancient in the pulpit `[S: audit]`. |
| **Presupposes** | Checked references, each verified in the WLC. (1) **The judges' period** — בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים (1:1) occurs nowhere else `[T]`. ***(v2.0 — sharpened by the 1:1–5 dig)*** The wider formula וַיְהִי בִּימֵי stands in **five verses**, and **four of the five date by a named ruler; Ruth alone does not** `[S: dig]` — a period named by the absence of a king, answered by a king's name at 4:17 and 4:22. (2) **Judah and Tamar** — 4:12 names Perez, Tamar and Judah `[T]`; the story is Genesis 38, and **Gen 38:29 glosses Perez's name by wordplay on פָּרַץ, the only one of the genealogy's ten names the canon explains** `[S: dig]`. (3) **Rachel, Leah, Ephrathah and Bethlehem** — 4:11; Bethlehem with Ephrath(ah) in five verses `[T]`. (4) **The house of Perez** — Perez with Hezron in five verses `[T]`. (5) **Land-redemption** — גֹּאֵל with קָרוֹב in three verses: Lev 25:25; Ruth 2:20; 3:12 `[T]`. Ruth never cites the law `[T]`. (6) **Gleaning** — ***(v2.0 — corrected and tightened)*** the statute's own verb pair **עָזַב with לָקַט stands in exactly three verses: Lev 19:10, Lev 23:22, Ruth 2:16** `[S: audit]`, and **Ruth 2:16 is its only occurrence outside the statute**. (7) **Raising the name of the dead** — Deut 25:5–10 is the nearest law `[I]`. (8) **Moab** — assumed, never explained `[I]`. ***(v2.0 — added, per the digs)*** (9) **Genesis 29, 38 and 48** for the closing scene; and (10) **Numbers 1–10**, since **Nahshon, the genealogy's one datable name, is the wilderness-generation chief of Judah** (Num 1:7; 2:3; 7:12; 10:14) `[S: audit]`. |
| **Handoff** | Thematic. In English and Greek order the next book is 1 Samuel, where Jesse is an Ephrathite of Bethlehem in Judah — a triple in two verses only, 1 Sam 17:12 and Ruth 1:2 `[T]`. In BHS order the next book is Song of Songs; no lexical handoff is claimed. |
| **Neighbours** | **Proverbs** (before): אֵשֶׁת חַיִל stands in three verses — Prov 12:4; 31:10; Ruth 3:11 `[T]`. The relation is **Prov 31:10–31 ↔ Ruth 3 at *moderate–high***, not book-to-book; **the adjacency is rated nil** `[S: audit]`. ***(v2.3 — now measured against the whole canon.)*** **Prov 31's densest partner chapter anywhere is Ruth 3 at 0.278, and no chapter in the canon reaches 0.3 against it; Ruth 1, 2 and 4 return zero. From Ruth's side Prov 31 ranks 88th of 859, behind Prov 22.** Against the 8.786 calibration that is 3.2% of a known textual relationship `[S: audit]`. **And אֵשֶׁת חַיִל also stands at Prov 12:4, so it does not single out the acrostic.** **Song of Songs** (after): no work demonstrated. |

**Canonical and compositional order.** The events fall in the judges' period `[T]`; the Hebrew canon files the book among the Writings `[T]`; the English and Greek canons after Judges `[S]`. ***(v2.0 — the Chronicles question is narrowed but still open.)*** The direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15 remains undetermined, but the overlap has now been measured: **three of seven begetting-clauses are word-identical, three differ by a name's spelling, one has no counterpart, and the divergences fall at the two ends** `[S: dig; audit]`. That narrows the field to copying in one direction or a common register and makes loose independent agreement unlikely — but does not choose.

## Presenting Situation

- **Narrator.** Anonymous, third person; reports inner states `[T]`. ***(v2.0)*** He does so **twice, both of Boaz at the threshing floor** — 3:7 and 3:8 — plus a narrated perception at 1:18 `[S: audit]`. The author is unnamed `[T]`; tradition names Samuel `[S]`. The custom at 4:7 belongs to "former times" `[T]`, so he writes at a distance `[I]`. The book ends on David with no royal title `[T]`.
- **Audience.** Bethlehem in the narrative world `[T]`. The compositional audience is contested — monarchic and post-exilic dates are both argued `[S]` — and **is not resolved here.** ***(v2.0 — one proposed date argument is now closed.)*** The marriage idiom at 1:4 was offered as evidence for a late date; **the inference is discarded**, because one of the six instances is **Judges 21:23**, outside the post-exilic corpus, and because **BHS marks Ezra 10:44 — the comparison verse — `crrp`, corrupt** `[S: audit]`. The canonical audience is the reader who already knows David `[I]`; interpretation defaults to that reader.
- **Occasion.** None stated `[T]`. Proposals are reconstructions `[S]`.
- **Pressure.** Emptiness. Famine, three deaths, a woman left without husband or sons `[T]`. Naomi names it "bitter" and "empty" and ascribes it to שַׁדַּי `[T]`. Ruth carries a second pressure: "the Moabitess" six times after 1:4, including at the moment Boaz acquires her `[T]`, and she calls herself נָכְרִיָּה `[T]`. ***(v2.0)*** The book contains **none** of the law's poverty-and-status labels — אַלְמָנָה, גֵּר, יָתוֹם, עָנִי, אֶבְיוֹן — all controlled; **the one poverty word it does contain is דַּל at 3:10, used of the young men, not of Ruth** `[S: audit]`. The law is present as action and absent as label.
- **Purpose.** To show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and to give Israel David as the fruit `[I]`. No divine speech occurs `[T]`. ***(v2.0)*** The narrator states a divine act **once in his own voice** — 4:13, וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן — and that is the only such sentence in eighty-five verses `[S: dig]`; at 1:6 YHWH's visit is what Naomi *heard* `[T]`.

### Text-critical and versional points to carry into the digs

***(v2.0 — this list is substantially revised. Items 8 and 9 are settled; item 11 is new and is the one a sermon will actually meet.)***

1. **1:1.** The Greek reads an infinitive and lacks "two" before "sons" `[T]`.
2. **1:21.** NASB95 "has witnessed against me"; Greek, Syriac and Vulgate read "afflicted" `[T]`.
3. **2:1.** The WLC prints the word for "kinsman" unpointed (ketiv מידע; qere מוֹדַע) `[T]`. גֹּאֵל does not appear until 2:20 `[T]`.
4. **Ketiv density.** Eleven unpointed words in nine verses, about 10.6 % of the book's verses `[T]` — **the fourth-densest book in the canon for this.** A search by pointed surface form will miss them.
5. **2:7.** MT's זֶה שִׁבְתָּהּ הַבַּיִת מְעָט against the Greek's "she did not rest in the field" — the book's worst crux `[T]`. **BHS cites no Hebrew manuscript** `[S: Logos L14]`; MT is the reading of record.
6. **4:5.** Ketiv קניתי / qere קָנִיתָה; Rahlfs and NASB95 read the second person; BHS suggests the imperative `[T]`. **BHS proposes reading with the versions here** `[S: audit]`.
7. **4:20–21.** ***(v2.0 — and the finding is stronger than first stated.)*** The WLC spells the same ancestor שַׂלְמָה (4:20) and שַׂלְמוֹן (4:21). **The Masoretic morphology files these as two distinct lemmas, each occurring exactly once in the whole Hebrew Bible** — two canonical hapax legomena in consecutive verses `[S: audit]`. 1 Chr 2:11 gives a third form, שַׂלְמָא. *A few Hebrew manuscripts level 4:21 to שלמה, and Alexandrinus levels the Greek to Σαλμων at both verses* `[T]` — both traditions smoothing the same irregularity. **All three English versions level it to "Salmon".**
8. **4:19 — SETTLED, and against BHS's conjecture.** ***(v2.0 — Logos round 3, raised to *high*.)*** BHS proposes reading אֲרָם for MT רָם, citing the Greek and Matt 1:3–4 `[T]`. **Swete's apparatus records no Ἀράμ variant at Ruth 4:19 at all** — only particles — so **both great uncials read Ἀρράν in Ruth** `[T]`. **And at 1 Chr 2:9 the apparatus records variants at two other names and *nothing whatever* at Ῥάμ or Ἀράμ**, so the doublet there is not a manuscript variant: both names stand in the text with no witness omitting either `[T]`. **Ἀράμ is one name written two ways in Greek, not a witness to a Hebrew אֲרָם.** The Hebrew reads רָם at Ruth 4:19, 1 Chr 2:9 and 1 Chr 2:10 `[T]`. **Position: MT. The conjecture gains nothing from the evidence it cites.**
9. **Paragraphing — and it is load-bearing.** ***(v2.0)*** The WLC carries **exactly one paragraph marker in all 85 verses, a petuchah after 4:17** `[T]`. Four solo digs each reported, as a limitation, that Leningrad marks no break at their boundaries. **This is the one seam the manuscript marks, and it coincides exactly with the 4:17 / 4:18 preaching boundary** — making that the only unit division in the book with external warrant. Still `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`: this is Leningrad as the WLC prints it, paragraphing is a reading tradition rather than authorial `[S]`, and **how widely the marker is attested has not been established.** A library pass on the spread is now worth more than it was.
10. **What the English hides.** NASB95 renders אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל (2:1) "a man of great wealth" and אֵשֶׁת חַיִל (3:11) "a woman of excellence"; the Hebrew uses one noun `[T]`.
11. **3:15 — the one a congregation will meet.** ***(v2.0 — new, from the 3:10–18 dig.)*** MT reads וַיָּבֹא הָעִיר, **masculine** — "and *he* came to the city." **NASB95 and ESV both follow a secondary reading ("she went into the city") against MT; NIV84 follows MT** `[S: dig]`. Chapter 3 carries **eleven BHS notes, the densest cluster in the book**, plus three ketivs in two verses and a qere with no ketiv at 3:17 `[S: dig]`.
12. **The versions' direction, stated honestly.** ***(v2.0 — a cross-unit claim tested and cut down.)*** In **most** of Ruth's roughly 44 versional divergences the witnesses are shorter, more explicit or more regular and the Masoretic reading is the harder one, 2:7 the clearest case. **But not at every substantive divergence: BHS itself proposes reading with the versions against L at 1:14, 4:5, 4:10, 4:11 and 4:19** `[S: audit]`. The claim that MT is *always* the harder reading is **discarded as a universal** — as stated it was close to unfalsifiable. **The BHS apparatus to Ruth cites no Qumran reading at all**, so the evidence base is 𝔊 𝔖 𝔙 𝔗 and Hebrew manuscripts.

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–5 | **Emptied** | Famine, a move to Moab, three deaths; Naomi left with two Moabite daughters-in-law. |
| 1:6–22 | **Return** | Naomi turns for Bethlehem; Orpah goes back, Ruth clings; Naomi arrives renamed "bitter". |
| 2:1–23 | **Gleaning** | Boaz introduced; Ruth gleans; Boaz's kindness; Naomi first calls him a redeemer (2:20). |
| 3:1–18 | **Threshing floor** | Naomi's plan, Ruth's request, Boaz's oath and the nearer redeemer named. |
| 4:1–12 | **The gate** | The nearer redeemer yields; Boaz buys the field and acquires Ruth before the elders. |
| 4:13–17 | **Filled** | A son born "to Naomi"; the women's blessing; Obed named. |
| 4:18–22 | **The line** | Ten names from Perez to David. |

**Device.** A linear narrative driven by reversal, the movements joined by repeated words rather than a chiastic frame `[I]`. Counts in the WLC: שׁוּב 15× (**12 in chapter 1** — *ten verses, not eleven* `[S: audit]`); גָּאַל / גֹּאֵל 21×, none before 2:20; לָקַט 12×, all in chapter 2; קָנָה 6×, all in chapter 4; שֵׁם 14×; יָלַד 14×, **thirteen of them in chapter 4** `[T]`.

**Seams.** "Return" enters at 1:6 and leaves at 4:15, where the child is מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ `[T]`. Chapter 3 ends on Naomi's "wait" and chapter 4 opens on Boaz "going up" `[T]`. **The break at 4:17 / 4:18 is marked by the manuscript's only paragraph marker and by a new heading** `[T]`.

***(v2.0 — a new structural finding, and the book's sharpest grammatical movement.)*** **One root runs through three voices in the book's last ten verses.** יָלַד appears as **qal with Ruth as subject** (4:13, 4:15), as a **passive with no agent at all** (4:17, יֻלַּד), and as **hiphil nine times with nine men** (4:18–22) `[S: dig]`, every form checked in the morphology. **Agency moves from Ruth, to nobody, to a line of men** — and the book prints all three in sequence rather than choosing `[I]`. *Moderate–high.* **The pulpit consequence is real: NASB95 renders all nine hiphils as passives ("to Perez was born Hezron"), the same English construction it uses for 4:17's genuine passive, so the third stage disappears; ESV's "fathered" keeps it.**

**The "ten" pattern — withdrawn as design, and now doubly secured.** The counts stand: about ten years (1:4), ten elders (4:2), ten names (4:18–22) `[T]`. The design does not. The numeral baseline found **two** nine times against **ten** twice, tying with **six** `[S: audit]`; the text never says "ten" of the genealogy; and the list gives Boaz's seventh position no formal marking. ***(v2.0 — and a textual argument now joins the statistical one.)*** **The list demonstrably omits generations.** Nahshon is the wilderness-generation chief of Judah and Boaz's grandfather here, so **two generations span the conquest and the whole judges period** `[S: dig]`. **A list that omits generations is not counting them.**

## Microscript

Famine drives a Bethlehem family to Moab, where death leaves Naomi with nothing but a Moabite daughter-in-law who will not leave her; back in Bethlehem, YHWH's kindness works through a harvest field, a threshing floor and a city gate, by people who pay for it, to turn Naomi's emptiness into a son who becomes Obed, grandfather of Jesse's son David. The keynotes are שׁוּב ("return") and then גֹּאֵל ("redeemer").

*Test.* Emptied supplies the emptiness; Return the first turning; Gleaning, Threshing floor and Gate the kindness at work and its cost; Filled the reversal; the line the result for Israel. Every movement serves the spine. ***(v2.0 — the sweep confirmed the spine and sharpened one end.)*** The digs add that the book **opens and closes without naming God and without reasoning** — 1:1–5 and 4:18–22 each carry no divine name and no causal, conditional, purposive or adversative connector `[S: dig]` — **but this is not a designed frame** (see the echo table). What it throws into relief is **1:6**, where the divine name and the book's first reasoning arrive together in two כִּי clauses.

### Density scan: accounting for the top ten

***(v2.0 — this whole section is recomputed and recast, and the recasting matters more than the figures.)***

**Two things changed.** First, **`contacts.py` was carrying a defect that dropped the homograph letter from its lemma key**, merging distinct words; it was found in audit 2, left unapplied, and **fixed on 15 November 2026** `[S: audit]`. Every rank below is recomputed with the corrected tool. Second, **audit 3 established what this metric can and cannot see.**

**The metric is a short-unit detector.** Density is *rare shared pairs ÷ verses of the compared unit*, so it rewards short partners; it cannot distinguish a chapter relationship from a single verse; one lemma can carry a whole "partnership"; and at book level it ranks short books, not partners `[S: audit]`. **The calibration that should govern every future use: a real textual relationship scores an order of magnitude higher — 2 Sam 6 → 1 Chr 13 = 8.786, 2 Kgs 22 → 2 Chr 34 = 7.182 — while Ruth's best *chapter-level* score is 0.357, which is the median of what an arbitrary real chapter achieves.** **Treat any density below about 1.0 as noise unless four checks say otherwise:** distinct partner verses hit, a proper-noun-excluded re-run, a drop-one-lemma check, and the figure set beside a known parallel.

**A null that does not work, recorded so it is not tried again:** a random-verse pseudo-book null passes everything at the 92nd percentile or above, because real chapters are topically coherent and random verse-bags are not. The discriminating baseline is the distribution over **real** chapters and books.

## Christological Trajectory

- **Redeemer (typology through an established category).** גֹּאֵל is the book's second keyword. Boaz acts as redeemer **at cost**: he takes on the dead man's land and the dead man's name, and the debt is his, not Ruth's `[I]`. ***(v2.0)*** The 3:1–9 dig grounds this at *high* on the book's own construction of the category — **Ruth's request at 3:9 is a formal claim on a defined obligation, with its ground-clause "for you are a redeemer"** `[S: dig]`, and the Ezek 16:8 contact shows the gesture is covenantal rather than romantic (row 13). The women's cry at 4:14 lays the category open. ***(v2.3 — new, and it reshapes chapter 4.)*** **Ruth's article usage is systematic: articular הַגֹּאֵל (4:1, 4:3, 4:6, 4:8) denotes exactly one person in the whole book — the unnamed nearer kinsman who declines. Boaz is never *the* redeemer**; he is *a* redeemer (3:9, 3:12) or one of a class (2:20), and 4:14 is anarthrous, with no variant in BHS `[S: audit; BHS]`. **The book moves from *a* redeemer as a category to *the* redeemer as a named party to a transaction — and the only man it ever calls *the* redeemer is the one who walks away.**
- **Trajectory (a longing fulfilled).** A Moabite widow comes "under the wings" of the God of Israel (2:12) and into the royal line. ***(v2.0 — and the New Testament picks up this book's own last five verses.)*** **Matthew 1:3–6 carries the same chain and hangs four mothers on it, each entering the line by an irregularity — Tamar at the roadside, Rahab out of Jericho, Ruth a Moabitess at a threshing floor, and Bathsheba taken by the king — while Ruth's own genealogy names none** `[S: dig]`. **The one Matthew declines to name is the one who had least choice.** *High on the data; moderate–high on the reading.*
- **Contrast (need answered).** Naomi's emptiness and the unredeemed field are a need no human effort alone meets; each kindness (1:8, 2:20, 3:10) is a human act with God named as its source, and **the only act the narrator states in his own voice is God's** (4:13) `[T]`.
- **What is not claimed.** The threshing-floor details are not allegorised. "Boaz is Christ" is not read detail by detail. ***(v2.3 — corrected; the v2.0 statement "Obed is not a type — he is named, then listed" is withdrawn as too flat.)*** **Obed is the referent of the anarthrous גֹּאֵל at 4:14** — the mainstream commentary reading, held by **Campbell (AB, 1975, 168)** and **Chisholm (Kregel, 2013, 640)**, both owned, with no owned commentary reading it as Boaz `[S: Logos L5]`. **But he is called that in the functional sense of sustainer — 4:15's מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ, the roles of a son and heir — not in the legal sense the book reserves for הַגֹּאֵל at the gate.** The lexical limit stands: **גאל is never predicated of Obed anywhere in the canon, and does not occur in Ruth after 4:14** `[S: audit]`. **Do not preach Obed as a second redeemer in the legal sense; do not deny that the women call him one.**
- **Moralism check.** The temptation is "be a Ruth" or "be a Boaz". Both are true to the text and neither is its argument. The kindness is God's before it is anyone's (1:6, 4:13), and the one who redeems a foreign widow at his own cost is the pattern for the greater Redeemer. Christ is the hero; Ruth and Boaz are the means.

## Preaching Traps

1. **The romance.** Ruth as a love story. Naomi drives the plot from her emptiness, and Boaz praises Ruth for *not* going after young men (3:10). *Bites at:* 2:5–9, 3:6–15.
2. **The wedding vow.** 1:16–17 as a marriage text. It is a refusal to turn back, addressed to a mother-in-law who has just told her to go, ending in an oath by YHWH. ***(v2.0)*** **And it is not even Ruth's longest speech, nor the book's: Naomi's instruction at 3:1–4 is 55 words, her dissuasion at 1:11–13 is 53, Ruth's is 34** `[S: audit]`. *Bites at:* 1:14–18.
3. **Allegorising the threshing floor.** The redeemer category carries the type; the feet, the cloak and the six measures do not. *Bites at:* 3:4, 3:7–9, 3:15.
4. **Judging Naomi's theology.** Neither endorsing nor correcting her verdict before the book does. *Bites at:* 1:6–22, 4:14–17.
5. **The legal label.** Calling 4:1–12 "levirate marriage". The redeemer is not a brother, the widow removes no sandal, and the act is tied to the field `[T]`. *Bites at:* 4:3–10.
6. **Stopping at 4:17.** ***(v2.0 — and the manuscript now argues the other way too.)*** The genealogy is not an appendix: **it is the one unit in the book the manuscript sets apart, and the part the New Testament picks up** `[S: dig]`. *Bites at:* 4:13–22.
7. **Preaching a pattern that has not been baselined.** *(v1.3.)* ***(v2.3 — the record is now 4 in 28, and the change is itself the finding.)*** Across **five** audits **twenty-eight design claims have been tested and four have survived** — the mirrored divine names at 1:20–21; every measured single contact held except where the words laid over it outran the count. **Audit 3 gave the reason — the tool that generates these claims is a short-unit detector** (see the density scan) — and **audit 4 gave the sharpest instance: a reading whose own supporting citations do not survive a check on the text.** *(A proposed new-exodus reading anchored on יֶלֶד at Exod 1:22, which reads הַיִּלּוֹד, and on בַּדֶּרֶךְ at Exod 13:17–21, which does not contain the form at all.)* ***(v2.3 — and audit 5 broke the run: all three new survivors came from it.)*** **Of Lau and Goswell's six design claims, none was discarded outright** — two confirmed with nuance, three reframed, one declared unmeasurable `[S: audit 5]`. **The difference is not scholarship against preaching: it is that they state designs at a scale the evidence carries, and concede the compositional question before they start.** What fails in them fails by one notch of overstatement — *every*, *always*, *the lone exception*, *thirteen* — and the finding underneath survives each correction. **That is the test to apply to any pattern before preaching it: is it stated at a size the count can bear?** A pattern is a sermon illustration until it has been measured.
8. **Preaching the study text's passives at 4:18–22.** ***(v2.0 — new.)*** The Hebrew is **active nine times over** — the hiphil of יָלַד — and **NASB95 renders all nine as passives**, using the same English construction as 4:17's genuine passive `[S: dig]`. *Corrective:* read 4:18–22 from the ESV, or supply the active voice. *Bites at:* 4:18–22.
9. **Treating Ruth's absence from the genealogy as a problem to defend or a grievance to air.** ***(v2.0 — new, and it is the question a congregation actually asks.)*** Both halves are true and neither is the whole: the form names fathers, **and Chronicles does the same with the same material**; but the immediately preceding unit grounded the house's future on **three named women** (4:11–12) `[S: dig]`. *Corrective:* answer it, then turn to **Matthew 1:5**, where the same stretch of the same list names her. *Bites at:* 4:11–22.

**For the series as a whole:** Ruth preaches as a book about a God who is almost never on stage and almost always on someone's lips, whose kindness arrives through people acting beyond what any statute requires, in a story that plants its vocabulary in the first chapter and collects it in the last. **The cumulative case is that the book's meaning is carried by its words rather than by its architecture** — which is why ten runs found dozens of durable two-member chains and three audits destroyed every large pattern proposed over them. A series built on the chains will be solid and will keep surprising the congregation; a series built on the patterns will be elegant and will not survive examination. **Three practical consequences:** preach chapter 1 as the planting, not the setup; know passage by passage where your pulpit text fails you (argument 4 is a short, specific list); and take the route to Christ through גֹּאֵל as an obligation discharged at cost, arriving at Matthew 1.

**Why chapter 3 was redivided.** The earlier division at 3:9 / 3:10 split a single exchange. **Three counts, each made before the boundary question arose, put the seam at 3:13:** יָטַב runs 3:1 · 3:7 · **3:10**, terminating past the old boundary; לַיְלָה runs 1:12 · 3:2 · 3:8 · **3:13**, terminating exactly where the new unit ends; and **3:13 itself closes on שִׁכְבִי עַד־הַבֹּקֶר, "lie until morning"** — the clause that ends the night the chapter has run on since 3:2 `[S: dig]`. **3:14 begins the morning.** The homiletic gain is that Ruth's formal claim at 3:9 and Boaz's sworn answer at 3:13 stay in one sermon, and each half carries one coherent pastoral concern — *no standing of our own* for 3:1–13, *waiting on an outcome settled elsewhere* for 3:14–18. **The alternative, if nine sermons are wanted: preach 3:10–18 but read from 3:6.** The exegesis is unaffected either way. ***(v2.3 — the external question is now closed, negatively.)*** **This division rests on narrative and lexical grounds alone, and there is no Masoretic warrant for any break inside chapter 3.** The WLC marks one division in the whole book, the petuchah at 4:17, and nothing in chapter 3 `[T]`. A report of a seder at 3:13 was chased through Logos round 5 and does not stand up: **the library holds one witness, it is the book under audit, and its footnote for that division cites nothing but a "cliffhanger effect"** — while **BHQ, the most recent critical edition, drops even the better-attested 2:12 division** `[S: Logos L5]`. **Say in the pulpit that this is an editorial choice, because it is.**

**The 4:17 / 4:18 boundary is the one division in this book with external warrant** — it coincides exactly with the manuscript's only paragraph marker `[T]`. ***(v2.2 — the warrant is wider than L, and still not a manuscript spread.)*** **Goswell reports the break at 4:17 as the only paragraph division in Ruth in the Tiberian Masoretic tradition**, and **Lau and Goswell add that the Leningrad genealogy is laid out as nine lines each beginning "he begat"** (NSBT 41:28) `[S: Logos]` — **an independent corroboration of the nine-hiphil finding at 4:18–22, from the page rather than from the lemma index.** BHS records no apparatus note at 4:17. **How widely the break is attested across medieval manuscripts is still unreported** — the library holds nothing from Kennicott, de Rossi or Ginsburg on it, so the finding keeps its `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` flag. **So the direction to preach 4:13–17 and 4:18–22 together is a homiletic decision taken across the one seam the text marks.** It is defensible — the genealogy is thin alone, and the three-voices-of-יָלַד finding exists only across both units — but it should be taken knowingly.

**Pulpit-text note for the series.** ***(v2.0 — the sweep's "the pulpit text is more transparent" headline is restated as a split.)*** Neither version is uniformly better in this book. **NASB95** preserves the 1:5 → 4:16 יֶלֶד chain and the 2:20 ambiguity; **ESV** preserves גֹּאֵל at 2:20, חַיִל at 2:1/3:11, the gate at 4:1 and the active voice at 4:18–22 `[S: digs]`. **And at 3:15 NASB95 and ESV both follow a secondary reading against MT while NIV84 follows MT.**
