## The Passage

**4:1** וּבֹ֨עַז עָלָ֣ה הַשַּׁעַר֮ וַיֵּ֣שֶׁב שָׁם֒ וְהִנֵּ֨ה הַגֹּאֵ֤ל עֹבֵר֙ אֲשֶׁ֣ר דִּבֶּר־בֹּ֔עַז וַיֹּ֛אמֶר ס֥וּרָה שְׁבָה־פֹּ֖ה פְּלֹנִ֣י אַלְמֹנִ֑י וַיָּ֖סַר וַיֵּשֵֽׁב׃

*So the closest relative said to Boaz, "Buy it for yourself." And he removed his sandal.*

**4:9** וַיֹּאמֶר֩ בֹּ֨עַז לַזְּקֵנִ֜ים וְכָל־הָעָ֗ם עֵדִ֤ים אַתֶּם֙ הַיּ֔וֹם כִּ֤י קָנִ֨יתִי֙ אֶת־כָּל־אֲשֶׁ֣ר לֶֽאֱלִימֶ֔לֶךְ וְאֵ֛ת כָּל־אֲשֶׁ֥ר לְכִלְי֖וֹן וּמַחְל֑וֹן מִיַּ֖ד נָעֳמִֽי׃
*Then Boaz said to the elders and all the people, "You are witnesses today that I have bought from the hand of Naomi all that belonged to Elimelech and all that belonged to Chilion and Mahlon.*

**4:10** וְגַ֣ם אֶת־ר֣וּת הַמֹּאֲבִיָּה֩ אֵ֨שֶׁת מַחְל֜וֹן קָנִ֧יתִי לִ֣י לְאִשָּׁ֗ה לְהָקִ֤ים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת֙ עַל־נַ֣חֲלָת֔וֹ וְלֹא־יִכָּרֵ֧ת שֵׁם־הַמֵּ֛ת מֵעִ֥ם אֶחָ֖יו וּמִשַּׁ֣עַר מְקוֹמ֑וֹ עֵדִ֥ים אַתֶּ֖ם הַיּֽוֹם׃
*"Moreover, I have acquired Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, to be my wife in order to raise up the name of the deceased on his inheritance, so that the name of the deceased will not be cut off from his brothers or from the court of his birth place; you are witnesses today."*

**4:11** וַיֹּ֨אמְר֜וּ כָּל־הָעָ֧ם אֲשֶׁר־בַּשַּׁ֛עַר וְהַזְּקֵנִ֖ים עֵדִ֑ים יִתֵּן֩ יְהוָ֨ה אֶֽת־הָאִשָּׁ֜ה הַבָּאָ֣ה אֶל־בֵּיתֶ֗ךָ כְּרָחֵ֤ל ׀ וּכְלֵאָה֙ אֲשֶׁ֨ר בָּנ֤וּ שְׁתֵּיהֶם֙ אֶת־בֵּ֣ית יִשְׂרָאֵ֔ל וַעֲשֵׂה־חַ֣יִל בְּאֶפְרָ֔תָה וּקְרָא־שֵׁ֖ם בְּבֵ֥ית לָֽחֶם׃
*All the people who were in the court, and the elders, said, "We are witnesses. May the LORD make the woman who is coming into your home like Rachel and Leah, both of whom built the house of Israel; and may you achieve wealth in Ephrathah and become famous in Bethlehem.*

**4:12** וִיהִ֤י בֵֽיתְךָ֙ כְּבֵ֣ית פֶּ֔רֶץ אֲשֶׁר־יָלְדָ֥ה תָמָ֖ר לִֽיהוּדָ֑ה מִן־הַזֶּ֗רַע אֲשֶׁ֨ר יִתֵּ֤ן יְהוָה֙ לְךָ֔ מִן־הַֽנַּעֲרָ֖ה הַזֹּֽאת׃
*"Moreover, may your house be like the house of Perez whom Tamar bore to Judah, through the offspring which the LORD will give you by this young woman."*

**Three words stand unpointed in these twelve verses** — the ketiv at 4:4 (ואדע), 4:5 (קניתי) and 4:6 (לגאול). They are printed here as the Westminster Leningrad Codex prints them, without the Masoretic vowels, and the qere readings are discussed under Textual Variants.

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools converging, each *high confidence* on its lexical base.

1. **The passage is the book's legal centre and its vocabulary proves it: four words that occur nowhere else in Ruth all occur here.** `[T]` קָנָה ("acquire, buy") six times, זָקֵן ("elder") four times, נַחֲלָה ("inheritance") three times, and עֵד ("witness") three times — every occurrence each word has in the book falls inside these twelve verses (Leningrad, by lemma). Add גָּאַל ("redeem"), which stands twelve times here out of twenty-one in the book, nine of the twelve in verses 4 and 6 alone. The scene is not a legal appendix to a love story; it is where the book's legal vocabulary lives. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Structure, Author's Purpose.

2. **Boaz's purpose clause is found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, and he says it twice.** `[T]` קוּם ("raise up") with שֵׁם ("name") and נַחֲלָה ("inheritance") in one verse stands in exactly two verses of the canon — Ruth 4:5 and Ruth 4:10 — and the ordered phrase לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת עַל־נַחֲלָתוֹ ("to raise up the name of the dead upon his inheritance") matches at those two verses alone, plain and skeletal. Deuteronomy 25:6–7 supplies the name-raising; Leviticus 25 supplies the inheritance-redemption; **the fusion is Ruth's own**, and it brackets the whole transaction. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Quotation/Allusion, Structure.

3. **Ruth 4 is not levirate marriage, and the measurement is decisive.** `[T]` *(v1.1, 14 November 2026 — restated; the first version of this finding was wrong, see the textual note at the foot of the report.)* The levirate vocabulary enters Ruth **once, in chapter 1, and never in chapter 4.** The masculine יָבָם ("husband's brother") stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Deuteronomy 25:5, Deuteronomy 25:7 and Genesis 38:8 — and Ruth never uses it. The feminine יְבָמָה stands in three — Deuteronomy 25:7, Deuteronomy 25:9 and **Ruth 1:15, where it occurs twice**, of Orpah. So the technical term appears in this book only inside Naomi's argument that levirate remedy is impossible (1:11–13, 1:15), and has gone by the time an actual legal remedy is transacted here. Conversely the governing term of this scene, גָּאַל ("redeem"), is absent from Deuteronomy 25 altogether; in the whole of Deuteronomy the participle stands only at 19:6 and 19:12, of the גֹּאֵל הַדָּם ("avenger of blood"). The sandal confirms it: Deuteronomy 25:9 uses חָלַץ ("pull off") and Ruth 4:7–8 uses שָׁלַף ("draw out"), and the two idioms share no verse. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion, Genre, Difficult Verses.

4. **Boaz opens the scene on the sound of the book's keyword: "sit" and "the returned one" are the same consonants.** `[T]` for the distribution, `[I]` for the design. יָשַׁב ("sit, dwell") occurs six times in verses 1–4 — more than in any other four verses of the book — and at 4:1 Boaz says שְׁבָה ("sit"), at 4:2 שְׁבוּ ("sit"), and at 4:3 he names Naomi הַשָּׁבָה ("the one who has returned"), from שׁוּב, the book's first keyword. Stripped of pointing, שְׁבָה and הַשָּׁבָה are the same three letters. The gate scene that settles the return is conducted in the sound of it. Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Original Languages, Tone.

5. **The one paragraph break in the entire book falls just after this passage's sequel, and this passage sits inside the book's single unbroken block.** `[T]` as a datum about one manuscript. The Westminster Leningrad Codex prints exactly one paragraph marker in Ruth — a petuchah after 4:17 — so the scribal tradition divides the book in one place only, between the naming of Obed and the genealogy. The legal scene, the marriage, the birth and the naming run without a break. *Witness named, not authorial, and the manuscript spread is unchecked* — see Textual Variants. Surfaced by: Structure, Textual Variants.

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

Extracted from the overview rather than re-derived, with its v1.4 correction in force.

- **Section:** Ketuvim, among the Megilloth; fourth in the sequence BHS prints.
- **Reading implication:** Ruth is read as a Writing, not as an appendix to Judges. **The Proverbs adjacency may not be used as an argument**: *Baba Batra* 14b places Ruth first among the Writings, before Psalms, and the Proverbs–Ruth sequence is the later arrangement — a move into the Megilloth dated between the sixth and ninth centuries AD `[S]`.
- **Presupposes:** the judges' period (1:1); the Torah's redemption and inheritance law (Leviticus 25; Deuteronomy 25); Genesis 38's Judah–Tamar episode, named outright at 4:12; the Rachel and Leah narratives of Genesis 29–35.
- **Handoff:** to David (4:17, 4:22), and so to Samuel.
- **Neighbours:** Proverbs before, Song of Songs after, in the printed sequence — with the caveat above.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Three things are outstanding when 4:1 opens. (i) At 3:12–13 Boaz has named a nearer redeemer — גֹּאֵל קָרוֹב מִמֶּנִּי ("a redeemer nearer than I") — and bound himself by an oath to settle it in the morning. (ii) At 3:18 Naomi has told Ruth to sit still, כִּי לֹא יִשְׁקֹט הָאִישׁ כִּי־אִם־כִּלָּה הַדָּבָר הַיּוֹם ("for the man will not rest until he has settled the matter today"), and the word she uses, דָּבָר ("matter"), is the word the custom of 4:7 exists לְקַיֵּם ("to confirm") `[T]`. (iii) Further back, at 2:20, Naomi has identified Boaz as מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ הוּא ("he is one of our redeemers") — planting a legal category the book has not yet executed.

**Necessity answer.** The passage exists here because everything the book has done so far has been private, and redemption in Israel is not private. A famine, a bereavement, a gleaning field, a threshing floor at night: the whole plot to this point happens between two or three people with no witnesses. Ruth 4:1–12 is where the book moves the transaction into daylight, into the gate, in front of ten elders and all the people, and says עֵדִים אַתֶּם הַיּוֹם ("you are witnesses today") twice `[T]`. It cannot come earlier, because the nearer redeemer's prior claim is not discovered until 3:12; it cannot come later, because the marriage of 4:13 and the birth of 4:14–17 both depend on the claim being legally clear.

**The gate is the specific hinge.** שַׁעַר ("gate") occurs four times in Ruth — 3:11, 4:1, 4:10, 4:11 `[T]`. The first is spoken at the threshing floor at night, when Boaz tells Ruth that כָּל־שַׁעַר עַמִּי ("all the gate of my people") knows she is a woman of worth. He names a venue he is not in. The other three are this passage. **The book names the gate in the dark and then goes to it in the light** `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

**Implication for this passage.** The scene should not be preached as procedural delay before the happy ending. It is the answer to the question the book has been asking since 1:21, where Naomi said אֲנִי מְלֵאָה הָלַכְתִּי וְרֵיקָם הֱשִׁיבַנִי יְהוָה ("I went out full, and YHWH has brought me back empty"). Emptiness in this book has a legal shape — a field that cannot be kept and a name that will be cut off — and 4:9–10 is where both are repaired in public, at a named man's expense.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens the answer. Leviticus 25 has been live since 2:20; Deuteronomy 25 arrives only here; and the passage fuses them in a formula the canon does not otherwise contain (Headline 2). The positional answer is therefore not only "this is where it becomes public" but "this is where two separate Torah provisions are made to meet over one woman and one field", which no earlier passage could have done.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** Ruth tells how YHWH's חֶסֶד ("covenant-kindness") turns an Israelite widow's emptiness into the line that produces David, and does it through people who pay for it. The book names God as actor in the narrator's own voice exactly once (4:13); everywhere else his kindness arrives through human hands that bear a cost.

**Passage-level.** This unit's contribution is to make the cost *legal, public and priced*. It does three things no other passage in the book does. It names what redemption actually costs — a field bought, a widow acquired, an inheritance jeopardised. It puts a man on the stage who calculates the cost correctly and declines, which is the only way to show that Boaz's willingness is not merely decent but expensive. And it converts a private kindness into a transaction with witnesses, so that the son born in 4:13 is legally Mahlon's name on Elimelech's land and not simply Boaz's child.

**The nearer redeemer is the passage's argument.** He is not a villain. He says לֹא אוּכַל ("I cannot") twice in one verse and gives an honest reason: פֶּן־אַשְׁחִית אֶת־נַחֲלָתִי ("lest I ruin my own inheritance") `[T]`. He is doing exactly what a prudent man should. The author has constructed a foil whose refusal is reasonable, which is what makes Boaz's acceptance cost something `[I]`, *high confidence*. Remove him and the chapter is paperwork.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above.* Preceding movement: the nearer redeemer named under oath (3:12–13), Naomi's "he will not rest until he has settled the matter today" (3:18), and the redeemer category planted at 2:20. Necessity: the book's whole plot has been private, and redemption in Israel is public; this is where the gate named in the dark at 3:11 is entered in daylight. Implication: the scene answers Naomi's emptiness of 1:21 in its legal form — a field and a name.

**Immediately before (3:16–18).** Ruth returns to Naomi with six measures of barley and the message that Boaz said she must not go back to her mother-in-law empty — רֵיקָם, the word of 1:21 `[T]`. Naomi tells her to sit, שְׁבִי בִתִּי ("sit, my daughter", 3:18), using the same verb that will dominate 4:1–4. **Naomi's last word before the gate scene is "sit", and the gate scene opens by seating three parties** `[T]`, which is a small structural seam worth noticing.

**Immediately after (4:13–17).** The marriage, the conception given by YHWH in the narrator's own voice, the birth, the women's blessing of Naomi, and the naming of Obed. 4:13's וַיִּקַּח בֹּעַז אֶת־רוּת ("so Boaz took Ruth") executes what 4:10 has declared; the verb is לָקַח ("take"), the same verb used of Boaz taking the ten elders at 4:2 `[T]`.

**Section.** The unit is the first half of the book's fourth and final movement. Its boundaries are clean at both ends: 4:1 opens with a change of place and of cast, and 4:13 changes the subject from the court to the household.

**Book.** Ruth has four movements — emptied in Moab (1:1–5), the road back (1:6–22), the field (2), the threshing floor (3) and the gate with its outcome (4). This passage is the only one set in a public legal venue.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 4:1–2 | **Convening** | Boaz goes up, seats the redeemer, seats ten elders. Three acts of sitting-down, five occurrences of the verb. |
| 4:3–4 | **The first offer: the field** | The property is put, the right of first refusal honoured, and the redeemer accepts: אָנֹכִי אֶגְאָל ("I will redeem"). |
| 4:5 | **The pivot** | Boaz adds Ruth and the name of the dead. One verse, and the transaction changes character. |
| 4:6 | **The refusal** | לֹא אוּכַל ("I cannot") at the start and כִּי לֹא־אוּכַל ("for I cannot") at the end — the verse is bracketed by its own refusal. |
| 4:7–8 | **The narrator's aside and the sandal** | The one place in the book where the narrator dates himself relative to his story, followed by the act he has just explained. |
| 4:9–10 | **Boaz's declaration** | Framed by עֵדִים אַתֶּם הַיּוֹם ("you are witnesses today") at the opening of 4:9 and the close of 4:10. |
| 4:11–12 | **The response and the blessing** | The people and elders answer עֵדִים ("witnesses"), then bless in three movements: Rachel and Leah, Ephrathah and Bethlehem, Perez and Tamar. |

**The binding device is a reversal at the centre, with two inclusios inside it** `[T]`.

The reversal: the redeemer says אָנֹכִי אֶגְאָל ("I will redeem", 4:4) and then לֹא אוּכַל לִגְאֹל ("I cannot redeem", 4:6). Between those two statements stands one verse. **Verse 5 is the hinge of the chapter and of the book's plot**, because everything after it follows from the nearer redeemer's withdrawal.

The first inclusio is inside 4:6, which opens and closes on לֹא אוּכַל ("I cannot") — the refusal stated, reasoned, and restated, with the transfer of the right of redemption in between `[T]`. The second is across 4:9–10: עֵדִים אַתֶּם הַיּוֹם ("you are witnesses today") stands at the head of Boaz's declaration and again at its foot, enclosing the field, the three dead men, Ruth, the purpose and the gate `[T]`. *High confidence* on both as distributions.

**Note on what the structure is not.** It is not a chiasm. The scene's beats do not mirror each other around verse 5; they pivot on it. A chiastic proposal here would have to force the sandal aside (4:7) into correspondence with the convening (4:1–2), and nothing in the vocabulary supports that `[I]`.

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative Hebrew carries its logic in verb forms more than in connectors, so this tool is thinner here than it would be in an epistle. Four are worth tracing.

- **וְהִנֵּה ("and behold", 4:1)** — the narrator's perception marker, placed at the moment Boaz sits down `[T]`. It is one of only three narratorial occurrences in the book (2:4, 3:8, 4:1), one in each of the last three chapters. See Tool 6.
- **אִם … וְאִם־לֹא ("if … and if not", 4:4)** — the conditional pair that frames the legal offer. Note the grammatical oddity: the first is second person (אִם־תִּגְאַל, "if you will redeem") and the second third person (וְאִם־לֹא יִגְאַל, "and if he will not redeem") `[T]`. The switch is abrupt; the BHS apparatus records that many manuscripts and the versions read the second as a second person too. See Textual Variants.
- **פֶּן ("lest", 4:6)** — the only occurrence in the book `[T]`. It carries the nearer redeemer's whole motive in one word.
- **כִּי ("for, that", 4:4, 4:6, 4:9)** — three occurrences, each doing different work: grounding the offer ("for there is no one but you"), grounding the refusal ("for I cannot"), and introducing the content of the witness ("that I have bought") `[T]`.

The connective that matters most is the one the Hebrew does **not** supply. Between 4:4's "I will redeem" and 4:5's "Then Boaz said" there is no adversative at all — just וַיֹּאמֶר בֹּעַז ("and Boaz said") `[T]`. The reversal is achieved without a "but", and both English versions reproduce the bareness ("And he said… Then Boaz said"). The effect is that the trap closes quietly.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as Hebrew poetic parallelism** — this is legal narrative prose, not verse.

One structure is parallel in a looser sense and repays notice: the **blessing of 4:11–12 is built in three matched members**, each naming a woman or a house and each invoking a precedent `[T]`:

> like Rachel and Leah, who built the house of Israel
> … act worthily in Ephrathah and call a name in Bethlehem
> … may your house be like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah

Each member moves from a person to a place or a house, and the three together move from the nation (the house of Israel) through the town (Ephrathah and Bethlehem) to the clan (the house of Perez) — a narrowing, not a widening `[I]`, *moderate*. The blessing arrives at the exact family the genealogy of 4:18–22 will then trace.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Two authorial intrusions, and one of them is the most overt in the book.

**(a) 4:7 — the antiquarian note.** וְזֹאת לְפָנִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל ("now this was formerly in Israel") `[T]`. The narrator steps entirely out of the scene to explain a custom his readers no longer practise, and he does it for a whole verse — the longest uninterrupted narratorial aside in Ruth. Three things follow. First, **it dates the narrator relative to his story**: the custom is לְפָנִים ("formerly"), so the telling is distant from the telling's events `[T]`, and the gap is the one piece of compositional evidence the book itself supplies. Second, it tells the reader that the sandal is not self-explanatory, which is a caution against confident reconstructions of what it meant. Third, the explanation is framed as *attestation*: וְזֹאת הַתְּעוּדָה בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל ("and this was the attestation in Israel"), which places the custom in the same category as the human witnesses of 4:9–11 `[I]`, *moderate–high*. The narrator is explaining how, before this scene's witnesses, a matter was made binding.

**(b) 4:1 — וְהִנֵּה ("and behold").** The narrator switches briefly to Boaz's viewpoint: Boaz sits, and *behold*, the redeemer is passing `[T]`. The reader sees the man arrive as Boaz sees him. The effect is to leave the arrival unexplained — the text does not say Boaz sent for him, and does not say it was providence either. Compare 2:3's וַיִּקֶר מִקְרֶהָ ("and her chance chanced"), where the narrator makes the same move and is equally reticent `[I]`, *moderate*.

**What the narrator does not say.** He gives the nearer redeemer no name, no motive beyond the one the man states himself, and no verdict. He does not call the refusal selfish. **A sermon that supplies a verdict here is supplying what the narrator withheld** `[T]` for the absence, `[I]` for the inference — and the inference is safe, because this narrator does pass verdicts elsewhere when he wants to (he tells us Boaz's heart was merry at 3:7, and he tells us YHWH gave conception at 4:13).

### 7. Vocabulary

Six words carry this passage. Every count below is made against the Westminster Leningrad Codex by lemma.

**גָּאַל ("redeem") and its nouns.** Twelve occurrences in these twelve verses, out of twenty-one in the book — and **nine of the twelve stand in verses 4 and 6 alone** `[T]`: five in 4:4 (תִּגְאַל, גְּאָל, יִגְאַל, לִגְאוֹל, אֶגְאָל — "if you will redeem, redeem; and if he will not redeem… to redeem… I will redeem") and four in 4:6. The word saturates the negotiation and then stops: it does not occur again until 4:14, where the women use it of the child. *High confidence* on the distribution. The related noun גְּאֻלָּה ("right of redemption", 4:6, 4:7) is almost a Leviticus word — it stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, **nine of them in Leviticus 25** (25:24, 26, 29, 31, 32, 48, 51, 52), with Jeremiah 32:7–8, Ezekiel 11:15 and these two `[T]`. *High confidence.* When the nearer redeemer hands over גְּאֻלָּתִי ("my right of redemption"), he is using the technical term of the Jubilee legislation.

**קָנָה ("acquire, buy").** Six occurrences, **all of them in this passage** — 4:4, 4:5, 4:8, 4:9, 4:10 `[T]`, with the sixth being the unpointed form at 4:5. *High confidence.* The same verb governs the field (4:4, 4:8, 4:9) and the woman (4:5, 4:10). The Hebrew does not soften this and neither should a sermon; see Difficult Verses.

**עֵד ("witness"), זָקֵן ("elder"), נַחֲלָה ("inheritance").** Three, four and three occurrences respectively — and **every occurrence each has in the book is here** `[T]`. *High confidence.* Two further rarities fall out of this. עֵד with זָקֵן in one verse stands in **two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Ruth 4:9 and Ruth 4:11** — the collocation belongs to this scene and nowhere else `[T]`. And שָׁחַת ("ruin, destroy") with נַחֲלָה ("inheritance") in one verse stands in **two verses: Deuteronomy 9:26 and Ruth 4:6** `[T]` — though the senses differ sharply, Moses pleading that God not destroy his people-as-inheritance against a man protecting his property, so this is a rare collocation rather than an allusion. *Moderate* on the rarity; *uncertain* that anything is being invoked.

**יָשַׁב ("sit, dwell") — and the paronomasia check.** Six occurrences in 4:1–4, which fires the repetition trigger `[T]`. The forms are וַיֵּשֶׁב ("and he sat", 4:1), שְׁבָה ("sit", 4:1), וַיֵּשֵׁב ("and he sat", 4:1), שְׁבוּ ("sit", 4:2), וַיֵּשֵׁבוּ ("and they sat", 4:2) and הַיֹּשְׁבִים ("those sitting", 4:4). Running the check: **the imperative שְׁבָה ("sit", 4:1) and the epithet הַשָּׁבָה ("the one who has returned", 4:3) are the same three consonants**, the second with the article `[T]`. The latter is from שׁוּב ("return"), the book's first keyword, and הַשָּׁבָה מִשְּׂדֵה מוֹאָב ("who has returned from the field of Moab") is Naomi's standing label — return with field and Moab stand together in four verses, all of them in Ruth: 1:6, 1:22, 2:6 and 4:3 `[T]`, so 4:3 is the label's fourth and final use. *High confidence* on the distribution; **the inference that the sound-play is designed is `[I]` and capped at *moderate*, with no chance baseline run** — it is queued as a design claim for the next claim audit, on the discipline this book's audit has just established.

**פְּלֹנִי אַלְמֹנִי ("a certain one, so-and-so", 4:1).** The nearer redeemer is never named. The expression stands in **three verses of the Hebrew Bible: 1 Samuel 21:2 [Heb 21:3], 2 Kings 6:8 and Ruth 4:1** `[T]`. *High confidence.* Both rivals are places where a name is deliberately withheld — David naming no rendezvous to Ahimelech, and the king of Aram naming no camp — so the idiom's canonical register is *withheld information*, not *anonymity by accident* `[I]`, *moderate*. The man who would not raise up a name does not get one.

**Proper-noun inventory (Tool 7c).** Boaz, Elimelech, Naomi, Ruth, Mahlon, Chilion, Rachel, Leah, Tamar, Judah, Perez, Israel, Moab, Ephrathah, Bethlehem; plus the number ten (4:2). Routine scenery: Elimelech, Mahlon, Chilion, Moab — all established earlier in the book with no fresh freight here. Candidates with canonical freight, taken up under Tool 11: **Rachel and Leah**, **Ephrathah and Bethlehem**, **Perez, Tamar and Judah**. The number **ten** gets one line: the claim audit withdrew the "ten" numerical design for this book, and the count of elders stands as a count `[S: audit]`.

### 8. Translations

**4:11 — the חַיִל word, and the chain the NASB95 breaks.** The Hebrew is וַעֲשֵׂה־חַיִל בְּאֶפְרָתָה ("and do/act חַיִל in Ephrathah"). NASB95: "may you achieve **wealth** in Ephrathah". ESV: "May you act **worthily** in Ephrathah." חַיִל stands three times in Ruth — 2:1 of Boaz (אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל, "a mighty man of worth"), 3:11 of Ruth (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל, "a woman of worth"), and here `[T]`. **The NASB95's "wealth" severs the third from the first two**; the ESV's "act worthily" keeps the word audible. *High confidence.* The congregation hearing the ESV can hear Boaz, Ruth and the blessing joined by one word; the congregation hearing the NASB95 cannot. The idiom עָשָׂה חַיִל ("do valiantly") itself stands in six verses — Numbers 24:18, 1 Samuel 14:48, Psalm 118:15, 118:16, Proverbs 31:29, Ruth 4:11 `[T]`.

**4:10–11 — the gate the NASB95 deletes twice.** שַׁעַר ("gate") stands at 4:1, 4:10, 4:11 (and 3:11). NASB95 has "gate" at 4:1 but "the court of his birth place" at 4:10 and "in the court" at 4:11; ESV has "gate" at all three `[T]`. **The ESV preserves three of the book's four gate references and the NASB95 only one.** *High confidence.* This matters because the gate chain is the book's own device for linking the threshing floor to the court (Phase 0.6), and a congregation hearing "court" twice will not hear it.

**4:9–10 — the verb the NASB95 varies.** The Hebrew has קָנִיתִי ("I have bought/acquired") at 4:9 and again at 4:10, identical in form. NASB95 renders the first "I have bought" and the second "I have acquired"; ESV has "I have bought" at both `[T]`. The variation is stylistic in English and invisible in Hebrew, and it slightly softens the point that the same verb governs the field and the woman. *Moderate* significance.

**4:4 — the idiom both versions dissolve.** אֶגְלֶה אָזְנְךָ is literally "I will uncover your ear". NASB95 "I thought to inform you"; ESV "I thought I would tell you of it" `[T]`. Neither congregation hears the idiom, and neither hears that it is overwhelmingly a Samuel idiom (Tool 11). The loss is small for the sense and real for the resonance.

**4:3 — a grammatical choice both versions make.** מָכְרָה is a perfect, "has sold" or "sold"; both versions render it as something not yet done — NASB95 "has to sell", ESV "is selling" `[T]`. The perfect is awkward if the sale has not happened, and the versions are resolving a genuine difficulty rather than inventing one. *Moderate confidence* that the sense is an offer to sell rather than a completed sale; flagged to Open Questions.

**Pulpit divergence note.** The declared pulpit text is the ESV. **At every one of the three load-bearing points above, the ESV is the more transparent** — it keeps חַיִל audible, keeps the gate, and keeps the repeated verb. This extends the sweep's cross-passage finding that for Ruth the pulpit text serves the congregation better than the study text, and brings the count of measured divergence points from five to eight. The preacher using the ESV has less repair work to do here than at any other point in the book.

**Ancient versions check.** The trigger is met at 4:10, where both moderns agree on a rendering that carries weight. **Rahlfs has ἐκ τῆς φυλῆς λαοῦ αὐτοῦ ("from the tribe of his people") where the Hebrew has מִשַּׁעַר מְקוֹמוֹ ("from the gate of his place")** `[T]`. This is the second time the Greek puts φυλή ("tribe") where the Hebrew has שַׁעַר ("gate") — the first is 3:11, πᾶσα φυλὴ λαοῦ μου against כָּל־שַׁעַר עַמִּי, which the sweep flagged as an unresolved open question. **Two occurrences of the same substitution in the same idiom is a translator's habit, not two independent variants**, and that substantially weakens the hypothesis of a Hebrew *Vorlage* reading שֵׁבֶט ("tribe") `[I]`, *moderate–high*. Classified as **category 1, translation choice**, not a substantive variant. This closes an open question the sweep left open; see Open Questions.

Two further Greek divergences, both category 2 and both minor, are taken up under Textual Variants: 4:3's ἣ δέδοται Νωεμιν ("which has been given to Naomi") against מָכְרָה נָעֳמִי ("Naomi has sold"), and 4:7's supply of a noun, τὸ δικαίωμα ("the ordinance"), where the Hebrew has none.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The dominant register is procedural restraint, and it is deliberate.** After three chapters of famine, bereavement, gleaning and a night on a threshing floor, the book's climax is conducted in the flattest prose it contains: men sit down, a property is offered, a condition is added, a refusal is given, a shoe changes hands, a declaration is made. There is no adjective of feeling anywhere in these twelve verses `[T]`.

The devices that produce the register:

- **Seating.** Five acts of sitting in two verses (Tool 7). Nothing in Hebrew narrative slows a scene like making everyone sit down first.
- **Legal formulae.** נֶגֶד הַיֹּשְׁבִים וְנֶגֶד זִקְנֵי עַמִּי ("before those sitting and before the elders of my people", 4:4); עֵדִים אַתֶּם הַיּוֹם ("you are witnesses today") twice; the attestation formula of 4:7 `[T]`.
- **An unnamed party.** The refusal is given by a man the text will not name (Tool 7).
- **Repetition to the point of redundancy.** Four forms of "redeem" in 4:6, three of "I cannot / cannot" `[T]`.

**Then the register changes completely at 4:11.** The people and elders answer in a blessing with three invocations, two matriarchs, two place names and a patriarchal house. The shift from the flattest prose in the book to its most expansive benediction, inside one verse boundary, is the passage's emotional architecture `[I]`, *high confidence*. **The scene is cold until the community speaks, and then it is warm.** A sermon that preaches the whole passage at one temperature has missed the design.

**Soundtrack.** For 4:1–10, a clock. For 4:11–12, the room standing up.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources carried in from the overview, at elevated attention:** Genesis (six distinct passages across the book), Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 25, 1–2 Samuel. **Candidates derived independently from this passage's own vocabulary before any source was consulted:** the redemption-and-inheritance vocabulary (גְּאֻלָּה, נַחֲלָה, קָנָה); the name-raising formula; the witness vocabulary (עֵד, זָקֵן, הַתְּעוּדָה); the sandal; the three women of the blessing; "uncover the ear".

#### Leviticus 25 → Ruth 4:3–8 *(high confidence on the vocabulary)*

*Source context.* Leviticus 25 is the Jubilee legislation. Its governing principle is stated at 25:23 — כִּי־לִי הָאָרֶץ ("for the land is mine") — and its mechanism is that land cannot be alienated permanently, so a kinsman גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") buys back what poverty has forced a relative to sell (25:25). The chapter deals with houses, fields and persons in that order, and it ends with the redemption of an Israelite sold to a resident alien (25:47–55). Its emotional register is protective: the law exists so that a family does not vanish from its holding.

*Book usage.* Leviticus 25 has been live in Ruth since 2:20, where Naomi calls Boaz מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ ("one of our redeemers") and קָרוֹב ("near") — the two words standing together in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, Leviticus 25:25, Ruth 2:20 and Ruth 3:12 `[T]`. So the source is used at the moment of recognition (2:20), at the moment of complication (3:12), and now at the moment of execution. **The book is tracking Leviticus 25's own sequence**: identify the redeemer, establish his priority, transact.

*OT-to-OT.* Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 25 are not in explicit conversation in the OT — they govern different things, land and lineage — and the fact that Ruth 4 needs both is the point. See the Deuteronomy row below.

*What it adds.* The measurement that was not in the overview: גְּאֻלָּה ("right of redemption"), the word the nearer redeemer uses when he hands his claim over at 4:6, stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible and **nine of them are Leviticus 25** `[T]`. *High confidence.* The technical term of this scene is the technical term of the Jubilee law, which means the field transaction is being conducted under Leviticus 25 and the reader is meant to know it. What the law does not supply is the woman.

#### Deuteronomy 25:5–10 → Ruth 4:5–10 *(moderate — the audit's verdict, with a new measurement)*

The claim audit rated this row **Needs reframing, moderate**, naming Deuteronomy 25:6 and 2 Samuel 14:7 as equal rivals for the name-raising idiom and the sandal verb as divergent `[S: audit]`. That verdict is accepted and not re-derived. This dig adds two measurements that sharpen it in the same direction.

*Source context.* Deuteronomy 25:5–10 legislates for brothers dwelling together when one dies childless: the surviving brother takes the widow, the firstborn succeeds to the dead brother's name, and if he refuses, the widow brings him to the elders at the gate, pulls off his sandal, spits in his face, and his house is called "the house of him whose sandal was pulled off". The register is shaming; the refusal is a disgrace.

*Book usage.* Deuteronomy 25 appears in Ruth only here. There is no earlier use to track.

*What the measurements add.* **First, the technical vocabulary of the institution is absent from this chapter, though not from the book** `[T]`. *(Restated at v1.1; the first version claimed it was absent from Ruth altogether, which is false.)* The masculine יָבָם ("husband's brother") stands at Deuteronomy 25:5, Deuteronomy 25:7 and Genesis 38:8, and Ruth never uses it. The feminine יְבָמָה stands at Deuteronomy 25:7, Deuteronomy 25:9 and Ruth 1:15, twice, where Naomi uses it of Orpah — in a related but distinct sense, the co-sister-in-law rather than Deuteronomy's widow who claims the duty. *High confidence* on both counts. This remains a **pattern-break absence of the admissible kind**, and a sharper one than the original claim: the pattern is citable, the book demonstrably knows the word, and it declines to use it at the one place where a transaction actually happens. **Second, the governing verb of Ruth 4 is absent from Deuteronomy 25.** גָּאַל ("redeem") does not occur in Deuteronomy 25 at all; in the whole book of Deuteronomy the participle stands only at 19:6 and 19:12, of the גֹּאֵל הַדָּם ("avenger of blood") `[T]`. *High confidence.* And third, as the audit found, the sandal verbs do not meet: חָלַץ ("pull off") with נַעַל ("sandal") stands in three verses — Deuteronomy 25:9, 25:10, Isaiah 20:2 — while שָׁלַף ("draw out") with נַעַל stands in two, Ruth 4:7 and 4:8, and nowhere else `[T]`.

*What it adds.* Deuteronomy 25 is **in view and not in force**. Boaz's purpose clause borrows its name-raising language; the institution it legislates is not the one being executed. The scene is a land-redemption under Leviticus 25 to which a marriage has been attached for a Deuteronomic purpose, and **no single law in the Torah governs the combination** — which is why the narrator has to stop and explain the procedure himself at 4:7.

#### Deuteronomy 19 → Ruth 4:9–11 *(moderate — new to this dig, and unreported in the overview or the sweep)*

*Source context.* Deuteronomy 19 has two halves. The first establishes the cities of refuge and is the only place in Deuteronomy where גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") appears — twice, as גֹּאֵל הַדָּם ("the avenger of blood", 19:6, 19:12), the kinsman whose office is to pursue a killer `[T]`. The second half (19:15–21) is the law of witnesses: עַל־פִּי שְׁנֵי עֵדִים אוֹ עַל־פִּי שְׁלֹשָׁה־עֵדִים יָקוּם דָּבָר ("on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses a matter shall be established", 19:15), with the elders adjudicating and a malicious witness punished.

*Why it surfaces here.* A rare-shared-vocabulary scan of the whole Hebrew Bible against Ruth 4 ranks **Deuteronomy 19 second of 859 chapters**, with contacts at Ruth 4:4, 4:6, 4:10 and 4:11 `[T]`. A second Deuteronomic witness law, chapter 17, ranks tenth: עֵד ("witness") with כֹּל ("all") and עַם ("people") in one verse stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible — Deuteronomy 17:7, Micah 1:2, Ruth 4:9 and Ruth 4:11 `[T]`.

*What it adds, and what it does not.* Two cautions first. The rank is a measurement, not an allusion; this project's claim audit has just withdrawn three findings that rested on ranks without nulls, and no null has been run for this one. And the shared vocabulary is partly legal commonplace — witnesses, elders, a matter established. **So this is reported at *moderate*, as a register rather than a citation.** What it contributes is real nonetheless: Deuteronomy 19 holds together the two things Ruth 4 holds together, a kinsman called גֹּאֵל and a matter made binding by witnesses, and 19:15's יָקוּם דָּבָר ("a matter shall be established") uses the same root as Ruth 4:7's לְקַיֵּם כָּל־דָּבָר ("to confirm any matter") `[T]`. The gate scene is being conducted by Deuteronomy's rules of evidence even though it is executing Leviticus's law of property.

#### Genesis 38 → Ruth 4:12 *(high confidence — named, not echoed)*

*Source context.* Genesis 38 interrupts the Joseph narrative. Judah withholds his third son from Tamar, his widowed daughter-in-law; Tamar disguises herself, intercepts Judah on the road to Timnah, conceives by him, and when accused produces his pledge; Judah says צָדְקָה מִמֶּנִּי ("she is more righteous than I", 38:26). Perez is the elder of the resulting twins, born with the midwife's exclamation at the breach (38:29). The chapter is about a widow denied the provision owed her who obtains by stratagem what the family withheld by right — and about a man who admits it. Note that Genesis 38:8 is one of the three verses in the canon carrying the levirate term יָבָם `[T]`: **the elders invoke the one narrative that does use the technical vocabulary Ruth avoids.**

*Book usage.* Genesis 38 is used twice within thirteen verses — at 4:12 by name, and at 4:18 where Perez heads the genealogy. Perez with Tamar and Judah in one verse stands in **two verses of the Hebrew Bible, 1 Chronicles 2:4 and Ruth 4:12** `[T]`. *High confidence.* Genesis is the book's most-contacted source (six verified passages), though the claim audit has withdrawn the claim that this amounts to a programme `[S: audit]`.

*OT-to-OT.* Genesis 38 and Deuteronomy 25 stand in a relation the elders are exploiting: Genesis 38 narrates the duty that Deuteronomy 25 later legislates, and the two share the levirate vocabulary `[T]`. By invoking Genesis 38 the elders bless a union in the category of Judah and Tamar's — irregular, legally contested, procured by a woman's initiative — at the exact moment the scene has demonstrated that the Deuteronomic law does not cover it.

*What it adds.* The blessing is not a compliment about fertility. **The community names the most irregular precedent in Judah's line and asks for a house like it** `[I]`, *moderate–high*. The book knows how unusual its own plot is, and says so through the mouths of the elders rather than the narrator.

#### Genesis 29–35 and 48 → Ruth 4:11 *(moderate–high)*

*Source context.* Rachel and Leah are the sisters Jacob marries in Genesis 29, the rivalry over childbearing runs through chapters 29–31, and Rachel dies in childbirth on the road to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem (35:16–19), a notice Jacob repeats on his deathbed (48:7).

*What the measurements show.* Rachel with Leah in one verse stands in **thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, twelve of them in Genesis 29–33 — and Ruth 4:11 is the only verse outside Genesis that names them together** `[T]`. *High confidence.* אֶפְרָתָה ("Ephrathah") stands in nine verses: Genesis 35:16, 35:19, 48:7, Ruth 4:11, Micah 5:2 [Heb 5:1], Psalm 132:6, 1 Chronicles 2:19, 2:50, 4:4 `[T]`. **Three of the nine are the Rachel burial notices.**

*What it adds.* The elders bless a woman entering a house at Bethlehem by naming the two women who built Israel — and the place they name, Ephrathah, is where one of those two died bearing her second son `[I]`, *moderate–high*. The blessing is generous and it is not naive: it invokes fruitfulness at the site of a mother's death in childbirth. Given that Ruth 4:13's conception is the only act the narrator attributes to YHWH in his own voice, the juxtaposition is worth the preacher's attention.

#### "Uncover the ear" → Ruth 4:4 *(moderate — idiom-capped)*

גָּלָה ("uncover") with אֹזֶן ("ear") stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible: 1 Samuel 9:15; 20:2, 12, 13; 22:8, 17; 2 Samuel 7:27; Isaiah 22:14; Job 33:16; 36:10, 15; 1 Chronicles 17:25; and Ruth 4:4 `[T]`. Thirteen verses makes it an idiom, so it may not be rated above *moderate* on wording. But the distribution is lopsided: **six of the thirteen are in 1–2 Samuel, and one more is the Chronicles parallel of 2 Samuel 7:27**, the Davidic-covenant prayer `[T]`. For a book whose last word is David, the register is worth naming. *Moderate.*

*Planted for later:*
- **שֵׁם ("name").** 4:11's וּקְרָא־שֵׁם בְּבֵית לָחֶם ("and call a name in Bethlehem") is answered six verses later when the women וַתִּקְרֶאנָה לוֹ שֵׁם ("called his name", 4:17) `[T]`, *high*.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew legal narrative, with an embedded antiquarian gloss (4:7) and an embedded blessing (4:11–12). Reading rules: the scene is presented as something that happened, the procedure is reported rather than prescribed, and the narrator's aside tells the reader that the procedure is obsolete at the time of writing.

One genre question is worth naming because it affects preaching. **This is not a law text and it is not a legal precedent.** It is a narrative that *describes* a transaction conducted under laws the reader is assumed to know, in a form the narrator has to explain. Treating 4:1–12 as a source for Israelite legal procedure asks it to do something it declines to do; the one procedural detail it gives, the sandal, it gives in the past tense `[T]`.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** The judges' period, before the monarchy — and the passage's whole point is to get the line to the monarchy. The genealogy that follows runs to David.

**Canonical position.** Ketuvim, among the Megilloth (Phase 0.55). The canonical reader arrives at Ruth having read the Torah and the whole of the Prophets, so they already know what the house of David became and how it ended. **A scene in which a man buys a field so that a dead man's name is not cut off reads differently to someone who knows the land was lost** `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

**What has happened since, that changes the reading.** The redeemer-kinsman institution presupposes inalienable tribal land, a functioning local court at the gate, and a family's survival through its name on its holding. None of these survives into the Christian's situation. What does survive is the institution's logic: that a person without resources is restored by a relative who can afford to act and chooses to pay. Hebrews takes exactly that logic and applies it to the incarnation — the redeemer must share the blood of those he redeems.

**The tool's warning applies here.** Do not transfer the land promise. The field of 4:3–9 is a real field in Bethlehem, and the inheritance it belongs to is Elimelech's, not the church's.

### 15. Who Am I?

- **Boaz** is primarily a **type of Christ** through the redeemer category, and only secondarily an example. The tests: the connection runs through an established theological category (redemption), the NT makes the same kind of connection repeatedly, and the antitype escalates — Boaz redeems a field and a name at the cost of part of his estate; Christ redeems persons at the cost of his life. See Christological Reading.
- **The nearer redeemer** is a **foil**. He exists to make Boaz's cost visible. He is not "the unbeliever" and not "the lukewarm Christian"; he is a prudent man, and the passage does not condemn him.
- **Ruth and Naomi** are, in this passage, the **beneficiaries who are not in the room**. If there is an identification to make, it is here: the people whose future is being settled are absent from the negotiation and have no vote in it.
- **The elders and the people** are the **community**, and the nearest thing in the passage to the congregation hearing it.

**Where this is commonly misread as "me":** the preacher who makes Boaz a model of godly manhood and the congregation his imitators. The passage will support that at the level of procedure (Tool 13) but it is not the argument. **Boaz is doing something for people who cannot do it for themselves, at a price they do not pay.**

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author is seeking.** That the reader should see the emptiness of 1:21 repaired by a named man's costly, public, legally watertight act — and should carry that into the genealogy, so that the line to David is read as the fruit of redemption rather than of romance or luck.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

*Worldview.* Three things this passage asks the reader to believe. **Redemption costs the redeemer.** The nearer redeemer's arithmetic is correct: taking on Ruth and the dead man's name really would have damaged his estate, and Boaz's acceptance really does cost him what the other man declined to spend. **Redemption is public.** The book will not let the decisive act happen in private, and insists on witnesses twice in two verses. **Redemption is for the absent.** The two women whose lives are settled here are not present.

*Behaviour.* Stop reading the chapter as the wedding that follows the courtship; the wedding is one verse (4:13) and the court case is twelve. Start asking what it costs to act for someone whose situation is not your fault — because that is precisely the question the nearer redeemer answers honestly and Boaz answers differently.

*Motivation — and the guard against moralism.* The passage gives no command. Its motive structure runs the other way: the only act in the whole book that the narrator attributes to God in his own voice is the conception of 4:13, immediately after this transaction. **The human cost and the divine gift stand side by side, and the text does not make the second a reward for the first.**

*Four audiences.* **For me:** where am I the nearer redeemer, with a correct calculation and a clean refusal? **For a Christian friend:** the one carrying a cost that is not their fault, who needs to hear that the gospel's shape is somebody else paying. **For the church:** we are the elders at the gate more often than we are Boaz — the community that witnesses and blesses. **For an unbeliever:** a man pays, publicly, so that a dead man's name is not erased and a foreign widow has a future; that is the shape of the Christian claim.

*Prayer.* That the redeemed would stop reading themselves into the redeemer's part; that those who can afford to act would; and thanks for a Redeemer who did not say לֹא אוּכַל ("I cannot").

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**The three unpointed words.** 4:4 ואדע, 4:5 קניתי, 4:6 לגאול — three ketiv forms in six verses, which is a high local density `[T]`. Ruth is already one of the densest books in the canon for unpointed forms, and this passage concentrates them at the three points where the legal sense is most at stake. Each is taken up under Textual Variants.

**The person-switch at 4:4.** אִם־תִּגְאַל גְּאָל וְאִם־לֹא יִגְאַל הַגִּידָה לִּי — "if **you** will redeem, redeem; and if **he** will not redeem, tell me" `[T]`. The protasis is second person and the apodosis third, which is awkward in any reading. BHS records that many manuscripts and the versions read the second verb as second person (תִגְאַל) `[T]`. Three explanations are live: a scribal slip; deliberate legal formality, the third person used as a distancing courtesy; or the narrator preserving the switch from a formulaic source. *Uncertain — a grammar would settle the range in a line.* Nothing doctrinal turns on it, and both English versions render it as second person throughout.

**קוּם in two stems.** The hiphil לְהָקִים ("to raise up", 4:5, 4:10) and the piel לְקַיֵּם ("to confirm", 4:7) `[T]`. See Tool 10. Note that the piel of this root for "confirm, ratify" is relatively late and administrative in flavour; the observation is worth flagging to a lexicon rather than asserting. *Uncertain — consider verifying.*

**שָׁלַף and what it usually does.** שָׁלַף ("draw out") stands in twenty-four verses of the Hebrew Bible. **In twenty of them the object is a sword** `[T]`; the only two with a sandal are Ruth 4:7 and 4:8, and the remaining two are Psalm 129:6 and Job 20:25. *High confidence.* The narrator reaches for the sword-drawing verb to describe a man taking off his shoe. Whether that is wit, archaism, or simply the available verb for drawing something off is not decidable from the text `[I]`, *moderate* — but it is the kind of detail that rewards being noticed rather than explained.

**Two rare nouns in one verse (4:7).** תְּמוּרָה ("exchange") stands in six verses — Leviticus 27:10, 27:33, Job 15:31, 20:18, 28:17, Ruth 4:7; the Leviticus pair concern exchanging a dedicated animal `[T]`. תְּעוּדָה ("attestation") stands in three — Isaiah 8:16, 8:20, Ruth 4:7 `[T]`, where Isaiah's are the sealed prophetic testimony. *High confidence* on both counts. The narrator's explanatory verse is built from two of the rarest legal-ish nouns in the canon, which is consistent with his having to explain the custom at all.

**The paronomasia.** See Tool 7: שְׁבָה ("sit", 4:1) and הַשָּׁבָה ("the one who has returned", 4:3) share their consonants, and the second is from the book's opening keyword שׁוּב ("return") `[T]` for the forms, `[I]` *moderate* for the design, **unbaselined and queued for audit**.

### Textual Variants

Three ketiv/qere readings and two Greek divergences. Each is classified under the three-way triage before being reported.

#### 4:5 — קניתי / קָנִיתָה · **category 2, and the passage's decisive variant**

- **The issue.** The consonants are קניתי. BHS records: ketiv קָנִיתִי ("I have acquired", first person), qere קָנִיתָה ("you have acquired", second person), with the note *frt l* קְנֵה *cf Vrs* — perhaps read the imperative "acquire!", with the versions `[T]`.
- **Manuscript and versional evidence.** BHS notes that a few manuscripts read with the qere `[T]`. Rahlfs has καὶ αὐτὴν κτήσασθαί σε δεῖ ("and you must acquire her"), which supports a second-person sense and makes the obligation explicit `[T]`. Both English versions follow the qere.
- **What the morphology adds, and it is new here.** The Westminster Leningrad Codex parses the unpointed form as a **first common singular perfect** — the same consonants, and the same parsing, as the form Boaz uses of himself at 4:9 and at 4:10, קָנִיתִי ("I have bought"), which there carries the pointing the ketiv lacks `[T]`. *High confidence.* So on the ketiv, Boaz says at 4:5 exactly what he will say publicly at 4:10, five verses early and to one man.
- **What turns on it.** On the qere, Boaz is stating the nearer redeemer's obligation: buy the field and you must take the widow too. On the ketiv, Boaz is announcing his own prior claim: on the day you buy the field, *I have acquired* Ruth — which is less a condition than a declaration that the redeemer will get the land but not the woman, and so not the name. The first makes the refusal a calculation about cost; the second makes it a calculation about a man who has already lost the part that mattered.
- **Position.** The qere is the easier reading and has the versions with it; the ketiv is the harder and is the form the chapter twice confirms in Boaz's own mouth. **I do not resolve it**, and the honest statement is that the scene works either way while the nearer redeemer's motive shifts. *Moderate confidence* that the qere gives the sense the book intends; *high confidence* that the ketiv is not a simple error, because of the 4:9–10 agreement.
- **Preaching implication.** Mention it only if the sermon turns on the nearer redeemer's motive. If it does, say plainly that the Hebrew is read two ways and that the English in the pew follows one of them.

#### 4:4 — ואדע / וְאֵדְעָה · **category 2, trivial**

- **The issue.** BHS: ketiv וְאֵדַע ("and I shall know"), qere with many manuscripts וְאֵדְעָה (cohortative, "that I may know") `[T]`.
- **Implication.** None. The cohortative is slightly more polite. Both versions render "that I may know". Do not mention in a sermon.

#### 4:6 — לגאול · **not a variant; a spelling**

The unpointed לגאול at 4:6 is the ketiv of the infinitive, and the verse's final word לִגְאֹל is the same infinitive spelled defectively `[T]`. Nothing turns on it. It is recorded here because a whitespace-based phrase search over this passage will silently miss the maqqef-joined form, which is the mechanical trap the gate exists to catch.

#### 4:3 — μακρά / "has sold" · **category 2, minor**

Rahlfs has ἣ δέδοται Νωεμιν ("which has been given to Naomi") where the Hebrew has מָכְרָה נָעֳמִי ("Naomi has sold") `[T]`; BHS records the Greek `[T]`. The Greek makes Naomi the recipient of the field rather than its seller, which resolves the perfect-tense difficulty the English versions resolve differently (Tool 8). *Moderate confidence* that the Hebrew is prior; the Greek looks like a translator smoothing the same awkwardness. Flagged to Open Questions.

#### 4:7 — τὸ δικαίωμα · **category 2, minor**

Rahlfs supplies a noun, τὸ δικαίωμα ("the ordinance"), where the Hebrew opens with וְזֹאת ("and this") and no noun at all; BHS reconstructs a possible Hebrew behind it `[T]`. The Hebrew is elliptical and the Greek is filling the ellipsis. *Moderate.* No preaching implication.

#### 4:10 — φυλή for שַׁעַר · **category 1, translation choice — and this closes an open question**

Rahlfs has ἐκ τῆς φυλῆς λαοῦ αὐτοῦ ("from the tribe of his people") for מִשַּׁעַר מְקוֹמוֹ ("from the gate of his place") `[T]`. The sweep raised the same substitution at 3:11 as a possible *Vorlage* reading שֵׁבֶט ("tribe") and left it open. **Two instances of the same substitution in the same idiom, in the same book, are a translator's habit.** Classified as category 1, not a variant. *Moderate–high confidence.* See Open Questions.

#### Apparatus feature: the paragraph division

**The Westminster Leningrad Codex prints exactly one paragraph marker in the whole book of Ruth: a petuchah after 4:17** `[T]` as a datum about this manuscript, verified with a positive control against a book known to carry markers. **This is a reading tradition, not authorial**, and the manuscript spread has not been checked, so the finding is tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` and capped at *moderate*. What it shows, subject to that caveat: the scribal tradition behind Leningrad treats Ruth 1:1–4:17 as a single unbroken block and marks off only the genealogy. This passage therefore carries no scribal division at all, and the division the tradition does make falls at the point where the claim audit located the book's clearest Genesis contact, the toledot heading of 4:18.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Bethlehem in the judges' period, at the city gate — the open space inside or beside the gateway where elders sat, transactions were witnessed, and disputes were heard. The gate is the town's court, market and council in one.

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

- **That the gate was where law happened.** שַׁעַר with זָקֵן ("elder") in one verse stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible — Deuteronomy 21:19, 22:15, 25:7, Joshua 20:4, Lamentations 5:14, Proverbs 31:23 and Ruth 4:11 `[T]` — and the Deuteronomic instances are all judicial. The venue signals the genre of what is about to happen before a word is spoken.
- **That ten men constituted a quorum.** The text says עֲשָׂרָה אֲנָשִׁים מִזִּקְנֵי הָעִיר ("ten men of the elders of the city") without explaining why ten `[T]`. That later Jewish practice requires ten for a congregation is `[S]` and post-biblical; the honest statement is that the number is specified and unexplained.
- **That land was not freely alienable.** The entire transaction presupposes Leviticus 25's principle that a family's holding returns to it, which is why a kinsman can be *required* to buy and why buying it can *damage* the buyer's own inheritance.
- **How a transaction was made binding before writing was usual.** The narrator explains this one (4:7) precisely because his readers no longer did it.
- **That a man's name on his land was his survival.** וְלֹא־יִכָּרֵת שֵׁם־הַמֵּת ("that the name of the dead may not be cut off", 4:10) is not sentiment; it is the difference between a family continuing and vanishing.

**What this changes about how we read.** The nearer redeemer's refusal is not meanness. If he buys Elimelech's field and fathers a son who inherits it as Mahlon's heir, he has spent his own capital to produce an heir who is not his and who carries another man's name — and his own estate is correspondingly reduced for his existing sons. **His calculation is correct, and the passage knows it is correct.** Understanding that is what makes Boaz's acceptance legible as cost rather than as generosity in the abstract.

*Confidence.* The institutional background is well attested `[S]`, *high*. The precise mechanics of how redemption and name-raising combined are **not** well attested, because no Torah law combines them — which is the passage's own problem and the reason for 4:7.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Writings. The canonical reader arrives having read the Torah and the whole of the Prophets — so they know Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 25, they know Genesis 38, they know Rachel died at Ephrath, and they know the house of David rose and fell. They read a scene about preserving a name on a holding from the far side of losing both.

**The first hearers.**
- **Who they were:** Israelites for whom kinship, land tenure and the gate court were the ordinary furniture of life, hearing a story about a Moabite woman entering the line of their king.
- **Their situation:** holding the promises and the history together — the unresolved tension the Writings inhabit.
- **What they brought:** the Torah's redemption law as a live obligation, and a settled expectation that a Moabite stood outside the assembly.

**Where the passage fits their world.**
- They would have followed the legal manoeuvre without help — **except for the sandal**, which the narrator stops to explain. That single aside tells us precisely where the author judged his audience's competence to run out `[T]`, which is unusually direct evidence.
- They would have heard the blessing of 4:11–12 as naming their own founding women, not as decorative.
- They would have registered הַמּוֹאֲבִיָּה ("the Moabitess") at 4:5 and again at 4:10 — **the gentilic is attached to Ruth twice inside the legal declaration**, at the one moment where it would have been most awkward `[T]`.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** that the line to David is secured by acquiring a Moabite widow, and that the elders bless it by invoking Tamar.
- **Surprising:** that the man with the prior right says no, and is not condemned for it.
- **Comforting:** that the law had a mechanism, that the community turned up, and that the transaction held.
- **Disturbing:** the verb. קָנָה ("acquire") is used of the field and of the woman in the same breath, and the Hebrew does not soften it.

**What we bring that they didn't.** Romantic marriage, which turns a legal settlement into a love story. Individualism, which makes the absent women's silence invisible. A property law in which land is simply a commodity, which removes the whole force of "lest I ruin my own inheritance".

**Implication for the sermon.** What has to be recovered is the **cost**, and the way to recover it is the nearer redeemer. Preach his arithmetic sympathetically and Boaz's acceptance becomes expensive; preach him as a stock villain and the whole passage flattens.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**Redemption.** This is the OT's clearest narrative display of the גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") institution in action, and its definition is operational rather than abstract: a redeemer is the relative who is *able* and *willing* to pay what is needed to restore what a family has lost. The passage supplies both halves — a relative who is able and unwilling, and a relative who is able and willing.

**Name and seed.** The thread runs from Genesis 38 (a widow securing offspring in Judah's line) through this passage (a name raised on an inheritance) to the genealogy and to David. The passage's own purpose clause fuses the two — the name of the dead *on his inheritance* — in words found nowhere else in the canon (Headline 2).

**The inclusion of the outsider.** הַמּוֹאֲבִיָּה ("the Moabitess") is attached to Ruth at the two most formal moments of the scene (4:5, 4:10), and the community's blessing places her with Rachel, Leah and Tamar. The book does not argue the case; it transacts it in public and has the elders bless it.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: typology, through the redeemer category, with a trajectory running to David.**

Running the four tests on the Boaz-as-redeemer typology:

1. **Theological-category test — passes.** The connection runs through redemption, an established category, not through an incidental detail. The sandal, the barley and the gate are *not* the carriers.
2. **NT-precedent test — passes in kind.** The NT does not cite Ruth 4. But it makes the same kind of connection repeatedly: the redeemer must share the blood of those he redeems (Hebrews 2:14–17), and redemption is described as a purchase at a price (1 Corinthians 6:20). The kinship-plus-cost structure is exactly what Hebrews argues.
3. **Escalation test — passes.** Boaz redeems a field and a name, at the cost of part of an estate, for one family. The escalation is not a matter of degree only: Boaz pays from his surplus and remains solvent, which is the point of 4:6's contrast.
4. **Authorial-pattern test — passes at book level.** The text itself makes גֹּאֵל a keyword, repeats it twelve times in twelve verses, and transfers the title to the child at 4:14.

**Four tests passed: *high confidence* typology.**

**How the passage points to Christ.** Three specific things, each anchored in the text rather than in the category.

- **The redeemer must be a kinsman, and the nearer one's failure makes the point.** The scene turns on who is *entitled* to redeem, and establishes that entitlement without willingness redeems nobody. Hebrews 2:14–17 argues the same two requirements — sharing flesh and blood, and being merciful and faithful — and argues them in the same order.
- **The redemption is for someone absent, and it is announced to witnesses.** Ruth is discussed in the third person and never speaks; the transaction is declared twice to a court. A redemption transacted *about* its beneficiary rather than *with* her is the shape the New Testament gives to the cross.
- **The price is named, and the one who pays it is not the one who benefits.** 4:6 is the verse that makes this visible: the nearer redeemer states exactly what it would cost him, and declines.

**Trajectory.** *What the passage anticipates:* a line preserved through a redeemer's cost, ending at David (4:17, 4:22). *What Christ fulfils:* the line's destination, and the institution's logic — Matthew's genealogy names Boaz, Obed, Jesse and Ruth herself (Matthew 1:5) `[S]`, *high*. *What remains:* the land is not restored; the inheritance the NT names is not a field.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Be a Boaz" — generous, decisive, godly in business. It is not false to the text and it is not the text's argument.
- **The gospel grounding:** the passage's only divine act in the narrator's own voice is the conception of 4:13, immediately after the human transaction, and the text does not make it a reward.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** Boaz is the subject of the verbs here, but the office is what carries. The right homiletical move is from *this is what a redeemer does* to *this is what our Redeemer did*, not from *Boaz was generous* to *so be generous*.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 4:5, 4:10 — a woman acquired with a commercial verb

- **Category:** ethical, with a pastoral edge.
- **The difficulty:** קָנָה ("acquire, buy") governs the field at 4:4, 4:8, 4:9 and the woman at 4:5, 4:10 `[T]`. A modern congregation hears this immediately, and the NASB95's variation between "bought" and "acquired" slightly masks it while the ESV does not.
- **Common misreadings:** (a) explaining it away as merely formulaic; (b) making it the passage's scandal and stopping there.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The verb is the legal term for the transaction, and the transaction really is a transfer of rights in which Ruth has no stated voice. The text neither celebrates this nor criticises it; it reports it. Two things sit alongside it without cancelling it: the stated purpose is not Boaz's benefit but the dead man's name (4:5, 4:10), and the woman the transaction concerns had herself initiated the approach at 3:9. **The right pulpit move is to name the discomfort and then show what it is buying**, because the costliness on which the redeemer typology depends is the same costliness the commercial verb expresses. Smooth the verb and the typology goes with it.

#### 4:6 — was the nearer redeemer wrong?

- **Category:** interpretive, and it decides the sermon's shape.
- **The difficulty:** the text gives his reason (פֶּן־אַשְׁחִית אֶת־נַחֲלָתִי, "lest I ruin my own inheritance") and no verdict `[T]`.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The narrator passes verdicts elsewhere when he wants to (3:7; 4:13), so the silence is a choice `[I]`, *moderate–high*. He is also left unnamed, and the idiom used of him is the canon's idiom for *withheld* information (Tool 7). Those two facts pull in different directions: the silence declines to condemn, the anonymity declines to honour. **The defensible position is that the passage uses him rather than judges him.**

#### 4:1–12 and the word "levirate"

- **Category:** doctrinal and definitional; it is the overview's trap 5.
- **The difficulty:** nearly every popular treatment calls this levirate marriage.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** Headline 3, with its three measurements: the levirate vocabulary is absent from this chapter and enters the book only at 1:15, inside the argument that levirate remedy is unavailable; גָּאַל is absent from Deuteronomy 25 and occurs in Deuteronomy only at 19:6 and 19:12; the sandal verbs do not meet. **Use "redemption", and if Deuteronomy 25 is mentioned, say that it is in view and not in force.**

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: calling it levirate marriage

- **What it looks like:** "Under the levirate law, the nearest relative had to marry the widow — and Boaz was next in line."
- **Why it's wrong:** the technical vocabulary of the levirate institution never appears in this chapter, and enters the book only at 1:15, where Naomi uses it to say the remedy is unavailable; גָּאַל, which governs this scene twelve times, never occurs in Deuteronomy 25; and the two sandal idioms share no verse. The nearer redeemer is not Mahlon's brother, and the widow does not remove his sandal — he removes his own.
- **The corrective:** call it redemption. If Deuteronomy 25 comes up, say it is in view and not in force, and that the fusion of name-raising with inheritance-redemption is Ruth's own — which is exactly why the narrator has to stop and explain the procedure at 4:7.

### Pitfall: the nearer redeemer as the villain of the piece

- **What it looks like:** "Here's a man who put his own interests first — don't be like him."
- **Why it's wrong:** the narrator gives him an honest reason and passes no verdict, and this narrator passes verdicts when he wants to. His arithmetic is correct: buying the field and fathering an heir who carries another man's name really would reduce his estate for his own sons.
- **The corrective:** preach his calculation sympathetically. The cost is the point, and it only registers if the audience can feel why a reasonable man declined.

### Pitfall: skipping the court for the wedding

- **What it looks like:** treating 4:1–12 as the paperwork before the happy ending, and preaching 4:13 onwards.
- **Why it's wrong:** the court case is twelve verses and the wedding is one. The book has deliberately moved its climax into a public legal venue and made the community witness it twice.
- **The corrective:** preach the gate. The passage's own emotional architecture — flat procedure until 4:11, then benediction — is the sermon's shape if the preacher will let it be.

### Pitfall: smoothing "acquire"

- **What it looks like:** "Boaz took Ruth as his wife" — the verb quietly replaced.
- **Why it's wrong:** the Hebrew uses the same commercial verb of the field and of the woman, six times in this passage and nowhere else in the book.
- **The corrective:** name the discomfort, then show what it buys. The redeemer typology depends on the costliness the verb expresses; soften the verb and the typology softens with it.

### Pitfall: preaching a pattern that has not been measured

- **What it looks like:** building a point on the "sit"/"returned" sound-play, or on the Deuteronomy 19 register, as though either were established.
- **Why it's wrong:** both are flagged in this report as unbaselined. This book's claim audit tested eight pattern claims and all eight failed.
- **The corrective:** use the measured single contacts — they are the findings that held. The patterns can wait for an audit.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **4:5 — ketiv or qere?** "I have acquired" against "you have acquired", with the imperative as a third possibility proposed by BHS. The morphological agreement with 4:9–10 is new evidence for the ketiv's seriousness; the versions and both English texts follow the qere. **A commentary that works the Hebrew would be worth consulting**, and this is the one textual question in the passage that changes a motive.
2. **4:3 — is Naomi selling, or has she sold?** The perfect מָכְרָה against both versions' future-leaning renderings, and against the Greek's "has been given to Naomi". A grammar or a technical commentary would settle the range of the perfect here.
3. **4:4 — the second/third person switch.** Scribal slip, legal formality, or preserved formula? *Uncertain.* Nothing doctrinal turns on it.
4. **Why ten elders?** The number is specified and unexplained. Later Jewish practice is `[S]` and post-biblical; is there earlier evidence?
5. **4:7 — how late is the piel of קוּם for "ratify"?** Flagged to a lexicon. If the administrative flavour is real it bears on the narrator's distance from his story.
6. **Deuteronomy 19 as a register — does the rank survive a null?** Deuteronomy 19 ranks second of 859 chapters against Ruth 4 and the finding is reported at *moderate* precisely because no null has been run. **This is the first thing a claim audit should test on this passage.**
7. **The "sit"/"returned" paronomasia — designed or incidental?** Queued as a design claim, capped at *moderate*, unbaselined.
