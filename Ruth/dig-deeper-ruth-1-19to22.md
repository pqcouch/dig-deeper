## Headline Findings

1. **This is the book's Job passage, and the measurement is exact.** `[T]` שַׁדַּי ("the Almighty") occurs twice in Ruth, both here, and nowhere else in the book; across the canon it stands in 48 verses, **31 of them in Job**. More sharply: the hiphil of מָרַר ("make bitter") with שַׁדַּי as subject stands in **exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible — Job 27:2 and Ruth 1:20**. Job: וְשַׁדַּי הֵמַר נַפְשִׁי ("and the Almighty has made my soul bitter"). Naomi: כִּי־הֵמַר שַׁדַּי לִי מְאֹד ("for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me"). *High confidence.* **Naomi indicts God in Job's words, in the one book of the canon that stands beside Job in the Writings.**

2. **Three independent lines now converge on the crux at 1:21: עָנָה בִי means "testified against me".** `[T]` for the first two, `[S: Logos]` for the third. (i) **Morphology:** the Westminster Leningrad Codex files 1:21's עָנָה under the same dictionary entry as the plain "answered" of 2:6 and 2:11 — the answering root, not the homonym meaning "afflict". (ii) **Canonical company:** שַׁדַּי with עָנָה in one verse stands in three verses — Job 31:35, Job 40:2 and Ruth 1:21 — and **both Job verses are explicitly forensic**, Job demanding that Shaddai answer his written indictment, and Shaddai demanding that his accuser answer him. (iii) **Grammar:** the qal עָנָה with the preposition בְּ is the legal idiom "testify against", attested at Exodus 20:16, Numbers 35:30 and 1 Samuel 12:3, while the "afflict" reading needs a piel that never takes its object with בְּ. *High confidence.* **Naomi is not saying God hurt her; she is saying God took the witness stand against her** — and both English versions say so.

3. **Naomi asks to be renamed and the book refuses, starting in the next verse.** `[T]` She says אַל־תִּקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי קְרֶאןָ לִי מָרָא ("do not call me Naomi; call me Mara"), and the very next word of narration is וַתָּשָׁב נָעֳמִי ("so Naomi returned"). **מָרָא occurs once in the Hebrew Bible, here; נָעֳמִי stands in 20 verses of Ruth — 21 occurrences — of which 15 verses fall after this demand**, and no one in the book ever calls her Mara. *High confidence.* This is a pattern-break absence of the admissible kind: the pattern is her own instruction, citable at 1:20, and the narrative contradicts it immediately and permanently.

4. **"Full" and "empty" meet in one verse, and in only one verse in the canon.** `[T]` מָלֵא ("full") with רֵיקָם ("empty") in one verse stands in **Ruth 1:21 alone**. And רֵיקָם's canonical register is the Torah's promise and law about *not* being sent away empty — Exodus 3:21 (Israel shall not go out empty), Deuteronomy 15:13 (do not send your freed slave away empty), Genesis 31:42 (Laban would have sent Jacob away empty). *High confidence* on the count, *moderate–high* on the register. **Naomi uses the word the Torah uses for what God does not do**, and the book answers her at 3:17, where Boaz sends Ruth home with the instruction אַל־תָּבוֹאִי רֵיקָם ("do not go empty").

5. **The narrator's closing sentence quietly contradicts everything Naomi has just said.** `[T]` In one verse (1:22) he calls her **Naomi**, names Ruth as present and as her daughter-in-law, and dates the arrival בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים ("at the beginning of barley harvest"). *High confidence.* She has said she came back empty; the sentence reporting it names the person she came back with and the season that will feed them. **The correction is made by arrangement, not by comment** — which is this narrator's habit throughout.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 1:6–18 has done two things this passage requires. It has reduced two daughters-in-law to one, so that a Moabite is walking into Bethlehem beside Naomi. And it has put a theological verdict in Naomi's mouth privately — כִּי־יָצְאָה בִי יַד־יְהוָה ("for the hand of the LORD has gone out against me", 1:13) — spoken to two foreign women on a road, where no one in Israel could hear it.

**Necessity answer.** The passage exists **to make the private verdict public, and to put it on the record before the book answers it.** Three things follow from its position. Naomi's complaint has to be said in Bethlehem, in front of the town, because the book's reversal will be announced by the same women in the same place at 4:14–17 — the complaint and its answer need the same audience. It has to be said *before* the harvest begins, because once Ruth goes to the field the answer is already under way. And it has to be said *after* 1:18, because Naomi's silence there ("she said no more to her") is what makes this her first speech since losing the argument.

**Why not elsewhere.** The thesis of emptiness could not be stated in chapter 1's opening, where the narrator reports the deaths without comment and passes no verdict; it needs a character to say it. And it could not be delayed, because 2:2 begins the remedy.

**Implication.** The passage is **the book's problem stated by the person who has it**, in public, with God named as the agent. Everything from 2:1 to 4:17 is the answer, and the answer is given to the same hearers — which is why 4:14's "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a redeemer" lands as it does.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 adds a frame. The only divine act the narrator has reported in his own voice so far is the giving of bread at 1:6; the next will be the giving of conception at 4:13. **Naomi's indictment stands exactly between the two things God is actually said to have done**, and she knows about neither of them.

## Tool 1 — Author's Purpose

The narrator's purpose in this unit is visible in what he lets stand and what he quietly corrects.

He gives Naomi the longest uninterrupted speech she has had, and he does not evaluate it. `[T]` There is no "but she spoke rashly", no "yet the LORD had not forsaken her". The verdict that God has made her bitter, emptied her, testified against her and done her harm is left on the page in her own voice. **This is the narrator's habit with speech, and it is not neutrality: it is the deferral of judgement to the arrangement of events.**

Then, in 1:22, he writes one sentence of his own. `[T]` It calls her by the name she has just repudiated, reports the presence of the person she has just omitted from her accounting, and dates the arrival to the season of grain. **Every one of the three facts bears on her complaint, and none of them is offered as an answer to it.**

So the purpose of the unit is twofold. It states the book's problem in its strongest form — strong enough that the Christian preacher is tempted to soften it `[I]` — and it plants the three data points that will dismantle it. The dismantling is left to chapters 2 to 4.

**Book-overview note.** The overview's microscript has the book moving from emptiness to fullness. This unit is where **"empty" is actually said** — and said by Naomi, not by the narrator. That distinction matters for the microscript's wording and is logged for the Finalise pass.

## Tool 2 — Context

**Immediately before (1:6–18).** Three women on a road; two speeches of dismissal; one of refusal. Naomi's theology is stated twice there (1:13, 1:20–21) but the first time only to Moabites. `[T]`

**Immediately after (2:1–3).** The narrator introduces Boaz with a disclosure the characters do not have — וּלְנָעֳמִי מוֹדָע לְאִישָׁהּ ("now Naomi had a kinsman of her husband's") — and Ruth goes out to glean. `[T]` **The relative is announced one verse after Naomi has declared herself empty.** The juxtaposition is the narrator's comment, made by sequence.

**The book's frame.** 1:1–5 took them out of Bethlehem in famine; 1:19–22 brings them back in harvest. `[T]` The frame is closed by vocabulary: בֵּית לֶחֶם ("Bethlehem", literally "house of bread") stands in six verses of Ruth — 1:1, 1:2, 1:19 (twice), 1:22, 2:4, 4:11, seven occurrences in all — so **five of the book's seven fall in chapter 1**, and the name means what the chapter is about.

**Wider canonical context.** The judges' period, which the book's opening dates by (1:1), and the Torah's gleaning provision, which 2:2 will assume.

## Tool 3 — Structure

The unit is shaped as a public hearing: arrival, question, answer, record.

| Verse | Element | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| 19a | The journey completed | narrator |
| 19b | The town's reaction and its question | the women of Bethlehem |
| 20 | Answer, part 1: the renaming demand, grounded | Naomi |
| 21 | Answer, part 2: the indictment, in two couplets | Naomi |
| 22 | The record of what happened | narrator |

**The speech itself is chiastically tight.** `[T]` In 1:20–21 the arrangement is:

- A — Do not call me Naomi
- B — call me Mara
- C — **for Shaddai has made me very bitter**
- C′ — I went out full; empty the LORD has brought me back
- B′ — Why call me Naomi
- A′ — **when the LORD has testified against me and Shaddai has done me harm**

The divine names stand at both ends of the centre and at the close: שַׁדַּי · יְהוָה · יְהוָה · שַׁדַּי. `[T]` **Four divine names in two verses, in a mirrored order** — and both of the book's only two occurrences of שַׁדַּי are the outer pair. **Confidence: moderate** on the chiasm as *design*; **high** on the name-order itself, which is simply on the page. Per the overview's new trap 7, the design claim is a proposal until a chance baseline is run; the name sequence is not a design claim but an observation.

## Tool 4 — Linking Words

Three connectives do real work, and one is missing where the English supplies it.

**כִּי at 1:20** — כִּי־הֵמַר שַׁדַּי לִי מְאֹד ("*for* the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me"). `[T]` This is causal and it is the ground of the renaming demand. Naomi is not expressing a mood; she is giving a reason, and the reason is a theological claim about an agent.

**לָמָּה at 1:21** — לָמָּה תִקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי ("*why* do you call me Naomi?"). `[T]` A question to her neighbours, not to God. **Naomi never asks God why.** She tells the town why they are wrong to use her name. That is a notable restraint — or a notable displacement — and the text does not say which. `[I]`

**The waw at 1:21b** — וַיהוָה עָנָה בִי ("*and* the LORD has testified against me"). `[T]` NASB95 and ESV both render it causally ("since the LORD has witnessed against me"). The Hebrew is a plain waw, and the causal force is a reading of the clause's relation to the question, not a word in the text. **The versions are interpreting, correctly in my judgement, but a preacher should know the "since" is not written.** `[I]`

**What is absent: no adversative in 1:22.** `[T]` The narrator does not write "but Naomi returned". He writes וַתָּשָׁב ("so/and Naomi returned") and lets the waw do nothing more than continue. The contradiction of her speech is left entirely to the content. **A preacher who reads 1:22 as the narrator's rebuttal is right about the effect and must not claim a connective for it.**

## Tool 5 — Parallels

The speech is poetic in texture even though the book is prose. `[T]`

**1:21a is antithetic parallelism of the tightest kind:**

> אֲנִי מְלֵאָה הָלַכְתִּי ("*I* went out full")
> וְרֵיקָם הֱשִׁיבַנִי יְהוָה ("and empty the LORD has brought me back")

Four contrasts in two lines: **full/empty; went/brought back; I as subject / the LORD as subject; and the emphatic אֲנִי against the named יְהוָה.** `[T]` The first line has Naomi acting; the second has her acted upon. **The grammar performs the complaint**: she went, she was returned.

**1:21b is synonymous parallelism with intensification:**

> וַיהוָה עָנָה בִי ("and the LORD has testified against me")
> וְשַׁדַּי הֵרַע לִי ("and the Almighty has done me harm")

A legal verb paired with a verb of harm, the two divine names distributed one to each line. `[T]` **The movement is from courtroom to consequence** — he testified, and so it went badly. *Confidence: high* on the parallelism; *moderate–high* on the forensic reading of עָנָה בִי (see Tool 7).

**1:20's demand is also parallel, and the parallelism is where the pun lives:**

> אַל־תִּקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי ("do not call me Naomi" — *pleasant*)
> קְרֶאןָ לִי מָרָא ("call me Mara" — *bitter*)

## Tool 6 — Narrator's Comment

The narrator comments three times in four verses, and never by evaluation.

**(i) 1:19b — וַתֵּהֹם כָּל־הָעִיר** ("all the city was stirred"). `[T]` The verb is הוּם, and it is **rare: 5 verses in the whole Hebrew Bible** (WLC lemma index, each reference verified) — 1 Samuel 4:5, 1 Kings 1:45, Micah 2:12, Psalm 55:3, Ruth 1:19. **Two of the four non-Ruth uses are a city's uproar at a decisive public event**: the ark arriving in the camp (1 Samuel 4:5) and Solomon's anointing (1 Kings 1:45). The other two are a noisy multitude (Micah 2:12) and personal agitation (Psalm 55:3). `[T]` *Confidence: moderate–high* on the register; a five-verse word with two clear parallels is a narrow enough base to describe, not narrow enough to call unique.

**Its force here:** a returning widow is reported with the verb used for an ark and a coronation. **The town's reaction is out of proportion to the event unless the event matters more than it looks.** `[I]`

**(ii) The question, left unanswered as asked.** הֲזֹאת נָעֳמִי ("Is this Naomi?") `[T]` is grammatically a question about identity, and it is the question the whole book answers — at 4:17, when the women speak again and name the child. The narrator reports the question and does not say what prompted it. `[T]` **An appearance so changed as to be doubted is the natural inference, and it is an inference.** `[I]`

**(iii) 1:22 — the record.** Discussed under Headline 5 and Tool 1.

## Tool 7 — Vocabulary

This is the unit's heaviest tool, and the lexical work is where its findings come from. All counts are on the WLC lemma index unless stated, verified at each reference; no count rests on a surface form.

**שַׁדַּי ("the Almighty") — 48 verses.** `[T]` Distribution: **Job 31; Genesis 6; Ezekiel 2; Numbers 2; Psalms 2; Ruth 2** (1:20, 1:21), with the remaining three singly in Exodus, Isaiah and Joel. **Two-thirds of the canon's occurrences are in Job.** Ruth's two are the book's only two.

**The name's company in Ruth 1:21 is itself rare.** `[T]` שַׁדַּי standing with יְהוָה in one verse occurs in **5 verses**: Genesis 17:1, Exodus 6:3, Isaiah 13:6, Joel 1:15, **Ruth 1:21**. Genesis and Exodus are the covenant-disclosure texts ("I am El Shaddai"; "I appeared… as El Shaddai, but by my name YHWH I was not known"); Isaiah and Joel are both the כְּשֹׁד מִשַּׁדַּי ("as destruction from the Almighty") day-of-the-LORD formula. **Naomi's pairing belongs to neither group**, and the rarity is worth stating without a claim of allusion. *Confidence: high* on the count; **no allusion claimed** — the shared vocabulary is two divine names, which is a set too common in sense to carry dependence.

**מָרַר / מָרָא ("bitter") — the pun and its reach.** `[T]` The adjectival name מָרָא at 1:20 is a **hapax legomenon**: it occurs in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, here. The verb מָרַר occurs in Ruth twice — 1:13 (הֵמַר לִי, "it is bitter for me") and 1:20 (הֵמַר שַׁדַּי לִי) — and **the two Ruth occurrences are Naomi's two statements of her case, the private one and the public one.** *Confidence: high.*

**The Job 27:2 contact — the unit's sharpest single measurement.** `[T]` מָרַר and שַׁדַּי stand together in one verse in **exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible**:

| Reference | Hebrew | Gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Job 27:2 | וְשַׁדַּי הֵמַר נַפְשִׁי | "and the Almighty has made my soul bitter" |
| Ruth 1:20 | כִּי־הֵמַר שַׁדַּי לִי מְאֹד | "for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" |

**Same verb, same hiphil stem, same divine name as subject, same complaint.** The difference is the object: Job's נֶפֶשׁ ("soul"), Naomi's bare לִי ("to me"). `[T]` *Confidence on the count: high.* **On allusion: moderate** — and deliberately capped there. The shared lemma set is two words, both of them available to anyone making this complaint, and the direction of dependence is unestablished. What the measurement licenses is a claim about **register**, not about citation: *when a character in the Hebrew Bible says that Shaddai has embittered them, there is one other place in the canon where that sentence is said, and it is Job.* `[I]` No rival was found: the search returned two verses and no third.

**עָנָה at 1:21 — the crux, and its three converging answers.** The question is whether עָנָה בִי means "afflicted me" (so KJV: "the Almighty hath afflicted me", and some older commentators) or "testified against me" (NASB95, ESV).

*(i) Morphology.* `[T]` The WLC files all three of Ruth's occurrences of this consonantal root under the **same dictionary entry** — 1:21, 2:6 and 2:11 — and at 2:6 and 2:11 the sense is unambiguous: וַיַּעַן הַנַּעַר ("the servant answered"), וַתַּעַן רוּת ("Ruth answered"). **The index does not treat 1:21 as the homonym meaning "afflict".** This is the strongest of the three lines, because it is a judgement made by the lexicographers of the edition the whole run is built on, visible in the data rather than argued.

*(ii) Canonical company.* `[T]` שַׁדַּי with עָנָה in one verse occurs in **3 verses**: Job 31:35, Job 40:2, Ruth 1:21. And both Job verses are lawsuit language with no room for doubt:

- **Job 31:35** — הֶן־תָּוִי שַׁדַּי יַעֲנֵנִי וְסֵפֶר כָּתַב אִישׁ רִיבִי ("behold, here is my signature; let the Almighty answer me, and the indictment written by my adversary"). Signature, answer, written charge, adversary-at-law.
- **Job 40:2** — הֲרֹב עִם־שַׁדַּי יִסּוֹר מוֹכִיחַ אֱלוֹהַּ יַעֲנֶנָּה ("shall a faultfinder contend with the Almighty? He who argues with God, let him answer it"). Contend, argue, answer.

**All three verses in the canon that pair this divine name with this verb are courtroom scenes.** *Confidence: high* on the count and on the Job readings.

*(iii) Grammar.* `[S: Logos]` The qal of עָנָה with בְּ is the standing legal idiom for testifying against, at Exodus 20:16, Numbers 35:30 and 1 Samuel 12:3; the "afflict" sense requires a piel which does not construct with בְּ. This line came from the Logos round and is held as secondary; the morphology and the canonical company were reached from the corpus first.

**Verdict: "the LORD has testified against me."** `[T]` + `[S: Logos]`, *high confidence*. **Naomi's charge is not that God hurt her but that God gave evidence against her** — she reads her losses as a public verdict on her life, delivered by the one witness no one can cross-examine. That is a harder and sadder thing to say than "God afflicted me", and it is what the pulpit text says.

**מְלֵאָה / רֵיקָם ("full" / "empty").** `[T]` The two stand together in one verse in **Ruth 1:21 alone** — the only verse in the Hebrew Bible to hold both. רֵיקָם itself occurs in **16 verses**, and its Torah uses are the ones that matter:

- **Genesis 31:42** — Jacob to Laban: "you would have sent me away empty"
- **Exodus 3:21** — "you will not go out empty"
- **Deuteronomy 15:13** — "you shall not send him away empty"
- **Deuteronomy 16:16** — "none shall appear before the LORD empty"

**Three of the four are about what one must not do to a dependent person, and one is about what God promised not to do to Israel.** `[T]` *Confidence: moderate–high* on the register — sixteen verses is above the threshold at which wording alone can carry a dependence claim, so **no allusion is claimed**; what is claimed is that Naomi has used a word with a known legal and covenantal home. `[I]`

**And the book answers it with the same word.** `[T]` רֵיקָם occurs twice in Ruth: 1:21 and **3:17**, where Ruth reports Boaz's words — אַל־תָּבוֹאִי רֵיקָם אֶל־חֲמוֹתֵךְ ("do not go empty-handed to your mother-in-law"). **Boaz uses, unknowingly, the exact word of Naomi's indictment, and reverses it.** This is a Move 4 internal echo and it is as clean as they come. *Confidence: high* on the verbal link; *moderate* on authorial design, pending a baseline.

**הַמּוֹאֲבִיָּה ("the Moabitess") at 1:22.** `[T]` The lemma stands in **7 verses** of Ruth (1:4, 1:22, 2:2, 2:6, 2:21, 4:5, 4:10), and in **5 of them it is attached to Ruth's name** — 1:22, 2:2, 2:21, 4:5, 4:10 — with 1:4 of the two wives collectively and 2:6 of "the Moabite young woman" unnamed. **The narrator does not let the reader forget it, at the moment of arrival and at the moment of acquisition.** *Confidence: high* on both counts, each reference verified.

**תְּחִלָּה · קָצִיר · שְׂעֹרָה ("beginning" · "harvest" · "barley") at 1:22.** `[T]` The three lemmas stand together in **2 verses**: 2 Samuel 21:9 and Ruth 1:22. **But the wording is not the same**, and the gate requires this to be said: the ordered phrase בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים returns Ruth 1:22 only, plain and skeletal; Samuel lacks the preposition בְּ, and **its word for "beginning" is unpointed in the WLC — a ketiv** — so a shape claim is unavailable. 2 Samuel 21:9 is the execution of Saul's sons, dated to the same season. *Confidence: moderate* on the lemma contact as an observation; **low** on any allusion, and none is claimed. **Audit status:** this contact is auditor-1 row **R17**, which is among the nine rows not yet transcribed into the audit file; it is therefore carried here as untested beyond my own count. `[S: audit — status pending]`

**A density check, reported with its modesty.** `[T]` A rare-pair scan of Ruth 1 against all 859 chapters of the Hebrew Bible (WLC, rare-pair threshold 10 verses) ranks **Job 2 thirtieth**. **Thirtieth of 859 is a presence, not a pattern**, and the Job case in this report rests on the two- and three-verse lemma-set measurements above, not on this rank. Reported so that a later reader does not mistake the rank for the evidence.

## Tool 8 — Translations

| | NASB95 | ESV | KJV |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1:20 name** | "call me Mara" | "call me Mara" | "call me Mara" |
| **1:20 verb** | "dealt very bitterly with me" | "dealt very bitterly with me" | "dealt very bitterly with me" |
| **1:21 עָנָה בִי** | "the LORD has witnessed against me" | "the LORD has testified against me" | "the LORD hath testified against me" |
| **1:21 הֵרַע** | "the Almighty has afflicted me" | "the Almighty has brought calamity upon me" | "the Almighty hath afflicted me" |
| **1:19 הוּם** | "all the city was stirred" | "the whole town was stirred" | "all the city was moved" |

**Three observations for the preacher.**

**(i) The crux is not a translation dispute.** All three versions, including the KJV, read עָנָה בִי forensically. `[T]` The "afflicted" reading of 1:21a is found in commentators, not in these versions; the KJV's "afflicted" is its rendering of **הֵרַע** in the *second* half of the verse, which is a different word. **A preacher who says "some translations have 'afflicted me' here" will be wrong about these three.** The pulpit text and the study text agree.

**(ii) ESV and NASB95 diverge on הֵרַע, and the gap is worth a sentence.** ESV's "brought calamity upon me" is broader and more impersonal; NASB95's and KJV's "afflicted me" is narrower and more direct. The Hebrew is the hiphil of רָעַע, "to do evil/harm to", and **the agent is named**. `[T]` **What the congregation will hear from the ESV is a little more cushioned than what Naomi said.** That is a note to the preacher, not a translation verdict.

**(iii) No English version keeps the pun — but the Greek does.** `[T]` נָעֳמִי / מָרָא is pleasant/bitter, and every English version transliterates both names, so the congregation hears two foreign words; all three footnote the sense rather than render it. **The pun has to be told them or it is lost.** The Greek translator made the opposite choice: at 1:20 Rahlfs has καλέσατέ με Πικράν — "call me *Bitter*", the name **translated**. `[T]` And שַׁדַּי is rendered ὁ ἱκανός, "the Sufficient One", at both 1:20 and 1:21 — neither transliterated nor given the usual παντοκράτωρ. **A reader of the Greek hears the wordplay and loses the divine name; a reader of the English keeps the name and loses the wordplay.**

## Tool 9 — Tone and Feel

The tone shifts three times in four verses, and the shifts are the point.

**1:19a is flat and fast.** `[T]` Two verbs and a destination: they went until they came to Bethlehem. The road that took five verses at the chapter's start is crossed in half a verse. **The narrator is not interested in the journey.**

**1:19b is a crowd scene.** The whole town is in uproar and the question is three words long: הֲזֹאת נָעֳמִי. `[T]` Short, startled, slightly cruel in its directness — and the crowd gets the shortest line in the unit.

**1:20–21 is the loudest thing in the book.** Four divine names, a demand, a reason, an antithesis, a rhetorical question, and two accusations, in two verses. `[T]` After a chapter in which Naomi's longest speech was an argument for her daughters-in-law to leave, this is a woman who has arrived home and found that her name has become a lie.

**1:22 is deliberately quiet.** `[T]` One sentence, three facts, no adjective, no comment. **The tonal drop from 1:21 to 1:22 is the largest in the chapter**, and it is how the narrator declines to argue with her.

**Where the pastoral weight sits.** This is a woman saying, in public, in front of the neighbours she left behind, that God has given evidence against her. `[I]` It is not rebuked. **A preacher who needs it rebuked before the sermon can proceed has a problem with the book, not with Naomi.**

## Tool 10 — Repetition

**Names.** נָעֳמִי ("Naomi") occurs **four times in these four verses** — 1:19, 1:20, 1:21, 1:22 `[T]` — out of **21 occurrences across 20 verses of the book**. **Nearly a fifth of the book's uses of her name are in the unit where she asks for it to stop being used.** *Confidence: high.*

**בֵּית לֶחֶם ("Bethlehem").** Twice in 1:19 and once in 1:22. `[T]` The doubling in a single verse — "until they came to Bethlehem… and when they had come to Bethlehem" — is **resumptive repetition**, the narrator's way of stopping the clock at the threshold before the scene begins. *Confidence: high* on the device.

**קָרָא ("call") in 1:20–21.** Three times: אַל־תִּקְרֶאנָה ("do not call"), קְרֶאןָ ("call"), תִקְרֶאנָה ("do you call"). `[T]` All three are second-person feminine plural, addressed to the women. **The repeated verb is the imperative mood of a woman trying to control how she is spoken of.**

**The divine names.** Four in two verses, mirrored (Tool 3). `[T]` **There is no other two-verse stretch in Ruth with four divine names.** This is a measured statement, not an impression: every divine name in the book was counted by lemma against the WLC index — יְהוָה in 15 verses (18 occurrences), אֱלֹהִים in 3 verses (4 occurrences), שַׁדַּי in 2 — and every pair of consecutive verses was then totalled. 1:20–21 reaches four (one Shaddai, then two YHWH and one Shaddai); **the highest any other pair reaches is three.**

**שׁוּב ("return").** וַתָּשָׁב… הַשָּׁבָה in 1:22 — the finite verb and the participle in one verse `[T]` — which closes the chapter's own heaviest repetition. The root stands in **13 verses of Ruth, 15 occurrences** — 1:6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 15 (twice), 16, 21, 22 (twice), then 2:6, 4:3, 4:15 — so **eleven of the thirteen verses are in chapter 1**. **The chapter is built on it, and the last two uses land on the two women together.**

## Tool 11 — Quotation and Allusion

No quotation. Three contacts of differing strength, and the Triad is run on the strongest.

### The Job register (1:20–21) — the unit's principal intertextual finding

**Move 1 — source context.** Job 27:2 opens Job's final speech in the dialogue cycles, and it is an **oath formula**: חַי־אֵל הֵסִיר מִשְׁפָּטִי וְשַׁדַּי הֵמַר נַפְשִׁי ("as God lives, who has taken away my right, and the Almighty, who has made my soul bitter"). `[T]` **The embittering is sworn to, in the same breath as a complaint that justice has been denied** — מִשְׁפָּט, a legal term. Job 31:35 and 40:2 are, as shown, explicit lawsuit speech. **The Shaddai of these texts is a defendant who will not appear, or a judge whose verdict is unbearable.**

**Move 2 — book usage.** How does Ruth use Shaddai language elsewhere? **It does not.** `[T]` These two verses are the book's entire use of the name, and after 1:21 the book's divine name is יְהוָה without exception — in Naomi's blessings (2:20, 3:10 via Boaz), in the harvest greeting (2:4), in Boaz's prayer (2:12), in the oath (3:13), and in the women's final blessing (4:14). **Shaddai is the name Naomi uses for the God who has ruined her, and she never uses it again.** *Confidence: high* on the distribution; *moderate* on the significance, which is an inference from an absence that has been positively controlled (the name was searched across the whole book and returns only these two verses).

**Move 3 — OT-to-OT.** Who else in the canon addresses Shaddai in complaint? **Job, and essentially only Job.** `[T]` Of the 48 verses holding the name, 31 are in Job, and the complaint-and-lawsuit uses are concentrated there. Genesis and Exodus use it for self-disclosure in covenant promise; Isaiah and Joel for the day of destruction; Numbers for Balaam's visions. **The complaint register is Job's.**

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Three, and they are the unit's best yield.

- **רֵיקָם at 1:21 → 3:17.** Naomi's "empty" is reversed by Boaz's "do not go empty". `[T]` *High* on the verbal link.
- **הֲזֹאת נָעֳמִי at 1:19 → 4:17.** The women of Bethlehem ask who this is; the women of Bethlehem answer, at the book's end, by naming a child — יֻלַּד־בֵּן לְנָעֳמִי ("a son has been born to Naomi"). `[T]` **The same speakers, the same name, the opposite sentence.** *Confidence: moderate–high*; the identification of the speaking group as the same in both places is `[I]`, since the text says הַנָּשִׁים ("the women") at 4:14 and "they said" at 1:19.
- **The levirate word at 1:15 → chapter 4.** As corrected in the 4:1–12 report `[S: audit]`, יְבָמָה ("sister-in-law") enters Ruth at 1:15 only, inside Naomi's argument. **This unit stands four verses later and is where Naomi states the conclusion that followed from it: the remedy is impossible and the case is closed.** `[I]`

**Verdict on the Job contact.** **Confirmed as register, at moderate confidence; not claimed as citation.** The measurements (two verses for מָרַר + שַׁדַּי; three for שַׁדַּי + עָנָה, both rivals in Job) are high-confidence and were made with rivals searched. What they will not bear is a claim that the author of Ruth had Job 27 open. **What they bear is this: Naomi's complaint is phrased in the canon's one established idiom for complaining about Shaddai, and that idiom belongs to Job.** The contact is in the Hebrew and only in the Hebrew — see the Textual Variants extension, where the Greek is shown to lose it. The design-claim cap applies: no baseline has been run for the Job-register claim as a *pattern*, so it is logged as a proposal and named below as an audit target.

### The "empty" register (1:21) — moderate, Move 1 only

Per the Triad's rule, a moderate-confidence allusion gets Move 1 only. **Move 1:** Exodus 3:21 and Deuteronomy 15:13 both use רֵיקָם of release from servitude with provision; Genesis 31:42 of a wage withheld. `[T]` All three concern **what is owed to a dependent on departure**. Naomi, who left with a husband and two sons and returned with neither, says the word about herself. *Confidence: moderate*, by the >10-verse idiom cap (16 verses). No allusion claimed.

### The barley-harvest notice (1:22) — low, no Triad

Two-verse lemma contact with 2 Samuel 21:9, wording not identical, Samuel's key word a ketiv, audit row untranscribed. `[T]` **Recorded, not claimed.**

## Tool 12 — Genre

Hebrew narrative prose with an embedded poetic speech. `[T]`

**What the genre licenses.** The narrator's refusal to evaluate Naomi's speech is **a convention of the genre, not an endorsement of the speech.** `[I]` Hebrew narrative routinely reports theologically defective speech without comment and corrects it by events — the book of Job's friends are the limiting case, where thirty-five chapters of unrebuked speech are overturned in one verse of divine comment. **A preacher must not treat 1:20–21 as the narrator's theology, and must not treat the absence of rebuke as the narrator's silence on the matter.** The rebuttal is 2:1 to 4:22.

**What the embedded poetry licenses.** 1:21's parallelism is tight enough to read as verse, and verse in Hebrew narrative marks **the emotional and theological peak of a scene** `[I]` — Jacob's blessing, Hannah's song, Lamech's boast. **The form tells the reader this is the scene's centre.**

**What the genre forbids.** An allegorical reading of the names. Naomi and Mara are a pun and a character's self-description, not a psychomachia. `[T]`

## Tool 13 — Copycat

**Do not copy Naomi.** Her speech contains a true report of her circumstances and a verdict on God that the book goes on to falsify. `[I]` She is not a model of lament to be imitated, and she is not a cautionary tale to be warned against. **She is a believer talking honestly while wrong about the most important thing.**

**What is imitable here, if anything, is the town** — which asks the question, hears the answer, and does not argue. `[I]` *Confidence: low*; the text says nothing about their response, and this is inference from silence.

**The copycat trap specific to this passage:** turning "call me Mara" into a licence for a theology of permanent self-description. `[I]` Naomi's renaming is **refused by the narrative within one verse.** A sermon that leaves the congregation naming themselves by their losses has stopped reading at verse 21.

## Tool 14 — Bible Timeline

**Where this sits.** Judges period (by 1:1's dating), pre-monarchy, before David. `[T]` The famine is over; the harvest has begun.

**What has happened in redemptive history.** Abrahamic promise given, Exodus accomplished, land occupied, no king. The Torah's provisions for the poor and for the widow are in force and will be invoked at 2:2. `[T]`

**What has not happened.** No king, no temple, no prophet in view. `[T]` **And no answer yet to the question of how a Moabite enters Israel** — which is this unit's live problem, since 1:22 puts her inside Bethlehem.

**Timeline significance of 1:22's date.** The barley harvest is the start of the grain season and coincides with Passover and the firstfruits offering (Leviticus 23:10–11). `[S]` **The narrator dates their arrival to the season of firstfruits**; whether he intends the liturgical resonance the text does not say, and I will not claim it. *Confidence: low* on intent, *high* on the calendrical fact.

## Tool 15 — Who Am I?

Not Naomi, primarily. `[I]`

**The reader's position in this unit is the town's.** `[I]` The reader has watched chapter 1 and knows what the town does not: the deaths, the road, the refusal, the Moabite's vow. The town asks "Is this Naomi?" and the reader already knows the answer is yes and no. **The reader is being invited to hear a complaint they have more information about than the complainant does** — because the reader has read 1:6, where the LORD gave bread.

**Where the reader is *not* positioned:** as the judge of Naomi's theology. `[I]` The text gives the reader no verdict to deliver, and withholding one is part of the reading experience it constructs.

**For the believing reader on this side of the cross:** the position is sharper still. The reader knows the name of the child the women will name, and the name of his descendant. `[I]` **Naomi's "the LORD has testified against me" is said four chapters before the book's genealogy and a millennium before the testimony is reversed.**

## Tool 16 — So What?

Three things the passage does, which a sermon might carry.

**(i) It licenses the complaint without endorsing the verdict.** The Bible lets a believer say that God has given evidence against her, and does not strike it from the record. `[I]` **A congregation needs to know that this sentence is in Scripture.**

**(ii) It shows how God answers such a complaint — by arrangement, not argument.** `[I]` No voice from heaven, no rebuke, no explanation. A kinsman one verse later, a harvest in the last clause, a Moabite standing beside her who she has not counted. **The answer to Naomi was already in the room while she was saying there was none.**

**(iii) It refuses the renaming.** `[T]` She asks to be called Bitter, and the book calls her Naomi twenty times. **Self-description is not the last word on a believer's identity, and the correction here is made by the narrator, not by a friend's reassurance.**

## Extension — Original Languages

Covered under Tool 7 for lexis. Three grammatical points remain.

**הֱשִׁיבַנִי (1:21) — hiphil with a suffix.** `[T]` "He caused me to return." The same root שׁוּב that the chapter has used eleven times of the women's own going back is here **causative, with Naomi as the object.** She has spent the chapter telling others to return and declaring her own return; in this clause she is returned by someone else. **The stem carries the theology of the complaint.**

**אֲנִי (1:21) — the independent pronoun.** `[T]` Hebrew does not need it; the verb הָלַכְתִּי already marks first person. Its presence is emphatic and it stands first: *I* went out full. **The pronoun sets up the contrast with the named יְהוָה in the next line.**

**קְרֶאןָ (1:20) — an unusual imperative form.** `[T]` The second-person feminine plural imperative of קָרָא, written with a final qamets-aleph in BHS. BHS notes the form; it is a morphological rarity rather than a textual problem, and nothing in the passage's sense turns on it. *Confidence: high* that it does not affect meaning.

## Extension — Textual Variants

**BHS apparatus on 1:19–22: nothing that affects preaching.** `[T]` The apparatus for these verses notes the form at 1:20 (above) and minor orthographic matters. **No variant bears on the crux at 1:21, on the Job contact, or on the harvest notice.**

**The Greek (Rahlfs, Logos export, verified in the export itself).** 1:21 reads καὶ κύριος ἐταπείνωσέν με ("and the Lord humbled/afflicted me"), which is **the "afflict" reading, not the forensic one.** `[T]` The verse's second verb is kept distinct — ὁ ἱκανὸς ἐκάκωσέν με, "the Sufficient One did me evil", for הֵרַע — so the Greek has not simply collapsed the couplet; it has made a lexical choice on the first verb. This is a real divergence and the triage applies:

- *Translation loss?* Partly — the translator has chosen between two Hebrew homonyms and taken the other one.
- *Substantive variant?* No evidence of one; the consonants are the same.
- *The Greek's own text?* **This is the right category.** The LXX translator read the root as the afflicting one. **That is where the "afflicted" tradition comes from**, and it is why some commentators still carry it. `[I]`

**The Greek also loses the Job contact — and that is itself informative.** `[T]` Checked in **Swete** (observation layer, named as such), Job 27:2 reads Ζῇ ὁ θεὸς… καὶ ὁ παντοκράτωρ ὁ πικράνας μου τὴν ψυχήν — "as God lives… and the Almighty who embittered my soul". So the **bitterness verb survives** into Greek in both places, the πικρ- root standing behind both Job's ὁ πικράνας and Ruth's ἐπικράνθη; **but the divine name does not match.** Job's translator rendered שַׁדַּי with παντοκράτωρ; Ruth's translator chose ὁ ἱκανός, a word that occurs nowhere in Swete's Job in that sense (one ἱκανῶς, an adverb, is the book's only ἱκαν- form at all). The forensic company fares no better: Swete's Job 31:35 has no divine name in the clause, and its 40:2 diverges substantially from the Hebrew.

**What follows.** The two-verse collocation that makes Headline 1 is **a Hebrew-level phenomenon, invisible to a Greek reader**, because the translators of Ruth and Job made independent lexical choices for the same divine name. `[I]` That cuts two ways and both are worth stating: it confirms the finding is in the Hebrew and not an artefact of a translation tradition, and it means no ancient Greek-reading interpreter could have seen it. *Confidence: high* on the Greek readings, each read in the editions named; *moderate* on the inference.

**The preaching note.** The Greek's reading at 1:21 is a witness to how the verse was understood in the Second Temple period, and it is not the reading the WLC's own lexicography, the canonical company, or any of the three English versions supports. `[T]` **Worth knowing; not worth preaching as an alternative.**

## Extension — Historical Background

**A city "stirred" by a returning widow.** In a settlement of a few hundred, the return of a woman who had emigrated a decade earlier with a household and come back with neither husband nor sons would be a public event. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate*; the inference is from the verb and the setting, not from the text's statement.

**Barley before wheat.** Barley ripens first, in spring; the wheat harvest follows some weeks later (as 2:23 confirms within the book). `[T]` **1:22's dating therefore gives the book its clock**, and chapter 2's gleaning and chapter 3's threshing-floor scene both depend on it.

**What a widow's position actually was.** Without a husband or son, a woman's access to land and livelihood depended on the Torah's gleaning provisions and on a relative's willingness. `[S]` **Naomi's "empty" is a materially accurate description of her legal and economic position**, whatever one makes of her theology. *Confidence: high* on the economics, and this is the part of her speech the book never corrects.

## Extension — Original Audience

**What the first hearers would have known that modern readers do not.** The names. `[I]` נָעֳמִי is transparently from נָעֵם ("to be pleasant"); מָרָא from מָרַר ("to be bitter"). **A Hebrew-speaking audience hears the pun instantly and needs no footnote** — which means the renaming demand lands as wordplay and grief at once. *Confidence: high.*

**What they would have felt at 1:22.** Two things simultaneously: **relief** (the harvest; they are home in the right season) and **unease** (the Moabitess, named as such, is now inside Bethlehem). `[I]` *Confidence: moderate* on the second; the narrator's insistence on the gentilic is text, the audience's discomfort is inference — though Deuteronomy 23:3 makes the inference a reasonable one. `[S]`

**What they would have expected next.** Not a romance. `[I]` A woman who has declared herself emptied by God, in a town at harvest, with a foreign daughter-in-law and no male relative mentioned — **the expected next move is destitution or charity, not a kinsman.** The narrator's 2:1 disclosure is a surprise by design.

## Extension — Biblical Theology

**The Shaddai thread.** The name appears at the covenant's inauguration (Genesis 17:1, "I am El Shaddai; walk before me and be blameless") and at its re-disclosure (Exodus 6:3). `[T]` **It is the name under which the promise of descendants was given to a childless couple.** Naomi uses it of the God who has left her childless. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate* on the resonance; I have not established that the author intends it, and the five-verse Shaddai+YHWH count above does not license a claim of allusion. **But the preacher who notices it is noticing something on the page: the childless woman complains to the God of Genesis 17 by the name he used there.**

**The emptying-and-filling thread.** Famine to bread (1:1, 1:6), empty to full (1:21 → 4:13–17), exile to return. `[T]` **Ruth's version of this thread is domestic rather than national**, and that is its contribution: the pattern that governs Israel's history is shown operating in one household, in a book with no king, no battle and no prophet.

**The widow-and-foreigner thread.** The Torah's triad of the vulnerable — the widow, the orphan, the sojourner — is the legal backdrop of the whole book. `[S]` **This unit puts two of the three inside Bethlehem in a single sentence** (1:22: a widow who has returned, and a Moabitess). `[T]`

## Extension — Difficult Verses

**"The LORD has testified against me" — is Naomi right?** `[I]`

The honest answer is **in part, and the part she is wrong about is the part the book is written to correct.** She is right that God is the agent: the narrator himself says the LORD gave bread (1:6) and later that the LORD gave conception (4:13), and the book nowhere suggests her losses were outside his hand. She is right about her position: she is empty. **She is wrong that the emptiness is a verdict.**

The difficulty for the preacher is that **the text does not say she is wrong.** It shows it. A sermon that asserts her error on verse 21 is reading ahead; a sermon that leaves her verdict standing has not read to the end. **The passage requires the preacher to hold a true complaint and a false conclusion in the same hand for as long as the text does.**

**"Call me Mara" — is self-naming by grief sinful?** The text passes no judgement and neither should the sermon. `[I]` What the text does is **refuse the name**, immediately and permanently. **The correction is God's providence and the narrator's pen, not a reprimand.**

## Extension — Christological Reading

**The category is contrast, and it is the strongest leg available here.** `[I]`

Naomi says four things in two verses: God has embittered me, emptied me, testified against me, harmed me. `[T]` **Three of the four are false of her and true of someone else.** The one who was made bitter, emptied, testified against and harmed — and for whom the verdict was real — is the one descended from the child the women will name at the end of this book. `[I]` *Confidence: this is a trajectory reading, not a typological claim about the text's intent; stated as such.*

**The forensic word is where the contrast is sharpest.** עָנָה בִי is testimony given against a person. `[T]` **Naomi believed she was under a verdict she had not earned. At the cross the verdict was earned by no one and borne by one.** The book's own answer to Naomi is a kinsman-redeemer who buys what she cannot and a child who reverses her name; the canon's answer is in her line.

**The moralism trap.** `[I]` This passage will not preach as "don't be like Naomi — trust God in your bitterness". That reading makes the passage about the quality of her faith, and **the text is about the reliability of God's providence while her faith is at its lowest.** The sermon's good news is not that she should have known better. It is that she was wrong and it did not matter, because the kinsman was already in the town.

## Preaching Pitfalls

**1. Correcting Naomi before the book does.** The commonest and the costliest. `[I]` Verse 21 is left standing by the narrator for three chapters. A sermon that answers it in the application of this passage has done in twenty minutes what the author declined to do in sixty verses — and has taught the congregation that complaint must be tidied before God will act.

**2. Preaching "afflicted me" when the text says "testified against me".** `[T]` All three versions have the forensic reading. The "afflicted" tradition comes from the Greek, and a preacher who offers it as the Hebrew's meaning is passing on the LXX's choice as the text's.

**3. Making the sermon about Naomi's bitterness.** The word is in the passage, but **the passage is not a diagnosis of her character.** `[I]` The emotional vocabulary is a doorway to a theological claim, and the claim is about God's conduct, not her mood.

**4. Losing the pun.** `[T]` Every English version transliterates both names. If the preacher does not say that Naomi means *pleasant* and Mara means *bitter*, verse 20 is a woman asking for a different foreign name.

**5. Claiming the Job connection as a quotation.** `[I]` The measurements are strong and the register claim is sound. "Naomi is quoting Job" is not what they show, and a congregation told that will have been given a claim the text cannot bear. **Say "there is one other place in the Bible where someone says this, and it is Job" — which is exactly true — and stop there.**

**6. Preaching 1:22 as the narrator's rebuttal.** `[I]` It functions as one, and it is not written as one. There is no "but". The preacher may say the sentence contradicts her; the preacher may not say the narrator contradicts her, because he does not.

**7. Skipping the harvest notice.** `[T]` It is the last clause of the chapter and it is doing the most work per word of anything in the unit. **The chapter ends with the word "barley", and chapter 2 begins in a barley field.**

## Open Questions

- **The chiasm of 1:20–21.** Real design or an artefact of two-verse speech with four divine names? **Needs a chance baseline** (e.g. how often a two-verse stretch of Hebrew narrative speech produces a mirrored name sequence). Capped at moderate until then.
- **The Job register as programme.** Is there anything else in Ruth that points to Job? The density scan's thirtieth-of-859 rank for Job 2 is weak. **Worth one targeted search of Ruth's full vocabulary against Job before the macro-synthesis**, so the Finalise pass knows whether this is one passage's idiom or a book-level move.
- **R17 — the 2 Samuel 21:9 contact.** Untranscribed from auditor 1. **Does the shared barley-harvest dating carry any weight, given that one of the three words is a ketiv in Samuel?** My own reading is that it does not, but the row should be tested rather than dropped.
- **הֲזֹאת נָעֳמִי and 4:17 — are the speakers the same group?** The text says "they said" at 1:19 and הַנָּשִׁים ("the women") at 4:14, 4:17. **The identification is an inference and should be flagged as one in the echo table.**
- **Whether the barley-harvest date carries firstfruits resonance.** The calendrical fact is certain; the intent is not. Left open.
- **Why Ruth's Greek translator chose ὁ ἱκανός for שַׁדַּי where Job's chose παντοκράτωρ.** A question about the translators, not about Ruth, and so outside this report — but it bears on how any Greek-reading interpreter could have handled 1:20–21, and a Logos question would settle what is known about it.
