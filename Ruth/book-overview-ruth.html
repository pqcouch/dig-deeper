Canonical Position

Field Statement
Section Ketuvim, fourth book in the sequence BHS prints: Psalms, Job, Proverbs, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles [T]. English Bibles and the Greek tradition set Ruth between Judges and 1 Samuel [S]. Codex Leningradensis itself puts Chronicles first among the Writings, but Ruth keeps the same neighbours in either sequence [S].
Reading implication In the Hebrew order Ruth is read as a Writing, after Proverbs, not as the tail of the history; its first clause fixes the setting in the judges’ period but does not shelve the book there. In the English and Greek order it reads as the counterpart to the closing chapters of Judges and the hinge into Samuel. Neither placement is the book’s own claim [I].
Presupposes Checked references, each verified in the WLC. (1) The judges’ period. The opening clause בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים (“in the days when the judges governed”, 1:1) occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible [T]. (2) Judah and Tamar. Ruth 4:12 names Perez, Tamar and Judah [T]; the story itself is Genesis 38. (3) Rachel, Leah, Ephrathah and Bethlehem. Ruth 4:11 names all four; Bethlehem and Ephrath(ah) stand together in only five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 35:19; 48:7; Mic 5:2 [Heb 5:1]; Ruth 4:11; 1 Chr 4:4 [T]. (4) The house of Perez. Perez and Hezron stand together in five verses: Gen 46:12; Num 26:21; Ruth 4:18; 1 Chr 2:5; 4:1 [T]. (5) The law of land-redemption. The kinsman-redeemer described as “near” (גֹּאֵל, “close relative”; קָרוֹב, “near”) is found in three verses only: Lev 25:25; Ruth 2:20; 3:12 [T]. Ruth never cites the law [T]. (6) Gleaning. Gleaning, reaping and field occur together in three verses: Lev 19:9; 23:22; Ruth 2:3 [T]. (7) Raising the name of the dead. Deut 25:5–10 is the nearest law (see the map, rows 8 and 9) [I]. (8) Moab. Ruth relies on the reader’s knowledge of Moab as an outsider people and never says why [I].
Handoff Thematic. The child Obed and the line to David (4:17, 22) hand the reader to David’s story. In English and Greek order the next book is 1 Samuel, where Jesse is introduced as an Ephrathite of Bethlehem in Judah (1 Sam 17:12); that triple (Ephrathite, Bethlehem, Judah) occurs in only two verses of the Hebrew Bible, 1 Sam 17:12 and Ruth 1:2 [T]. In BHS order the next book is Song of Songs; no lexical handoff is claimed.
Neighbours Proverbs (before): the phrase אֵשֶׁת חַיִל (“woman of excellence”) stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, Prov 12:4; 31:10; Ruth 3:11 [T]. Proverbs ends by describing such a woman and Ruth shows one; whether the adjacency is doing work is a proposal [I], untested against a baseline, and a first target for the claim audit. Song of Songs (after): no work demonstrated. The grouping of the five Megilloth, with Ruth read at Shavuot, is later practice [S] and not evidence about the text.

Canonical and compositional order. The events fall in the judges’ period [T]; the Hebrew canon files the book among the Writings [T]; the English and Greek canons file it after Judges [S]. The direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15 is open: the BHS apparatus itself cross-refers the two at 4:18 [T], but which text draws on which is not settled here. Both orders are reported and neither is preferred.

Presenting Situation

  • Narrator. Anonymous, third person; reports inner states (Boaz’s heart was merry, 3:7) [T]. The author is unnamed [T]; tradition names Samuel [S]. The narrator explains a custom as belonging to “former times in Israel” (4:7) [T], so writes at a distance from the events [I]. The book ends on David’s name with no royal title attached (4:17, 22) [T], so it reached its present form after David was known [I].
  • Audience. The narrative-world audience is Bethlehem, its field and its gate [T]. The compositional audience is contested: a monarchic date and a post-exilic date are both argued [S], and neither is resolved here. The canonical audience is the reader of the Ketuvim, or of Judges–Samuel, who already knows David [I]. Interpretation defaults to that canonical reader.
  • Occasion. None is stated [T]. Proposals (the legitimacy of David’s line; post-exilic marriage questions) are reconstructions [S].
  • Pressure. Emptiness. A famine drives a family out of Bethlehem; three men die in Moab; a woman is left without husband or sons; her only companion is a Moabite widow [T]. Naomi names it herself as “bitter” (מָרָא, “Mara”, 1:20) and “empty” (רֵיקָם, “empty”, 1:21), and ascribes it to שַׁדַּי (“the Almighty”, 1:20–21) [T]. Ruth carries a second pressure: she is called “the Moabitess” six times after chapter 1:4 (1:22; 2:2, 6, 21; 4:5, 10), including at the moment Boaz acquires her [T], and calls herself a foreigner (נָכְרִיָּה, “foreigner”, 2:10) [T]. The book asks whether YHWH’s kindness (חֶסֶד, “kindness”, 1:8; 2:20; 3:10) reaches such a household [I].
  • Purpose. To show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and to give Israel David as the fruit [I]. No divine speech occurs in the book [T]. The narrator himself states a divine act once (4:13, “the LORD enabled her to conceive”); at 1:6 YHWH’s visit to his people is what Naomi heard [T].

Text-critical and versional points to carry into the digs

  1. 1:1. BHS notes that the Greek reads an infinitive (“when the judges were judging”, Rahlfs ἐν τῷ κρίνειν) and lacks “two” before “sons” [T].
  2. 1:21. NASB95 has “the LORD has witnessed against me”. The Greek (Rahlfs ἐταπείνωσέν με, “humbled me”), Syriac and Vulgate read “afflicted” [T]; the Hebrew verb can carry either sense [S].
  3. 2:1. The WLC prints the word for “kinsman” unpointed (a ketiv, מידע); the qere is מוֹדַע (BHS apparatus) [T]. The word גֹּאֵל (“close relative”) does not appear until 2:20 [T].
  4. Ketiv density. The WLC prints eleven unpointed words in nine verses (1:8; 2:1; 3:3; 3:4; 3:12; 3:14; 4:4; 4:5; 4:6), about 10.6 % of the book’s verses [T]; the apparatus adds qere-only “to me” at 3:5 and 3:17 [T]. A search by pointed surface form will miss these words.
  5. 4:5. BHS prints the ketiv קניתי (“I have acquired”) with the qere קָנִיתָה (“you have acquired”); Rahlfs reads the second person (αὐτὴν κτήσασθαί σε δεῖ, “you must acquire her”), as does NASB95 (“you must also acquire”), and the apparatus suggests the imperative קְנֵה [T]. The reading decides whether Ruth is placed on the nearer redeemer or on Boaz.
  6. 4:14. The Hebrew has “his name” (שְׁמוֹ); Rahlfs has “your name” (τὸ ὄνομά σου) [T]. The antecedent of “redeemer” in 4:14–15 is, on the Hebrew, most naturally the child [I].
  7. 4:20–21. The WLC spells the same ancestor שַׂלְמָה (4:20) and שַׂלְמוֹן (4:21); the apparatus records some manuscripts with שלמה at 4:21 and Σαλμαν in Codex B [T].
  8. 4:19. BHS records the Greek name forms Αρραν (𝔊ᴬᴮ) and Αραμ (𝔊ʳᵉˡ) and proposes reading אֲרָם, citing Matt 1:3–4 [T]. The genealogy’s fourth name is therefore contested, and the proposed reading is the one the Gospel’s list uses.
  9. Paragraphing. The WLC reading file carries a single paragraph marker, פ, after 4:17 [T]. The BHS text file was not opened, so BHS’s own paragraphing is not reported.
  10. What the English hides. NASB95 renders אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל (2:1) “a man of great wealth” and אֵשֶׁת חַיִל (3:11) “a woman of excellence”; the Hebrew uses the same noun for both [T].

Structural Arc Map

Verses Movement Function
1:1–5 Emptied Famine, a move to Moab, three deaths; Naomi left with two Moabite daughters-in-law.
1:6–22 Return Naomi turns for Bethlehem; Orpah goes back, Ruth clings; Naomi arrives renamed in her own mouth as “bitter”.
2:1–23 Gleaning Boaz is introduced; Ruth gleans; Boaz’s kindness; Naomi first calls him a redeemer (2:20).
3:1–18 Threshing floor Naomi’s plan, Ruth’s night-time request, Boaz’s promise and the nearer redeemer named.
4:1–12 The gate The nearer redeemer yields; Boaz buys the field and acquires Ruth before the elders.
4:13–17 Filled A son is born “to Naomi”; the women’s blessing; Obed named.
4:18–22 The line Ten names from Perez to David.

Device. A linear narrative driven by reversal, with the movements joined by repeated words rather than a single chiastic frame [I]. Counts in the WLC:

  • שׁוּב (“return”) occurs 15 times, 12 of them in chapter 1, one in 2:6 and two in 4:3 and 4:15 [T].
  • גָּאַל / גֹּאֵל (“redeem” / “redeemer”) occurs 21 times, none before 2:20: one in chapter 2, seven in chapter 3, thirteen in chapter 4 [T].
  • לָקַט (“glean”) occurs 12 times, all in chapter 2 [T].
  • קָנָה (“buy, acquire”) occurs six times, all in chapter 4 [T].
  • שֵׁם (“name”) occurs 14 times: five in chapter 1, two in chapter 2, seven in chapter 4 [T].
  • יָלַד (“bear, beget”) occurs 14 times: one in chapter 1 and thirteen in chapter 4, nine of them in the genealogy [T].

Seams. The verb “return” enters at 1:6 and, from the point of view of the book’s argument, leaves at 4:15, where the women call the child “a restorer of life” (מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ, 4:15); Rahlfs keeps the chain in Greek (ἐπιστρέφω and its compounds through chapter 1, ἐπιστρέφοντα ψυχήν at 4:15) [T]. Chapter 3 ends with Naomi’s “wait” (3:18) and chapter 4 opens on Boaz “going up” to the gate [T]. The break at 4:17 / 4:18 is marked by the WLC’s paragraph marker and by a new heading, וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת פָּרֶץ (“now these are the generations of Perez”) [T].

The “ten” pattern — proposed, tested, and withdrawn as design (v1.2). The counts stand: about ten years in Moab (1:4), ten elders at the gate (4:2), ten names from Perez to David with Boaz the seventh and David the tenth (4:18–22) [T]. The design reading does not. A baseline of every numeral in the book (claim audit queue R1, 13 November 2026) found two nine times — eight of them in chapter 1 — against ten twice, which ties it with six (the measures of barley at 3:15, 3:17) and puts it one above seven [T]. Ten is not this book’s number. Further, the text never says “ten” of the genealogy at all, so that leg is a count the reader supplies; and the list gives Boaz’s seventh position no formal marking — nine identical “X fathered Y” formulas with no expansion anywhere, where Genesis 5 marks its seventh from Adam with an expanded entry [T]. Recorded as a distribution, not a pattern, and it should not reach a sermon as one.

Microscript

Famine drives a Bethlehem family to Moab, where death leaves Naomi with nothing but a Moabite daughter-in-law who will not leave her; back in Bethlehem, YHWH’s kindness works through a harvest field, a threshing floor and a city gate, by people who pay for it, to turn Naomi’s emptiness into a son who becomes Obed, grandfather of Jesse’s son David. The keynotes are שׁוּב (“return”) and then גֹּאֵל (“redeemer”).

Test. Emptied supplies the emptiness; Return the first turning; Gleaning, Threshing floor and Gate the kindness at work and its cost; Filled the reversal; the line the result for Israel. Every movement serves the spine.

Intertextual Map

Plain statement. Ruth contains no marked quotation of earlier Scripture: no citation formula and no quoted line [T]. Its links to earlier texts come in four ways: names (Judah, Tamar, Perez, Rachel, Leah, Ephrathah, Bethlehem, Moab), legal vocabulary it never cites (gleaning, redeeming, raising a name), a few rare lexical pairings, and a small group of images shared with other books. The book’s argument is carried mainly by internal repetition (see the echo table), so passage digs should lean on Move 4 more than on Move 2. Genesis as a book. Six distinct Genesis passages are touched (2:24; 12:1; 24:27; 29–35 with 48:7; 38; 46:12) but no one passage is used more than twice. “Genesis is Ruth’s live source” is a proposal [I], unbaselined. (v1.3 — claim audit, R20: live source kept, programme discarded.) The baseline has run. Per chapter Genesis is only Ruth’s fifth-densest partner book (0.102 at K=5), behind 1 Samuel 0.125, 2 Samuel 0.120, Judges 0.114 and Esther 0.110; the same ordering appears against every arbitrary narrative target tested, and Ruth’s Genesis figure sits inside the band those targets produce (0.039–0.103), with 2 Samuel 13–14 yielding a slightly higher Genesis density than Ruth does. Genesis supplies the most top-fifty partner chapters only because it has the most chapters — 2.75× expectation against 1 Samuel’s 3.33×. So Genesis remains a live source on the strength of its six verified contacts, five of them at the seams of 2:11 and chapter 4, and not on density; and any claim that Ruth is written out of Genesis as a programme is withdrawn. The densest partner book per chapter is 1 Samuel, which no draft of this overview said.

Christological Trajectory

  • Redeemer (typology through an established category). The book’s second keyword is גֹּאֵל (“redeemer”). Boaz acts as redeemer at cost: he takes on the dead man’s land and the dead man’s name, and the debt is his, not Ruth’s. This is a type through the redeemer category, not through incidental detail [I], moderate to high confidence. The women’s cry in 4:14 (“blessed is the LORD who has not left you without a redeemer today”) lays the category open.
  • Trajectory (a longing fulfilled). A Moabite widow comes “under the wings” of the God of Israel (2:12) and into the royal line. The line to David (4:17, 22) is the line to the Messiah; the Gospel of Matthew names Perez, Boaz, Obed and Jesse, and names Ruth among four women (Matt 1:3–6) [S]. High confidence on the trajectory [I].
  • Contrast (need answered). Naomi’s emptiness and the unredeemed field are a need the book does not itself meet by human effort alone; each kindness (1:8, 2:20, 3:10) is a human act with God named as its source, and the only act the narrator states in his own voice is God’s (4:13) [T].
  • What is not claimed. The threshing-floor details (the feet, the cloak, the six measures) are not allegorised. “Boaz is Christ” is not read detail by detail.
  • Moralism check. The temptation is “be a Ruth” (loyal) or “be a Boaz” (generous). Both are true to the text but are not its argument. The gospel grounding: the kindness is God’s before it is anyone’s (1:6, 4:13), and the one who redeems a foreign widow at his own cost is the pattern for the greater Redeemer, who makes outsiders members of the house. Christ is the hero; Ruth and Boaz are the means, not the model for earning anything.

Preaching Traps

  1. The romance. Reading Ruth as a love story (waiting for your Boaz). It sounds warm and is wrong: Naomi drives the plot from her emptiness, and Boaz praises Ruth for not going after young men, rich or poor, as a kindness to the dead (3:10). Bites at: chapters 2–3, especially 2:5–9 and 3:6–15.
  2. The wedding vow. Using 1:16–17 as a text for marriage or for generic commitment. It is a refusal to turn back, addressed to a mother-in-law who has just told her to go (1:15), and it ends in an oath by YHWH. Bites at: 1:14–18.
  3. Allegorising the threshing floor. Treating the feet, the cloak and the six measures as codes. The redeemer category carries the type; the details do not. Bites at: 3:4, 3:7–9, 3:15.
  4. Judging Naomi’s theology. Either endorsing her verdict that the Almighty has dealt bitterly (1:20–21) or correcting it before the book does. The narrator neither endorses nor corrects it; the story answers it. Bites at: 1:6–22 and 4:14–17.
  5. The legal label. Calling 4:1–12 “levirate marriage”. The redeemer is not Mahlon’s brother, the widow does not remove a sandal, and the nearer redeemer’s act is tied to the field. The case combines land-redemption and the name of the dead; no single law is applied [T]. A related error imports the Moabite law (Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4]) or modern intermarriage debates into a book that cites neither. Bites at: 4:3–10.
  6. Stopping at 4:17. Treating the genealogy as an appendix. The book closes on David, and the movement from emptiness to David is the argument. Bites at: 4:13–22.
  7. Preaching a pattern that has not been baselined. (v1.3 — added by the claim audit.) The temptation on this book is strong, because its patterns are pretty: a numerical design in the tens, a verb for each movement, a Torah frame between Exodus and Genesis, a Judges block behind the opening, a Genesis programme, a chain of “leaving”, a descent answered by an ascent, a cluster of narratorial asides, a betrothal type-scene. Every one of those was tested against chance and every one failed — eight of eight. What survived, without exception, was the opposite kind of claim: a single rare wording contact, counted and with its rivals named. The discipline this book teaches is therefore blunt. A pattern is a sermon illustration until it has been measured against what the book does elsewhere, and the pulpit is not the place to try one out.
