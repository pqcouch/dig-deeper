What a sweep is, and is not

This is a map, not ten solo digs. Each unit runs at a fixed, deliberately limited depth: position, structure, up to five text-first findings, allusion work, the internal-echo check, the places where the study text and the pulpit text part company, a live difficulty, and a note to the overview. Every count and chain is verified by lemma in the WLC before it is stated; the design claims a sweep generates are capped at moderate and named for the claim audit. For any ⭐ HIGH unit, run a full solo dig before the pulpit. The ranked list of which to take first is at the end, and it is this report’s main deliverable.

Warrant tags: [T] the text states or shows it; [I] inference from the text; [S] supplied by a secondary source. Confidence is flagged on every lexical, manuscript and intertextual claim.

Canonical position (Phase 0.55)

Taken from the overview rather than re-derived. Ruth stands fourth in the Ketuvim as BHS prints them — Psalms, Job, Proverbs, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles [T]. It is a Writing, not an appendix to Judges; the English and Greek placement after Judges is a later arrangement [S]. The canonical reader of the Writings arrives having read the Prophets through to Malachi — post-exilic, knowing David and knowing how the monarchy ended, living with promises not yet resolved [S]. That reader is the one this sweep assumes.

Live sources for the book, carried forward as elevated priors for the allusion work (overview’s intertextual map, now extended by this sweep): Genesis — confirmed live and strengthened materially at unit 10; Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 25 — the redemption and name-raising laws, never cited; Judges 17–19 — Bethlehem-in-Judah vocabulary; Proverbs — the “woman of excellence” phrase; Exodus — newly surfaced in this sweep at unit 2; Lamentations — newly surfaced at unit 9, a Ketuvim neighbour. (v1.3 — claim audit, R20.) Genesis survives as a live source but not as a programme. The programme is discarded: per chapter Genesis is only Ruth’s fifth-densest partner book (0.102 at K=5), behind 1 Samuel 0.125, 2 Samuel 0.120, Judges 0.114 and Esther 0.110, and Ruth’s Genesis density sits inside the band five arbitrary narrative targets produce (0.039–0.103). Genesis supplies the most top-fifty partner chapters because it has the most chapters — 2.75× expectation against 1 Samuel’s 3.33×. The warrant for Genesis is therefore the six individually verified contacts, five of them at the seams of 2:11 and chapter 4, not density. And the densest partner book per chapter is 1 Samuel, which no draft said.

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools and units. Each is high confidence and measured.

  1. The narrator attributes exactly one act to God in his own voice, and it is the conception at 4:13. — Surfaced by: Narrator’s Comment, Repetition, the internal-echo check, and the cross-passage count. Everywhere else in eighty-five verses YHWH appears in characters’ speech, and at 1:6 his visitation is what Naomi heard. The book’s providence is asserted by its author once, at the point where the law has done all it can — which is where a sermon on Ruth should locate its theology.
  2. The plot turns on a two-word chain that the study text hides. — Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, internal echo, Translations. כָּנָף (“wing”) occurs twice in the book: Boaz prays that Ruth has come to take refuge under YHWH’s wings (2:12), and Ruth asks Boaz to spread his wing over her (3:9). The ESV lets a congregation hear it; the NASB95’s “covering” does not.
  3. Ruth carries an exodus formula at its turning point and the Pentateuch’s toledot heading at its close. (v1.4 — Logos round 1, L7 and L8: both legs checked against the literature, and the novelty claim withdrawn.) Neither observation is new to this sweep. On 1:6, the commentators do record פָּקַד as the vocabulary of divine visitation and group Ruth 1:6 with Gen 21:1, Gen 50:24–25 and Exod 4:31 (Wilch, Concordia Commentary, 152; Driesbach, Cornerstone, 513; NET notes) [S] — but none argues that Ruth deliberately evokes the exodus; they treat it as shared covenantal idiom. The three-verse count for the ordered phrase stands as measured, and the allusion reading should be stated as a possibility the wording permits rather than one the literature supports. On 4:18, the toledot formula is widely observed: Queen-Sutherland calls its appearance in Ruth “curious” and suggestive of a correlation with the Genesis ancestral stories (Smyth & Helwys, 160), Fischer ties the Perez heading to the final toledot of Genesis at 37:2a, and Mathews and Block treat it as a functional “hinge” convention (NAC 1a:33) [S]. Campbell notes the near-universal critical view that 4:18–22 is a genealogical appendix not original to the story, its style matching P — while also observing that in Genesis no P genealogy ever functions as a colophon to a story, so Ruth’s use is unique and its significance hard to assess (Anchor Bible 7:172) [S]. That last point is the strongest form of the finding and the sweep did not have it. — Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion, Vocabulary, the cross-passage synthesis. “Heard” with “visited” and YHWH stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible — Exod 4:31 and Ruth 1:6; and the clause itself, כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה אֶת (“for the LORD had visited”), stands in three — Exod 4:31, 1 Sam 2:21 (of Hannah) and Ruth 1:6 [T]. “And these are the generations of” is the Pentateuch’s own heading, and in Ruth’s spelling it is a canon-wide hapax; heading-type uses of the noun do stand outside the Torah, in Chronicles. Both are new to this sweep and neither is in the overview. (v1.2 — the third occurrence of the clause and the Chronicles qualification were added by claim audit 1.)
  4. Ruth’s case fits no single law, and the text says so lexically. — Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion, the legal frame of unit 8. The redeemer draws off his own sandal with a different verb from Deuteronomy 25’s, where the widow pulls it off in contempt; the scene combines Leviticus 25’s land-redemption with Deuteronomy 25’s name-raising and follows neither exactly. The irregularity is why there are ten elders, three declarations of witness, and a narrator’s footnote on an obsolete custom.
  5. For this book the pulpit text is more transparent than the study text, at five load-bearing points. — Surfaced by: Translations, across all ten units. The ESV preserves חַיִל, the rest/security cognate, כָּנָף, “bitter” and three of four gate references; the NASB95 breaks all five. Build on the ESV here, and use the NASB95 where the ESV itself closes a question down (2:20, 3:11).

Unit 1 — Ruth 1:1–5 · Emptied in Moab

Overview weighting: Standard

Position. Nothing in the book precedes this, so Question 1 is answered canonically rather than internally: the preceding movement is the canon itself, and what it has set up is a famine-and-migration pattern the patriarchs already ran — Abram to Egypt, Isaac among the Philistines, Jacob’s sons to Egypt [I]. Question 2: the unit exists here because the book’s whole argument is a reversal, and a reversal needs a floor. Five verses spend a decade and three lives to establish emptiness; the rest of the book spends eighty verses undoing it [T].

Structure. A single accelerating paragraph: setting (v.1), names (v.2), first death (v.3), marriages and ten years (v.4), two more deaths (v.5). The narrator hits the accelerator hard — a life each for Elimelech, Mahlon and Chilion, dispatched in three clauses — and the acceleration is the point: this is backstory, cleared out of the way at speed [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. A famine at the house of bread, and nowhere else in the canon. רָעָב (“famine”) and בֵּית לֶחֶם (“Bethlehem”) stand together in one verse of the Hebrew Bible: Ruth 1:1 (WLC) [T]. The name contains לֶחֶם (“bread”); the common noun לֶחֶם occurs twice in the book, at 1:6 and 2:14 [T]. The irony is structural, not decorative — the house of bread has no bread, and the book’s turn comes when YHWH gives לֶחֶם (1:6) and Boaz offers it (2:14) [I]. High confidence on the collocation; moderate on the pun as authorial design, since a name-pun cannot be measured.
  2. The opening clause is unique. בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים (“in the days when the judges governed”) occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, plain or skeletal (WLC) [T]. High confidence.
  3. Every name is loaded, and the narrator never says so. אֱלִימֶלֶךְ (“my God is king”), נָעֳמִי (“my pleasantness”), מַחְלוֹן and כִּלְיוֹן (sounding on sickness and consuming), עָרְפָּה, רוּת [T] for the forms, [I] for the senses. The proper-noun inventory for the unit: Bethlehem, Judah, Moab, Ephrathites, and the five personal names. Of these, Bethlehem–Ephrathah and Moab carry canonical freight (units 8 and 10); the rest are scenery here. Moderate confidence on the name senses — the narrator supplies no gloss, unlike Ichabod or Nabal, so this is etymology without authorial signal.
  4. “Bereft of her two children.” The noun יֶלֶד (“child”) occurs twice in the book — 1:5 and 4:16 [T], verified by lemma. Naomi loses יְלָדֶיהָ here and receives הַיֶּלֶד there. High confidence on the chain; moderate on the reading.
  5. The men are named, then removed; the book runs on the women. Naomi is named 21 times in the book, Boaz 20, Ruth 12 (WLC, by lemma) [T]. For a book called Ruth, Naomi is the most-named character — a fact worth a preacher’s attention. High confidence.

Tool 11 — allusion. No quotation, no citation formula [T]. Famine-driven sojourn in a foreign land is a Genesis narrative pattern (Gen 12:10; 26:1; 42–47) — a conceptual echo at moderate confidence, carried by shape rather than wording; the shared vocabulary (famine, sojourn, wife, sons) is common. Moab needs no citation to carry its freight; the reader supplies it [I]. The density scan ranks Gen 38 sixth among chapters of the Hebrew Bible against Ruth 1, on kin vocabulary only — not an allusion, and the overview was right to say so.

Internal (Move 4). Planted for later: the two sons (answered at 4:15, “better to you than seven sons,” and 4:17, “a son has been born to Naomi”); יֶלֶד (answered at 4:16); the field of Moab (repeated as Naomi’s standing label at 1:22, 2:6, 4:3); Mahlon and Chilion, unattached to wives here, resolved at 4:10 where Ruth is named Mahlon’s widow [T]. High confidence on all four chains.

Translations. NASB95 “in the land of Moab” and ESV “in the country of Moab” both render שְׂדֵי מוֹאָב, literally “the fields of Moab”; the ESV itself switches to “fields” at 1:6 within one verse. Nothing bears on a finding — the congregation loses only the agricultural flavour. No divergence worth preaching.

Difficulty. The unit invites the question whether leaving Bethlehem was sin. The text passes no verdict [T], and the narrator who later tells us Boaz’s heart was merry (3:7) would have told us. Preach the silence; don’t fill it.

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s first movement and its “Emptied” title. Extends it in one place: the famine/Bethlehem collocation is unique in the canon, which the overview did not state.

Unit 2 — Ruth 1:6–18 · The road back ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH

Position. Question 1: vv.1–5 have emptied the household and left three widows in Moab; this unit provides the first motion back, and the mechanism is a report about YHWH [T]. Question 2: it exists here, immediately, because the book cannot let emptiness stand as its subject — the turn has to begin before Naomi can interpret it, which is why her lament (unit 3) comes after the journey rather than before. Position makes the famous speech of vv.16–17 a response to a command to leave, not an opening declaration [T].

Structure. Three movements bound by one verb. (a) v.6–7: the news and the setting out. (b) v.8–13: Naomi’s two dismissals, each opening with שֹׁבְנָה (“return”), the second closing on the hand of YHWH. (c) v.14–18: the parting of the ways — Orpah kisses and goes, Ruth clings and speaks, Naomi falls silent. The device is repetition, not chiasm: שׁוּב (“return”) is the hinge of every movement [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. The unit is the densest concentration of the book’s keyword. שׁוּב (“return”) occurs fifteen times in Ruth, twelve of them in chapter 1 (WLC, by lemma), and ten of those twelve fall in this unit — 1:6, 7, 8, 10, 11, 12, 15 (twice), 16, and then 1:21, 22 outside it [T]. It carries three distinct senses here: Naomi returning to Judah, the daughters-in-law returning to Moab, and at v.16 Ruth’s refusal to return. High confidence.
  2. An exodus formula, in a book nobody expects to carry one. שָׁמַע (“heard”) with פָּקַד (“visited”) and יְהוָה stand together in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Exodus 4:31 and Ruth 1:6 (WLC) [T]. At Exod 4:31 the people hear that YHWH has visited the sons of Israel and bow in worship; it is the moment Israel learns rescue has begun, and it completes a chain running from Joseph’s “God will surely visit you” through Gen 50:24–25 and Exod 3:16 to 13:19 (each verified by lemma) [T]. Ruth 1:6 puts that formula in the mouth of a report heard by a bereaved Israelite in Moab, and what YHWH has visited his people with is bread. This is new to this sweep; the overview does not have it. High confidence on the collocation (two verses, no closer rival); moderate–high that the exodus visitation is being invoked — the wording is distinctive and the thematic fit exact, but Ruth never names Egypt.
  3. “Her mother’s house” is a marked substitution. Naomi sends them each to בֵּית אִמָּהּ (“her mother’s house”, 1:8) [T]. The default formula is בֵּית אָב (“father’s house”), which stands in 213 verses of the Hebrew Bible; בַּיִת with אֵם in one verse stands in 17, among them Gen 24:28 (Rebekah running to her mother’s household) and Song 3:4; 8:2 (WLC) [T]. The substitution is measurable, and in a book where no father appears it reads as deliberate [I]. Moderate confidence — baselined, but not unique.
  4. Ruth’s speech is a refusal built from a legal oath. The core is six clauses of matched syntax, closing with כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה לִי וְכֹה יֹסִיף (“thus may the LORD do to me, and more also”) — an oath formula standing in twelve verses of the Hebrew Bible [T], which makes it idiom and bars a confidence above moderate on wording alone. The force is not that the formula is rare but that Ruth, a Moabite, swears by יְהוָה [I]. High confidence on the oath’s presence; moderate on its weight.
  5. דָּבַק is the covenant word. רוּת דָּבְקָה בָּהּ (“Ruth clung to her”, 1:14); the verb occurs four times in the book — 1:14, 2:8, 2:21, 2:23 [T]. Its covenantal home is Deuteronomy and Joshua, where Israel is to cling to YHWH (Josh 23:8, 12) [S], and the four lemmas of Gen 2:24 — “leave”, “father”, “mother”, “cling” — stand together in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Gen 2:24, with the only other place inside a three-verse window being the Ruth 2:8–11 region [T]. Moderate confidence on Gen 2:24 as the frame: the vocabulary is there and the cluster is tight, but Ruth 1:14 and 2:11 are four verses apart and the leaving and clinging have different objects.

Tool 11 — allusion. Four rows, one line each. Exod 4:31 → 1:6: shared שמע + פקד + יהוה, two verses in the canon; moderate–high (see finding 2; the full source pass is in that finding, since this is the unit’s load-bearing link). Gen 2:24 → 1:14 with 2:11: shared leave/father/mother/cling; moderate. Gen 12:1 → 2:11, flagged forward to unit 4: land, birthplace, go and father in two verses only, Gen 12:1 and Ruth 2:11; high on wording. Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] → the Moabite label: conceptual only, no shared wording, Ruth lacks “assembly”; moderate. No direct quotation anywhere in the unit, so no full Triad is owed.

Internal (Move 4). Planted: מְנוּחָה (“rest”, 1:9) — answered at 3:1 by its cognate מָנוֹחַ (“security”), a different word from the same root, so the row reads cognate, not identical [T]; the kindness Naomi wishes (חֶסֶד, 1:8) — the first of the book’s three uses, extended at 2:20 and 3:10 [T]; “bitter” (מָרַר, 1:13) — answered at 1:20, the only two uses of the root in the book [T]. Answers: nothing earlier; this is the book’s opening movement. All chains high confidence.

Translations. Two divergences that bear on findings, and in both the pulpit text is closer to the Hebrew than the study text — the reverse of the usual expectation, and worth the preacher knowing.

  • 1:13. NASB95 “it is harder for me than for you”; ESV “it is exceedingly bitter to me for your sake”. The Hebrew is מַר (“bitter”), the root that returns at 1:20 as Naomi’s new name. The ESV congregation will hear the link; a NASB95 reader will not.
  • 1:9 with 3:1. NASB95 “find rest” then “seek security”; ESV “find rest” then “seek rest”. The Hebrew words are cognate, not identical, so the ESV slightly over-levels while the NASB95 breaks the link altogether. On balance the ESV serves the chain better here, and the preacher should say “rest” at both points.
  • Ancient versions check. The moderns agree at 1:6 on “visited”; Rahlfs has ἐπέσκεπται, the same sense, so no split. One line, no finding.

Difficulty. 1:16–17 is the most-misused text in the book — read at weddings as a marriage vow. The pitfall section below carries it. Note here only that the verse is spoken to a mother-in-law who has just ordered her to leave [T], and that preaching it as a vow of romantic commitment inverts the speaker, the addressee and the occasion.

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s movement 2 and its ⭐ weighting. Extends it materially: Exodus is not in the overview’s intertextual map at all, and on the evidence of 1:6 it belongs there as a live source. The overview’s “no marked quotation” judgement stands — this is an unmarked formula, not a citation.

Unit 3 — Ruth 1:19–22 · “Call me Mara” ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH

Position. Question 1: the journey of vv.6–18 has brought two women to Bethlehem, and the town’s question — “Is this Naomi?” — requires an answer from Naomi herself. Question 2: the lament sits here, after the return and before the harvest, because the book needs its theological problem stated by a character before it is answered by events [I]. Had it come in Moab it would be despair; coming at the gate of Bethlehem, in the barley harvest, it is a verdict passed in the moment it is already being overturned [T] for the harvest notice, [I] for the irony.

Structure. Four verses, two voices, one frame. The town speaks (v.19b), Naomi answers in two matched couplets (vv.20–21), and the narrator closes with a notice that contradicts her (v.22). The device is the narrator’s frame: Naomi gets the last word in her own speech, the narrator gets the last word in the unit [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. Naomi’s speech is the book’s densest theology, and it is all hers. Four divine ascriptions in two verses: שַׁדַּי (“the Almighty”) twice — 1:20, 21, the only two uses in the book — plus יְהוָה twice [T]. She assigns agency to God for every loss, four ways: he has dealt bitterly, brought back empty, testified against, done harm [T]. The narrator neither endorses nor corrects it [T]. High confidence.
  2. The name-change is real, and it never takes. She asks to be called מָרָא (“bitter”, 1:20); the narrator calls her נָעֳמִי in the very next verse and everywhere after, twenty-one times in the book [T]. The renaming is refused by the narrative, not argued down [I]. High confidence on the data; moderate on the reading. Note the apparatus: BHS records many manuscripts reading מָרָה at 1:20, as both English versions’ transliteration “Mara” reflects only the Leningrad form — a reading-tradition point, naming its witness, not a claim about “the Masoretic text” [T].
  3. “Empty” is answered exactly once, and the preacher should know where. רֵיקָם (“empty-handed”) occurs twice in the book: 1:21 and 3:17 [T], verified by lemma. Boaz’s six measures of barley are sent precisely so that Ruth will not go back to her mother-in-law רֵיקָם. High confidence on the chain — this is the cleanest verbal reversal in Ruth.
  4. “Full” and “full” are two different words. Naomi went out מְלֵאָה (“full”, 1:21); Boaz prays her wages be שְׁלֵמָה (“full”, 2:12) [T], and the lemmas differ. Rahlfs renders both with πλήρης, so the answer is audible in Greek and only conceptual in Hebrew [T]. This is translation loss in reverse and belongs in category 1 of the triage — a fact about the languages, not a variant. Moderate confidence on the conceptual link; the row must not say “the same word”.
  5. The barley notice is the unit’s hinge, and it is the narrator’s. בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים (“at the beginning of barley harvest”, 1:22) [T]. The three lemmas — beginning, harvest, barley — stand together in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Ruth 1:22 and 2 Sam 21:9 (WLC) [T]. The 2 Samuel verse spells its first word as a ketiv, unpointed, so an ordered-phrase search misses it and only a lemma search finds it; the row therefore reads shared vocabulary, not verbatim [S: overview, confirmed here]. Low–moderate confidence that 2 Sam 21:9 is in view at all; it may simply be the calendar.

Tool 11 — allusion. No quotation [T]. שַׁדַּי is the Writings’ divine name of affliction, at home in Job (31 of its occurrences) — a canonical-register link rather than a citation, moderate confidence, and exactly the elevated-prior case the clustering rule describes for a Writings book [S]. 2 Sam 21:9 as above, low–moderate. No Triad owed.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 1:1–5’s emptying, now named by the woman who suffered it [T]. Planted: רֵיקָם (answered 3:17); שַׁדַּי (never used again — the book drops the name after Naomi’s lament, which is itself a finding [I], moderate); the barley harvest (frames chapters 1–3, closing at 2:23 with the end of barley and wheat) [T]; “Is this Naomi?” — the town’s question, answered by the town’s women at 4:14–17 [I], moderate.

Translations. 1:21 is the unit’s one real translation problem, and both versions make the same choice. NASB95 “the LORD has witnessed against me”; ESV “the LORD has testified against me”. Both read עָנָה בִי as legal testimony. Rahlfs has ἐταπείνωσέν με (“humbled me”), and BHS records the Greek with the Syriac and Vulgate for that sense [T]. The consonantal text is identical in both readings — the divergence is over which root is in play, not over a different Hebrew text, so this is not a substantive variant and must not be filed as one. It is an interpretive split between the ancient and modern traditions over the same words. What the congregation hears either way is a courtroom; what the ancient versions heard was affliction, which fits the parallel line (“the Almighty has brought calamity upon me”) rather better [I]. Moderate confidence. Worth one sentence in the pulpit, and a solo dig should settle it.

Difficulty. Naomi’s theology. The pitfall section carries it; the exegetical point is that the narrator’s refusal to adjudicate is deliberate and the harvest notice is the only comment he makes [T].

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview throughout, including its careful handling of 2 Sam 21:9 as non-verbatim. Extends it in one place: שַׁדַּי is used twice and then dropped, which the overview records as a count but does not read.

Unit 4 — Ruth 2:1–16 · Under his wings ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH (overview unit 2:1–16)

Position. Question 1: 1:22 has put two destitute women in Bethlehem at the start of a harvest, which is the one moment in the agricultural year when a landless person can eat [I]. Question 2: the unit exists here because the law’s provision has to be shown working before the book can ask for anything beyond the law — and everything Boaz does in this unit is beyond it [T]. The gleaning scene is the floor from which chapter 3’s request becomes intelligible.

Structure. Five scenes with a disjunctive opening. v.1 is a background clause, not narrative sequence — Hebrew puts the subject first to introduce Boaz off-stage [T]. Then: Ruth asks and goes (vv.2–3), Boaz arrives and asks whose she is (vv.4–7), Boaz speaks to Ruth (vv.8–9), Ruth answers and Boaz blesses (vv.10–13), the meal and the secret orders (vv.14–16). The scene is built on who speaks to whom about Ruth: about her in vv.5–7, to her from v.8 [I].

Text-first findings.

  1. Gleaning is the unit’s word, and it is confined to this chapter. לָקַט (“glean”) occurs twelve times in Ruth, every one of them in chapter 2 (WLC, by lemma) [T]. High confidence. The legal background is unmistakable and uncited: gleaning, reaping and field stand together in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Lev 19:9; 23:22; Ruth 2:3 [T]. (v1.3 — claim audit, R18.) The legal background is fuller than Leviticus. Deuteronomy 24:19–21 supplies the whole scene in three consecutive verses: 24:19 has קָצַר (“reap”), קָצִיר (“harvest”), עֹמֶר (“sheaf”) and the triad הַגֵּר הַיָּתוֹם וְהָאַלְמָנָה (“the sojourner, the orphan and the widow”); 24:20 has חָבַט (“when you beat your olive tree”) with the same triad; 24:21 the vineyard with it again [T]. Deuteronomy 24 also ranks 26th of 859 chapters against Ruth 2 on rare shared vocabulary. The gleaning vocabulary of Isaiah 17:5 and 27:12, which the overview rated uncertain, is not a rival to Ruth but a second user of the same Torah stock — and the only prophetic text drawing on it.
  2. Boaz’s instructions exceed the law at three points, and the text shows it. The law lets the poor glean the edges and what is dropped. Boaz orders his men to let her glean among the sheaves (2:15), to pull grain out of the bundles for her (2:16), and not to touch or rebuke her (2:9, 16) [T]. The verb at 2:16 is שָׁלַל in an emphatic construction — “you shall surely pull out” — an infinitive-plus-finite doubling that both English versions flatten to “purposely” or “also pull out” [T]. High confidence that the provision is extra-legal; this is where the unit’s חֶסֶד lives, before the word is used of Boaz at all [I].
  3. Boaz reads Ruth as Abraham. 2:11: she left her father, her mother and אֶרֶץ מוֹלַדְתֵּךְ (“the land of your birth”) and came to a people she did not know. The four lemmas — land, birthplace, go, father — stand together in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 12:1 and Ruth 2:11 (WLC); Gen 24:4 and Num 10:30 share three of the four [T]. High confidence on the wording; moderate that Boaz is deliberately casting her as an Abraham figure, since the speaker is a character and the narrator does not gloss it.
  4. “Under his wings” is the Psalter’s idiom, with four equal rivals. 2:12, תַּחַת־כְּנָפָיו (“under his wings”). חָסָה (“take refuge”) with כָּנָף (“wing”) stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Ps 36:7, 57:1, 61:4, 91:4 and Ruth 2:12 (WLC; the psalm numbers are the English — the Hebrew numbers them 36:8, 57:2, 61:5, 91:4) [T]. There is no closest source and four equal rivals, so this is the Psalter’s register rather than a citation. Moderate confidence, and it may not be rated higher.
  5. The word Boaz is introduced with is the word Ruth is praised with. חַיִל at 2:1 (אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל), 3:11 (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל) and 4:11 (עֲשֵׂה־חַיִל) — three occurrences, one noun [T], verified by lemma. High confidence. And אֵשֶׁת חַיִל as an ordered phrase stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, plain and skeletal: Prov 12:4; 31:10; Ruth 3:11 [T].

Tool 11 — allusion. Lev 19:9 / 23:22 → 2:2–3, 15–17: shared gleaning/reaping/field, three verses; high that the gleaning law is the background, and the excess over it is the finding. Gen 12:1 → 2:11: two verses in the canon; high on wording, moderate on the Abraham typing. Psalter (four psalms) → 2:12: five verses, no closest rival; moderate, idiom-level. Gen 24:27 → flagged forward to unit 5. No direct quotation, so no Triad owed; the Gen 12:1 row is the one that would carry a full Triad in a solo dig.

Internal (Move 4). Planted: כָּנָף (2:12) — answered at 3:9, where Ruth asks Boaz to spread his כָּנָף over her; the only two uses in the book, and the hinge of the whole plot [T], high on the chain, moderate on the reading that she is asking him to become the means of the refuge he invoked. חַיִל (2:1) — answered at 3:11 and 4:11 [T]. דָּבַק (2:8, 21, 23) — replants 1:14, now with Boaz’s young women as the object, and 2:23 closes “and she lived with her mother-in-law”, so the clinging does not replace Naomi [T]. שִׁפְחָה (“maidservant”, 2:13, twice in the verse) — changed to אָמָה (“maid”) at 3:9, twice in that verse [T]; whether that is a step up rests on the ranking of the two terms, which is low–moderate and a solo-dig question. Answers: 1:9’s “find rest” begins to be answered in a field rather than a house [I], moderate.

Translations. This unit contains the sweep’s clearest pulpit-text finding, and it runs against the study text.

  • 2:1 with 3:11 and 4:11. NASB95: “a man of great wealth” / “a woman of excellence” / “may you achieve wealth”. ESV: “a worthy man” / “a worthy woman” / “may you act worthily”. The Hebrew uses one noun at all three points. The ESV congregation hears the chain; the NASB95 reader does not. For once the pulpit text is the more transparent, and the preacher should build on it rather than correct it.
  • 2:16. Both versions flatten the emphatic doubling. The congregation hears an instruction; the Hebrew has an insistence.
  • Ancient versions check. The moderns agree at 2:12 on “wings”, and Rahlfs has πτέρυγας — no split. At 2:14 NASB95 has “vinegar” and ESV “wine” for חֹמֶץ (sour wine); nothing turns on it.

Textual variants. 2:7b is the hardest clause in the book, and the versions divide over it. BHS has זֶה שִׁבְתָּהּ הַבַּיִת מְעָט — word for word “this her sitting the house a little”, with שִׁבְתָּהּ an infinitive of יָשַׁב (“sit, dwell”) and הַבַּיִת (“the house”) standing with no preposition [T]. The apparatus records the Greek as reflecting a different Hebrew at two points: עֶרֶב (“evening”) for עַתָּה (“now”), and לֹא שָׁבְתָה בַשָּׂדֶה — “she has not rested in the field” — for the MT’s clause, which Rahlfs carries as οὐ κατέπαυσεν ἐν τῷ ἀγρῷ μικρόν [T]. The elegance of the problem is that the consonants שבתה can be pointed as either יָשַׁב (“sit”) or שָׁבַת (“cease, rest”); the genuine consonantal difference is only הַבַּיִת against בַשָּׂדֶה. Category 2 of the triage — a substantive variant with versional support, not translation loss. What turns on it: on the MT the servant reports that Ruth took a short rest indoors; on the Greek’s Vorlage he reports that she has not rested in the field at all. The one speech in the book in which a third party characterises Ruth either notes a break or insists she never took one. Uncertain — the MT clause is awkward enough that the Greek may be an attempt to relieve it, and awkwardness is an argument for originality. Both English versions follow the MT and render it differently: NASB95 “she has been sitting in the house for a little while”; ESV “except for a short rest”, which quietly drops the house. A preacher who leans on Ruth’s unbroken diligence should know the text is contested here.

Difficulty. 2:9 and 2:22 together imply that a foreign woman gleaning alone was at risk of assault, which the ESV makes explicit at 2:22 (“lest in another field you be assaulted”) where the NASB95 has “so that others do not fall upon you” [T]. A preacher should know this is in the text before a hearer raises it, and should not sentimentalise the field.

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s row on Gen 12:1 — including, now independently, the two-verse rarity count. Confirms the Psalter row at moderate and the refusal to rate it higher. One extension: the extra-legal character of Boaz’s three orders is the unit’s exegetical centre and the overview’s intertextual map, being a map of sources, does not record it.

Unit 5 — Ruth 2:17–23 · Kindness to the living and the dead

Overview weighting: Standard

Position. Question 1: vv.1–16 have produced an unexplained abundance and an unidentified benefactor; the unit exists to get the name “Boaz” from the field into Naomi’s hearing, and with it the word “redeemer” [T]. Question 2: it sits here because Naomi has to learn who the man is before she can form the plan of chapter 3 — the plot turns on information moving from field to house [I].

Structure. Measure, journey, interrogation, revelation, blessing, coda. The unit’s weight falls on v.20, Naomi’s first speech since her lament, and the shift in her is the structural point: her previous word on YHWH was that he had testified against her, and now she blesses him [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. “Redeemer” enters the book here, and then dominates it. גָּאַל/גֹּאֵל occurs twenty-one times in Ruth, none of them before 2:20: one in chapter 2, seven in chapter 3, thirteen in chapter 4 (WLC, by lemma) [T]. High confidence. The book’s second keyword is introduced in a single clause by Naomi, and the distribution is the plot.
  2. Naomi’s blessing is a verbatim Genesis formula. אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָזַב חַסְדּוֹ (“who has not withdrawn his kindness”) stands as an ordered phrase, plain and skeletal, in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 24:27 and Ruth 2:20 (WLC) [T]. In Genesis, Abraham’s servant blesses YHWH at the moment the bride is found; here Naomi blesses him and extends the kindness “to the living and to the dead”. High confidence on the wording — this is the closest thing to a quotation in the book; moderate on the wider betrothal parallel.
  3. The kindness chain is complete and tiny. חֶסֶד occurs three times in Ruth — 1:8, 2:20, 3:10 — and all three are in characters’ speech, never the narrator’s [T]. High confidence. The theology of the book’s central word is voiced by its people, not asserted by its author [I].
  4. “Near” plus “redeemer” is Leviticus 25’s vocabulary. 2:20, קָרוֹב (“near”) with גֹּאֵל; the two stand together in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Lev 25:25; Ruth 2:20; 3:12 (WLC) [T]. Ruth never cites the law [T]. High confidence on the vocabulary; moderate that the Levitical procedure is what is being invoked, since Ruth’s case will turn out not to be Leviticus 25’s case.
  5. An ephah is a great deal of barley. 2:17, about an אֵיפָה — on the usual reckoning some 22 litres, a fortnight’s grain for one person from a single day’s gleaning [S], moderate confidence and dependent on a reconstructed measure. The narrator gives the figure without comment; it is his way of showing the excess of v.16 [I].

Tool 11 — allusion. Gen 24:27 → 2:20: ordered phrase, two verses; high. Lev 25:25 → 2:20: shared near/redeemer, three verses; high on vocabulary. The idiom עָשָׂה + חֶסֶד + יְהוָה stands in fifteen verses [T] — idiom, and barred from a rating above moderate on wording. No quotation formula, no Triad owed.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 1:8, Naomi’s wish that YHWH deal in חֶסֶד with her daughters-in-law, now said of YHWH himself [T], high; 1:21, “the LORD has testified against me”, now “blessed be the LORD” from the same mouth [I], moderate. Planted: גֹּאֵל (2:20) — answered through chapters 3 and 4, and climactically at 4:14 where the women bless YHWH for not leaving Naomi without one [T], high; קָרוֹב (“near”, 2:20) — answered at 3:12 by a nearer one [T], high.

Translations. 2:20. NASB95 “May he be blessed of the LORD who has not withdrawn his kindness”; ESV “May he be blessed by the LORD, whose kindness has not forsaken”. The Hebrew leaves the subject of the relative clause genuinely open — the kindness may be Boaz’s or YHWH’s — and the ESV’s construction closes it toward YHWH while the NASB95’s “his” leaves it ambiguous. The congregation hearing the ESV will not know the question exists. Moderate confidence that the ambiguity is deliberate; it is a good candidate for the solo dig. Elsewhere no divergence bearing on a finding.

Difficulty. None live. The unit’s one trap is reading Naomi’s turn as a conversion; the text records a blessing, not a change of mind about 1:21 [T].

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s Gen 24:27 row, its חֶסֶד chain and its Lev 25 row, all at the same confidences. No tension.

Unit 6 — Ruth 3:1–9 · At the threshing floor ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH

Position. Question 1: 2:20 has supplied the category “redeemer” and 2:23 has run the harvest to its end, which closes the gleaning solution — the grain is finished and the women are still destitute [T]. Question 2: the unit exists here because the lawful provision has expired and nothing in the law obliges anyone to do more, so the next move must be asked for rather than claimed [I]. That is precisely what the unit is: a request, not an entitlement.

Structure. Instruction and execution, matched clause for clause. Naomi gives five imperatives (vv.2–4: wash, anoint, dress, go down, uncover-and-lie); Ruth performs them (vv.6–7) and then adds a sixth thing Naomi never told her to do — she speaks (v.9) [T]. The structural point is the gap: Naomi said “he will tell you what to do”; Ruth tells him [I]. Moderate–high confidence — the departure from the instructions is visible in the text, the motive is not.

Text-first findings.

  1. “Lie down” is the unit’s verb, and the chapter owns it. שָׁכַב (“lie down”) occurs eight times in Ruth, every one in chapter 3 — 3:4 (three times), 3:7 (twice), 3:8, 3:13, 3:14 (WLC, by lemma) [T]. High confidence. The narrator is not being coy; he is being insistent, and the insistence is what makes the scene’s restraint legible [I].
  2. The washing-and-anointing set is used in two other places, and both are loaded. רָחַץ (“wash”) with סוּךְ (“anoint”) stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: 2 Sam 12:20; Ezek 16:9; Ruth 3:3 (WLC) [T]. Add the garment and the set reduces to two: 2 Sam 12:20 and Ruth 3:3 [T]. At 2 Sam 12:20 David washes and anoints to end mourning; at Ezek 16:8–9 YHWH spreads his כָּנָף over Jerusalem and then washes and anoints her — and that whole set, including the spread wing, stands only in Ezek 16:8–9 [T]. Moderate confidence on Ezekiel as a live source: the cluster is tight and thematically exact, but the direction of dependence cannot be shown and Ruth’s date is contested. The overview caps this at moderate and that cap should hold. Moderate on 2 Sam 12:20 as the end-of-mourning signal, which is the more useful reading for the preacher: Naomi is dressing Ruth to stop being a widow [I]. (v1.3 — claim audit, R30.) Ezekiel 16 as a register or source is discarded; the individual contacts stand. Against the whole of Ruth, Ezekiel 16 ranks 291st of 859 chapters at K=5 and 349th at K=10 — near chance for a chapter drawn at random, and below where some thirty Ezekiel chapters sit; Ezekiel 4 ranks 29th and Ezekiel 8 80th. At 63 verses Ezekiel 16 has more opportunities for a rare pair than almost any rival and still yields one of Ezekiel’s lowest rates. What the three contacts share is subject matter — a woman, a birth-land, washing and anointing, a garment spread in marriage — which is where that vocabulary lives. So keep 16:8 ~ 3:9 and 16:9 ~ 3:3 as rare wording contacts at their existing ratings, and drop the register.
  3. Ruth’s request quotes Boaz’s blessing back to him. 2:12 “under whose כָּנָף you have come to take refuge”; 3:9 “spread your כָּנָף over your maid.” Two occurrences in the book, and they are the only two [T], verified by lemma. High confidence on the chain. The word means both “wing” and “the skirt of a garment”, so the request is at once a marriage proposal and a claim on the protection Boaz had assigned to YHWH [I], moderate–high. Note that פָּרַשׂ (“spread”) with כָּנָף stands in twelve verses [T] — idiom, so the link rests on the shared noun and the context, not on the phrase.
  4. “Uncover” ties the floor to the gate, and no English reader can see it. גָּלָה (“uncover”) occurs three times in Ruth: 3:4, 3:7 — Ruth uncovers Boaz’s feet — and 4:4, where Boaz says אֶגְלֶה אָזְנְךָ, “I will uncover your ear” [T], verified by lemma. Both English versions render the third as “tell” or “inform”, so the verbal link between the night scene and the legal scene is invisible in English. High confidence on the chain; moderate that it is designed rather than idiomatic — “uncover the ear” is itself a set phrase (see unit 8).
  5. She calls herself by a different word than before. שִׁפְחָה (“maidservant”) twice at 2:13; אָמָה (“maid”) twice at 3:9 [T]. The change is real and the direction of it is not demonstrable from Ruth alone; whether אָמָה ranks above שִׁפְחָה is a lexicon question. Low–moderate confidence, and a solo-dig item.

Tool 11 — allusion. Ezek 16:8–9 → 3:3, 3:9: shared wash/anoint/spread/wing, the full set in one place only; moderate, direction unshown. 2 Sam 12:20 → 3:3: shared wash/anoint/garment, two verses; moderate. Deut 25:5–10 → the whole scene conceptually, through 3:9’s “you are a redeemer”; the sister-in-law term with rise and name stands in one verse, Deut 25:7 [T], but that term is not used here — it was used at 1:15 of Orpah. Moderate, and the mismatch is itself the finding (unit 8). No quotation; no Triad owed.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 2:12’s wings (3:9) [T], high; 1:9’s “rest” by way of 3:1’s cognate “security” [T], high on the chain, moderate that the cognate is deliberate; 2:20’s “near” redeemer, about to be complicated at 3:12 [T]. Planted: “uncover” (answered 4:4) [T], high; the six measures (3:15) and “empty-handed” (3:17), which answer 1:21 — carried in unit 7 [T].

Translations. 3:9 is the sweep’s single most consequential divergence. NASB95 “spread your covering over your maid”; ESV “Spread your wings over your servant”. The Hebrew is the same word as 2:12. The ESV congregation hears Ruth asking Boaz to be the wings Boaz invoked; the NASB95 reader hears a blanket. This is the third place in the book where the pulpit text is more transparent than the study text, and on a chain the whole plot turns on. Also 3:1: NASB95 “security”, ESV “rest” — see unit 2. Ancient versions check: the moderns agree at 3:9 on a garment-sense for כָּנָף, and Rahlfs has τὸ πτερύγιόν σου, “your skirt/wing”, preserving the same double sense — no split, one line.

Difficulty. The unit is the book’s pastoral landmine. The text is deliberately ambiguous about nothing except what it leaves unsaid: it says Boaz ate and drank and his heart was merry (3:7) [T], it says Ruth lay at his feet (3:7, 14) [T], and it says he blessed her for חֶסֶד in not going after young men (3:10) [T] — which is the narrator’s own man passing a verdict of chastity on the night. A preacher should establish that verdict before describing the scene, not after.

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s row on Ezek 16:8–9 at moderate and its refusal to go higher. Extends it: the גָּלָה chain from 3:4 and 3:7 to 4:4 is not in the overview’s echo table and should be added — it is one of the book’s quiet links, invisible in every English version checked.

Unit 7 — Ruth 3:10–18 · The offer and the wait

Overview weighting: Standard

Position. Question 1: Ruth’s request at 3:9 demands an answer, and the answer has to be legal rather than merely willing — she has invoked a status, not asked a favour [T]. Question 2: the unit exists here to introduce the obstacle. Had Boaz simply said yes, the book would end at chapter 3 with a marriage and no redemption of the field; the nearer redeemer exists so that the cost can be made public at the gate [I].

Structure. Blessing (v.10), promise (v.11), complication (v.12), oath (v.13), dawn and discretion (v.14), measure and sending (v.15), report and wait (vv.16–18). The pivot is וְעַתָּה (“and now”) at vv.11 and 12 — twice, the second introducing the obstacle [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. Boaz names Ruth with Proverbs’ phrase — and the relation is with the acrostic, not with the book. 3:11, אֵשֶׁת חַיִל (“a woman of excellence”), an ordered phrase standing in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, plain and skeletal: Prov 12:4; 31:10; Ruth 3:11 (WLC) [T]. High confidence on the phrase. (v1.3 — restated after claim audit, R2.) The relation has now been baselined, and it is stronger than the draft claimed and in a different place. With the acrostic Prov 31:10–31 as target, Ruth 3 ranks first of 832 chapters on rare shared vocabulary at K=5 and first at K=10, while Ruth 1, 2 and 4 score zero at K=5; against eight other Proverbs blocks of comparable size Ruth 3 scores zero rare pairs against six and one against the other two. At book level Proverbs is unremarkable — its best chapter ranks 76th. The relation is therefore Prov 31:10–31 ↔︎ Ruth 3, at moderate–high, carried by four or five rare pairs, of which the sharpest is new: יָדַע (“know”) with שַׁעַר (“gate”) in one verse stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible — Josh 2:5, Amos 5:12, Prov 31:23 (“her husband is known in the gates”) and Ruth 3:11 (“all the gate of my people know”) [T]. The two books also share עָשָׂה חַיִל (“do valiantly”), six verses, at Prov 31:29 and Ruth 4:11. What remains untested is the canonical order, which the corpus cannot reach at all: the adjacency assumes BHS’s sequence of the Ketuvim, while Baba Batra 14b places Ruth first among the Writings. The adjacency argument stays uncertain until Logos L1 answers which ancient sequences put Ruth where, and it may not be preached before then.
  2. “All the gate of my people” — and the gate is where chapter 4 happens. 3:11 reads כָּל־שַׁעַר עַמִּי, literally “all the gate of my people” [T]. שַׁעַר (“gate”) occurs four times in Ruth: 3:11, 4:1, 4:10, 4:11 [T], verified by lemma. High confidence. The first use is here, in Boaz’s bedroom-floor promise, and it names the venue of the public scene before the reader knows there will be one [I], moderate.
  3. The oath is by YHWH, and it is the book’s only one. חַי־יְהוָה (“as the LORD lives”, 3:13) [T]. Boaz binds himself under oath to a procedure he has not yet secured [I].
  4. “Empty-handed” closes the book’s sharpest reversal. 3:17, רֵיקָם — the second and last of two occurrences, answering 1:21 [T], verified by lemma. High confidence. The six measures are not a gift to Ruth; they are a message to Naomi, and Ruth reports them as such [T].
  5. Six measures of what, exactly, the text does not say. 3:15, שֵׁשׁ־שְׂעֹרִים (“six of barley”) with no unit named [T]. Both English versions supply “measures” in italics or plain. The quantity is therefore unknown; what is known is that it was carried [T]. High confidence that the unit is unstated; a solo dig should note it rather than compute it.

Tool 11 — allusion. Prov 31:10 / 12:4 → 3:11: ordered phrase, three verses; high on the phrase. Prov 31:23 → 3:11: יָדַע with שַׁעַר, four verses in the canon; moderate–high, new from the claim audit (v1.3). Prov 31:10–31 as a block → Ruth 3: first of 832 chapters on rare shared vocabulary, baselined against eight other Proverbs blocks; moderate–high for the relation, uncertain for the canonical adjacency pending Logos L1. Deut 25:5–10 → 3:12–13, conceptually, through the redeemer’s priority; moderate. No quotation; no Triad owed.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 1:21 (רֵיקָם at 3:17) [T], high; 2:1 (חַיִל at 3:11) [T], high; 2:20’s “near” (the nearer redeemer at 3:12) [T], high. Planted: שַׁעַר (3:11 → 4:1, 10, 11) [T], high; the nearer redeemer (3:12 → 4:1, where the narrator identifies him as “the redeemer of whom Boaz spoke”) [T], high.

Translations. 3:11. NASB95 “all my people in the city”; ESV “all my fellow townsmen”. Both versions delete the gate, so neither congregation hears 3:11 anticipating 4:1 — a loss the preacher has to supply by hand. The ancient-versions check fires here because the moderns agree and the rendering carries weight: Rahlfs has πᾶσα φυλὴ λαοῦ μου, “all the tribe of my people”, which looks like a Greek translator reading שֵׁבֶט (“tribe”) where BHS has שַׁעַר (“gate”) [T]. BHS does not record this at 3:11 [T]. If the Greek reflects a different consonantal text, that is a category-2 variant; if it reflects a translator smoothing an odd idiom, it is not. Uncertain — consider verifying, and flagged to Open Questions rather than resolved.

Difficulty. 3:10’s “you have not gone after young men” can be preached as a compliment on Ruth’s sexual restraint, which is half of it; the clause is governed by חֶסֶד, and the kindness is to the dead man’s name, not to Boaz’s feelings [T].

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s 3:12 → 4:1 row and its חַיִל row. Extends it: the four-fold שַׁעַר chain beginning at 3:11 is not in the overview’s echo table, and the overview’s Proverbs row should record that both English versions remove the gate word.

Unit 8 — Ruth 4:1–12 · At the gate ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH

Position. Question 1: 3:12–13 has named a nearer redeemer and put Boaz under oath, and 3:18 has left Naomi waiting on a man who “will not rest until he has settled it today” [T]. Question 2: the unit exists here because the book’s problem has been private throughout — a field, a widow, a night — and redemption in Israel is a public, legal act. The scene exists to make the cost witnessed [I]. The word “witnesses” occurs three times in it, at 4:9, 4:10 and 4:11 [T].

Structure. A legal transaction in five beats: convening (vv.1–2), the offer of the field (vv.3–4), the raised price (v.5), the refusal (v.6), the narrator’s explanatory aside on the sandal (v.7), the transfer and the proclamation (vv.8–10), the blessing (vv.11–12). The structural oddity is v.7: the narrator steps out of the scene to explain a custom belonging to “former times in Israel” [T], which is the one place in the book he dates himself relative to his story [I].

Text-first findings.

  1. “Acquire” is the unit’s verb, and the book’s last keyword. קָנָה (“buy, acquire”) occurs six times in Ruth, all in this chapter — 4:4, 4:5 (twice), 4:8, 4:9, 4:10 (WLC, by lemma) [T]. High confidence. The same verb governs the field and the woman, which is the unit’s hardest fact and the reason the scene is uncomfortable [T].
  2. The purpose clause stands twice, word for word, and nowhere else. לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת עַל־נַחֲלָתוֹ (“to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance”) is an ordered phrase matching, plain and skeletal, at Ruth 4:5 and 4:10 only (WLC) [T]. High confidence. It is the stated purpose of both the challenge and the acquisition, and it brackets the whole transaction [I].
  3. Ruth’s case is not the law’s case, and the sandal proves it. Rise, name and dead stand together in Deut 25:6 and also in 2 Sam 14:7 — an equal rival [T]. But the sandal differs: Ruth uses שָׁלַף (“draw off”) at 4:7–8, and the two lemmas שָׁלַף with נַעַל (“sandal”) stand together in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, both of them Ruth 4:7 and 4:8; Deut 25:9 uses a different verb, חָלַץ [T], verified by lemma. In Deuteronomy the widow pulls the sandal off the refusing brother in contempt; in Ruth the refusing redeemer draws off his own and hands it over as attestation [T]. High confidence on the lexical difference; moderate that Deut 25 is nonetheless the frame. This is the exegetical centre of the unit: the scene combines land-redemption (Leviticus 25) with name-raising (Deuteronomy 25) and follows neither procedure exactly [I].
  4. “I will uncover your ear” is Samuel’s idiom. 4:4, אֶגְלֶה אָזְנְךָ [T]. גָּלָה with אֹזֶן stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible: 1 Sam 9:15; 20:2, 12, 13; 22:8, 17; 2 Sam 7:27; Isa 22:14; Job 33:16; 36:10, 15; Ruth 4:4; 1 Chr 17:25 (WLC) [T]. Thirteen verses makes it idiom, so it may not be rated above moderate on wording — but six of the thirteen are in 1–2 Samuel, and one of the remaining is the Chronicles parallel of 2 Sam 7:27, the Davidic-covenant prayer [T]. For a book that ends on David’s name, the distribution is worth the preacher’s notice [I], moderate.
  5. The elders’ blessing names three women and two towns, and every one carries freight. 4:11–12: Rachel, Leah, Ephrathah, Bethlehem, Tamar, Judah, Perez [T]. Rachel and Leah with בָּנָה (“build”) stand together in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Ruth 4:11 [T]. Bethlehem with Ephrath(ah) stands in five: Gen 35:19; 48:7; Mic 5:2 [Heb 5:1]; Ruth 4:11; 1 Chr 4:4 [T] — and two of those five are Rachel’s burial-place notices, so the blessing invokes the matriarch who died in childbirth at the very place [I], moderate–high. Perez and Tamar are named outright at 4:12, which makes Genesis 38 the one earlier narrative the book cites by name [T], high.

Tool 11 — allusion. This unit carries the book’s only named citation, so it receives the full Triad.

Genesis 38 → Ruth 4:12 (high confidence — named, not echoed)

Source context: Genesis 38 interrupts the Joseph narrative to tell how Judah withheld his son from Tamar, how Tamar secured offspring by disguising herself and intercepting him on a road, and how Judah pronounced her “more righteous than I” (38:26). Perez is the elder of the resulting twins (38:29). The chapter is a story of a widow denied the legal provision owed her, who obtains by stratagem what the family would not give her by right, and is vindicated — and it sits inside Judah’s line, not outside it.

Book usage: Perez is named again seven verses later at the head of the genealogy (4:18), and Perez with Hezron stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, two of them Ruth 4:18 and 1 Chr 2:5 [T]. So the book uses Genesis 38 twice in thirteen verses — once as a blessing-formula and once as the genealogy’s starting point. Genesis is the book’s most-used source (six distinct passages across the sweep) and this is its most explicit use [T].

OT-to-OT: Genesis 38’s own canonical conversation is with Deuteronomy 25 — the brother-in-law duty Judah withholds from Tamar is the duty Deuteronomy later legislates, and the two are read together in Jewish and Christian tradition alike [S]. The elders therefore invoke a text that already stands in relation to the law this scene is partly applying, which is why the citation does the work it does.

What it adds: The elders are not paying Ruth a compliment about fertility. They are blessing a union that stands in the same category as Judah and Tamar’s — irregular, legally contested, procured by a woman’s initiative, and productive of the line that runs to David [I]. It tells the reader that the book knows exactly how unusual its own plot is, and that the canon has a precedent for it. High confidence that Gen 38 is invoked; moderate on the thematic parallel’s extent.

Other rows, one line each: Lev 25:25 → 4:3–6, shared sold/redeem in seven verses; high on vocabulary. Deut 25:5–10 → 4:5–10, see finding 3; moderate. Gen 35:19 / 48:7 → 4:11, five-verse rarity on Bethlehem–Ephrathah; moderate–high. 1 Sam 17:12 → flagged forward: Ephrathite with Bethlehem and Judah stands in two verses, 1 Sam 17:12 and Ruth 1:2 [T]; moderate–high, and it is the handoff to David’s story.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 3:12’s nearer redeemer, identified at 4:1 as “the redeemer of whom Boaz spoke” [T], high; 3:11’s gate, now the venue [T], high; 3:4 and 3:7’s “uncover”, now of an ear [T], high; 1:2’s Mahlon and Chilion, with 4:10 finally stating that Ruth was Mahlon’s wife [T], high; 1:22, 2:6’s “returned from the field of Moab”, repeated of Naomi at 4:3 [T], high. Planted: שֵׁם (“name”) — seven of the book’s fourteen occurrences are in chapter 4, and 4:11’s “call a name in Bethlehem” is answered at 4:17 when the women name the child [T], high.

Translations. 4:11. NASB95 “become famous in Bethlehem”; ESV “be renowned in Bethlehem”. The Hebrew is וּקְרָא־שֵׁם, “and call a name” — the chapter’s keyword, which both versions dissolve into fame. Six verses later the women “call his name Obed”, and neither congregation hears the connection. 4:10–11. NASB95 renders שַׁעַר as “court” at both, and “in the city” at 3:11; ESV has “gate” at 4:1, 4:10 and 4:11. The ESV preserves three of the four gate references, the NASB95 none — the fourth and most interesting is lost in both. Ancient versions check: the moderns agree at 4:7 that the sandal was removed by the refusing party, and Rahlfs agrees (ὑπελύετο, “he drew off his own”) — no split.

Difficulty. 4:5 and 4:10 use a commercial verb of a woman, and a modern congregation will hear it. The honest exegesis: the verb is the same one used of the field, the Hebrew does not soften it, and the text’s own frame is that an obligation to a dead man is being discharged at a living man’s expense [T]. The preacher should name the discomfort rather than smooth it; smoothing it loses the costliness on which the whole redeemer typology depends. Note also the ketiv at 4:5 (below).

Textual variants. 4:5 — BHS prints the ketiv קָנִיתִי (“I have acquired”) with the qere קָנִיתָה (“you have acquired”); Rahlfs reads the second person (αὐτὴν κτήσασθαί σε δεῖ, “you must acquire her”), as do both English versions, and the apparatus proposes the imperative קְנֵה [T]. The reading decides whether Boaz is telling the nearer redeemer that he must take Ruth, or announcing that Boaz himself has already acquired her. Category 2 of the triage — a genuine variant inside the Hebrew tradition, with versional support for the qere. Moderate confidence that the qere is right, on the strength of the versions and of 4:6’s reply, which only makes sense if a demand has been made of the redeemer. The preacher who follows either English version is following the qere, and should know there is a question.

Book-overview note. Confirms every overview row touching this unit, including the sandal distinction and the two-verse purpose clause. Extends it: the “uncover the ear” idiom and its Samuel concentration is not in the overview, and bears on the overview’s own claim that the book hands off to David.

Unit 9 — Ruth 4:13–17 · A son born to Naomi ⭐ HIGH

Overview weighting: ⭐ HIGH

Position. Question 1: everything from 1:5 onward has been generated by an absence of sons, and 4:10 has stated the transaction’s purpose as raising a name [T]. Question 2: the unit exists here because the legal act of unit 8 does not by itself produce a child — the gate can transfer a field and a widow, and it cannot give conception. So the one divine act the narrator reports in his own voice falls here, immediately after the law has done all it can [T]. Position is the theology: 4:13 is the hinge of the book.

Structure. Four moves and a renaming. Marriage and conception (v.13), the women’s blessing of YHWH (v.14), the women’s blessing of Ruth (v.15), Naomi and the child (v.16), the naming (v.17). The women of Bethlehem bracket the book: they asked “Is this Naomi?” at 1:19 and they name her grandson at 4:17 [T].

Text-first findings.

  1. The narrator attributes one act to God in his own voice, and it is this one. וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן (“and the LORD gave her conception”, 4:13) [T]. Everywhere else in the book YHWH is spoken of by characters — in blessings, wishes and Naomi’s complaint [T]. At 1:6 the visitation of YHWH is what Naomi heard; here it is what the narrator says [T]. High confidence, and this is the book’s theological spine [I].
  2. “A restorer of life” is a Writings phrase, and it stands in three verses. The hiphil participle of שׁוּב (“restore”) with נֶפֶשׁ (“life, soul”) in one verse stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Ps 19:7 [Heb 19:8], Lam 1:16, and Ruth 4:15 (WLC) [T]. In the psalm the Torah restores the soul; in Lamentations there is no one to restore it (“far from me is a comforter, a restorer of my soul”); in Ruth a child is one [T]. All three are Ketuvim, which is exactly the canonical-neighbour register the clustering rule predicts for a Writings book [S]. New to this sweep. High confidence on the collocation, measured; moderate on Lamentations as a deliberate counterpart, since direction cannot be shown.
  3. The keyword returns at the end, in the women’s mouths. שׁוּב (“return”), which ran twelve times through chapter 1, appears for the last time at 4:15 as מֵשִׁיב (“restorer”) [T], verified by lemma. The verb that described Naomi’s bitter journey home ends up describing the child who gives her life back [I], moderate–high.
  4. “Better than seven sons” shares common vocabulary with Hannah’s song, and nothing more. 4:15, מִשִּׁבְעָה בָּנִים [T]. “Seven” with “bear” stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, most of them genealogies, and the thematic neighbours are 1 Sam 2:5 (“the barren has borne seven”), Jer 15:9 and Job 1:2 (WLC) [T]. Thirteen verses is idiom, so this may not be rated above moderate, and the conceptual link to Hannah’s song — reversal, barrenness, fullness and hunger — rests on theme rather than wording: Ruth’s “full/empty” pair uses different words from Hannah’s “sated/hungry” [T]. Moderate–low confidence. A preacher may use it as resonance; a report may not call it an allusion. (v1.3 — claim audit, R26.) The rival the draft never tested is closer: שִׁבְעָה בָנִים (“seven sons”) as an ordered phrase returns Job 1:2 alone on the plain pass (Psalm 17:14 appears only on the skeletal pass and was read and rejected as a spelling false positive), while “seven” with “sons” spans 73 verses [T]. So Job is nearer on wording than Hannah’s song is, and the Hannah link at 4:15 is thematic only — low–moderate. What does hold verbally for Hannah is elsewhere: שֶׁבַע (“seven”) with עֲקָרָה (“barren”) in one verse stands at 1 Sam 2:5 alone in the Hebrew Bible, and a contact new to the audit sits at 2:13 — חֵן (“favour”) and מָצָא (“find”) with שִׁפְחָה (“maidservant”) in one verse stand in three verses: Gen 32:6 [Eng 32:5], 1 Sam 1:18 (Hannah to Eli) and Ruth 2:13 [T], with the same speech situation of a woman deferring to a man of standing. 1 Samuel 1 ranks 8th of 859 chapters against Ruth 2, beating nine of ten arbitrary nulls; 1 Samuel 2 ranks 224th and fails its null outright. Report the relation as 1 Samuel 1 contacts within a general Samuel affinity, at moderate — not as a Hannah–Ruth register.
  5. The child is Naomi’s, by the narrator’s arrangement and the women’s words. 4:16 Naomi takes הַיֶּלֶד (“the child”) — the second and last of two occurrences of the noun, answering 1:5 [T], verified by lemma; 4:17 the women say יֻלַּד־בֵּן לְנָעֳמִי (“a son has been born to Naomi”) [T]. Ruth is not named in vv.16–17 [T]. High confidence. The book that bears Ruth’s name ends its story by giving her son to Naomi, and the preacher should notice that it is the women of the town who say so, not the narrator [I].

Tool 11 — allusion. Ps 19:7 / Lam 1:16 → 4:15: three-verse collocation; high on wording, moderate on Lamentations as counterpart. 1 Sam 2:5 → 4:15: shared vocabulary common, conceptual only; moderate–low. Gen 29–30 / 35 → 4:13 conceptually, through YHWH opening a barren womb; Rachel and Leah having been named at 4:11, the echo is live by adjacency; moderate. No quotation, so no Triad owed; the Lamentations row is the one a solo dig should take further.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 1:5’s loss of her children (4:16, the only other use of the noun) [T], high; 1:11–12’s “have I yet sons?” (4:15, “better to you than seven sons”) [T], high; 1:19’s “Is this Naomi?” from the women of the town, now answered by the same women naming her grandson [I], moderate; 2:20’s “has not withdrawn his kindness” and 4:14’s “has not left you without a redeemer”, both blessings of YHWH in the negative, by two different sets of speakers [T], moderate–high; 1:20–21’s complaint against שַׁדַּי, never retracted by Naomi and never answered in words [T], high — the book answers it with a child, not an argument [I]. Planted: David’s name (4:17) → the genealogy [T].

Translations. No divergence between NASB95 and ESV bearing on a finding in this unit; both render 4:15’s “restorer of life” transparently, which is fortunate, since the phrase is the unit’s best lexical finding. Ancient versions check: fires at 4:14, where the moderns agree on “his name” and carry exegetical weight. See Textual Variants.

Textual variants. 4:14 — BHS has שְׁמוֹ (“his name”); Rahlfs has τὸ ὄνομά σου (“your name”), and the apparatus records the Greek as reflecting שְׁמֵךְ [T]. A one-consonant difference that changes the referent: the Hebrew blesses the child’s or the redeemer’s renown, the Greek blesses Naomi’s. Category 2 — a genuine variant with versional support. Moderate confidence that the Hebrew is right, on the strength of 4:17’s naming of the child and of the harder reading; but the Greek is intelligible and makes the women’s blessing land on the woman they are addressing. Worth a footnote, not a sermon point.

Book-overview note. Confirms the overview’s rows on 1:5 ↔︎ 4:16, on the sons, and on the women’s blessing. Extends it in two places: the three-verse “restorer of life” collocation adds Lamentations and Psalm 19 to the intertextual map as Ketuvim neighbours, neither of which the overview has; and the overview’s Christological section should record that 4:13 is the narrator’s only first-voice divine act, which is the strongest textual warrant the book gives for reading its providence theologically.

Unit 10 — Ruth 4:18–22 · The line to David

Overview weighting: Standard (preach with 4:13–17)

Position. Question 1: 4:17 has ended the story on David’s name without explanation, and the reader who has arrived at Ruth through the Prophets knows exactly whose name that is [S]. Question 2: the genealogy exists here because the book’s claim is not that a kind woman was rewarded but that this household is the line — and a line is proved by a list, not by a story [I]. The WLC and BHS both print a paragraph marker, פ, after 4:17, so the manuscript itself treats the genealogy as a distinct unit [T]; this is a feature of the Leningrad manuscript as those editions print it, a reading tradition rather than authorial, and its spread across manuscripts has not been checked here [unchecked — apparatus spread].

Structure. A heading followed by nine begettings in a fixed formula: X הוֹלִיד (“fathered”) Y [T]. יָלַד occurs thirteen times in chapter 4, nine of them the genealogy’s formula [T], verified by lemma.

Text-first findings.

  1. Ruth 4:18 uses the Pentateuch’s toledot formula, and in Ruth’s spelling the heading is a canon-wide hapax. (v1.2 — restated after claim audit 1, which showed the original wording, “the only book outside the Torah to do so”, to be too strong: 1 Chr 1:29 אֵלֶּה תֹּלְדוֹתָם, “these are their generations”, is a heading outside the Torah, with eight further occurrences of the noun in 1 Chronicles. What is unique to Ruth is the combination of וְאֵלֶּה with the fully plene noun and a named ancestor. The audit also named Gen 11:27 and Gen 25:19 as the specific models, since they alone continue “name, same name, fathered” as Ruth 4:18 does.) וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת (“and these are the generations of”) matches as an ordered phrase at Ruth 4:18 in the plain pass; the skeletal pass adds Gen 2:4; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10; 11:27; 25:12; 25:19; 36:1; 36:9; 37:2 and Num 3:1 (WLC) [T]. The pass difference is spelling, not wording: Genesis spells תּוֹלְדֹת defectively and Ruth spells it תּוֹלְדוֹת plene [T]. The noun itself occurs thirteen times in Genesis, thirteen in Numbers, three in Exodus, nine in 1 Chronicles — and once in the rest of the Hebrew Bible, here [T]. High confidence, measured. The row must say the same formula, differently spelled — not “verbatim”. This is the sweep’s strongest single piece of evidence for the overview’s proposal that Genesis is Ruth’s live source, and the overview did not have it.
  2. Ten names, with Boaz seventh and David tenth. Perez, Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmah, Boaz, Obed, Jesse, David [T], counted from the index. High confidence on the count and the positions. That the arrangement is designed is [I], unbaselined, capped at moderate, and remains the claim audit’s first target — as is the overview’s wider “ten” pattern (ten years at 1:4, ten elders at 4:2). (v1.3 — claim audit, R1: the design is withdrawn.) Every numeral in the book was counted by lemma: שְׁנַיִם (“two”) 9 occurrences, אֶחָד (“one”) 2, עֶשֶׂר (“ten”) 2, שֵׁשׁ (“six”) 2, שֶׁבַע (“seven”) 1. Ten is not the book’s number — it ties with six and stands one above seven, against nine of two — and the text never says “ten” of the genealogy, so that leg is a count the reader supplies. Boaz’s seventh position carries no formal marking: the list runs nine identical הוֹלִיד (“fathered”) formulas with no expansion anywhere, unlike Genesis 5, where the seventh from Adam is marked. The one formal irregularity falls at the sixth name — שַׂלְמָה at 4:20 against שַׂלְמוֹן at 4:21. The count and the positions stand [T]; the design does not, and may not be preached as a pattern.
  3. The list agrees with Chronicles, and the direction of dependence is open. Perez with Hezron stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, two of them Ruth 4:18 and 1 Chr 2:5, and the BHS apparatus itself cross-refers 1 Chr 2:5, 9–15 at Ruth 4:18 [T]. Which text draws on which is not settled and is not settled here [I]. High confidence that the lists agree; direction undetermined, and a report should leave it so.
  4. The same ancestor is spelled two ways in two consecutive verses. שַׂלְמָה at 4:20 and שַׂלְמוֹן at 4:21 [T] — and the WLC’s index files them as two different entries, so this is not merely orthographic. BHS records manuscripts with שלמה at 4:21 and Σαλμαν in Codex B [T]; Rahlfs prints Σαλμαν at both. High confidence on the data. Both English versions level it to “Salmon” twice, so no congregation will hear it [T].
  5. A genealogy of ten that names no woman. Tamar, Rachel, Leah and Ruth are named in the blessing seven verses earlier (4:11–12) and none of them appears in the list [T]. This is a pattern-break absence of the admissible kind: the pattern is the book’s own, established in the immediately preceding unit, where the elders ground the house’s future on three named women [T]. Moderate confidence on the observation; the reading — that the genealogy is deliberately formal where the blessing was personal — is [I] and no higher than moderate.

Tool 11 — allusion. The Genesis toledot formula → 4:18: see finding 1; high on the formula, and the strongest external link in the book after the named citation of Genesis 38. Gen 46:12 / Num 26:21 / 1 Chr 2:5–15 → 4:18–22: shared names, five-verse rarity on Perez–Hezron; high that the lists agree. 1 Sam 17:12 → via 1:2: Ephrathite with Bethlehem and Judah in two verses; moderate–high as the handoff. No quotation formula; the toledot heading is a formula, not a citation, so Move 1 treatment suffices and is given in finding 1.

Internal (Move 4). Answers: 4:12’s “house of Perez”, now the list’s head [T], high; 4:11’s “call a name in Bethlehem”, now a line of names [T], moderate; 1:1’s “in the days when the judges governed” — a period answered by a king [I], moderate; 4:17’s David, repeated as the last word of the book [T], high. Planted: nothing — the book ends here.

Translations. No divergence bearing on a finding, beyond the levelling of Salmah/Salmon noted above, which both versions share. ESV “fathered” and NASB95 “to X was born” render the same causative; the ESV is closer to the active Hebrew [T].

Difficulty. The genealogy is routinely skipped in preaching, which is the pitfall below. The one real question a hearer may raise is why Ruth is absent from a list her own book exists to explain; finding 5 is the honest answer available from the text, and it should be given as an observation, not a grievance.

Book-overview note. Extends the overview materially. The toledot formula is the hardest evidence the book offers for Genesis as a live source, and the overview’s intertextual map does not record it. The overview’s “Genesis as a book” caveat — six passages touched, none used more than twice, so the live-source claim is a proposal — should now be revised: with the toledot heading added, Genesis is used by name (4:12, 4:18), by formula (4:18) and by matriarchal blessing (4:11), which is a materially stronger case than the overview states. It remains short of a baselined design claim.

Cross-Passage Convergent Findings

What only becomes visible when the ten units are read in sequence. Every distribution below was counted by lemma in the WLC; every reading built on one is capped as marked.

1. The movement verbs are each concentrated in one chapter — but so is the scene-furniture, and the design reading has been withdrawn (v1.1). The distribution stands, and is measured: שָׁוב (“return”) 15 times with 12 in chapter 1; לָקַט (“glean”) 12 times, all in chapter 2; שָׂכַב (“lie down”) 8 times, all in chapter 3; קָנָה (“acquire”) 6 times, all in chapter 4; גָּאַל (“redeem”) 21 times, none before 2:20 and 20 of them in chapters 3–4 [T]. High confidence on those counts. But the chance baseline this report asked for has now been run (claim-audit queue R3, 13 November 2026), and it does not support the design reading. Of the 86 lemmas occurring four or more times in Ruth, nine — one in ten — are wholly confined to a single chapter, and most are ordinary scene-furniture: קָצַר (“reap”) seven times in chapter 2, נַעַר (“young man”) six times in chapter 2, עַיִן (“eye”) four times in chapter 2, מַרְגְּלֹת (“the place of the feet”) four times in chapter 3, זָקֵן (“elder”) four times in chapter 4, גֹּרֶן (“threshing floor”) four times in chapter 3 [T]. Single-chapter confinement is what a narrative with distinct settings produces: when the scene moves from field to floor to gate, its furniture moves with it. (שָׁוב is 80 % rather than 100 % in chapter 1, and גָּאַל 62 % in chapter 4, so neither is in the confined set at all.) The verb-per-movement reading is withdrawn, not merely capped. What survives is the distribution, and the observation that this book’s settings are unusually discrete.

2. The book’s reversals are built as two-point chains from chapter 1 to the end, not as a general arc. רֵיקָם (“empty-handed”) at 1:21 and 3:17, the only two uses; יֶלֶד (“child”) at 1:5 and 4:16, the only two uses; “sons” at 1:11–12 answered at 4:15; כָּנָף (“wing”) at 2:12 and 3:9, the only two uses [T]. High confidence on all four. The pattern is that Ruth does its reversing with single matched pairs rather than repeated refrains, which is why the book reads as spare rather than liturgical [I], moderate.

3. “Mother-in-law” is Ruth’s word, and its one other canonical use is the bond’s collapse. חָמוֹת (“mother-in-law”) occurs eleven times in the Hebrew Bible: ten of them in Ruth, across nine verses, and one at Micah 7:6 — “a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law” — which also carries כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”), Ruth’s other kinship keyword (WLC) [T]. High confidence, measured canon-wide. Micah 7:6 is the one verse in Scripture that uses Ruth’s vocabulary for the one relationship Ruth exists to display, and it uses it of that relationship breaking [I], moderate–high. This also explains, properly, why the overview’s density scan ranked Micah 7 fifth against the whole book: not noise, but the canon’s only lexical neighbour. (v1.4 — Logos round 1, L9: the count is independently confirmed and the reading has a pedigree.) TWOT gives the same eleven occurrences and notes that Micah 7:6 “decries the deep moral corruption evidenced in the breakdown of societal bonds, especially the bond between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law” (Coppes, TWOT, 295–296) [S]. Smith-Christopher adds a striking methodological point: the translation of חָמוֹת at Micah 7:6 “is based entirely on the use of the same Hebrew term in Ruth” (Old Testament Library, 213) [S]. And the counter-image reading is not ours — Lange already set Ruth’s household against Micah’s, noting that the unhappy mother-in-law relation “elsewhere usual, must in general have been unknown in Israel”, since otherwise the prophet could not use its reversal as the sign of extreme social ruin (Commentary on the Holy Scriptures: Ruth, 18) [S]. (v1.3 — claim audit, R7: strengthened.) חָמוֹת (“mother-in-law”) and כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”) share no verse in Ruth — zero [T], so Micah 7:6 is the only verse in the canon that pairs the two words on which the whole book is built. The inversion reading is firmer than the draft rated it, not weaker.

4. Two strong Torah contacts stand at the book’s opening and close — but “frame” has been withdrawn (v1.1). Both contacts hold: 1:6 carries the exodus visitation formula — שָׁמַע (“hear”) with פָּקַד (“visit”) and יְהוָה, two verses in the canon, Exod 4:31 and Ruth 1:6; and 4:18 carries the Pentateuch’s toledot heading וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת (“and these are the generations of”), which outside the Torah stands only here [T]. High confidence on both collocations, and each is queued for audit on its own merits (R5, R6). The frame reading is not supported. Mapping every verified Torah contact in the book (claim-audit queue R4, 13 November 2026) gives thirteen, spread throughout and densest in chapter 4: 1:6; 1:14 with 2:11; 2:3; 2:11; 2:20 twice over, Genesis and Leviticus; 3:12; 4:3; 4:5 and 4:10; 4:7–8; 4:11; 4:12; 4:18 [T]. A frame requires its ends to be marked; here they are the first and last of a continuous series, and nothing distinguishes them from the eleven between.

5. YHWH acts in the narrator’s own voice exactly once in eighty-five verses. Everywhere else he is in characters’ speech — blessings, wishes, oaths, Naomi’s complaint — and at 1:6 his visitation is reported as something Naomi heard. At 4:13 the narrator says it himself: “the LORD gave her conception” [T]. High confidence, and this is the single most useful structural fact in the book for a preacher: the providence everyone talks about is asserted by the author once, at the point where the law has done all it can [I], moderate–high.

6. חֶסֶד is never the narrator’s word. Three occurrences — 1:8, 2:20, 3:10 — all in characters’ speech [T]. High confidence. The book’s central term is confessed by its people rather than asserted by its author, which is the same reticence as finding 5 [I].

7. The gate is named before it is a place, and the venue is announced from a threshing floor. שַׁעַר at 3:11, then 4:1, 4:10, 4:11 [T]. High confidence on the chain; moderate that 3:11 is anticipatory rather than idiomatic.

8. Naomi carries a standing label, repeated four times. “Returned from the field(s) of Moab”: return, field and Moab stand together in four verses of the book — 1:6, 1:22, 2:6, 4:3 [T]. High confidence. It is how the narrator, a servant and Boaz each identify her, and it keeps Moab attached to the household right up to the purchase [I].

9. The two English versions diverge from each other at five load-bearing points, and at every one the pulpit text is the more transparent. חַיִל (2:1, 3:11, 4:11), מְנוּחָה/מָנוֹחַ (1:9, 3:1), כָּנָף (2:12, 3:9), מָרַר (1:13, 1:20), שַׁעַר (3:11, 4:1, 4:10, 4:11) [T]. The ESV preserves the chain or its cognate at all five; the NASB95 breaks all five. High confidence — this is a comparison of printed texts, checked verse by verse. This is the sweep’s most immediately useful practical finding, and it inverts the usual expectation that the wooden study text will be the transparent one. Where this book is concerned, the preacher should build on the pulpit text and use the study text for the places the ESV itself closes down (2:20, 3:11).

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: 1:16–17 preached as a marriage vow

  • What it looks like: “Whither thou goest” read at a wedding, or applied to any strong commitment.
  • Why it’s wrong: The speech is addressed to a mother-in-law who has just twice commanded Ruth to leave (1:8, 11, 12, 15); it is a refusal, not a pledge; and it ends in a self-maledictory oath sworn by YHWH [T]. The one thing it is not is a bride’s promise to a husband.
  • The corrective: Preach it as what it is — a destitute foreigner binding herself to a bitter woman and to that woman’s God, with nothing in it for her. That is a harder and far better sermon than the wedding one.

Pitfall: chapter 3 preached as romance, or allegorised

  • What it looks like: “Waiting for your Boaz”; or the feet, the cloak and the six measures decoded as spiritual symbols.
  • Why it’s wrong: Naomi drives the scene from her emptiness (3:1), the request is legal in form (“for you are a redeemer”, 3:9), and Boaz’s own verdict is that Ruth’s action was חֶסֶד to the dead rather than attraction to him (3:10) [T]. The redeemer category carries the typology; the details do not, and allegorising them is the trap the Christological extension names.
  • The corrective: Let the scene be as risky, as restrained and as legally precise as it is. The eight occurrences of “lie down” are the narrator’s way of making the restraint visible, not of hinting at something else.

Pitfall: correcting Naomi’s theology, or endorsing it

  • What it looks like: Either “Naomi was wrong to blame God” or “the Almighty really had dealt bitterly with her, and that’s the lesson”.
  • Why it’s wrong: The narrator does neither [T]. He lets her verdict stand, and sets a barley-harvest notice immediately after it (1:22). The book answers her with a child, not with an argument — and the one thing it never does is make her retract.
  • The corrective: Preach the lament without a rebuttal, and let 4:13–17 do the answering. A sermon that resolves 1:20–21 in its own voice has pre-empted the book.

Pitfall: calling 4:1–12 “levirate marriage”

  • What it looks like: Explaining the gate scene straight out of Deuteronomy 25:5–10.
  • Why it’s wrong: Boaz is not Mahlon’s brother; the widow does not remove the sandal — the refusing redeemer draws off his own (4:7–8); the verb differs from Deuteronomy’s; and the transaction is tied to a field, which Deuteronomy 25 never mentions [T]. The scene combines land-redemption and name-raising and follows neither procedure exactly.
  • The corrective: Say that the case is unprecedented, which is why it needs ten elders, three declarations of witness and a narrator’s footnote on an obsolete custom. The irregularity is the point, and it is what the blessing’s appeal to Tamar acknowledges.

Pitfall: stopping at 4:17

  • What it looks like: Ending the series on the baby in Naomi’s lap, with the genealogy skipped as an appendix.
  • Why it’s wrong: The book’s last word is David [T], and 4:18 opens with the Torah’s own toledot heading — the formula Genesis uses ten times and no book outside the Pentateuch uses but this one [T]. The list is the claim: this household is the line.
  • The corrective: Preach 4:13–22 as one unit, and let the ten names carry the weight. A hearer who knows how the monarchy ended will hear more in that list than a hearer who is only told a kind woman was rewarded.

Pitfall: making Ruth or Boaz the hero

  • What it looks like: “Be loyal like Ruth”; “be generous like Boaz.”
  • Why it’s wrong: Both are true to the text and neither is its argument. The kindness is God’s before it is anyone’s (1:6; 2:20), and the narrator attributes exactly one act in the book to God in his own voice — the conception (4:13) [T]. A sermon built on either character’s virtue has quietly replaced the book’s subject.
  • The corrective: Boaz redeems at cost, taking on a dead man’s land and a dead man’s name with no gain to himself, and the category he acts in is the one the canon develops to Christ. Preach the redeemer, not the role model.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

Carried forward for the claim audit, the solo digs, or a Logos session.

  1. 1:21 — does עָנָה בִי mean “testified against me” or “afflicted me”? — Answered, on grammatical grounds (v1.4 — Logos round 1, L6). “Testified against me.” The qal עָנָה with the preposition בְּ is a legal idiom, “testify against”, attested at Exod 20:16, Num 35:30 and 1 Sam 12:3; the “afflicted” reading requires taking the consonants as the piel עִנָּה, and that piel never introduces its object with בְּ — where the preposition appears with it, it is adverbial (Chisholm, Kregel Exegetical Library, 611) [S], high confidence. LaCocque grants that the context suggests “afflict” but concedes that ענה ב is read “testify against” consistently elsewhere — 2 Sam 1:16; Isa 3:9; Jer 14:7; Mic 6:3; Hos 5:5; 7:10 (Continental Commentary, 58) [S]. The Septuagint and Peshitta read the piel, and the NLT follows them, its margin carrying the Masoretic legal phrase (Driesbach, Cornerstone, 519) [S]. So both English versions are right, and the point is sharper than “afflicted” would be: Naomi says God has taken the witness stand against her. Original wording of this question: Both English versions take the first; Rahlfs, and per BHS the Syriac and Vulgate, take the second [T]. Same consonants, different root, so this is not a variant — an interpretive split between the ancient and modern traditions. The parallel line (“the Almighty has brought calamity”) slightly favours the ancients [I]. Unresolved; a lexicon and a commentary would settle it.
  2. 3:11 — gate or tribe? BHS has שַׁעַר (“gate”); Rahlfs has πᾶσα φυλὴ λαοῦ μου, which looks like a Vorlage reading שֵׁבֶט (“tribe”), and BHS does not record it [T]. Either a genuine category-2 variant or a translator smoothing an unusual idiom. Uncertain — consider verifying against a critical apparatus to the Greek.
  3. 2:7b — “she sat in the house a little” or “she has not rested in the field”? BHS against a Greek Vorlage the apparatus reconstructs as לֹא שָׁבְתָה בַשָּׂדֶה [T]. Category 2; the consonants שבתה are pointable either way and only the house/field word differs materially. Uncertain, and the third of the three cruxes the earlier brief flagged for a library pass. It decides how the servant characterises Ruth.
  4. 4:5 — ketiv or qere? Whether Boaz says “you must acquire” (qere, with the versions and both English texts) or “I have acquired” (ketiv) [T]. 4:6’s reply favours the qere [I]. The solo dig on unit 8 should settle it; it changes the scene’s dramatic shape, not its outcome.
  5. 4:14 — “his name” or “your name”? BHS שְׁמוֹ against Rahlfs τὸ ὄνομά σου [T]. Category 2, versional support, moderate for the Hebrew.
  6. Is אָמָה a step up from שִׁפְחָה? Ruth uses the first of herself at 3:9 and the second at 2:13 [T]. The ranking is a lexicon question Ruth alone cannot answer. Low–moderate; flagged in the overview too.
  7. Direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15. Open, and left open [I]. The BHS apparatus cross-refers them without adjudicating [T].
  8. Ruth’s date, which the Ezekiel 16 row depends on. The wash-anoint-spread-wing cluster at Ezek 16:8–9 is the tightest external parallel to 3:3 and 3:9, and whether it is source, borrower or coincidence cannot be settled without a date [I]. The row stays at moderate either way.
  9. Does the plene spelling of תּוֹלְדוֹת at 4:18 carry any dating weight? Genesis spells it defectively, Ruth plene [T]. Whether that is a datable orthographic feature or scribal variation is outside what this corpus can show. Worth a Logos session — it bears on the composition question the overview leaves open.
  10. The size of an ephah (2:17). Reconstructed, not stated [S]. Any figure a sermon gives should be hedged.
  11. Closed by this sweep: the overview left Micah 7 as unexplained density. It is now accounted for — Micah 7:6 is the only verse outside Ruth with חָמוֹת, and it also carries כַּלָּה [T]. Recorded as closed rather than deleted, so the gate shows its working.

The earlier brief’s three claims, adjudicated

The brief asked the sweep to rule explicitly on three claims from the old v0.1.0 overview’s health note. They are worth answering even though that overview has been replaced, because two of the three survive into v1.1 in substance.

(a) “YHWH is the subject of a narrated verb only at 1:6 and 4:13.” — Confirmed with nuance, and the nuance strengthens it. Tested by taking every verse in which יְהוָה occurs — 1:6, 8, 9, 13, 17, 21; 2:4, 12, 20; 3:10, 13; 4:11, 12, 13, 14 [T] — and sorting narration from speech. Thirteen of the fifteen fall inside characters’ speech: wishes (1:8, 9), Naomi’s complaints (1:13, 21), oaths (1:17, 3:13), greetings (2:4), blessings (2:12, 20; 3:10; 4:11, 12, 14). At 1:6 the clause “that YHWH had visited his people” is the content of what Naomi heard, governed by שָׁמְעָה — reported information, not a narrated act [T]. That leaves 4:13 as the only verse in the book where YHWH is the subject of a verb in the narrator’s own voice [T]. High confidence. The claim as originally worded over-counted by one; corrected, it is sharper than it was, and it is the sweep’s fifth cross-passage finding.

(b) “2:20 is the structural centre.” — Confirmed as to its facts; the reading remains a proposal. Both supporting facts hold: גֹּאֵל enters the book at 2:20 and occurs twenty more times after it, none before [T]; and 2:20 is the first time Naomi speaks well of YHWH, having said at 1:21 that he testified against her [T]. High confidence on both. That these make 2:20 the structural centre is [I] and stays at moderate — a keyword’s first appearance and a character’s turn are not the same thing as a structural centre, and no baseline has been run.

(c) “4:18–22 is canonical anchoring rather than narrative climax.” — Confirmed, and on harder evidence than the old overview had. Both props hold and one is much stronger than claimed: the paragraph marker after 4:17 is printed by both BHS and the WLC [T] (spread across manuscripts unchecked, and tagged as such at unit 10); and the Genesis toledot formula is not merely similar but is the Pentateuch’s own heading, which outside the Torah stands at Ruth 4:18 alone [T]. High confidence. The brief expected this to be “the structural claim most likely to be corrected”; it is in fact the best-evidenced of the three.
