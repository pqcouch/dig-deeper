## The Passage

**4:13** וַיִּקַּח בֹּעַז אֶת־רוּת וַתְּהִי־לוֹ לְאִשָּׁה וַיָּבֹא אֵלֶיהָ וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן וַתֵּלֶד בֵּן׃
*So Boaz took Ruth, and she became his wife, and he went in to her. And the LORD enabled her to conceive, and she gave birth to a son.*

**4:14** וַתֹּאמַרְנָה הַנָּשִׁים אֶל־נָעֳמִי בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה אֲשֶׁר לֹא הִשְׁבִּית לָךְ גֹּאֵל הַיּוֹם וְיִקָּרֵא שְׁמוֹ בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל׃
*Then the women said to Naomi, "Blessed is the LORD who has not left you without a redeemer today, and may his name become famous in Israel."*

**4:15** וְהָיָה לָךְ לְמֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ וּלְכַלְכֵּל אֶת־שֵׂיבָתֵךְ כִּי כַלָּתֵךְ אֲשֶׁר־אֲהֵבַתֶךְ יְלָדַתּוּ אֲשֶׁר־הִיא טוֹבָה לָךְ מִשִּׁבְעָה בָּנִים׃

*Then Naomi took the child and laid him in her lap, and became his nurse.*

*The neighbour women gave him a name, saying, "A son has been born to Naomi!" So they named him Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David.*

## Headline Findings

1. **The women who were told not to call her Naomi are the women who call her name over the child.** *(Restated at v1.1 — claim audit 2, A22. The first version of this finding said "it is the same verb form", which the audit falsified: the forms differ in person. The corrected measurement is narrower in one respect and stronger in another.)*

   `[T]` The consonantal form תקראנה, under the root קָרָא ("call"), occurs in the Hebrew Bible **only in Ruth, and only in these four places**: אַל־תִּקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי ("do not call me Naomi", 1:20); לָמָּה תִקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי ("why do you call me Naomi?", 1:21); and at 4:17 **twice** — וַתִּקְרֶאנָה לוֹ… שֵׁם and וַתִּקְרֶאנָה שְׁמוֹ עוֹבֵד. *The one other verse in the canon printing that string, Exodus 1:10, is from a different root and means "befall".*

   **But the four are not one form, and the difference is the point.** `[T]` The index parses 1:20 as a jussive and 1:21 as an imperfect, both **second** person feminine plural — Naomi addressing the women — and both occurrences at 4:17 as waw-consecutive **third** person feminine plural: the narrator reporting what the women did. **So the verb, the root, the gender and the number are shared, and the person changes from "you call" to "they called"** — which is exactly the movement from her prohibition to their act. What they call out is **יֻלַּד־בֵּן לְנָעֳמִי**, "a son has been born to **Naomi**." *High confidence on the distribution and the parsings, both verified against the index's morphology.* **The book's last narrative act is the women of Bethlehem using the name Naomi forbade them, about a child that is not hers.**

2. **"Conception" is a three-verse word in the Hebrew Bible, and its other two homes are a curse and a judgement.** `[T]` הֵרָיוֹן stands by lemma at **Genesis 3:16, Hosea 9:11 and Ruth 4:13** — nowhere else. Genesis 3:16 is the sentence on the woman (הַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה עִצְּבוֹנֵךְ וְהֵרֹנֵךְ, "I will greatly multiply your pain and your conception"); Hosea 9:11 is Ephraim stripped of it (מִלֵּדָה וּמִבֶּטֶן וּמֵהֵרָיוֹן, "no birth, no womb, no conception"). **In Ruth it is the object of a verb whose subject is the LORD.** *High confidence on the lemma count; the full spelling הֵרָיוֹן occurs at Hosea 9:11 and Ruth 4:13, Genesis 3:16 having הֵרֹן, so no claim of identical wording is made.*

3. **The book's only two uses of "child" are the children she lost and the child she holds.** `[T]` יֶלֶד stands in **two verses of Ruth: 1:5 and 4:16**. At 1:5, וַתִּשָּׁאֵר הָאִשָּׁה מִשְּׁנֵי יְלָדֶיהָ — "the woman was left from her two children." At 4:16, וַתִּקַּח נָעֳמִי אֶת־הַיֶּלֶד — "Naomi took the child." *High confidence; both verses read.* And the verb: **יָלַד stands in ten verses of Ruth, nine of them in chapter 4 — the tenth is 1:12, where Naomi says she is too old to bear.** **The book's bearing-word is spoken once as an impossibility and then nine times as fact.**

4. **Five verses, four collocations that occur nowhere else in Scripture.** `[T]` Each was searched across the whole corpus with the verse total checked first:

   | Reference | The pairing | Verses in the canon |
   |---|---|---|
   | 4:14 | שָׁבַת hiphil ("cause to cease") + גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") | **Ruth 4:14 alone** |
   | 4:15 | כּוּל ("sustain") + שֵׂיבָה ("old age") | **Ruth 4:15 alone** |
   | 4:15 | טוֹב ("better") + שֶׁבַע ("seven") + בֵּן ("son") | **Ruth 4:15 alone** — *but see the correction at v1.2: the uniqueness is an artefact of searching on the number, and 1 Samuel 1:8 has the same construction with ten* |
   | 4:16 | חֵיק ("bosom") + שִׁית ("set") | **Ruth 4:16 alone** |

   *High confidence on each.* **The women's blessing and Naomi's gesture are phrased in language the rest of the Hebrew Bible never assembles.**

5. **And the root that carried chapter 1 closes the book here, in the same stem.** `[T]` שׁוּב stands in **13 verses of Ruth, ten of them in chapter 1** *(corrected at v1.1 — claim audit 2, A21; the first version said eleven)*, and **its last occurrence in the book is 4:15's מֵשִׁיב** — a hiphil participle. At 1:21 Naomi said **הֱשִׁיבַנִי** — a hiphil — "the LORD has **brought me back** empty." At 4:15 the child is **לְמֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ**, "a **bringer-back** of life." *High confidence; same root, same stem, and the distribution was verified at each of the thirteen verses.* **The verb of her complaint is the verb of the answer, and the book does not use it again.**

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

Extracted from the overview rather than re-derived, with its v1.4 correction in force.

- **Section:** Ketuvim, among the Megilloth, fourth in the sequence BHS prints.
- **Reading implication:** read as a Writing. **The Proverbs adjacency may not be used as an argument** (*Baba Batra* 14b places Ruth first among the Writings) `[S]`.
- **Presupposes:** the patriarchal narratives — and in this unit specifically Genesis 29 (Rachel), Genesis 38 (Perez), Genesis 48 (the redeeming angel and the naming), each measured below.
- **Handoff:** to David — **and this unit is where the handoff is made**, in its last clause.
- **Neighbours:** Job two books earlier in the printed sequence; Proverbs immediately before.

**One structural fact belongs here and is properly an apparatus feature.** `[T]` **As BHS prints it, the book of Ruth carries exactly one paragraph marker: the פ after 4:17.** This was established by testing for standalone one-letter samekh/pe tokens across the book, with the identical test run on Leviticus 23 as a positive control, which returned seven markers there. **So the Masoretic paragraphing marks one break in Ruth, and it falls at the end of this unit, dividing the narrative from the genealogy.** This is a reading tradition rather than an authorial division, and is stated as such; it is nonetheless the only external warrant in the book for any unit boundary, and it supports this one.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 4:1–12 completed the legal transaction at the gate and ended in a blessing from the assembly: may the LORD make this woman like **Rachel and Leah**, may your house be like the house of **Perez**, from the offspring the LORD gives you (4:11–12). `[T]` **Two requests have therefore been put to God on the record, in public, by witnesses — and neither has been granted when the chapter's eleventh verse ends.**

**Necessity answer.** This passage exists **to grant what 4:11–12 asked, and to hand the result to the women who asked the book's first question.** Three things make the position necessary.

The blessing of 4:11–12 demands a birth, and 4:13 supplies it in one clause with the LORD as subject. `[T]` **The vocabulary confirms the join and is measurable:** Rachel and Leah stand together in **thirteen verses of the canon, twelve in Genesis 29–33 and the thirteenth being Ruth 4:11**; Tamar and Judah in **five verses, three in Genesis 38, with 1 Chronicles 2:4 and Ruth 4:12**. `[T]` The blessing reaches for Genesis and the next verse answers it.

It must come before the genealogy, because the genealogy needs Obed named, and **4:17 is where he is named.** `[T]`

And it must be given to the women. `[T]` The book's opening scene in Bethlehem had the town ask הֲזֹאת נָעֳמִי ("Is this Naomi?", 1:19) and Naomi forbid them her name (1:20–21). **The complaint was made publicly to them, so the reversal is announced publicly by them** — which is why 4:14 and 4:17 put the two speeches in the mouths of הַנָּשִׁים and הַשְּׁכֵנוֹת rather than in the narrator's.

**Why not elsewhere.** The birth could not precede the gate scene without making the legal transaction unnecessary, and it could not follow the genealogy without leaving the genealogy's first name unexplained.

**Implication.** The unit is **the book's answer delivered to the book's original questioners**, and the narrator keeps himself almost entirely out of it: of these five verses, two are the women speaking and one is a single clause about the LORD.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 4 shows the design is tighter than the plot requires. **Every one of the unit's five verses answers something from chapter 1** — the conception against 1:12's impossibility; the redeemer against 1:5's bereavement; the restorer-of-life against 1:21's "brought me back empty"; the child in the bosom against 1:5's two children lost; and the naming against 1:20's prohibition. `[T]` *The individual links are each verified; calling the five-fold correspondence a deliberate design is `[I]` and is capped below.*

## Tool 1 — Author's Purpose

The narrator's purpose is visible in how little he says and who he lets say it.

**He reports the birth in six Hebrew words and does not comment on it.** `[T]` וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן וַתֵּלֶד בֵּן — "and the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son." No wonder, no reflection, no "and Naomi was comforted". **The one sentence in the book that reports God acting directly is the shortest sentence in the chapter.**

**He then gives two whole verses to the women and says nothing himself.** `[T]` 4:14–15 is their speech, and it is the book's longest blessing. **They interpret the birth; the narrator does not.** And what they say is not what a reader expects: the blessing's grammar runs to Naomi (לָךְ four times in two verses), and its stated reason is Ruth — כִּי כַלָּתֵךְ אֲשֶׁר־אֲהֵבַתֶךְ יְלָדַתּוּ, "for your daughter-in-law who loves you has borne him."

**And he closes with a name he lets them give.** `[T]` 4:17's naming is done by הַשְּׁכֵנוֹת, the neighbour women, not by the parents. **In the Hebrew Bible a child is normally named by mother or father**; here the mother is not in the sentence and the father was last mentioned four verses earlier. *Confidence: moderate–high* on the unusualness, which is a comparative claim about naming scenes rather than a measured count, and is tagged accordingly. `[I]`

**So the purpose of the unit is to hand the book's verdict to its witnesses.** `[I]` The narrator has spent four chapters declining to evaluate Naomi's complaint (1:20–21 left standing; 2:3's "chance"; 2:15–16 reported without praise). **Here he declines again, and lets the town do it** — which is why the last words about Naomi in the book are spoken by her neighbours and not by him.

**Book-overview note.** The overview's trap 4 says the story answers Naomi's verdict and the narrator does not. **This unit confirms it precisely and locates the answer**: 4:14–17, in the mouths of the women — which is what the 1:19–22 dig predicted and queued. Confirmed, not merely consistent.

## Tool 2 — Context

**Immediately before (4:1–12).** The gate, the transaction, the witnesses, the blessing. `[T]` Boaz has acquired the field and Ruth; the assembly has asked for a house like Rachel's, Leah's and Perez's.

**Immediately after (4:18–22).** The genealogy from Perez to David, in the toledot form. `[T]` **And the one paragraph marker in the book stands between 4:17 and 4:18** (Phase 0.55), so the Masoretic tradition reads the genealogy as a distinct block.

**The unit's own boundary.** 4:13 begins a new scene (new subject, new place, no gate) and 4:17 ends with David's name and the paragraph break. `[I]` **Both edges are supported** — the first by subject change, the second by the apparatus.

**The book's frame, now closed.** `[T]` 1:1–5 took a family out of Bethlehem and reduced it to one woman; 4:13–17 returns a child to a Bethlehem household and names him into the royal line. The frame is held by vocabulary measured under Tool 7 and Move 4, not by theme alone.

## Tool 3 — Structure

Five verses, four voices, and the narrator speaks least.

| Verse | Element | Voice |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | Marriage, conception, birth | narrator — one clause for God's act |
| 14–15 | The blessing: redeemer · name · restorer · sustainer · the reason, which is Ruth | **the women** |
| 16 | Naomi takes the child, sets him in her bosom, becomes his nurse | narrator — three verbs, no comment |
| 17a | The naming, with its declaration | **the neighbour women** |
| 17b | "He is the father of Jesse, the father of David" | narrator — the handoff |

**The arrangement is a ring.** `[T]` A narrator's sentence, a speech, a narrator's sentence, a speech, a narrator's clause. **The two speeches are the book's verdict and the three narrator's sentences are the bare facts it rests on.** *Confidence: moderate* on the ring as design; the alternation is `[T]`, calling it deliberate is `[I]` and unbaselined.

**Within the blessing, the movement is from God to the child to Ruth.** `[T]` 4:14 blesses **the LORD**; 4:15a describes **the child's** future function; 4:15b gives **Ruth** as the cause. **The blessing's logic ends where the book began — with the Moabite's loyalty — and it is the women who put it there.**

**One structural observation the preacher should not miss.** `[T]` **Ruth does not speak in this unit, and after 4:13 she is not mentioned again in the book.** She is spoken *about* at 4:15 and absent from 4:16 and 4:17. *The observation is `[T]`; what to make of it is contested and is handled under Difficult Verses.*

## Tool 4 — Linking Words

**The bare waw at 4:13.** `[T]` Four consecutive verbs with nothing but waw between them: he took, she became, he went in, the LORD gave. **No "and behold", no "and it came to pass".** The book's climactic event is joined to its sentence by the plainest connective Hebrew has.

**אֲשֶׁר לֹא at 4:14.** בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה אֲשֶׁר לֹא הִשְׁבִּית לָךְ גֹּאֵל. `[T]` **The blessing is framed as a negative**: not "who has given you a redeemer" but "who has not caused a redeemer to cease for you". **The women bless God for a non-subtraction** — which is a precise answer to a woman who had itemised what was taken from her.

**כִּי at 4:15.** כִּי כַלָּתֵךְ אֲשֶׁר־אֲהֵבַתֶךְ יְלָדַתּוּ. `[T]` Causal, and it governs the whole preceding blessing. **The grammar makes Ruth the reason for the child's future usefulness to Naomi**, not merely the means of his birth.

**מִן comparative at 4:15.** טוֹבָה לָךְ מִשִּׁבְעָה בָּנִים, "better to you than seven sons". `[T]` **Comparative מִן against a number is strong**, and the number is the Hebrew figure for completeness. NASB95 "is better to you than seven sons"; ESV "is more to you than seven sons". **The ESV's "more" is the weaker of the two and loses the evaluative force of טוֹב.**

**לֵאמֹר at 4:17.** `[T]` The quotation marker before the women's declaration — so יֻלַּד־בֵּן לְנָעֳמִי is presented as **their words quoted**, not the narrator's summary. **A preacher may not attribute that sentence to the narrator.**

## Tool 5 — Parallels

Prose, but 4:15's first half is a matched pair:

> וְהָיָה לָךְ לְמֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ ("and he shall be to you a restorer of life")
> וּלְכַלְכֵּל אֶת־שֵׂיבָתֵךְ ("and to sustain your old age")

`[T]` Two infinitive constructions governed by one verb, the first abstract and the second concrete, the second naming the stage of life. **And both halves hold a one-verse collocation or a once-in-the-book word** (Tool 7): the pairing כּוּל + שֵׂיבָה occurs in no other verse of the canon, and נֶפֶשׁ occurs **once in Ruth, here**.

**4:16 is a three-verb sequence with no parallelism and maximum economy:**

> וַתִּקַּח … וַתְּשִׁתֵהוּ בְחֵיקָהּ … וַתְּהִי־לוֹ לְאֹמֶנֶת

`[T]` Took, set, became. **The book's emotional climax is three verbs and no adjective.**

## Tool 6 — Narrator's Comment

Three comments, each a single clause, each doing more than its length suggests.

**(i) 4:13b — וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן.** `[T]` **This is one of only two places in the book where the narrator states in his own voice that the LORD did something.** The other is 1:6, where the LORD had visited his people לָתֵת לָהֶם לָחֶם ("to give them bread"). Verified: נָתַן stands with the divine name in **five verses of Ruth — 1:6, 1:9, 4:11, 4:12, 4:13** — of which **1:9, 4:11 and 4:12 are prayers** ("may the LORD grant"), leaving **1:6 and 4:13 as the narrator's two statements of fact.** `[T]` *High confidence.* **The LORD gives bread, and the LORD gives conception; the book reports nothing else of him directly.**

**This closes a loop opened in the 1:19–22 report**, which observed that Naomi's indictment at 1:21 stands between the only two things God is said to have done. **The measurement now exists and the claim holds.**

**(ii) 4:16 — the three verbs.** `[T]` Discussed above. The narrator does not say Naomi was comforted, restored, or filled. **He says she took the child and became his nurse**, and leaves the reader to set that beside 1:5.

**(iii) 4:17b — הוּא אֲבִי־יִשַׁי אֲבִי דָוִד.** `[T]` Five words, and the first mention of David in the book. **The narrator's last act is to tell the reader what the characters cannot know.** This is the same technique as 2:1 — information given to the reader over the characters' heads — and here it is the book's final sentence of narrative.

## Tool 7 — Vocabulary

The unit's heaviest tool. Every count is on the WLC lemma index, **verified by lemma with the homograph suffix carried**, at each individual reference; the corpus verse total (23,213) was printed and checked before any canon-wide count, and a key control confirmed the suffix-aware lemma key before any result was used — both per the faults recorded in the claim audit at v1.1 and in the 2:1–16 report.

### 4:13 — the birth

**הֵרָיוֹן ("conception") — three verses by lemma: Genesis 3:16, Hosea 9:11, Ruth 4:13.** `[T]` Treated as Headline 2; the companions were read, not recalled. **The spelling caveat matters and is stated there.** *High on the count.*

**נָתַן ("give") with the divine name — five verses in Ruth, of which two are the narrator's.** `[T]` Treated under Tool 6.

**יָלַד ("bear") — ten verses in Ruth: 1:12, 4:12, 4:13, 4:15, 4:17, 4:18, 4:19, 4:20, 4:21, 4:22.** `[T]` **Nine of the ten are in chapter 4.** The tenth is 1:12, Naomi's וְיָלַדְתִּי בָנִים inside her argument that it cannot happen. *High confidence.*

### 4:14 — the blessing

**שָׁבַת hiphil ("cause to cease") with גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") — Ruth 4:14 alone in the canon.** `[T]` *High.* שָׁבַת hiphil is a 65-verse word (13 in Ezekiel, 8 in Isaiah), overwhelmingly used of God or an enemy **stopping** something — songs, sacrifices, a kingdom. **Here it is used of what God has declined to stop.**

**A non-link that must be stated.** `[T]` At 1:5 Naomi **was left** without her two children — וַתִּשָּׁאֵר, from שָׁאַר. At 4:14 God **has not left** her without a redeemer — הִשְׁבִּית, from שָׁבַת. **These are different verbs, and there is no verbal echo between them.** The correspondence is real at the level of sense and absent at the level of words, and a sermon that claims the latter is claiming what the index denies. *Checked directly: 1:5's lemma set does not contain the 4:14 verb.*

**גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") — ten verses in Ruth: 2:20, 3:9, 3:12, 3:13, 4:1, 4:3, 4:4, 4:6, 4:8, 4:14.** `[T]` Ruth holds ten of the canon's 83, and **4:14 is the book's last occurrence.** *High confidence.* **The last voice in the book to use the word is the women's, and whom they mean is genuinely open** — see Difficult Verses.

**The Genesis 48:16 contact, measured and then trimmed.** `[T]` גֹּאֵל with קָרָא and שֵׁם in one verse stands in **four verses: Genesis 48:16, Isaiah 43:1, Isaiah 54:5, Ruth 4:14.** Genesis 48:16 is Jacob blessing Joseph's sons — הַמַּלְאָךְ הַגֹּאֵל אֹתִי מִכָּל־רָע … וְיִקָּרֵא בָהֶם שְׁמִי ("the angel who redeemed me from all evil … and let my name be named on them"). The two Isaiah verses are redeemer-texts of the same kind ("I have called you by name"; "your Redeemer … shall be called").

**But the ordered-phrase search trims the claim, and this is worth showing.** `[T]` The phrase וְיִקָּרֵא שְׁמוֹ returns **seven verses on a plain pass — Genesis 25:26, 35:8, 38:29, 38:30, Exodus 17:15, Isaiah 9:5, Ruth 4:14 — and Genesis 48:16 is not among them**, because its wording is reordered and its suffix different (וְיִקָּרֵא בָהֶם שְׁמִי). **So the Genesis 48 contact is a lemma-level contact of four verses and not a shared phrase.**

**And the seven are not one phrase either** *(added at v1.1 — claim audit 2, A28)*. `[T]` **Six of the seven are qal — וַיִּקְרָא, "and he called his name" — narrative, with a human subject. Ruth 4:14 alone is niphal: וְיִקָּרֵא, "and let his name be called."** Verified against the index's morphology at all seven. **So the identical consonants carry two different constructions, and the sense the English versions give — "may his name be renowned" — belongs to Ruth 4:14 and to none of the other six.** *High confidence.* What looked like a seven-verse convention is a six-verse convention plus one verse doing something else, and the one is this one. *Confidence: high on both counts; **no allusion claimed** in either direction — four verses with three rivals is a register, and a seven-verse naming formula is a convention.* **Two of the seven are the naming of Perez and Zerah (Genesis 38:29–30), and Ruth 4:12 has just invoked the house of Perez** — recorded as a convergence, rated *moderate*, and not claimed as design.

### 4:15 — the women's reasons

**כּוּל ("sustain") with שֵׂיבָה ("old age") — Ruth 4:15 alone in the canon.** `[T]` *High.* כּוּל is a 36-verse word (11 in 1 Kings) normally used of provisioning armies or households; שֵׂיבָה is a 19-verse word (5 in Genesis) for grey-headed age. **Neither occurs anywhere else in Ruth.**

**טוֹב ("better") with שֶׁבַע ("seven") and בֵּן ("son") — Ruth 4:15 alone** `[T]` — **and at v1.2 that measurement has to be read with its own method fault attached.** *(Logos round 2, and the fault is mine.)*

**The count is right and the significance was wrong.** `[T]` **1 Samuel 1:8 reads הֲלוֹא אָנֹכִי טוֹב לָךְ מֵעֲשָׂרָה בָּנִים — "am I not better to you than ten sons?"** — Elkanah to the childless Hannah. **Ruth 4:15 reads אֲשֶׁר־הִיא טוֹבָה לָךְ מִשִּׁבְעָה בָּנִים.** Same adjective, same לָךְ, same comparative מִן, same בָּנִים; **only the number differs.** Both are said to a grieving woman about her childlessness.

**So the one-verse result was guaranteed by the search, not found by it.** Keying the set on **seven** could only ever return the verse that has seven. And a lemma search cannot recover the parallel, because in both verses the מִן is prefixed to the numeral rather than standing as its own token. **The construction has to be seen; it cannot be searched for on these keys.** `[I]`

**The honest form:** the construction *"better to you than N sons"* stands in **two verses of the Hebrew Bible — 1 Samuel 1:8 and Ruth 4:15** — and Ruth's is the only one with seven. **That is a better finding than the one it replaces, and the commentary tradition had it first** (see below). *Confidence: high.* **The fault class is new and is recorded in the Text-First Declaration: a collocation search that includes an incidental element manufactures its own uniqueness.**

**אָהַב ("love") — one verse in Ruth: 4:15.** `[T]` *High confidence.* The book's **only** love-word, and it is used by the women, of Ruth, about Naomi. **Not of Boaz and Ruth.** A 195-verse root canon-wide, so no allusion follows from it — but the Ruth-internal fact is the finding: **in a book routinely preached as a love story, the word occurs once and describes a daughter-in-law's loyalty to her mother-in-law.**

**And its one measured company is the Rachel story.** `[T]` אָהַב with שֶׁבַע in one verse stands in **four verses: Genesis 29:18, 29:20, 29:30, Ruth 4:15** — all three rivals being Jacob's seven years' service because he loved Rachel. **The density scan independently ranks Genesis 29 seventh of 859 chapters against Ruth 4.** *Confidence: high on the count; **moderate** on significance, and the sense differs — Genesis has seven years, Ruth has seven sons.* **What makes it more than noise is 4:11, four verses earlier, where the assembly asked that Ruth be made like Rachel.** No citation claimed.

**כַּלָּה ("daughter-in-law") — seven verses in Ruth: 1:6, 1:7, 1:8, 1:22, 2:20, 2:22, 4:15.** `[T]` **Ruth holds seven of the canon's 34 occurrences — more than any other book**, in four chapters. *High confidence.* **4:15 is the book's last use, and the women use it to identify the person the blessing is really about.** (At Isaiah 61:10 the same lemma means "bride" — a different sense, read and checked, and excluded.)

**נֶפֶשׁ ("life") — one verse in Ruth: 4:15.** `[T]` **שׁוּב with נֶפֶשׁ is a 35-verse idiom** canon-wide (6 each in Jeremiah and Psalms, 3 in Lamentations), **so no allusion is claimed** — it is above the threshold at which wording alone can carry dependence. **What is claimed is the book-internal bracket** of Headline 5.

### 4:16 — the nurse

**חֵיק ("bosom") with שִׁית ("set") — Ruth 4:16 alone.** `[T]` *High.* And **שִׁית occurs twice in Ruth — 3:15 and 4:16**: Boaz sets the barley on her, and Naomi sets the child in her lap. `[T]` **The book's two acts of laying something in someone's arms.**

**אֹמֶנֶת ("nurse") — one of two feminine participles of אָמַן in the canon, and the only one in the absolute.** *(Restated at v1.1 — claim audit 2, A26, which falsified the first version's claim that it is the only feminine form.)* `[T]` The qal participle of אָמַן in the nursing or guardian sense stands at Numbers 11:12 (הָאֹמֵן), Esther 2:7 (אֹמֵן), 2 Kings 10:1 and 10:5 (הָאֹמְנִים), Isaiah 49:23 (אֹמְנַיִךְ) — **all masculine** — and in the feminine at **two** places: **2 Samuel 4:4 (אֹמַנְתּוֹ, construct with a suffix) and Ruth 4:16 (לְאֹמֶנֶת, absolute).** *High confidence; every participle was read off the index with its morphology.*

**And the correction improves the finding rather than weakening it.** `[T]` 2 Samuel 4:4 is Mephibosheth's nurse, who took up a child not her own and carried him when his household fell. **So the canon's two feminine nurse-participles both describe a woman taking up a child who is not hers, in a house that has lost its heir** — which is a two-verse contact where the report previously claimed a unique form. *Confidence: high on the count; the parallel is `[I]` and no citation is claimed.* **The non-Ruth parse should be confirmed in Logos before it is preached, since the project cites BHS and this is a WLC morphological judgement.**

**A method note belongs here.** A first surface search returned a false positive at Psalm 89:29, whose word is נֶאֱמֶנֶת, a nifal in a quite different sense; and the claim of uniqueness survived that check only to fail a second one. **Checking the forms individually was not enough — what was needed was the index's morphology column, which this report did not use.**

**And the nurse has one measured companion.** `[T]` אָמַן with חֵיק in one verse stands in **three verses: Numbers 11:12, Micah 7:5, Ruth 4:16** — and Micah 7:5 uses אָמַן in the sense of trusting, not nursing, so **the nurse-and-bosom pairing is Numbers 11:12 and Ruth 4:16.** Numbers 11:12 is Moses complaining: *did I conceive this people, that you should say to me, carry them in your bosom as a nurse carries a nursing child?* **Moses protests at being made a nurse; Naomi becomes one without being asked.** *Confidence: high on the count; moderate on the contrast, and no citation claimed.*

### 4:17 — the naming

**תִּקְרֶאנָה — four occurrences in the book: 1:20, 1:21, 4:17 twice.** `[T]` Headline 1. *High confidence; located by exact token search, the loose-prefix error of the 2:1–16 run deliberately avoided.*

**קָרָא ("call") — six verses in Ruth, seven occurrences: 1:20 (×2), 1:21, 4:11, 4:14, 4:17 (×2).** `[T]` **Two clusters, and nothing in between.** All of chapters 2 and 3 pass without the verb. *High confidence.* **The book calls names at its complaint and at its resolution, and not once in the middle.**

**שָׁכֵן ("neighbour") — one verse in Ruth: 4:17.** `[T]` A 20-verse word canon-wide (6 in Psalms, 5 in Jeremiah). **A second method note: a first surface search for the feminine plural returned six false positives, every one of them מִשְׁכָּן ("dwelling"), a different word that my prefix-permissive pattern admitted. The lemma check removed them all.**

**עוֹבֵד ("Obed") — nine verses: six in Chronicles, three in Ruth (4:17, 4:21, 4:22).** `[T]` *High.* **The name is from עָבַד, "to serve"** — a transparent Hebrew name, as Naomi's and Mara's were (1:20). *The etymology is `[T]`; what the narrator means by it he does not say, and I will not supply it.*

## Tool 8 — Translations

| | NASB95 | ESV | The Hebrew |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4:13** | "the LORD enabled her to conceive" | "the LORD gave her conception" | וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן — a three-verse word |
| **4:14** | "may **his** name become famous" | "may **his** name be renowned" | שְׁמוֹ — but the Greek reads "your name"; see below |
| **4:15** | "is **better** to you than seven sons" | "is **more** to you than seven sons" | טוֹבָה — the evaluative word |
| **4:16** | "laid him in her lap… became his nurse" | "laid him on her lap… became his nurse" | חֵיק, "bosom"; אֹמֶנֶת, a unique form |
| **4:17** | "The neighbor women gave him a name" | "the women of the neighborhood gave him a name" | הַשְּׁכֵנוֹת |

**Three notes for the preacher.**

**(i) 4:13 — ESV is the more literal and the difference is theological.** `[T]` The Hebrew has the LORD **give** (נָתַן) her conception; ESV keeps the verb, NASB95's "enabled her to conceive" turns a gift into a permission. **What the congregation hears from the pulpit text is the stronger and more accurate of the two**, which is worth saying since the study text is usually the more literal.

**(ii) 4:15 — the ESV's "more" is weaker than the Hebrew.** `[T]` טוֹבָה is an evaluative adjective and NASB95's "better" carries it. **"More to you than seven sons" could be quantitative; "better to you than seven sons" is a judgement**, and the women are making a judgement.

**(iii) 4:14 is the unit's one genuine textual fork, and both versions take the same side.** See Textual Variants.

## Tool 9 — Tone and Feel

**4:13 is abrupt.** `[T]` After twelve verses of legal procedure, four verbs dispatch a marriage, a pregnancy and a birth. **The book spends more words on who owned a field than on the birth of David's great-grandfather.**

**4:14–15 is warm and public.** `[T]` A blessing with God's name first, four second-person-feminine pronouns, and a verdict about a daughter-in-law. **It is the sound of a town changing its mind in company.**

**4:16 is almost silent.** `[T]` Three verbs and no comment — the most restrained sentence in the book at the moment of greatest feeling. **A preacher who wants pathos here must get it from the restraint, not from the words.**

**4:17 is ceremonial, then suddenly wide.** `[T]` The naming formula, the quoted declaration, the name — and then, without transition, **"he is the father of Jesse, the father of David."** The scale changes in five words from a household to a dynasty.

**Where the pastoral weight sits.** `[I]` On a woman who said she was empty, holding a child who is not hers, with the neighbours saying her name. **The passage does not tell the reader what she felt, and the honest sermon will not either.**

## Tool 10 — Repetition

**נָעֳמִי ("Naomi") — three times in five verses: 4:14, 4:16, 4:17.** `[T]` Out of 21 occurrences across 20 verses in the book. **The name she tried to retire is the name the last scene uses three times**, and 4:17 puts it in the women's mouths.

**הָיָה לְ ("became") — three times: 4:13 (she became his wife), 4:15 (he shall be to you), 4:16 (she became his nurse).** `[T]` **The same construction does Ruth's new status, the child's future function and Naomi's new role.** *Confidence: high* on the repetition; *moderate* on it being designed.

**קָרָא and שֵׁם at 4:14 and 4:17.** `[T]` The blessing asks that his name be called in Israel; the women then call his name. **The request and its performance are three verses apart.**

**The women, twice named and twice distinguished.** `[T]` הַנָּשִׁים ("the women", 4:14) and הַשְּׁכֵנוֹת ("the neighbour women", 4:17). **Two different designations for what may or may not be the same group** — the text does not say, and the 1:19–22 report flagged this identification as an inference. **It remains one.**

**בֵּן ("son") — 4:13, 4:15, 4:17.** `[T]` A son born, seven sons outmatched, a son born to Naomi.

## Tool 11 — Quotation and Allusion

No quotation. Three measured contacts and one internal field, and Move 4 is where this unit's weight lies.

### Genesis, from three directions — the unit's principal intertextual finding

**Move 1 — source context.** Three Genesis texts are in measured contact with this unit, and they are different in kind. `[T]`

- **Genesis 3:16** is the sentence on the woman, and it contains one of the canon's three occurrences of הֵרָיוֹן. **It is a curse on conception.**
- **Genesis 29** is Jacob's seven years for Rachel, and it holds all three rivals to the אָהַב + שֶׁבַע pairing of 4:15. **It is love measured in service.**
- **Genesis 48:16** is Jacob's deathbed blessing, and it is one of four verses holding גֹּאֵל with קָרָא and שֵׁם. **It is a redeemer invoked over children being named.**

**Move 2 — book usage.** Does Ruth use Genesis elsewhere? **Repeatedly, and the earlier digs measured it.** `[T]` The 1:6–18 report found the עָזַב/דָּבַק pair of Genesis 2:24 split across 1:14 and 1:16, with Genesis 2:24 the canon's only verse holding both. The 1:19–22 report found רֵיקָם at Genesis 31:42; the 2:1–16 report found מַשְׂכֹּרֶת at Genesis 29:15, 31:7 and 31:41, three of that word's four verses. And this unit's own context names **Rachel and Leah** (4:11, the thirteenth and only non-Genesis verse pairing them) and **Tamar and Judah** (4:12, one of five). **Genesis is beyond question a live source for this book.**

**Move 3 — OT-to-OT.** Who else reaches for Genesis this way at a birth? `[T]` Hosea 9:11 shares הֵרָיוֹן with Genesis 3:16, and uses it to **remove** what Genesis imposed and Ruth receives. **The three-verse distribution therefore runs curse — deprivation — gift**, which is a statement about the word's canonical range and not about dependence. *Moderate; the direction of any relation is unestablished.*

**Move 4 — internal echo. This is the unit's richest yield, and it is why the unit exists.** Five correspondences with chapter 1, each verified:

| Chapter 1 | This unit | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1:12 — Naomi: too old to **bear** (יָלַד) | 4:13 — she **bore** a son; and nine of the book's ten uses of the verb are in this chapter | `[T]` *high* |
| 1:5 — the woman **was left** from her two **children** (יֶלֶד) | 4:16 — Naomi took **the child** (יֶלֶד); **the book's only two uses** | `[T]` *high* |
| 1:21 — the LORD **brought me back** (הֱשִׁיבַנִי) empty | 4:15 — a **restorer** (מֵשִׁיב) of life; **same root, same stem, and the book's last use of the root** | `[T]` *high* |
| 1:20–21 — **do not call me** Naomi (תִּקְרֶאנָה) | 4:17 — **they called** (וַתִּקְרֶאנָה), twice: "a son is born to **Naomi**" | `[T]` *high* |
| 1:19 — the town asks "Is this Naomi?" | 4:17 — the neighbour women answer by naming a child | `[I]` *moderate–high*; the identification of the two groups is an inference |

**And the fifth is not a verbal echo, which must be said.** `[T]` 1:5's "was left" (שָׁאַר) and 4:14's "has not left" (שָׁבַת hiphil) are **different verbs**. The sense corresponds; the words do not.

**Verdict.** **The individual echoes are high-confidence single contacts and they hold.** The claim that the five together constitute a deliberate closing design is **`[I]`, capped at moderate, and unbaselined** — no null has been run for how often the last scene of a short Hebrew narrative re-uses its opening's vocabulary. **Per the overview's trap 7, it is a proposal.** *What may be preached is each link; what may not be preached is "the author built a five-fold inclusio", until someone measures it.*

### What this unit does not contain — tested

**No quotation of the gleaning or redemption legislation, and no Deuteronomy 25 levirate vocabulary.** `[T]` The legal words that carried chapters 2 and 4:1–12 are absent here: the gleaning pair, גְּאֻלָּה, יְבָמָה. **The transaction is over; the vocabulary goes with it.** *This was checked rather than assumed; the words' distributions were established in the earlier reports and none falls in 4:13–17.*

## Tool 12 — Genre

Hebrew narrative closing into genealogy. `[T]`

**What the genre licenses.** The narrator's silence at 4:16 is convention, not coldness. `[I]` **Hebrew narrative reports the gesture and withholds the emotion**, and the reader supplies what the three verbs imply. A sermon may draw the feeling out; it may not claim the text states it.

**What the genealogy's presence licenses.** `[T]` The toledot form at 4:18–22 is the Torah's genealogical formula, and **the paragraph marker before it** (Phase 0.55) shows the Masoretic tradition read it as a distinct block. **The narrative ends at 4:17 with David named; the genealogy then says it again formally.** *The redundancy is the point: the handoff is made twice.* `[I]`

**What the genre forbids.** Reading 4:16 as a legal adoption. `[I]` **The text says Naomi became his אֹמֶנֶת — a nurse or guardian — not his mother**, and the naming at 4:17 is done by neighbours, not by a parent exercising a right. *Confidence: moderate–high*; the vocabulary is `[T]`, and the legal question is contested among commentators and not settled by these words.

## Tool 13 — Copycat

**The women are the imitable party in this unit, and that is unusual enough to notice.** `[I]` They do four things, each `[T]`: they bless God rather than congratulate the parents (4:14); they frame the gift as something God declined to withhold (4:14); they name the person actually responsible, who is the foreigner (4:15); and they say the name their neighbour had forbidden, because it is now true (4:17). **A congregation can do all four of those this week.**

**Naomi is imitable in one respect and not in others.** `[T]` She takes the child. She says nothing. **The text records no speech from her in this unit, and her last words in the book are at 3:18** — which is a fact worth checking before preaching her restoration as something she articulates.

**Boaz drops out.** `[T]` He acts at 4:13 and is not mentioned again until the genealogy. **The redeemer disappears once the redemption is accomplished**, which is a narrative fact and not obviously a theological one. `[I]`

**The copycat trap here.** Making the passage a lesson about having children, or about God rewarding patience. `[I]` **Naomi did not wait patiently — she said God had testified against her — and the child is not her reward.** **The passage is about a town's verdict being reversed, not about a virtue being paid.**

## Tool 14 — Bible Timeline

**Where this sits.** The end of the judges period, one generation before Jesse and two before David. `[T]`

**What it supplies to the timeline.** The link from the patriarchal promises to the monarchy. `[T]` **The book's last word is דָּוִד**, and the genealogy that follows runs from Perez — Judah's son by Tamar — to David. **Ruth stands at the join between Genesis and Samuel**, which is a canonical observation, not a compositional one. `[I]`

**What has not happened.** No king, no temple, no prophet. `[T]` **The monarchy is four names away and nobody in the scene knows it** — the narrator tells the reader at 4:17 and no character is told.

**Where it points.** Forward through David. `[T]` **The overview's handoff claim is executed in this unit's last clause**, and the oath formula finding from the 1:6–18 dig (כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה, eleven of thirteen plain-pass verses in Samuel–Kings) now looks like part of the same orientation. *That connection is `[I]` and belongs to the macro-synthesis.*

## Tool 15 — Who Am I?

**The reader's position is, for the last time, above the characters.** `[I]` 4:17's final clause gives the reader David while leaving every person in the scene with a baby and a blessing. **The book's first disclosure of this kind was 2:1; this is its last, and it is the largest.**

**And the reader is placed with the women.** `[I]` *Confidence: moderate.* They are the ones who interpret, and the reader has been given exactly the information needed to agree with them: that the LORD gave the conception (4:13), and that Ruth's loyalty began on a road in chapter 1. **The reader is in a position to say what the women say, and the narrator gives nobody else a verdict to deliver.**

**Where the reader is not placed:** as Naomi. `[I]` **Her interior life is closed to the reader in this unit by design** — she neither speaks nor is described.

## Tool 16 — So What?

**(i) God's answer came through a person, and the witnesses say so.** `[T]` The blessing's stated cause is not a miracle but a daughter-in-law: כִּי כַלָּתֵךְ אֲשֶׁר־אֲהֵבַתֶךְ יְלָדַתּוּ. **A sermon that makes the child the whole answer has dropped the clause the women thought was the reason.**

**(ii) The reversal is announced publicly, by the people who heard the complaint.** `[T]` Naomi's verdict was delivered to the town (1:19–21); the town delivers the answer (4:14–17). **Grief that was made public is answered in public, and the same voices do both.**

**(iii) What God is said to have done in this book is give bread and give a child.** `[T]` Two clauses, in 1:6 and 4:13, measured under Tool 6. **Everything else in four chapters is people acting — and the book treats that as providence, not as its absence.**

**(iv) The name that was renounced is the name that is used.** `[T]` **"Call me Mara" (1:20) is answered not by an argument but by neighbours saying "Naomi" over a cradle.**

## Extension — Original Languages

Lexis is under Tool 7. Four points remain.

**No ketiv in this unit.** `[T]` A scan of all 22 verses of Ruth 4 for unpointed forms returns three — at 4:4, 4:5 and 4:6, all inside the gate scene — **and none in 4:13–17.** **The פ at 4:17 is the corpus's own paragraph marker, not a word**, and is treated as such (Phase 0.55). *This is the markup-as-text trap named in the method log, and it was handled by testing for standalone one-letter tokens with a positive control on Leviticus 23.*

**יֻלַּד at 4:17 — a passive.** `[T]` A pual or qal-passive perfect: "a son **has been born** to Naomi". **The women do not say Ruth bore a son for Naomi; they say a son has been born, with no agent named.** The grammar leaves the relation unstated, which is precisely the ambiguity the versions preserve ("A son has been born to Naomi").

**הִשְׁבִּית at 4:14 — causative of a verb of ceasing.** `[T]` Literally "he has not caused a redeemer to cease for you". **English cannot keep both the causative and the negation gracefully**, and both versions render it idiomatically ("has not left you without").

**לְמֵשִׁיב and לְכַלְכֵּל at 4:15 — two ל-plus-infinitive-or-participle phrases in sequence.** `[T]` The first is a participle used as a noun ("a restorer"), the second an infinitive ("to sustain"). **The asymmetry is in the Hebrew, and both versions smooth it.**

## Extension — Textual Variants

**The unit's one material fork is at 4:14, and it changes whose name is at stake.** `[T]`

MT reads **וְיִקָּרֵא שְׁמוֹ בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל** — "and may **his** name be renowned in Israel". The BHS apparatus records at 4:14: the Greek has τὸ ὄνομά σου = **שְׁמֵךְ**, "**your** name". The Rahlfs text confirms it: *καὶ καλέσαι τὸ ὄνομά σου ἐν Ισραηλ.*

**The triage.**

| | MT | Greek |
|---|---|---|
| the suffix | שְׁמוֹ — "his" | שְׁמֵךְ — "your" (fem. sg., i.e. Naomi's) |
| whose renown | the child's | Naomi's |
| category | — | **a substantive variant, one consonant** |

**Why it matters for preaching.** `[I]` On MT, the women pray that the child will become famous in Israel — which the genealogy then grants. On the Greek, they pray that **Naomi's** name will be renowned — which the next three verses then grant in a different sense, since 4:17's declaration is precisely about her name. **Both readings are coherent and both are answered by the context**, which is what makes the variant interesting rather than decisive. *Confidence: high that the divergence is real; **I do not adjudicate it**, and the apparatus does not either.*

**At v1.2 the apparatus was read directly in Logos, and it says something this report could not say from the export alone.** `[T]` BHS records at 4:14 only **𝔊 τὸ ὄνομά σου = שְׁמֵךְ** — **the Greek, with no Hebrew manuscript support cited, and no proposal to emend.** The contrast within the same column is the evidence: at **4:15** BHS cites *sic L, mlt Mss Edd*, and at **4:21** *pc Mss* — **Hebrew manuscripts, named, where they exist.** At 4:14 there are none. **So "your name" is the Greek's reading and not an attested Hebrew variant**, and MT's שְׁמוֹ stands as the reading of record rather than as one option among two. **NASB95 and ESV both follow MT, so the pulpit and study texts agree and the congregation will hear "his name".**

**Two further apparatus notes on the unit.** `[T]` At **4:15** the apparatus records a form variant on אֲהֵבַתֶךְ ("who loves you") — L reads as printed, many manuscripts and editions otherwise — which does not affect the sense. At **4:16** the Syriac omits the verse entirely. **Neither bears on preaching.**

**A pattern across the digs, now at three instances and still not claimed as one.** `[I]` At 1:21 the Greek resolved a Hebrew homograph against the Hebrew tradition (עָנָה as "afflict"); at 2:7 it did so again (שִׁבְתָּהּ as "rest"); here the divergence is of a different kind — **a one-consonant suffix, not a homograph.** **So the pattern proposed in the 2:1–16 report does not extend to this unit**, and recording that is as useful as recording a confirmation would have been.

## Extension — Historical Background

**Naming by neighbours.** `[I]` In the Hebrew Bible a child is normally named by a parent. **Here the neighbour women name him**, and the mother is not in the sentence. *Confidence: moderate* — the generalisation about naming scenes is a comparative impression rather than a count, and is tagged accordingly.

**"Better than seven sons."** `[S]` Seven is the Hebrew figure for completeness, and sons were a widow's security in old age — which is what 4:15's "sustainer of your old age" names directly. **The compliment is therefore economic as well as affectionate**, and the women are saying that Ruth has done for Naomi what a full complement of sons would have done. *Confidence: high on the function of sons; moderate on the force of the figure.*

**A nurse in the household.** `[S]` The אֹמֵן / אֹמֶנֶת elsewhere is a guardian or foster-parent of some standing — the guardians of Ahab's sons (2 Kings 10:1, 10:5), Mordecai to Esther (Esther 2:7), kings as foster-fathers (Isaiah 49:23). `[T]` **So Naomi's new role is not merely sentimental: it is a recognised position in a household.** *Confidence: moderate–high*; the comparative uses are `[T]`, the social reconstruction is `[S]`.

## Extension — Original Audience

**What the first hearers knew that changes the last clause.** `[I]` **They knew who David was.** The book's final five words are not information to them but a resolution — the line they live under, traced back to a Moabite widow and a childless bitter woman. *Confidence: high* that David was known; the effect is an inference.

**What they would have heard in 4:15.** `[I]` Not primarily romance but security: a sustainer of old age. **An audience without pensions hears "better to you than seven sons" as a statement about survival.**

**What they would have felt about the naming.** `[I]` *Confidence: moderate.* The women say "a son has been born to Naomi" over a child whose mother is Moabite and alive. **An audience holding Deuteronomy 23:3 would notice that the child entering the royal genealogy is hers** — and the book has insisted on the gentilic five times (1:22, 2:2, 2:21, 4:5, 4:10) before dropping it here. `[T]` **The word "Moabitess" does not occur in this unit.** *That absence is `[T]` and was checked; what it means is not stated by the text.*

## Extension — Biblical Theology

**The emptiness-to-fullness thread, closed.** `[T]` 1:21's רֵיקָם, 2:14's שָׂבַע and יָתַר, 3:17's רֵיקָם reversed, and here 4:15's מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ — **the book's last word on the subject using the root of Naomi's own complaint.**

**The seed-and-promise thread.** `[T]` 4:12 asked for a house like Perez's "from the offspring the LORD gives you"; 4:13 has the LORD give; 4:18–22 traces Perez to David. **The request, the gift and the line are three consecutive units.**

**The redeemer thread, and where it stops.** `[T]` גֹּאֵל runs through ten verses of Ruth and **ends at 4:14, in the women's mouths.** The word's canonical centre is elsewhere — 23 of its 83 verses are in Isaiah, 15 in Leviticus. **Ruth takes a word that is legal in Leviticus and theological in Isaiah, and spends it on a man buying a field and then on a baby.** *Confidence: high on the distributions; the characterisation is `[I]`.*

**The conception thread.** `[T]` Treated at Headline 2 and Move 3: curse (Genesis 3:16), deprivation (Hosea 9:11), gift (Ruth 4:13). **Three verses, and the gift is last.**

## Extension — Difficult Verses

**Who is the גֹּאֵל at 4:14 — Boaz or the child?** `[T]` The Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous. **Boaz** has just performed the redemption and has been called גֹּאֵל throughout chapter 4; **the child** is the nearest antecedent for 4:15's "he shall be to you a restorer of life", and the Greek's "your name" reading (above) pushes the focus toward Naomi's household rather than Boaz's act. **The words do not settle it — but the commentary tradition leans further than this report first allowed.** *(Logos round 2.)* `[S: Logos]` Of the sources returned from the library, **both take the gō'ēl to be the child**: Driesbach (*Cornerstone*, 545) argues the redeemer is the newborn who continues the family line, on the strength of what verse 15 goes on to say; Farmer (*New Interpreter's Bible*, 2:940–941) also reads the child as Naomi's redeemer. **The division they represent is not Boaz-against-child but a division over the *sense* in which the child redeems** — the legal-genealogical function (the heir who maintains the dead man's name with his property) against the personal-relational one (the one who reverses the emptiness that embittered her). *Two commentaries is a thin base and the question stays open; but "commentators divide between Boaz and the child" is not what the library shows.* *My own reading, offered as a reading:* the blessing moves from the LORD (4:14a) to a redeemer provided (4:14b) to the child's function (4:15a), and the simplest construal takes the child as the subject of 4:15 without requiring him to be the גֹּאֵל of 4:14. **Confidence: low. Flagged, not resolved**, and a preacher should say that the verse can be taken either way rather than choose silently.

**Does Naomi displace Ruth?** `[T]` The facts are these and they are uncomfortable: the blessing is addressed to Naomi; Naomi takes the child; the women say a son is born to Naomi; **and Ruth is not mentioned after 4:13.** **But the same blessing names Ruth as its reason and calls her better than seven sons** — the women's highest statement about anybody in the book. `[I]` *My reading:* the unit honours Ruth by making her the ground of a blessing she does not receive, which is of a piece with a book whose heroine never once asks for anything for herself. **Confidence: moderate**, and the discomfort is real enough that a sermon should name it rather than smooth it.

**Is 4:16 an adoption?** Treated under Tool 12. **The text says nurse, not mother**; the legal question is contested and these five verses do not decide it. `[I]`

**Why does the narrator not report Naomi's response?** `[T]` He does not, and **her last recorded speech in the book is at 3:18.** **The silence is the text's**, and filling it with joy, tears or a restored theology is reconstruction. *Flagged, not resolved.*

## Extension — Christological Reading

**Both earlier legs converge here, and the convergence is the finding.** `[I]`

The 1:19–22 report established the **contrast** leg: a woman who said God had testified against her, wrongly. The 2:1–16 report established the **type** leg with four textual features: a redeemer who keeps the law, exceeds it at his own cost, acts for one with no claim, and conceals it from her. **This unit supplies the third element — the line** — and it does so in five words the characters never hear (4:17b).

**What the trajectory runs on here.** `[I]` A child born to a foreign woman, named by neighbours, held by a woman who is not his mother, and placed in the genealogy of the king. **Every one of those is `[T]`.** The trajectory is not that Obed is a type of Christ — the text makes no such claim and he does nothing in the story — but that **the line runs through the household the book has spent four chapters rescuing, and through a Moabite.** *Stated as a trajectory reading, not as a claim about the author's intent.*

**The moralism trap, in its last and subtlest form.** `[I]` This passage will preach as "God rewards faithfulness" — Ruth was loyal, so she got a husband and a son. **The text resists it at two points.** The reward in 4:15 is given **to Naomi**, who was not loyal and said so. And the thing the narrator says God gave is **conception**, which nobody earned. **A sermon that makes the birth a payment has reversed the direction the book runs in.**

**What this passage will not bear.** `[T]` Obed as a Christ-figure. He is a baby who is named and then listed. **The overview's trap 5 applies at full strength: the typology is in the shape of the book's redemption, not in this child's person.**

## Preaching Pitfalls

**1. Preaching the child as the answer and dropping Ruth.** `[T]` The blessing's own כִּי clause makes the daughter-in-law the reason, and **the book's only occurrence of "love" is in that clause.** A sermon on 4:13–17 that does not quote 4:15b has omitted the women's actual verdict.

**2. Making the birth a reward for faithfulness.** `[T]` The blessing is addressed to Naomi, who had said God testified against her; and the thing God is said to give is conception. **The direction of the book is gift, not payment.**

**3. Claiming a verbal echo at 4:14 of 1:5.** `[T]` "She was left" and "he has not left you" are **different Hebrew verbs**. The correspondence of sense is real and worth preaching; the verbal echo does not exist.

**4. Choosing silently between Boaz and the child as the redeemer of 4:14.** `[T]` The text is ambiguous and the versions do not resolve it. **Say that it can be taken either way.**

**5. Preaching 4:16 as an adoption.** `[T]` The word is אֹמֶנֶת, nurse or guardian — **one of only two feminine participles of that verb in the canon, the other being Mephibosheth's nurse at 2 Samuel 4:4** *(corrected v1.1)* — and the naming at 4:17 is done by neighbours. **The legal question is not settled by these verses.**

**6. Supplying Naomi's feelings.** `[T]` She does not speak in this unit, and her last words in the book are at 3:18. **The restraint is the text's and the sermon should respect it** — the three verbs of 4:16 carry more if nothing is added to them.

**7. Treating "a son has been born to Naomi" as the narrator's statement.** `[T]` לֵאמֹר marks it as the women's quoted speech. **It is the town's verdict, which is the point.**

**8. Preaching a five-fold inclusio as established.** `[I]` Each of the individual echoes is high-confidence and may be preached. **The claim that chapter 4's last scene was built as a systematic answer to chapter 1 has had no baseline** and is, per the overview's trap 7, a proposal.

## Text-First Declaration

The Hebrew of Ruth 4 was read from the corpus before any English version and before any secondary source. Every count, chain and collocation in this report was made against the **Westminster Leningrad Codex** lemma index, **verified by lemma with the homograph suffix carried**, at each individual reference; the corpus verse total (23,213) was printed and checked before any canon-wide count, and **a key control — asserting that the suffix-aware key matches a verse a lemma is known to occupy — was run before any result was used**, per the silent-zero fault recorded in the 2:1–16 report.

**Two faults were caught during this run, and both were mine rather than the index's.**

The first was the **markup-as-text trap** named in the method log. The פ at the end of 4:17 is the corpus's own paragraph marker, and an unpointed-word scan reports it as a word. A second attempt to count the book's paragraph markers then failed in the opposite direction: a character-class search for samekh and pe matched **those letters inside ordinary words**, returning 35 verses of Ruth as though each carried a marker. **The correct test is for standalone one-letter tokens, and it was validated by a positive control on Leviticus 23, which returned seven markers there.** On that test, Ruth carries exactly one.

The second was **two false positives from a prefix-permissive surface pattern**. Searching for אֹמֶנֶת admitted **נֶאֱמֶנֶת** at Psalm 89:29 — a nifal meaning "stands firm", a different stem and sense — and searching for שְׁכֵנוֹת admitted **six occurrences of מִשְׁכָּן ("dwelling")**. **Both were removed by checking the lemma and by reading the words, and the claims about the nurse-form and the neighbour-word stand only because of that check.** Together with the suffix-aware-key fault of the previous run, the lesson is consistent: **a surface search must constrain its prefixes and be cross-checked against the lemma before any count from it is reportable.**

Every absence claim passed a **positive control run on the identical search**, per the correction recorded in the 4:1–12 report at v1.1: the paragraph-marker test was controlled on Leviticus 23; the claim that this unit contains no ketiv was made by scanning all 22 verses of Ruth 4 and returning the three that do (4:4, 4:5, 4:6); the claim that the gentilic and the legal vocabulary are absent here was made against distributions established and verified in the earlier reports.

The words *only*, *alone* and *unique* are used of seven measurements, each licensed by the search actually run: the four one-verse collocations at 4:14, 4:15 (twice) and 4:16; the two-verse יֶלֶד distribution; the single occurrences of אָהַב, נֶפֶשׁ, שֵׂיבָה and כּוּל in the book; and the feminine form אֹמֶנֶת, established by reading every lemma-539 participle in the canon. **One shape claim is made and one is withdrawn.** An ordered-phrase search, run plain and skeletal, established that וְיִקָּרֵא שְׁמוֹ stands in seven verses — **and that Genesis 48:16 is not among them**, so the Genesis 48 contact is reported as a four-verse lemma contact and **not** as a shared phrase. Where lemmas correspond without shared wording, the report says so: at 4:14 against 1:5, the verbs are simply different, and that is stated as a non-link.

One claim is **capped by the design-claim rule** and named as the audit's target: the five-fold correspondence between this unit and chapter 1 as deliberate closing design. **The individual echoes are high-confidence; the pattern has no baseline.** The **BHS** apparatus for Ruth 4 (Logos export) was consulted for the variants and the ketiv/qere and is the edition cited; the Greek is **Rahlfs** (Logos export), read in the export itself. Swete was not opened. Secondary sources — the book overview, the claim audit, the Logos round, and **all five** neighbouring solo digs — were consulted only at Phase 5.5, after the tools were worked.
