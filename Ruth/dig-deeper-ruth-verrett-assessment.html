v2.0 · audited. Four GearTalk talks, transcribed by the user, weighed against this project’s completed Ruth work: ten solo dig-deeper runs, the whole-book sweep, four claim audits, three Logos rounds, the v2.0 book overview and the macro-synthesis. Text-first; warrant-tagged; confidence-flagged.

Three things in v1.0 were wrong and are now fixed:

  • §3.1 — the לֶחֶם reading. The count held; the reading did not. Ruth 2:14 contains no verb of giving. What v1.0 called “the single most useful item in the four talks” was a sermon sentence with nothing under it.
  • §4 — the Exodus 15 finding is not new. It is Brad Embry, JTI 7:2 (2013), 262–264, and v1.0’s “nine places” was a tool artefact whose own breakdown summed to ten. The novelty claim is withdrawn — the third time this project has had to do that.
  • §2.6 — the Shaddai count. “Five of six” needed the next verse to do unacknowledged work; three is the figure, and Gen 43:14 inverts the theme rather than lacking it.

Two things were overstated and are now capped: §3.2 (the pillar is a datum, not a gloss — the canon glosses neither Jachin nor Boaz) and §5.6 (a common-noun nil cannot refute a name-pun).

Everything in §6 is strengthened, not weakened. The auditors’ measurements are far more damaging to Verrett’s designs than v1.0’s reasoning was, and §6 now carries them. The design claims went 0 of 6, bringing this project’s record to 124 tested and one surviving.

And three real contacts were recovered from underneath the designs — see §4A. That is the round’s most valuable output, and none of them is Verrett’s.

How this was done, and what it is not

Verrett’s four talks were inventoried into thirty-one testable claims — lexical, structural, intertextual and theological. Each was put to the WLC lemma index in _texts/hebrew-wlc/, accent- and maqqef-insensitively, and then set beside whatever this project had already established on the same ground.

Editions. Every count below is from the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures morphhb) unless named otherwise — the same manuscript BHS prints, but not BHS’s apparatus. Greek is Swete for the OT and SBLGNT for the NT. No finding here turns on a variant or an apparatus reading, so no Logos citation of record was required; the two places where one would be are flagged in §8.

This is not a claim audit. The two blind auditors have not been run over Verrett. My gate is the main session’s own, which audit 3 showed is the weaker of the two: the homograph trap fired on the main session as well as on both auditors in a single sitting. Verrett’s claims are therefore queued for audit 4 (§9). Where I say confirmed, I mean confirmed at main-session level against the WLC, which is enough to stop an error reaching a sermon and not enough to settle a design claim.

A note on the name. The transcript’s byline reads “Brian Venner”; the user’s note and the podcast give Verrett, which is used throughout. GearTalk Biblical Theology is associated with Hands to the Plow and jasonderouchie.com [S: web] — which places these talks in the same biblical-theology school as the Gentry article this project assessed on 11 September 2026, where the argument also ran through DeRouchie’s discourse grammar. That is worth knowing before weighing the method, and it is the only biographical claim made here.

2. Confirmed exactly

Thirteen claims where the text says what he says it says. These are listed because a preacher can use them tomorrow, and because the hit rate matters when judging the man.

2.1 — The three two-member chains. This project’s second macro-argument is that the book argues by two-member chains, planted in chapter 1. Verrett found three of them without the tools:

  • יֶלֶד (“child”) stands in exactly two verses of Ruth — 1:5 and 4:16 [T]. Verrett: “The word used for Mahlon and Chilion is the same word that is used when Obed is born.” Correct, and it is the whole distribution. He also sees what it does — the children lost answered by the child held.
  • רֵיקָם (“empty”) stands in exactly two — 1:21 and 3:17 [T]. Verrett quotes both and draws the line between them: “You must end her emptiness.”
  • כָּנָף (“wing”) stands in exactly two — 2:12 and 3:9 [T]. Verrett: “Ruth came to seek refuge under the Lord’s wings and finds refuge under His wings by taking refuge under Boaz’s wings.” That is the project’s own reading of the pair, reached independently.

Three for three, on a device he nowhere names. High confidence on all three counts.

2.2 — The famine clause reaches the patriarchs. Verrett: “The mention of a famine brings to mind the famines faced by Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.” וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ as an ordered clause stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Gen 12:10, Gen 26:1 and Ruth 1:1 [T]. High confidence. He is right, and §3.4 shows he is righter than he states.

2.3 — אֵשֶׁת חַיִל. The ordered phrase stands in exactly three verses — Prov 12:4, Prov 31:10, Ruth 3:11 [T], on the plain pass and the consonant-skeleton pass alike. Verrett: “‘Excellent wife’ and ‘worthy woman’ are the same.” Correct.

2.4 — Proverbs 31:29 says daughters. רַבּוֹת בָּנוֹת עָשׂוּ חָיִל [T]. Verrett: “Literally it says, ‘many daughters have done excellently.’” Correct, and a good catch — every English version prints “women”.

2.5 — Ruth 3:11 says gate. כִּי יוֹדֵעַ כָּל־שַׁעַר עַמִּי [T]. Verrett: “A more literal translation has Boaz saying… ‘all the gate of my people know that you are a worthy woman.’” Correct — and this project’s sweep found that no version in any language carries שַׁעַר at 3:11, which names the venue of chapter 4 before the reader knows there will be a scene there. Verrett is the only outside reader in view who has noticed.

2.6 — Shaddai and fruitfulness. Verrett: “in the first few times that the name El Shaddai occurs in the Bible, it is linked with fruitfulness and bearing children (e.g., Gen 28:3; 35:11; 49:25).” שַׁדַּי stands in 48 verses of the canon, six of them in Genesis — 17:1, 28:3, 35:11, 43:14, 48:3, 49:25 [T]. (v2.0 — corrected by audit 4; v1.0 said “five of the six” and that needed the next verse to do unacknowledged work.) Three of the six put the fruitfulness word in the same verse as the name: 28:3 and 35:11 — the only two canon verses pairing שַׁדַּי with פָּרָה [T] — and 49:25 (blessings of breasts and womb, on a pun with שָׁדַיִם). Gen 17:1 needs 17:6 and Gen 48:3 needs 48:4, five verses and one verse away respectively. High confidence on the three; the wider reading is a reading of six printed verses, not a count.

And the sixth is better than either Verrett or v1.0 made it. Gen 43:14 does not stand outside the pattern — it inverts it: כַּאֲשֶׁר שָׁכֹלְתִּי שָׁכָלְתִּי, “as I have been bereaved, I am bereaved”, with the name invoked over the loss of children in order to get sons back [S: audit 4]. That is the more interesting datum, and calling it the exception gets it backwards — which matters here, because Naomi does precisely the same thing with the same name at Ruth 1:20–21.

2.7 — The verb at 1:4 is “lift”. וַיִּשְׂאוּ לָהֶם נָשִׁים [T]. Verrett: “The Hebrew literally says, ‘They pick up (נשׂא) wives.’” The form is right. The reading he puts on it is not — see §5.4.

2.8 — Both endings land on David. Ruth 4:17 ends אֲבִי דָוִד; 4:22 ends אֶת־דָּוִד [T]. Verrett: “the narrative ends with the word ‘David’ (Ruth 4:17). The book also has a genealogy that ends with the word ‘David’ (4:22).” Exactly right, and a genuinely fine observation.

2.9 — Ten generations. Perez, Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Nahshon, Salmon, Boaz, Obed, Jesse, David [T]. Ten. Correct.

2.10 — Baba Batra 14b puts Ruth first among the Writings. Confirmed independently by this project’s Logos round 1: Baba Batra 14b places Ruth before Psalms on the rabbis’ chronological reasoning that Samuel wrote it and David came of Ruth’s line (Queen-Sutherland, Ruth & Esther, 2; Block, Ruth, ZECOT, 47) [S: Logos L1].

2.11 — Leningrad puts Ruth after Proverbs. Confirmed: Codex Leningradensis, and therefore BHS, place Ruth between Proverbs and Qoheleth (Block, 47) [S: Logos L1]. This is the order this project’s own standing rule follows. Verrett calls it “probably the majority view” among OT scholars, which is loose — it is the Leningrad sequence, which is why it is the one scholars meet.

2.12 — שָׂבַע at 2:14. The root stands once in Ruth, at 2:14 [T]. Verrett cites it correctly for the fullness theme, and 2:14 is the right verse to hang it on — see §4.

2.13 — Boaz exceeds what he is asked. Verrett: “Boaz tells her that she may even glean among the sheaves… He also tells his men to pull out bundles of grain for her. Boaz is being amazingly kind.” This project’s first macro-argument puts a measurement under it: עָזַב with לָקַט in one verse stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Lev 19:10, Lev 23:22 and Ruth 2:16 [T] — and Boaz’s order exceeds the statute, which commands leaving the standing edge and the fallen gleanings, not pulling grain out of the bundles. Verrett sees the generosity; the project can say by how much.

3. Confirmed, and he is righter than he knows

Six places where the measurement strengthens him, usually by supplying the hard datum his rhetoric is reaching for.

3.1 — לֶחֶם stands in exactly two verses of Ruth — and the second half of what v1.0 said about them is false. Verrett’s governing claim about Boaz is that “whatever the Lord does, Boaz does… In chapter one, the Lord gives bread; in chapters two and three, Boaz gives bread.” לֶחֶם stands in exactly two verses of the book — 1:6 and 2:14 [T]. High confidence on the count.

(v2.0 — corrected by audit 4, and this was the most damaging error in v1.0.) v1.0 read those two verses as parallel donations with the giver changed, and called it the best thing in the four talks. Ruth 2:14 contains no verb of giving at all. Its verbs are אמר, נגש, אכל, טבל, ישב, צבט, אכל, שבע, יתר; the bread is governed by וְאָכַלְתְּ מִן־הַלֶּחֶם — “eat of the bread”, partitive — and the one thing Boaz actually hands over, וַיִּצְבָּט־לָהּ, has קָלִי (roasted grain) as its object [T].

The better form, which is sounder and still carries Verrett’s point. The two acts are not a symmetry but a sequence: at 1:6 YHWH gives (נתן) bread to עַמּוֹ in Judah — which Naomi only hears about, and she is not yet among the fed — and at 2:14 Boaz invites Ruth to eat from bread that is already there. The bread YHWH gave is the bread she eats at his table. The echo-table row is reworded, not raised [S: audit 4].

Why the error is worth leaving on the page. “YHWH gives bread at 1:6, Boaz gives bread at 2:14” is a sermon sentence, and it would have preached. It is the project’s named error class in its purest form — a count settled correctly, then described in words the count cannot see — and it passed a main-session gate before an auditor caught it.

3.2 — Boaz is the name of a pillar. Verrett derives Boaz from “strength” and builds his whole Exodus 15 argument on it (§4), without ever citing the one place the canon uses the name of something whose function is to hold weight. בֹּעַז stands in 24 occurrences across 22 verses — eighteen verses in Ruth and four outside it: 1 Kgs 7:21, where Solomon names the left-hand pillar of the temple portico בֹּעַז; 2 Chr 3:17, which repeats it; and 1 Chr 2:11 and 2:12 in the genealogy [T]. High confidence on the distribution. (v2.0 — capped by audit 4.) A pillar called Boaz is a datum, not a gloss: the canon nowhere explains either Jachin or Boaz, and the index files man and pillar under one lemma with no homograph sub-entries, so the pillar cannot be made to do an etymology’s work [S: audit 4]. Both verses name the left/northern pillar. (Corrected 18 November, before the audit: this section first read “nineteen in Ruth, 1 Chr 2:11, 2:12 — and 1 Kgs 7:21”, which mis-assigned one Chronicles verse to Ruth and dropped 2 Chr 3:17 from the enumeration while naming it in the next clause. The verse total was right and the breakdown was not — the project’s own recorded error class, caught only on re-reading the lemma output to write the audit brief. See §8.1: this is the second kind of count the method says will drift, and it drifted.) A pillar called Boaz is the strongest canonical support available for the sense Verrett needs, and he does not use it.

3.3 — חַיִל is already Boaz’s word before it is Ruth’s. Verrett’s Proverbs 31 case rests on אֵשֶׁת חַיִל at 3:11. חַיִל stands in exactly three verses of Ruth — 2:1 (אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל, of Boaz), 3:11 (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל, of Ruth), 4:11 (עֲשִׂי־חָיִל, to Boaz) [T]. High confidence. He quotes 2:1 (“a man of substance… a worthy, upright, strong, thick substance man”) and 4:11 (“may you act worthily in Ephrathah”) without noticing that all three are one noun.

This matters for his argument, both ways. It weakens the Proverbs quotation reading — the book is running its own internal pairing, Boaz and Ruth matched by a single word — and it strengthens the relation, because עָשָׂה with חַיִל stands in six verses, two of them Prov 31:29 and Ruth 4:11 [T]. So the Proverbs acrostic touches Ruth at two points, not one, and the second is the verse Verrett quotes for a different purpose.

3.4 — The famine clause is pointed, not merely evocative. The three verses carrying וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ are not three neutral famines. At Gen 12:10 Abram goes down to Egypt, and it goes badly. At Gen 26:1 the clause is immediately followed by God forbidding Isaac to go down. At Ruth 1:1 a man of Bethlehem goes. High confidence on the count; the reading is [I] and moderate. Verrett’s “brings to mind the famines” is true and tame; the canon has set up a question about leaving, and answered it once in each direction, before Elimelech moves.

3.5 — The gate contact lands on Proverbs 31:23, not 31:31. Verrett’s neatest Proverbs claim is that Ruth 3:11 “combines the very first and very last lines” of the acrostic — 31:10’s אֵשֶׁת חַיִל and 31:31’s “praise her in the gates”. The first half is exact (§2.3). The second half lands one verse short of where he puts it: יָדַע with שַׁעַר in one verse stands in four verses of the WLC — Josh 2:5, Amos 5:12, Prov 31:23 (“her husband is known in the gates”) and Ruth 3:11 (“all the gate of my people know”) [T]. High confidence. Prov 31:31 has the gates but not the knowing; 31:23 has both, in Ruth’s own pairing. The contact is real and tighter than he claims; the “first and last line” framing is rhetoric and should go.

3.6 — The elders invoke the one story that uses the word Ruth refuses. Verrett is right that Genesis 38 matters, and right that Ruth 4:12 is the author telling you so. This project’s 4:1–12 dig sharpens it to a point he does not reach: the masculine levirate term יָבָם stands in three verses of the canon — Deut 25:5, Deut 25:7 and Gen 38:8 — and Ruth never uses it [T]. So the elders, at the one moment a legal remedy is actually transacted, bless the union by naming the only narrative in the canon that does carry the technical vocabulary this book has declined [T] for the counts, [I] for the reading. Moderate–high.

That is Verrett’s instinct, vindicated and made measurable — and it is also the stick that breaks his own frame, because the reason Ruth avoids יָבָם is that chapter 4 is not a levirate case (§5.1).

4. His best contribution: Exodus 15 — published in 2013, and it does not survive the audit

(v2.0 — this section is the one v1.0 got most wrong, in two different directions. It claimed novelty the finding does not have, and it credited an exodus frame the measurements refuse.)

This project had never tested it, and that was the problem. A search of all fourteen Ruth documents for Exodus 15, for קָנָה against גָּאַל, for בְּעָזְּךָ or for any discussion of Boaz’s etymology returns nothing. v1.0 therefore measured it from scratch and called it new.

It is not new. It is Brad Embry, “‘Redemption-Acquisition’: The Marriage of Ruth as a Theological Commentary on Yahweh and Yahweh’s People,” Journal of Theological Interpretation 7:2 (2013), 262–264 [S: Logos Q7]. Embry has the verb pair, has חֶסֶד and עֹז as further affinities, has the Boaz / בְּעָזְּךָ link — which he frames as “resonates phonetically” rather than as wordplay — and has three contacts neither Verrett nor this project had: Moab at Exod 15:15, נַחֲלָה at Exod 15:17, and Marah at Exod 15:23 against Ruth 1:20. JTI is in the library, so the article is readable.

This is the third novelty claim this project has withdrawn for want of asking first, after L7 and L8. The question belongs in every Logos round, not at the end of one.

His claim. Ruth 4:5–6 pairs קָנָה (“acquire”) with גָּאַל (“redeem”); Exod 15:13–16 applies both to what YHWH did at the sea; and Exod 15:13’s “by your strength” is בְּעָזְּךָ, which is the name Boaz. Therefore “the Lord’s new Exodus work is through Boaz.”

What the text gives.

Exod 15:13 — נָחִיתָ בְחַסְדְּךָ עַם־זוּ גָּאָלְתָּ נֵהַלְתָּ בְעָזְּךָ אֶל־נְוֵה קָדְשֶׁךָ

Exod 15:16 — … עַד־יַעֲבֹר עַם־זוּ קָנִיתָ

Three findings, all [T], all high confidence on the count.

  1. The two verbs are bound by a formula confined to this poem. עַם־זוּ (“this people”) stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Exod 15:13, Exod 15:16 and Isa 43:21. In the two Exodus verses the formula is completed by a second-person perfect, and the two verbs chosen are גָּאָלְתָּ and קָנִיתָ. So the Song of the Sea says this people you redeemed and this people you acquired, in identical syntax, three verses apart. The pairing Verrett claims is not loose thematic company; it is a matched couplet. He states it as “the same exact language” without showing the formula that binds it.

  2. The pair in one verse is rare, and two of its four verses are Ruth’s. גָּאַל with קָנָה in one verse stands in exactly four verses of the canon — Lev 25:30, Ps 74:2, Ruth 4:4 and Ruth 4:8 [T]. And Ps 74:2 is itself exodus language — “remember your congregation, which you acquired of old, the tribe of your inheritance, which you redeemed” — running both verbs as 2ms qal perfects in a single verse, so nothing about the Exodus 15 syntax is unique to the Song [S: audit 4]. High confidence.

    (v2.0 — v1.0’s “nine places within four verses” is withdrawn.) It was an artefact of find.py near’s greedy windowing, and its own breakdown convicted it: 1 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 2 sums to ten against a stated nine, because the tool gives four Leviticus 25 windows and v1.0 wrote five. The replacement is tool-independent: גָּאַל and קָנָה stand within four verses of one another in exactly five chapters of the Hebrew Bible — Exodus 15, Leviticus 25, Leviticus 27, Psalm 74, Ruth 4 — with zero cross-chapter contacts anywhere in the canon [S: audit 4].

    And the company is not evenly weighted, which v1.0 missed. Leviticus 25 against the whole of Ruth scores 0.218, with 12 pairs over 9 distinct partner verses, carried by exactly גָּאַל and קָנָה — six times Exodus 15’s figure, on precisely these verbs [S: audit 4]. Leviticus 25 is the centre of gravity, not the Song. One further nuance that cuts against the reading: Lev 25:30 is an antithesis, not a pair — niphal יִגָּאֵל (“if it is not redeemed”) against the participle לַקֹּנֶה (“to the buyer”), redeemer versus purchaser.

  3. The name claim is a consonantal pun, and must be stated as one. בְּעָזְּךָ is the preposition בְּ + the noun עֹז + a second-person suffix. בֹּעַז is a proper noun, lemma 1162, morphologically unrelated and differently pointed. The consonants ב־ע־ז coincide; the words do not. Verrett’s “It’s the name for Boaz!” is false as morphology and available as paronomasia [I], and the honest form is: the Song of the Sea says YHWH led his redeemed people בְּעָזְּךָ, “by your strength”, and the man through whom he feeds and redeems them in Ruth is called Boaz — a name Solomon gave to a pillar. That is preachable. “It is the same word” is not.

Verdict, after audit 4: the verb pair holds and the Exodus 15 frame does not.

Exodus 15 against Ruth 4 — the target the claim needs — scores 0.000. Zero rare pairs, rank 229, in the tied-zero floor [S: audit 4]. Against the whole of Ruth it manages 0.037: one pair, one distinct partner verse, and dropping חֶסֶד takes it to zero. Reversed, 193 chapters of the canon beat Ruth’s best figure against Exodus 15.

The obvious defence is removed by measurement. Exodus 15 is not a verse-dense poem that scores against everything: 67.6% of canon chapters return zero against it, and its top partner is Num 33 at 0.571 — the itinerary that genuinely re-narrates Marah and Elim. It finds a real partner when it has one. It does not find Ruth.

And Embry’s own extra elements do not survive. Marah is falsified at the lemma level — Ruth 1:20’s מָרָא is lemma 4755, a canon hapax; Exod 15:23’s מָרָה is lemma 4785; two entries, not one [S: audit 4]. חֶסֶד, נַחֲלָה and Moab are furniture at 245, 222 and 181 canonical occurrences. So his evidence does not support his conclusion. It supports Ruth 4 being shaped by Leviticus 25 — which needs no Song of the Sea to explain it.

What stands: the four-verse verb pair, the five-chapter window, and the Leviticus 25 relation — moderate–high, as a redemption-vocabulary finding and not an exodus one. What falls: the Exodus 15 source claim, and with it the frame Verrett built on it. The name identification is low and §6.5 now caps it harder than v1.0 did.

4A. What the audit recovered from underneath the designs

(v2.0 — new. This is the round’s most valuable output, and none of it is Verrett’s.) Four of the six design claims had a genuine rare contact buried in them, which the design was hiding. The pattern is by now this project’s most reliable finding about itself.

  • Ruth 1:6 ~ Exodus 4:31. כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה stands in exactly four verses of the Hebrew Bible — Exod 4:31, 1 Sam 2:21, Zech 10:3, Ruth 1:6 [T] — and Exod 4:31 and Ruth 1:6 are the two where the object is the afflicted people as a collective. In both, what follows the visitation is a departure. A clean, rare, checkable allusion at the book’s hinge. Moderate–high on the phrase; L7’s literature verdict stands, so no exodus frame may be built on it.
  • Ruth 2:20 ~ Exodus 15:13. חֶסֶד with גָּאַל in one verse stands in exactly four verses — Exod 15:13, Isa 54:8, Ps 103:4, Ruth 2:20 [T]. The right verse was identified for the wrong reason: if anything ties Ruth to the Song it is 2:20, where redemption and covenant loyalty arrive in one breath and the redemption plot is born — not a name sharing three consonants with the next clause.
  • Ruth 3:3 and 3:9 ~ Ezekiel 16:8–9, and this is the find of the round. פָּרַשׂ with כָּנָף of a person stands in exactly two verses — Ezek 16:8 and Ruth 3:9 (nine of its twelve canonical instances are cherubim or an eagle); רָחַץ with סוּךְ stands in exactly three — 2 Sam 12:20, Ezek 16:9, Ruth 3:3 [T]. Ezekiel carries both of Ruth 3’s signature gestures in consecutive verses, in a betrothal scene. Ruth 3:3 shares not one lexeme with Gen 38:14. The overview already has the כָּנָף half at row 13; the wash-and-anoint half joining it next door is new, and makes this the best-attested source relation in the book.
  • Ruth 4 follows Deuteronomy, not Genesis 38. Gen 38:8 is הָקֵם זֶרַע — raise up seed — and Ruth is not among the thirteen verses carrying קוּם + זֶרַע. Ruth 4:5 and 4:10 are לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת — raise up the name — which is Deuteronomy 25:7’s formula [T]. This single distinction locates Ruth 4 better than the whole eighteen-row table.

5. Errors on the text

Nine. Four are trivial slips of reference; five would damage a sermon.

5.1 — Ruth 4 is not levirate marriage. This is his most consequential error.

Verrett calls it levirate throughout: “An ancient practice called Levirate marriage… (Dt 25:5-10)”; “In both stories, the purpose of the Levirate marriage is to raise up offspring for a dead brother”; “A transaction is made to validate the Levirate marriage”.

This project’s measurement is decisive, and it is [T] four times over:

  • The levirate term enters Ruth once, in chapter 1, and never in chapter 4. יָבָם stands in three verses (Deut 25:5, 25:7, Gen 38:8) and Ruth never uses it; יְבָמָה stands in three (Deut 25:7, 25:9, Ruth 1:15, where it occurs twice) — inside Naomi’s argument that levirate remedy is impossible. The technical vocabulary is present only where the book is explaining why the institution cannot help.
  • The governing verb of Ruth 4 is absent from Deuteronomy 25. גָּאַל does not occur in Deuteronomy 25 at all; in the whole book the participle stands only at 19:6 and 19:12, of the גֹּאֵל הַדָּם, the avenger of blood — a different institution.
  • The sandal idioms do not meet. חָלַץ with נַעַל stands in three verses (Deut 25:9, 25:10, Isa 20:2); שָׁלַף with נַעַל stands in two, Ruth 4:7 and 4:8, and nowhere else.
  • And the case does not fit the law. The redeemer is not a brother, the widow removes no sandal, and the act is tied to the field.

This is preaching trap 5 in the book overview, by name: “The legal label. Calling 4:1–12 ‘levirate marriage’.” Verrett walks into it and builds on it. The correction is not pedantry: the whole force of the chapter is that a man discharges an obligation the statute does not impose on him, which is also the route this project takes to Christ. Calling it levirate converts a free act into a legal duty and throws the sermon away.

5.2 — The name is raised for Mahlon, not Elimelech; and Boaz is not Elimelech’s brother. Verrett’s Genesis 38 table reads: “To raise up a son for Elimelech, Boaz’s brother (4:5).” Ruth 4:10 names the dead man: וְגַם אֶת־רוּת הַמֹּאֲבִיָּה אֵשֶׁת מַחְלוֹן — “Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of Mahlon” [T]. The name raised on the inheritance is Mahlon’s. And Boaz is מִמִּשְׁפַּחַת אֱלִימֶלֶךְ, “of the clan of Elimelech” (2:1) [T]; the word “brother” at 4:3 (אָחִינוּ) is clan usage in Boaz’s own mouth, not kinship. High confidence. Both halves of the table cell are wrong.

5.3 — Boaz calls Ruth “my daughter” three times, not twice. Verrett: “Boaz calls Ruth ‘my daughter’ twice”, with a footnote giving 2:8 and 3:10. בַּת stands in eleven verses of Ruth; Boaz’s בִּתִּי is at 2:8, 3:10 and 3:11 [T]. He quotes 3:11 himself, twice, in the same talk. High confidence. A small error that matters only because the count is doing rhetorical work.

5.4 — “They picked up wives” reads a dead idiom as a live metaphor. Verrett: “The Hebrew literally says, ‘They pick up (נשׂא) wives.’ They lift up wives. They ‘lift’ some wives for themselves.” נָשָׂא with אִשָּׁה in one verse stands in 23 verses of the WLC, and the subset meaning “marry” clusters in Judg 21:23, Ruth 1:4, Ezra 10:44, 2 Chr 11:21, 13:21 and 24:3 [T] — a marriage idiom, concentrated in the later books, which is why it is normally discussed as a dating marker rather than as characterisation.

This project’s own audit 3 handled the same datum and is worth quoting against both parties: the raw co-occurrence is 23 verses, not 6 — the six is sense-filtered — so the honest ratio is about 6 : 76, and BHS marks Ezra 10:44 crrp, so even the dating argument cannot rest on it [S: audit]. Verrett’s “pick up” is etymologising a formula. The project’s discarded date inference was the opposite error on the same verse. The verb at 1:4 means “married”. It says nothing about how the sons regarded their wives.

(v2.0 — and audit 4 found something here that neither party had, which the auditor rated the most valuable item in its report.) Reading all 23 verses, נָשָׂא governs אִשָּׁה as its object in exactly six, and in all six it means “marry”, with no ambiguous cases and no counter-examples — the other seventeen are unrelated clauses, eight of them “lift the eyes” or “lift the voice” [S: audit 4]. The six are Judg 21:23, Ruth 1:4, Ezra 10:44, 2 Chr 11:21, 2 Chr 13:21 and 2 Chr 24:3 — and nowhere else in the canon. That is a post-exilic register displacing the classical לָקַח אִשָּׁה, with one narrative companion: Judges 21:23, the seizing of the dancers of Shiloh. Both of Ruth 1:4’s companions concern wives obtained irregularly from outside the proper channel, and Ruth 1:4’s wives are Moabite. So the verb does say something after all — not about the sons’ regard for their wives, but about the register in which the narrator reports the marriages. [T] on the distribution; the reading [I] at moderate.

5.5 — “On the way, on the way” is one occurrence of a very common noun. Verrett quotes Ruth 1:7 as “And as they went toward the Promised Land, they went on the way, on the way” and ties it to Exod 13:17–21. דֶּרֶךְ stands in exactly one verse of Ruth — 1:7, וַתֵּלַכְנָה בַדֶּרֶךְ [T]. High confidence. The doubling is his, not the text’s; and דֶּרֶךְ is one of the commonest nouns in Hebrew. This is the weakest link in his exodus chain and it is presented as one of the strongest.

5.6 — Orpah’s “neck” is available and unactivated — and the book glosses no name but one. Verrett: Orpah “is named ‘neck,’ or, more specifically, the back of the neck, because Orpah will turn around and walk away.” The consonants of עָרְפָּה and עֹרֶף do coincide. But Ruth contains no form of the common noun עֹרֶף anywhere [T], against a 32-verse positive control, and at 1:15 Orpah’s departure is described with שׁוּב, the book’s own leading verb, not with any neck word.

(v2.0 — and audit 4 says v1.0 pressed this too hard.) A lemma nil cannot refute a name-pun, because Orpah stands at 1:4 and 1:14 under her own proper-noun lemma, 6204, and consonantally her name is עֹרֶף plus a he [S: audit 4]. The negative refutes a Leitwort reading; it does not reach Verrett’s. What still answers him is the general point below — that this book glosses no name — and not this particular count. Confidence that the pun is unexercised: lowered from high to moderate.

The general point is more serious than the particular one. Verrett’s name layer — Mahlon “little sick one”, Chilion “little finished one”, Orpah “neck”, Boaz “strength”, Naomi “sweet”, Bethlehem “house of bread” — rests on the premise he states outright: “Everybody who’s anybody in the book gets named and the names always have significance.” This project’s 4:18–22 dig found the control case: the one name-wordplay in view is Perez / פָּרַץ, and it is Genesis’s (Gen 38:29), not Ruth’s — Ruth repeats the name without glossing it [T]. Perez is the only one of the genealogy’s ten names the canon explains, and Ruth is not the book that explains it.

Ruth glosses exactly one name-meaning: Naomi / Mara at 1:20 — and that is a character renaming herself in her own mouth, not a narrator’s etymology. A book which declines to gloss any name, in a canon that glosses freely, is making a choice; a reading that supplies nine etymologies has reversed it. Bethlehem survives best, because לֶחֶם does stand in the book, twice (§3.1).

5.7 — The sandal is attestation, not a transaction enabling intimacy. Verrett: “a transaction is made so that the women can be intimate with their kinsman-redeemers… Everything will be legitimate if and when Ruth has a child with Boaz.” Ruth 4:7 presents the sandal as הַתְּעוּדָה, the attestation of a transfer, in a scene whose explicit witnesses are the elders and the people (4:9–11) [T]. It validates the conveyance of a right. Reading it as a licence for intercourse imports Genesis 38’s pledge into a legal formality that has no sexual content.

5.8 — The unnamed redeemer: Verrett supplies a verdict the narrator withholds. “This close relative is unnamed because he’s no good… He is not given the honour of a name.” The man’s stated reason is self-interested (4:6) and may fairly be called so. But the narrator evaluates nobody here, and this project’s third macro-argument is that the narrator reports one divine act in eighty-five verses and says almost nothing else in his own voice [T]. Preaching trap 4 is the same move applied to Naomi: judging her theology before the book does. The redeemer’s anonymity is a datum; “he is not given the honour of a name” is a sermon pretending to be exegesis. פְּלֹנִי אַלְמֹנִי stands in three verses of the canon and is a formula for a person deliberately not specified — which is at least as likely a narrative convention as a moral demotion.

5.9 — Four reference slips. Naomi’s astonishment and “his name is Boaz” is 2:19, not 2:16 (cited twice). Boaz being startled awake is 3:8, not 3:7. Ruth’s asking to glean is 2:2, cited as 2:7 for the “early in the morning” point — and 2:7 says מֵאָז הַבֹּקֶר, “since the morning”, not “while it was yet night”, so the Prov 31:15 (בְּעוֹד לַיְלָה) parallel is weaker than he draws it. And “Ruth is representing all of the Gentiles just as Naomi is representing all of Egypt” is plainly a transcription slip for Israel.

6. Where the method fails

Five designs. This is the heart of the assessment, and the standard applied is not scepticism but this project’s own record: 0 of 8, 1 of 14, 0 of 8 — and now 0 of 6.

(v2.0 — everything in this section is strengthened by audit 4, not weakened.) v1.0 argued these designs down from prior results and the absence of lexical support, and said at §8.4 that this should be read as “unsupported by the measurements available” rather than falsified. For three of the five, the auditors have now supplied the measurement, and it is adverse rather than merely absent. The headline figure governs everything below: nothing in the canon reaches a density of 1.0 against Ruth at any target. The book’s best partner in the whole Hebrew Bible is Judges 19 at 0.700 — one twelfth of a known relationship (2 Sam 6 → 1 Chr 13 = 8.786). Ruth has no measurable source-level relationship with anything, which is a fact about the book and not about Verrett. Full verdicts: claim audit Part 8.

The specific additions are marked in each sub-section. The best case against these verdicts is recorded at Part 8 §8.5 and is worth reading: a typology asserts shared shape, which a lemma-pair scanner cannot see, and heavy verbal overlap would falsify a typology rather than confirm it. It was still rejected, decisively, because two of Verrett’s three lexical anchors are not weak but factually wrong — and a design argued from citations that do not survive a check on the text cannot then be credited on the strength of its shape.

6.1 — The canon-position argument is withdrawn, not merely uncertain

Verrett’s first talk is built on it: three placements, each licensing a reading, and “we can see that there’s legitimacy to all of these perspectives.”

This project ran the decisive test and it went against the claim — Logos round 1, question L1, where it is recorded as “decisive, and against the claim” [S: Logos L1]:

  • Baba Batra 14b places Ruth first among the Writings, before Psalms.
  • Codex Leningradensis, and so BHS, place Ruth between Proverbs and Qoheleth.
  • Campbell states the consensus: “almost all students of the subject agree” that Ruth’s position at the head of the Writings antedates its being moved in among the Megillot (Ruth, Anchor Bible, 7:34); Webb dates that move to between the sixth and ninth centuries AD (Five Festal Garments, NSBT, 10:52).

So the Proverbs–Ruth–Song sequence postdates the text by centuries and cannot carry a claim about composition or canonical design. And the inference reverses: Campbell suggests the Leningrad placement may itself have been prompted by the אֵשֶׁת חַיִל link — Proverbs closing on the acrostic, Ruth called one at 3:11. On that reading the lexical link is the cause of the adjacency, which corroborates that the link is real and early-noticed while destroying everything built on the order.

What this does to Verrett. His Proverbs 31 material is his best work in the first talk and it survives intact — the measurements in §2.3, §2.4, §3.3 and §3.5 make it stronger than he leaves it. What does not survive is the frame: that the placement tells you how someone interpreted the book, and that reading Ruth “immediately after Proverbs 31” is a legitimate interpretive stance. The placement tells you a scribe noticed the same phrase Verrett noticed. The project’s overview states the consequence in terms: “The Proverbs-adjacency argument is withdrawn and may not be preached.”

The same applies, more mildly, to his Baba Batra material. The tractate’s reasoning is a datum about rabbinic opinion and he reports it fairly. But “the book of Ruth is being presented as an end-of-exile book” is his gloss on “the suffering of Ruth has a future of hope and redemption”, not the tractate’s claim — and he says so himself: “What that future of hope and redemption is not explained; it is just stated.” He then explains it anyway, in the direction his fourth talk needs. That is the move to watch throughout: a gap in a source filled by the thesis the source is being cited to support.

6.2 — The new-Exodus reading is the class of claim this project has falsified sixteen times

Verrett’s fourth talk argues Ruth 1 repeats the exodus: famine drives a family out, the head dies, time passes, the boys die, YHWH visits, they leave with a Gentile, they go “on the way”. Elimelech is Jacob, Moab is Egypt, Naomi is Israel, Ruth is the mixed multitude, Bethlehem is the promised land.

This project tested the nearest form of the claim and withdrew it. Claim audit 1, R4 — the Exodus–Genesis frame — proposed that Ruth frames itself between Exodus at the opening (1:6) and Genesis at the close (4:18). Verdict: needs reframing, the frame withdrawn. The reasoning: Ruth has thirteen verified Torah contacts, they run through the whole book and are densest in chapter 4, and nothing distinguishes the first and last from the eleven between them. “A frame requires the ends to be marked; here they are simply the first and last of a continuous series.”

And Verrett’s single best exodus datum has been checked in the literature. Logos round 1, L7, on Ruth 1:6 — כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה אֶת־עַמּוֹ לָתֵת לָהֶם לָחֶם, against Exod 4:31: commentators do group Ruth with the visitation vocabulary of Gen 21:1, Gen 50:24–25 and Exod 4:31 (Wilch, Concordia, 152; Driesbach, Cornerstone, 513) — “but none argues that Ruth deliberately evokes the exodus. They treat פָּקַד as shared covenantal idiom.” [S: Logos L7] The three-verse ordered-phrase count stands; the allusion reading is a possibility the wording permits and the literature does not support.

Three further measurements bear on his correspondences.

  • His “on the way” is one occurrence of דֶּרֶךְ (§5.5).
  • His “baby boys die in the foreign land” rests on יֶלֶד at 1:5 — which is real, and is a two-member chain with 4:16, inside the book [T]. Verrett sees that chain correctly in his second talk (§2.1) and then spends it twice, once on Obed and once on the slaughter of the Hebrew infants. The second use adds nothing the first did not already have, and the word’s canonical company is not Exodus 1.
  • His Naomi-as-Israel and Ruth-as-the-mixed-multitude are not lexical claims at all, and the corpus cannot test them. What it can say is that Ruth contains no form of the exodus’s own departure vocabulary at the points his scheme needs, and that Genesis as a programme was tested and discarded (R20): Genesis is Ruth’s fifth-densest partner book per chapter, behind 1 Samuel, Judges, 2 Samuel and Esther, and “the Samuel–Judges–Esther–Kings profile is what any Hebrew narrative yields” [S: audit].

(v2.0 — audit 4 measured it, and two of his three anchors turn out to be factually wrong.) Against the whole of Ruth, Exodus 1 scores 0.000, Exodus 13 scores 0.000 and Exodus 14 scores 0.000 — not one rare lemma pair between Ruth and any of the three chapters his scheme most needs; Exod 12 manages 0.039 [S: audit 4]. And:

  • Exodus 1:22 does not contain יֶלֶד. Its word is הַיִּלּוֹד, lemma 3209 — a different entry [T].
  • Exodus 13:17–21 does not contain בַּדֶּרֶךְ at all. 13:17 and 13:18 are construct (דֶּרֶךְ אֶרֶץ פְּלִשְׁתִּים, דֶּרֶךְ הַמִּדְבָּר) and 13:21 is articular; the ba‑ forms in Exodus stand at 4:24, 18:8, 23:20 and 33:3 [T].

Two of the three texts he anchors to do not contain the words he anchors with. That is a different order of problem from an unmeasured pattern, and it is what decides this section.

The verdict, stated as fairly as I can make it. Verrett’s exodus reading has one real contact in its favour, and it is not the one he leads with — see §4A: כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה at Ruth 1:6 and Exod 4:31, four verses in the canon, with a departure following the visitation in both. The Exod 15 pair of §4 is real as vocabulary and fails as an Exodus 15 relation. Everything else is correspondence of plot and role, assembled by accumulation. That is not nothing: the pattern is genuinely elegant, and an elegant pattern is this book’s characteristic trap. But the good contact does not need the design and is damaged by it — bundled into an eighteen-point typology with two broken anchors, Ruth 1:6 ~ Exod 4:31 gets discarded along with them, which on this project’s record is exactly what happens. Audit 3 found the reason the traps keep working: the metric that generates such claims is a short-unit detector, and the calibration is brutal — a real textual relationship scores 2 Sam 6 → 1 Chr 13 = 8.786, while Ruth’s best chapter-level score against anything is 0.357, the median of what an arbitrary real chapter achieves. [S: audit]

Preaching trap 7, by name: “Preaching a pattern that has not been baselined. A pattern is a sermon illustration until it has been measured.”

6.3 — The Genesis 38 table: the right source, the wrong kind of argument

Verrett’s longest piece of work is an eighteen-row table of correspondences between Genesis 38 and Ruth, and he is explicit about the inference: “All of these similarities can’t be accidental.”

He is right that Genesis 38 is live, and he is right for the right reason — the book cites it by name at 4:12 and heads its genealogy from its child at 4:18. This project rates it high that the story is invoked, moderate for any thematic parallel [S: overview, row 5], and its 4:1–12 dig reaches a sharper conclusion than Verrett’s: “By invoking Genesis 38 the elders bless a union in the category of Judah and Tamar’s — irregular, legally contested, procured by a woman’s initiative — at the exact moment the scene has demonstrated that the Deuteronomic law does not cover it.”

What the table adds, and what it costs. Several rows are real and unsurprising: both women are Gentile widows in Judah’s line; both are told to go to a parent’s house (and Ruth 1:8 / Gen 38:11 are genuinely close); both stories turn on a refusal and a transaction; both end in a birth in the same lineage. Several rows are manufactured.

  • “The same reason is given” for Onan’s refusal and the near redeemer’s. Onan refuses a levirate duty — Gen 38:8 is one of the canon’s three יָבָם verses; the near redeemer refuses a redemption (גָּאַל). The book marks the difference by vocabulary and Verrett’s table erases it.
  • “Both women get dressed in preparation” sets Tamar’s disguise as a prostitute beside Naomi’s instruction to wash and anoint. This project’s measurement sends that detail somewhere else entirely: wash with anoint stands in three verses, and with “spread the wing” the set stands only at Ezek 16:8–9; פָּרַשׂ with כָּנָף stands in twelve verses, but only Ezek 16:8 and Ruth 3:9 use it of spreading a garment-wing over a person [T]. The gesture is covenantal, not seductive — held by Campbell, Taylor and Webb [S: Logos L10]. Verrett’s row quietly imports the prostitute reading of the threshing floor that this project’s Logos round found to be unsupported: the verb at 3:8 is reflexive niphal (NIDOTTE 2:810), and the disreputable-threshing-floor background is withdrawn as overstated, resting on one contested reading of Hosea 9:1 [S: Logos L11].
  • “The leading women go to the place where the men are working”, “they meet them in secret”, “they wait until the men see them” are three rows for one event, which is how an eighteen-row table gets to eighteen.

The structural objection is the one that matters. A table of correspondences cannot distinguish a deliberate retelling from two stories about widows, levirate obligation and a disputed birth in the line of Judah — because that description already predicts most of the rows. This project met the same problem in a cleaner form at R27, where the Genesis 24 / Genesis 29 / Exodus 2 betrothal type-scene was proposed for Ruth 2: two of the three exemplars registered nothing, and Exodus 2 scored literally zero shared rare vocabulary with Ruth 2 while the positive controls passed in the same run. The type-scene was discarded; what survived was better — Ruth 2:9’s “drink from what the young men draw” uses Genesis 24’s distinctive verb in Genesis 24’s distinctive pairing, a collocation in five verses of the canon [S: audit].

That is the shape of the correction Verrett needs on Genesis 38: fewer rows, measured. The book’s own signal is the explicit citation at 4:12 plus the genealogy’s head at 4:18, and the sharpest finding available is that Perez with Tamar and Judah in one verse stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible — 1 Chr 2:4 and Ruth 4:12 [T].

(v2.0 — audit 4 measured the table, and the result is worse for it than anything v1.0 argued.)

  • Ruth 3 against Genesis 38 scores 0.000 — rank 708 — and rows 8 to 12 of the eighteen are all drawn from Ruth 3 [S: audit 4]. The chapter supplying most of the table shares not one rare lemma pair with Genesis 38.
  • Judges 14 (0.400) and 2 Samuel 13 (0.282) both outrank every chapter of Ruth against Genesis 38, as does Deuteronomy 25 (0.263).
  • Drop-one is fatal: remove the single lemma כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”) and the Gen 38 ↔︎ Ruth figure collapses from 0.167 to 0.033. One lemma carries the whole apparent partnership — and it is the word any story about a woman and her in-laws must use.
  • “The same reason is given” is refuted by vocabulary. יָבַם stands in exactly three verses — Gen 38:8, Deut 25:5, Deut 25:7 — and never in Ruth, while גָּאַל in Deuteronomy stands only at 19:6 and 19:12, of the blood-avenger. Disjoint vocabularies, disjoint institutions. The two stated reasons share one word, and it is “not”.
  • “To raise up offspring for a dead brother” is refuted precisely. See §4A: Genesis says seed, Ruth says name, and the formula Ruth uses is Deuteronomy’s.
  • And the rows are cheap. Applying Verrett’s own latitude to Genesis 19:30–38, Lot’s daughters, against Genesis 38 yields roughly nine or ten of the eighteen row-types from a narrative nobody proposes as a source — and it beats Ruth lexically on the “raise up seed” row, since Gen 19:32 and 19:34 share זֶרַע with Gen 38:8 and Ruth does not [I: audit 4]. (That comparator is the auditor’s judgement, not a measurement, and is flagged as such.)

And the table was pointing at the wrong book. Ruth 3:3 shares not one lexeme with Gen 38:14 — while Ezekiel 16:8–9 carries both of Ruth 3’s signature gestures in consecutive verses (§4A). Of the eighteen rows, perhaps three or four are independent and textually real, and the strongest of them is an explicit citation rather than a correspondence.

6.4 — The two-panel reading of chapters 2 and 3 has to suppress the book’s own keyword

Verrett: “In Ruth 2-3, the first issue is addressed, that is, food… The two chapters revolve around getting food, getting bread. They’re basically the same.” He tabulates five matching elements and assigns the family problem to chapter 4.

The plot parallel is real — consultation, going to Boaz’s place, commendation, grain, return — and as a homiletical shape it is defensible and useful. The scheme it serves is not, and the reason is measurable.

  • גָּאַל / גֹּאֵל occurs 21 times in ten verses of Ruth, none of them before 2:20 [T]. The redeemer question opens inside Verrett’s food section, at the end of chapter 2, and he notices — “Now, Naomi knows that Boaz has a legal right to marry into the family” — without letting it disturb the scheme.
  • Ruth 3:9 is not a step in a food narrative. This project’s 3:1–9 dig rates it high: it is a formal claim on a defined obligation, with its ground-clause כִּי גֹאֵל אָתָּה, “for you are a redeemer” [T].
  • לֶחֶם does not occur in chapter 3 at all (§3.1). The chapter’s grain is שְׂעֹרָה; “bread” is not its word.
  • And chapter 3 has its own internal frame, which argues it is a unit rather than a panel. מִי־אַתְּ stands in exactly two verses of Ruth — 3:9 in the dark and 3:16 at dawn [T], and יָטַב binds 3:1 to 3:7 to 3:10 [T]. Chapter 3 is bracketed by its own question.

(v2.0 — audit 4 quantified it, and found an inversion v1.0 missed.) גָּאַל / גֹּאֵל by chapter: ch. 1 — 0 · ch. 2 — 1 · ch. 3 — 7 · ch. 4 — 13 [T]. Chapter 3 carries seven occurrences of the redeemer root against chapter 2’s one, so the family problem is not reserved for chapter 4 — chapter 3 is about almost nothing else. And the proposal’s first matching element runs backwards: at 2:2 Ruth proposes to Naomi; at 3:1 Naomi proposes to Ruth. The initiative reverses [T] — a chiastic inversion, which is a better literary observation than the parallel it is offered as, and incompatible with two panels of the same story. The right description is an ascending pair: the same staging used twice to move from food to family, with 2:20 as the pivot that plants the second theme inside the first [S: audit 4].

Verdict. The sequence parallel stands as narrative observation [I], moderate. “They’re basically the same” is false: chapter 2 ends by naming the redeemer and chapter 3 is the claim on him. Verrett’s “bread and a boy” is clean, memorable, and buys its cleanness by holding גֹּאֵל out of the first three chapters, where the text has it from 2:20. A preacher who follows the scheme will arrive at chapter 4 having never introduced the book’s second keyword.

6.5 — The character typology, and Obed

Obed is a new David figure; Naomi is Israel; Ruth is the Gentiles; Boaz is the LORD’s strength.

The Boaz leg is the strongest and it has real support — §3.1’s לֶחֶם chain with the giver changed, §3.2’s pillar, §4’s redemption pair, and the כָּנָף pair at 2:12/3:9 where Ruth hands Boaz his own blessing back as a claim [T]. Stated as Boaz is the means by which what YHWH is said to do gets done, it is close to this project’s own third macro-argument from the other side. Stated as Boaz represents the Lord’s strength, it is an allegory, and it runs into preaching trap 3: the redeemer category carries the type; the feet, the cloak and the six measures do not.

The Obed leg is directly contradicted by this project’s audit. The overview’s Christological trajectory, under What is not claimed: “Obed is not a type — he is named, then listed” [S: audit]. Obed is named at 4:17 and appears in the genealogy at 4:21–22; the book attaches no role to him. Verrett’s “Obed is like a new David figure” and his reading of 4:15 as making the boy a food-source — “already in the book he’s been compared to food” — are both imported. מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ and כַלְכֵּל at 4:15 are restoration and sustenance language [T]; the comparison to bread is Verrett’s, not the text’s.

(v2.0 — audit 4 goes further, and the correction is neat.) The food reading inverts its own grammar. כַּלְכֵּל takes שֵׂיבָתֵךְ, “your grey head”, as its object: the child will sustain Naomi’s old age. The child is the provider, not the provision [T] — cast in the role Joseph and the ravens occupy (Gen 45:11, 47:12, 50:21; 1 Kgs 17:4, 17:9). And מֵשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ’s nearest formal parallel is Lam 1:16, מְנַחֵם מֵשִׁיב נַפְשִׁי, where the restorer is a human comforter; only two of the thirty-five שׁוּב + נֶפֶשׁ verses pair the idiom with food vocabulary [S: audit 4].

On Obed himself the audit is blunter than v1.0. עוֹבֵד stands in three verses of Ruth, all in the closing genealogical material — 4:17, 4:21, 4:22. He is named, begotten, and begets. He is never the subject of a verb of action, never speaks, is never spoken to, and nothing is said of him in his own right anywhere in the canon [T]. In 4:13–16 he is not even called Obed: he is בֵּן at 4:13 and הַיֶּלֶד at 4:16, the book’s last use of יֶלֶד, answering 1:5. The text does not make Obed a new David; it makes David the point of Obed. (Note too that the subject of 4:15 carries from גֹּאֵל at 4:14, so whatever 4:15 predicates, it predicates of the unnamed redeemer-child two verses before the name Obed exists.)

And “the birth of Obed answers exile” is not a claim the text makes. The book’s stated problem is famine, bereavement and a line about to end; its stated resolution is a גֹּאֵל and a name not cut off (4:10, 4:14). Exile is a frame a reader brings from the book’s canonical position — which returns us to §6.1, where that position was shown to postdate the text by centuries.

And the seed-of-the-woman leg rests on one word. Verrett’s third talk makes Genesis 3:15 “an even greater theme than bread in the book of Ruth”. זֶרַע stands in exactly one verse of Ruth — 4:12, in the elders’ blessing, in the verse that cites Genesis 38 [T]. High confidence. The canonical seed theme is real and Ruth 4:12 participates in it. It is not a theme of the book of Ruth, and it cannot be the book’s greater theme on one occurrence in a character’s mouth.

Where this leaves his Christology. Verrett’s destination is true and his road is unauthorised. His bridge to Christ is bread → John 6:35, and no New Testament text connects Ruth to the bread of life. The one New Testament text that does take up this book takes up its last five verses: Matthew 1:3–6 contains all ten of Ruth’s names and hangs four maternal references on them — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and at 1:6 a fourth left unnamed, ἐκ τῆς τοῦ Οὐρίου — against none in Ruth’s own genealogy [T]. That is why this project rates its Christological route high and would rate Verrett’s moderate at best: the difference is not piety, it is that one chain is picked up by a New Testament writer and the other is not. Verrett reaches Matthew 1:1 and quotes it for “son of David”, and never notices that five verses later Matthew names Ruth — the one thing her own book declines to do.

7. Not assessable from the corpus

Three claims the primary texts cannot settle, recorded so they are not mistaken for verdicts.

7.1 — The etymologies of Mahlon, Chilion and Ruth. מַחְלוֹן and כִּלְיוֹן are plausibly from חלה (“be sick”) and כלה (“come to an end”), which is what Verrett reports, and they are equally plausibly ordinary Canaanite names. The book glosses neither (§5.6). The corpus can say Ruth does not explain them; it cannot say what they meant.

7.2 — Ruth’s name and the Song’s “beloved”. Verrett: the Song’s word for the female beloved “comes from the same root as ‘Ruth.’” רַעְיָה (lemma 7474) stands in ten verses — nine in the Song, plus Judg 11:37; רוּת (lemma 7327) stands in twelve, all in Ruth and Matthew’s line [T]. They are distinct lemmas. The derivation of רוּת from רְעוּת (“companionship”) requires the loss of the ʿayin; it is the majority etymological proposal and it is not a textual datum. Verrett’s “same root” overstates what can be known, and his Ruth → Song of Songs handoff (“we follow her into the bedroom”) depends on it. This project’s overview records the handoff honestly: “Song of Songs (after): no work demonstrated.”

7.3 — The Ruth Targum. Verrett reports that it lists the righteous descending from Ruth, beginning with David and ending with King Messiah. That is a claim about a document not in this corpus and not checked here. It is reported as history of interpretation, which is the right register for it, and it is the one secondary source in the four talks he handles without pressing beyond what it says.

8. Critical assessment of this assessment

Per the standing rule that précis and assessment work carries its own critique, to guard against the echo-chamber effect. These are the places where this document is weakest, and two of them could reverse a verdict above.

8.1 — This is a main-session gate, and the main session is the weaker auditor. Every count above was run once, by me, with the positive-control discipline the method requires but without the two blind auditors. Audit 3’s record is explicit that the homograph bug in contacts.py fired on the main session’s own gate in the same sitting it fired on both auditors, all three having been briefed on it. No count here has been independently reproduced. The ones most exposed are those where a form or an order matters rather than a lemma — which audit 3’s auditor 1 identified as this project’s entire error class: “the counts settled by a single lemma query were all right; the counts needing a form or an order checked are the ones that drifted.” In this document that means §2.2 (וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ), §3.5 (יָדַע with שַׁעַר) and §4’s עַם־זוּ — all three are phrase or order claims, and all three should be re-run by an auditor before any of them enters a sermon or an overview.

And it has already cost one error, found before the auditors were briefed. §3.2’s Boaz distribution was first written as “nineteen in Ruth”, which mis-assigned a Chronicles verse and dropped 2 Chr 3:17 from a list that named it one clause later. It is corrected in place with the fault recorded, per this project’s practice. One error of this class in a document of this length is not a reassuring rate, and it is the strongest argument in this file for §9.

8.2 — contacts.py was not used, and that cuts both ways. No density or ranking figure in this document is new; the ranks quoted in §6 are the project’s own corrected figures. That is deliberate — audit 3 established the metric is a short-unit detector — but it means I have not independently tested whether Verrett’s exodus correspondences would register on the one tool that might have scored them. It is possible — I would say unlikely, but possible — that an Exodus block scores better against Ruth 1 than the project’s existing top ten, in which case §6.2 would need reframing rather than discarding.

8.3 — Two findings in §4 should be cited from Logos and have not been. The עַם־זוּ count and the גָּאַל/קָנָה four-verse set are from the WLC, which is the right observation layer and the wrong citation layer. Neither is apparatus-dependent, so nothing turns on it at present — but if the Exodus 15 relation is promoted into the overview, the counts should be reproduced from the BHS export, and Exod 15:13’s בְּעָזְּךָ should be checked for any variant before a sermon leans on the wordplay. Also unchecked: whether the Exodus 15 ↔︎ Ruth 4 relation is already in the literature. It is a sufficiently obvious pairing that I would expect it to be, and this project has twice withdrawn a novelty claim on exactly that ground (L7 and L8). The Logos question is queued at §9; until it is answered, §4 should not be described as new to scholarship — only as new to this project.

8.4 — I may be too hard on the correspondence method, and the reason is institutional. This project has spent three audits destroying pattern claims and has arrived at a settled disposition against them. A disposition is not a result. The honest position is that accumulated correspondence is weak evidence, not no evidence, and that the project’s calibration figure — treat density below 1.0 as noise — was derived from lexical scans and does not straightforwardly apply to parallels of plot and role, which are what Verrett mostly offers and which the tool cannot see at all. §6.2 and §6.3 should therefore be read as “unsupported by the measurements available”, not as “falsified”. The difference is real and I have tried to keep it, but a reader who finds this document’s tone more confident than its evidence is reading it correctly in those two sections.

8.5 — The thirty-one-claim inventory is mine and unreviewed. I decided what counted as a separable claim, and a different partition would give different totals. The table in §1 should be read as a shape, not a score. In particular the thirteen “confirmed exactly” items are not of equal weight: §2.1’s three chains are substantial findings and §2.9’s count of ten generations is arithmetic.

8.6 — A transcript is not a lecture. Several of the errors in §5.9 are the kind a speaker makes aloud and would not write, and the user’s transcription is at one remove from the recordings. The reference slips should not be held against Verrett’s scholarship, and I have kept them in a single sub-section rather than scattered through the document for that reason. The errors in §5.1 to §5.8 are different: those are positions, repeated, and load-bearing.

8.7 — What a fair reader might say back. Verrett’s strongest reply to this whole document would be that it judges a four-part sermon series by the standards of a critical commentary, and that the canon-position material in his first talk is offered as “reading the book in any one of these perspectives can give us a fresh perspective” — a heuristic, not a compositional claim. That reply is good for the first talk and fails for the fourth, where “Ruth is a new Exodus” and “it’s the same story” are stated flatly and built on. The assessment above tries to honour the distinction; where it has not, the error is mine.

On his humility, which the user noted.
