## Headline Findings

1. **Boaz's private order to his men is phrased in the gleaning law's own two verbs, and the pair stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — the law twice, and Boaz once.** `[T]` לָקַט ("glean") with עָזַב ("leave") in one verse occurs at **Leviticus 19:10, Leviticus 23:22 and Ruth 2:16**, and nowhere else. Both Leviticus verses *are* the gleaning law ("you shall not glean… you shall **leave** them for the poor and the sojourner"). Boaz says: **וַעֲזַבְתֶּם וְלִקְּטָה** — "leave it, and she will glean". *High confidence; rivals searched and named — they are the law itself.* **Boaz is not being generically kind. He is quoting the statute, to his own staff, about a Moabite.**

2. **And Ruth uses none of the law's status words. Every absence was positively controlled.** `[T]` The gleaning law provides for four named classes, and the book of Ruth contains not one of the four nouns: **אַלְמָנָה ("widow") — 54 verses in the canon, none in Ruth; גֵּר ("sojourner") — 83 verses, none in Ruth; יָתוֹם ("orphan") — 42 verses, none; עָנִי ("poor") — 74 verses, none.** Nor שָׁכַח ("forget", 95 verses) or פֵּאָה (the field's "edge", 59 verses), the law's other operative terms. Each search was run again against a verse the word is known to occupy, and each control returned it. Sharper still: **the verb גּוּר ("to sojourn") occurs once in Ruth — at 1:1, of Elimelech going to Moab — and never of Ruth.** *High confidence.* **The law is present in this chapter as action and absent as label. Nobody files Ruth under a category.**

3. **Ruth names herself with the one word the law does not cover, and the resulting wordplay is unique in the Hebrew Bible.** `[T]` At 2:10 she asks why Boaz should **הַכִּיר** her — "recognise, acknowledge" — **וְאָנֹכִי נָכְרִיָּה**, "and I a foreigner". The two words are from one root, and **the two lemmas stand together in exactly one verse of the canon: Ruth 2:10.** *High confidence.* Note what she has not said: not גֵּר, the resident alien the Torah legislates for, but נָכְרִי, the outsider it does not. **She asks to be *recognised* while calling herself *unrecognisable*, and she claims no legal standing at all.**

4. **2:14 is the first time anyone in the book has enough, and the verb-pair that says so stands in two verses of the canon.** `[T]` שָׂבַע ("be satisfied") with יָתַר ("have left over") occurs at **2 Chronicles 31:10 and Ruth 2:14** — and nowhere else. 2 Chronicles 31:10 is the priest's report to Hezekiah on the restored tithe: they ate, had enough, and this great quantity was left. Ruth's version is one woman with roasted grain. *High confidence on the count.* **Against 1:21 this is the book's turn: Naomi said רֵיקָם ("empty"), and the next thing that happens to her household is שָׂבַע ("satisfied") and a surplus carried home.**

5. **Boaz's blessing prays in the Psalter's idiom, and its central wordplay occurs nowhere else.** `[T]` כָּנָף ("wing") with חָסָה ("take refuge") in one verse stands in **five verses: Psalm 36:8, 57:2, 61:5, 91:4, and Ruth 2:12** — **all four rivals in the Psalms.** Of those four, **Psalm 91:4 is the nearest**: it alone shares Ruth 2:12's ordered phrase תַּחַת־כְּנָפָיו ("under his wings"), which an ordered-phrase search returns at Ruth 2:12 on a plain pass and at Psalm 91:4, 1 Kings 8:6 and 2 Chronicles 5:7 on a skeletal pass — so **the wording is near but not identical, the spelling differing**, and the two Kings–Chronicles verses are the cherubim over the ark, not refuge. Within the verse, שָׁלֵם is used twice, as two different words — the verb "repay" and the adjective "full" — and that pairing occurs in **Ruth 2:12 alone** in the canon. And מַשְׂכֹּרֶת ("wages") is a four-verse word whose other three are **Genesis 29:15, 31:7 and 31:41 — Jacob's wages, changed ten times by his kinsman.** *High on each count; the Psalter link rated as register, not citation, because the rivals are four.*

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 1:19–22 ended with a woman declaring herself emptied by God, in public, and with the narrator's one-sentence reply: he called her Naomi, named the Moabitess beside her, and dated the arrival בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים ("at the beginning of barley harvest"). `[T]` **The chapter's last word is "barley."** A thesis of emptiness has been stated and a food supply has been named in the same breath.

**Necessity answer.** This passage exists **to begin answering the complaint by the means the Torah already provided, through an agent who goes past it** — and it must stand here for three reasons.

It must follow 1:22 because gleaning requires a harvest, and the harvest has just been dated. `[T]` It must precede chapter 3 because Naomi cannot send Ruth to the threshing floor until there is a man to send her to, and that man must be established as *both* a relative (2:1) and generous beyond obligation (2:8–16) before 3:1 is credible rather than reckless. `[I]` And it must come before 2:20, where Naomi first says the word גֹּאֵל ("redeemer") — because **the reader is told at 2:1 what Naomi does not say she knows until 2:20.** `[T]`

**Why not elsewhere.** The provision could not be narrated before the complaint, or it would answer a question nobody had asked. It could not be delayed past chapter 2, because the barley harvest ends (2:23) and with it the legal occasion.

**Implication.** The unit is **the hinge between the book's problem and the book's machinery.** Naomi's emptiness is met not by a miracle but by a field, a statute, and a man who keeps it generously — and the woman at the centre of it is still being called the Moabitess (2:2, 2:6) and calling herself a foreigner (2:10).

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 4 sharpens this. The verb עָזַב ("leave, forsake") runs through the book in four verses: Ruth would not **leave** Naomi (1:16); she **left** her father and mother (2:11); Boaz tells his men to **leave** grain for her (2:16); and Naomi blesses the LORD, who has not **left off** his kindness to the living and the dead (2:20). `[T]` **The verb of Ruth's loyalty becomes the verb of Boaz's provision and then the verb of God's faithfulness — and all four occurrences are reached by the end of this chapter.**

## Tool 1 — Author's Purpose

The narrator's purpose here is visible in a technique he uses twice: **he tells the reader things the characters do not know, and he lets Boaz act where Ruth cannot hear.**

**The first disclosure is 2:1.** `[T]` The verse is not part of the scene; it is an aside to the reader, and it is placed one verse after Naomi has said she came back empty. The reader learns there is a relative with means before Ruth has taken a step. **Naomi does not say she knows this until 2:20.** The narrator thereby arranges for the reader to watch chapter 2 knowing that "chance" (2:3) is not chance, while Ruth walks into a field at random.

**The second is 2:15–16.** `[T]` Boaz's most generous instruction is given **to his young men, about Ruth, after she has got up and gone back to work.** She is not in the conversation. The provision that will fill her shawl is arranged behind her back, and the narrator shows the reader the arrangement while leaving her ignorant of it.

**So the purpose of the unit is to display providence as management rather than miracle.** `[I]` Nothing supernatural occurs. A man comes from Bethlehem, greets his workers, asks who the woman is, gives orders. The narrator's whole contribution is to let the reader see the seams — the kinsman at 2:1, the orders at 2:16 — which the woman being provided for cannot see.

**Book-overview note.** The overview's Christological trajectory is, for this unit, best served by the *type* leg rather than the *contrast* leg that dominated 1:19–22. **The agent of provision here keeps the law, exceeds it, pays for it himself, and conceals it from its beneficiary.** That is a set of four features, each `[T]`, and it is a stronger foundation than a redeemer-cipher. Logged for the Finalise pass.

## Tool 2 — Context

**Immediately before (1:19–22).** The complaint, and the harvest notice. `[T]`

**Immediately after (2:17–23).** The ephah of barley, the return to Naomi, Naomi's blessing with the word גֹּאֵל, and the notice that Ruth stayed through to the end of the wheat harvest. `[T]` **2:20 is where Naomi's theology changes**, and it changes in response to the grain, not to an argument.

**The unit's own boundary.** 2:16 is the right end-point: 2:17 shifts subject from Boaz's orders to Ruth's labour and moves the scene out of the field. `[I]` The seam is a change of agent, not a formula.

**Wider book frame.** This is the first of the book's three Boaz scenes — field (ch. 2), threshing floor (ch. 3), gate (ch. 4) — each one a more public and more legally binding setting than the last. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate*; the progression is a reading of the three settings, not a stated structure.

**Canonical context.** Leviticus 19:9–10 and 23:22 (gleaning), Leviticus 23:10–15 (firstfruits and the barley sheaf), Deuteronomy 24:19 (the forgotten sheaf). `[T]` These are the statutes the scene is conducted under, and the vocabulary evidence for each is under Tool 11.

## Tool 3 — Structure

Five scenes, alternating between what Ruth hears and what she does not.

| Verses | Scene | Ruth present? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narrator's disclosure: Naomi has a kinsman | **no — aside to the reader** |
| 2–3 | Ruth asks, goes, and "happens" on the field | yes |
| 4–7 | Boaz arrives, greets the reapers, asks the foreman whose she is | yes, but spoken *about*, not *to* |
| 8–9 · 10 · 11–12 · 13 | Boaz speaks to her; she answers; he answers; she answers | **yes — the only dialogue** |
| 14 | The meal | yes |
| 15–16 | Boaz's private orders to his men | **no — she has gone back to work** |

**The shape is a frame.** `[T]` The unit opens with information withheld from the characters and closes with generosity withheld from its recipient; the dialogue sits in the middle. *Confidence: moderate* on the frame as design — it is an observation about who is present in which verse, which is `[T]`, but calling it a deliberate structure is `[I]` and no baseline has been run.

**Within the dialogue, the exchange is strictly alternating and escalating.** Boaz's first speech (8–9) is about safety; her reply (10) asks why; his second (11–12) explains and blesses; her reply (13) accepts. `[T]` **Each of his speeches is longer than the last, and each of hers is shorter.**

**One structural observation worth the preacher's notice.** `[T]` Boaz speaks three times in this unit about Ruth to other people (2:5 to the foreman, 2:15 and 2:16 to his men) and twice to Ruth herself. **He talks about her more than he talks to her — and everything he says about her is in her favour.**

## Tool 4 — Linking Words

**וְ at 2:1 — the disclosure connector.** וּלְנָעֳמִי, "now to Naomi…". `[T]` A simple waw with the indirect object first, which in Hebrew narrative marks a shift out of the action into background information. **It is the grammar of an aside**, and the English "Now Naomi had…" catches it in both versions.

**אֲשֶׁר at 2:2 — the indefinite one.** אַחַר אֲשֶׁר אֶמְצָא־חֵן בְּעֵינָיו, "after whoever I may find favour with". `[T]` **The relative is deliberately unspecified.** Ruth does not name a field or a man; she proposes to follow whoever will let her. NASB95's "after one in whose sight I may find favour" and ESV's "after him in whose sight I shall find favour" both supply a referent the Hebrew leaves open. *This matters*: the indefiniteness at 2:2 is what makes 2:3's "chance" the narrator's point rather than a coincidence of plotting.

**וַיִּקֶר at 2:3 — the verb of the whole chapter, and it has no connective at all.** `[T]` Simply וַיִּקֶר מִקְרֶהָ, "and her chance chanced". No "but", no "however", no "behold". **The most theologically loaded moment in the chapter is joined to its sentence by a bare waw consecutive.** See Tool 7.

**הֲלוֹא twice, at 2:8 and 2:9.** הֲלוֹא שָׁמַעַתְּ ("have you not heard?") and הֲלוֹא צִוִּיתִי ("have I not commanded?"). `[T]` Two rhetorical negatives in two verses, both from Boaz, both inviting assent. **The second one is the point: he tells her he has already given the order.** The protection predates the conversation.

**גַּם at 2:15 and וְגַם at 2:16.** `[T]` "**Even** among the sheaves let her glean" and "**and also** pull out for her from the bundles". The particle is additive and it is used twice in two verses, each time to push the concession further. **The grammar is a man raising his own bid.**

## Tool 5 — Parallels

Prose, so no sustained parallelism — but two places where the lines fall into pairs.

**2:12 is a two-line blessing with the divine name in both lines:**

> יְשַׁלֵּם יְהוָה פָּעֳלֵךְ ("may the LORD repay your work")
> וּתְהִי מַשְׂכֻּרְתֵּךְ שְׁלֵמָה מֵעִם יְהוָה ("and may your wages be full from the LORD")

`[T]` The second line restates the first and intensifies it: *repay* becomes *full wages*, and the preposition מֵעִם ("from with") locates the source. **And the two lines pun on one root** — יְשַׁלֵּם and שְׁלֵמָה — a pairing found in no other verse of the canon (Tool 7). *Confidence: high* on the wordplay; *moderate* on calling the verse designed parallelism.

**2:16 is a three-part command with an escalating structure:**

> שֹׁל־תָּשֹׁלּוּ לָהּ מִן־הַצְּבָתִים ("pull out for her from the bundles")
> וַעֲזַבְתֶּם וְלִקְּטָה ("and leave it, and she will glean")
> וְלֹא תִגְעֲרוּ־בָהּ ("and do not rebuke her")

`[T]` Two positive commands and one negative, the first using an **infinitive absolute with its finite verb** (שֹׁל־תָּשֹׁלּוּ) — the Hebrew construction for emphasis. **"You shall definitely pull out."** Both versions render it with an adverb ("purposely", "also pull out"), which is the best English can do.

## Tool 6 — Narrator's Comment

The narrator comments three times, and the middle one is the chapter's crux.

**(i) 2:1 — the disclosure.** Treated under Tool 1.

**(ii) 2:3 — וַיִּקֶר מִקְרֶהָ.** `[T]` A verb and its own cognate noun: "and her chance chanced upon". **This is the narrator's single most important word in the chapter and it is the word for randomness.** The lexical measurements are under Tool 7; the comment to make here is about technique. **The narrator does not say "and the LORD led her".** He says the opposite, and leaves the reader — who has just read 2:1 — to supply what he has withheld. *Confidence: high* that the irony is intended; the reader has been given 2:1 precisely so that 2:3 cannot be read flatly. `[I]` on the intention, `[T]` on the arrangement.

**(iii) 2:7 — the foreman's report, and the text's hardest clause.** `[T]` זֶה שִׁבְתָּהּ הַבַּיִת מְעָט. NASB95: "she has been sitting in the house for a little while"; ESV: "except for a short rest". **The two versions do not agree, and neither is obviously right.** See Textual Variants: this is the one place in the unit where a variant genuinely bears on the sense, and it turns on a Hebrew consonantal form that can be read from two different roots.

**What the narrator never does in this unit:** evaluate Boaz. `[T]` There is no "and Boaz was a righteous man", no "and the LORD was pleased". **The man is characterised entirely by what he says and what he orders**, including the order she does not hear.

## Tool 7 — Vocabulary

The unit's heaviest tool. Every count is on the WLC lemma index, **verified by lemma with the homograph suffix carried**, at each individual reference, with the corpus verse total (23,213) printed and checked before any canon-wide count was made.

### The gleaning-law vocabulary — what Ruth uses

**לָקַט ("glean") — the chapter's leading word.** `[T]` The verb stands in **10 verses of Ruth, 12 occurrences, and every one of them is in chapter 2** (2:2, 3, 7, 8, 15 ×2, 16, 17 ×2, 18, 19, 23); **seven of the twelve are inside this unit.** Across the canon it is a 34-verse word. *Confidence: high.* **A word that occurs a dozen times in one chapter of a four-chapter book and nowhere else in it is that chapter's subject.**

**The decisive pair: לָקַט with עָזַב.** `[T]` In one verse, **three verses in the Hebrew Bible — Leviticus 19:10, Leviticus 23:22, Ruth 2:16.** Rivals searched; there are none beyond the two Leviticus verses, which are the law itself:

| Reference | The clause | |
|---|---|---|
| Lev 19:10 | לֶעָנִי וְלַגֵּר תַּעֲזֹב אֹתָם | "you shall **leave** them for the poor and the sojourner" |
| Lev 23:22 | לֶעָנִי וְלַגֵּר תַּעֲזֹב אֹתָם | "you shall **leave** them for the poor and the sojourner" |
| Ruth 2:16 | וַעֲזַבְתֶּם וְלִקְּטָה | "and you shall **leave** it, and she will **glean**" |

*High confidence.* **Boaz's order is the statute's verb in the statute's construction, given as a private instruction about one woman.**

**לָקַט with קָצַר ("reap") — five verses:** Isaiah 17:5, Leviticus 19:9, Leviticus 23:22, Ruth 2:3, Ruth 2:7. `[T]` Two of the five are the law; one is a harvest simile. **Ruth holds two of the five.**

**עֹמֶר ("sheaf") — an eight-verse word, and its company is decisive.** `[T]` Deuteronomy 24:19, Job 24:10, Leviticus 23:10, 11, 12, 15, Ruth 2:7, Ruth 2:15. **Every occurrence in the canon is in a gleaning, firstfruits or harvest-poverty context, and Ruth holds two of the eight.** *High confidence.*

**A homograph caught at the gate, and it matters.** `[T]` The *other* עֹמֶר — the dry measure of Exodus 16 — is **a different lemma in the index**, standing in six verses, all of them in Exodus 16. **Ruth's sheaf-omer and the manna-omer are not the same word**, and a search that merged them would have manufactured a link between this chapter and the manna narrative that the text does not supply. The two are kept apart here, and the Exodus 16 question is handled on its real evidence below.

| שָׁכַח ("forget") | 95 | **none** | Deut 24:19 returned — pass |
| פֵּאָה (field's "edge") | 59 | **none** | Lev 19:9 returned — pass |

**And the refinement that makes the pattern sharp:** the verb גּוּר ("to sojourn") does occur in Ruth — **once, at 1:1, of Elimelech going to Moab** — and never of Ruth coming to Bethlehem. `[T]` *High confidence; 79-verse word, control passed.*

**What this licenses, and what it does not.** It licenses the statement that **the book conducts a gleaning scene in the law's procedural vocabulary while declining every one of its social labels.** `[I]` It does not license a claim that the author is polemicising against the categories; that would be a motive, and the text supplies none. **The honest form is the measurement plus the observation that Ruth is the one who supplies a status word, and it is the wrong one.**

### The 2:10 wordplay

**הַכִּיר with נָכְרִי — one verse in the Hebrew Bible.** `[T]` לְהַכִּירֵנִי ("to recognise me", 41 verses canon-wide) with נָכְרִיָּה ("foreign", 45 verses) stands together in **Ruth 2:10 alone.** *High confidence; the correct homograph-suffixed lemma was used after a first search on the unsuffixed form returned a false zero (see the Text-First Declaration).* **The two common words meet in one verse of Scripture and this is it.**

**The sense of the pun.** נָכְרִי is not the law's גֵּר. Of its 45 verses, nine are in Proverbs, where the אִשָּׁה נָכְרִיָּה is the dangerous foreign woman. `[T]` **Ruth applies to herself the canon's word for the outsider who does not belong — and the verb from the same root for what she cannot expect anyone to do.** *Confidence: high* on the counts; *moderate* on the register, and **the Proverbs adjacency is not used as an argument**, per the overview's v1.4 correction.

**הַכִּיר in Ruth — three verses: 2:10, 2:19, 3:14.** `[T]` A small chain worth noting: she asks why he **recognised** her; Naomi asks where she worked and blesses the man who **took notice** of her (2:19); and at 3:14 Boaz is concerned that she not be **recognised** on the threshing floor.

### The meal at 2:14

**שָׂבַע ("be satisfied") with יָתַר ("have left over") — two verses in the canon:** 2 Chronicles 31:10 and Ruth 2:14. `[T]` *High confidence.* **שָׂבַע occurs once in the whole book of Ruth — here.** `[T]` And יָתַר twice: 2:14 and 2:18, where the surplus reaches Naomi.

**קָלִי ("roasted grain") — five verses:** Leviticus 23:14, 1 Samuel 17:17, 25:18, 2 Samuel 17:28, Ruth 2:14. `[T]` **Leviticus 23:14 is the firstfruits law, which forbids eating קָלִי until the sheaf offering has been brought** — and Ruth 1:22 has dated the arrival to the beginning of the barley harvest. *Confidence: high* on the count; **the implication for the timing is left open** (Open Questions), because the text does not say whether the offering has been made.

**חֹמֶץ ("vinegar") — five verses:** Numbers 6:3, Proverbs 10:26, 25:20, Psalm 69:22, Ruth 2:14. `[T]` **The versions divide here**: NASB95 "vinegar", ESV "wine". The Greek has ὄξος, vinegar. See Tool 8.

**Three hapax legomena in this one unit.** `[T]` Each occurs in a single verse of the Hebrew Bible, and that verse is here:

- **צָבַט** (2:14) — Boaz "held out / heaped up" the roasted grain for her.
- **שָׁלַל** (2:16) — "pull out" from the bundles.
- **צֶבֶת** (2:16) — the "bundles" themselves.

**Two of the three are in verse 16.** *High confidence on each; all three verified as single-verse lemmas.* **Boaz's most generous sentence is built from words that occur nowhere else in Scripture** — which is a fact about the vocabulary and, honestly, a limit on how precisely any of the three can be glossed. `[I]`

### 2:3's "chance"

**מִקְרֶה ("chance, happening") — eight verses:** 1 Samuel 6:9, 20:26, Ecclesiastes 2:14, 2:15, 3:19, 9:2, 9:3, Ruth 2:3. `[T]` **Five of the eight are in Ecclesiastes**, where the word carries Qohelet's argument that one fate meets the wise and the fool alike.

**And the construction is tighter than the word.** `[T]` קָרָה ("happen") with מִקְרֶה in one verse — the cognate accusative — stands in **three verses: Ecclesiastes 2:14, Ecclesiastes 2:15, Ruth 2:3.** Both rivals are Qohelet, at the exact point of his complaint: מִקְרֶה אֶחָד יִקְרֶה אֶת־כֻּלָּם, "one fate happens to them all". *High confidence on the count; moderate on the register.* **The narrator of Ruth describes God's provision with the construction whose only other home in the canon is the argument that nothing is providential.** The irony is an inference `[I]` — but it is an inference from a three-verse measurement, not an impression.

### 2:12's blessing

**שָׁלֵם twice, as two words — one verse in the canon.** `[T]` The piel verb "repay" (יְשַׁלֵּם) and the adjective "full" (שְׁלֵמָה) stand together in **Ruth 2:12 alone.** *High confidence.*

**מַשְׂכֹּרֶת ("wages") — four verses:** Genesis 29:15, 31:7, 31:41, Ruth 2:12. `[T]` **All three rivals are Jacob and Laban** — the wages a kinsman changed ten times. And **מַשְׂכֹּרֶת with שָׁלֵם is Ruth 2:12 alone.** *High confidence on the counts.* **Boaz prays that a kinsman's household will pay in full the wages a woman is owed, using the canon's word for wages withheld by a kinsman.** *Confidence on the resonance: moderate* — four verses is a narrow base, but the direction of dependence is unestablished and no citation is claimed.

**A convergence with the previous dig, worth flagging.** `[T]` Genesis 31 is now contacted from two Ruth passages on two different words: רֵיקָם ("empty") at Genesis 31:42, cited in the 1:19–22 report, and מַשְׂכֹּרֶת here at 31:7 and 31:41. **Both concern a dependent sent away unpaid.** Queued for the Finalise pass as a possible map row; **not claimed as design**, since two words reaching one chapter from two passages has had no baseline.

**כָּנָף ("wing") with חָסָה ("take refuge") — five verses:** Psalm 36:8, 57:2, 61:5, 91:4, Ruth 2:12. `[T]` **Four rivals, all Psalms.** חָסָה occurs **once in Ruth** (2:12); of its 36 canon-wide verses, 24 are in the Psalms. `[T]`

**And the rivals are not equally close.** `[T]` An ordered-phrase search for תַּחַת־כְּנָפָיו ("under his wings") returns **Ruth 2:12 on the plain pass**, and on the skeletal pass **Ruth 2:12, Psalm 91:4, 1 Kings 8:6 and 2 Chronicles 5:7**. So:

- **Psalm 91:4 — וְתַחַת־כְּנָפָיו תֶּחְסֶה ("and under his wings you will take refuge") — shares both the phrase and the verb with Ruth 2:12**, and is the closest verse in the canon. **The wording is near but not identical: the match is skeletal, the spelling differing**, so the words *identical* and *verbatim* are not used of it.
- **Psalm 36:8 has בְּצֵל כְּנָפֶיךָ ("in the shadow of your wings")** — the same image in different words, and it does not share the phrase.
- **1 Kings 8:6 and 2 Chronicles 5:7 share the phrase but not the sense**: they are the wings of the cherubim over the ark, with no refuge verb, and are therefore outside the five-verse collocation.

*Confidence: high on the counts and on the phrase result; the claim is **register, not citation**, and is rated **moderate** because the Psalter supplies four rivals and no mechanism of dependence is established.* **Boaz prays like a psalmist, and closest of all like Psalm 91.**

**And כָּנָף occurs twice in Ruth — 2:12 and 3:9.** `[T]` *High confidence; the two-verse distribution was verified.* At 2:12 Boaz prays that she has come to take refuge under **YHWH's** wings; at 3:9 Ruth asks Boaz to spread **his** wing over her. **The book's only two uses of the word are a prayer and a request that it be answered through the man praying.** See Move 4.

### The chapter's other chains

**חֵן ("favour") — three verses, all in this unit: 2:2, 2:10, 2:13.** `[T]` And **עַיִן ("eye") — four verses, all in chapter 2: 2:2, 2:9, 2:10, 2:13.** The formula מָצָא חֵן בְּעֵינֵי is a **40-verse idiom** canon-wide (13 in Genesis), so **no allusion is claimed** — it is above the threshold at which wording alone can carry dependence. `[T]`

**But one four-word set is narrow.** `[T]` מָצָא + חֵן + עַיִן + שִׁפְחָה ("maidservant") in one verse stands in **three verses: Genesis 32:6 [Eng 32:5], 1 Samuel 1:18, Ruth 2:13.** And Genesis 32:6 is a different kind of case, read and checked: there שִׁפְחָה sits in an inventory of livestock and slaves Jacob is reporting to Esau, not a self-designation. **So in two verses of the Hebrew Bible a woman calls herself "your maidservant" while asking to find favour in the eyes of the man before her: Hannah at Shiloh, and Ruth in the field.** *Confidence: high* on the count; *moderate* on the parallel, with the Hannah context noted and no citation claimed.

**דָּבַק ("cling") — four verses: 1:14, 2:8, 2:21, 2:23.** `[T]` Ruth clung to Naomi; Boaz tells her to cling to his young women; she reports it; she does it. **A chain running from the road into the field.** *High confidence.*

**עָזַב ("leave") — four verses: 1:16, 2:11, 2:16, 2:20.** `[T]` Treated at Phase 0.6 and Move 4. *High confidence.*

**חַיִל ("strength, worth") — three verses, one for each principal: 2:1 (Boaz, גִּבּוֹר חַיִל), 3:11 (Ruth, אֵשֶׁת חַיִל), 4:11 (the blessing, עֲשֵׂה־חַיִל).** `[T]` *High confidence on the distribution.* **The word the narrator uses to introduce Boaz is the word Boaz will use of Ruth, and the word the elders will use of the household.** The phrase גִּבּוֹר חַיִל itself is a **49-verse military formula** (18 in Chronicles), so **no allusion is claimed**; what is claimed is the book-internal triad.

**יָדַע ("know") — eight verses, the first two in this unit: 2:1 (the ketiv) and 2:11.** `[T]` The ketiv at 2:1 is from this root — Boaz is Naomi's "known one" — and at 2:11 Boaz commends Ruth for going to **a people she did not know**. *Confidence: high* on the distribution; *moderate* on the wordplay, since the ketiv/qere question (below) makes the 2:1 form uncertain.

## Tool 8 — Translations

| | NASB95 | ESV | The Hebrew |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2:1 of Boaz** | "a man of great wealth" | "a worthy man" | גִּבּוֹר חַיִל — the canon's military-valour formula, 49 verses |
| **2:1 relation** | "a kinsman" | "a relative" | the ketiv/qere word — see below |
| **2:7 end** | "she has been sitting in the house for a little while" | "except for a short rest" | זֶה שִׁבְתָּהּ הַבַּיִת מְעָט — genuinely obscure |
| **2:10** | "take notice of me… a foreigner" | "take notice of me… a foreigner" | the one-verse wordplay, lost in both |
| **2:14** | "in the **vinegar**" | "in the **wine**" | חֹמֶץ — five verses; the Greek has ὄξος |
| **2:16** | "purposely pull out" | "also pull out some" | infinitive absolute + finite verb |

**Four notes for the preacher.**

**(i) The 2:1 divergence is substantive and bears on chapter 3.** `[T]` NASB95's "great wealth" and ESV's "worthy man" are two readings of חַיִל, and the word returns at **3:11 of Ruth** (אֵשֶׁת חַיִל) and at **4:11 of the household**. **A congregation hearing "a man of great wealth" at 2:1 and "a worthy woman" at 3:11 will not hear that the same word has been used of both.** The ESV keeps the link and loses the wealth; NASB95 keeps the wealth and loses the link. *This is a note to the preacher, not a translation verdict* — but the link is the more useful of the two, and it is worth saying aloud that Boaz calls Ruth what the narrator called him.

**(ii) 2:14's vinegar/wine is a real gap, and the ancient evidence favours NASB95.** `[T]` חֹמֶץ is the word used at Numbers 6:3 of what a Nazirite must avoid and at Psalm 69:22 of what is given to the thirsty; the Greek renders it ὄξος, vinegar. **The ESV's "wine" softens a detail that is meant to be plain** — sour wine as a dip for bread is a labourer's condiment, not hospitality on a scale.

**(iii) Neither version can keep the 2:10 wordplay, and neither tries.** `[T]` Both have "take notice of me… foreigner", which is accurate and loses the root-play entirely. **The preacher has to say it: the word she uses for *recognise* and the word she uses for *foreigner* are the same word.** The Greek loses it too (ἐπιγνῶναι / ξένη).

**(iv) 2:7's divergence is a textual matter, not a stylistic one**, and is treated below.

## Tool 9 — Tone and Feel

**2:1 is cool and administrative.** `[T]` A relative, a clan, a name. No warmth and no comment — the narrator is laying a card on the table.

**2:2–3 is quick and unassuming.** Ruth's request is seven words in Hebrew and contains a particle of entreaty (נָּא). Naomi's reply is two words: לְכִי בִתִּי, "go, my daughter". `[T]` **Naomi, who has just made a speech about God's hostility, says nothing about God here and does not offer to come.**

**2:4 changes the air.** `[T]` יְהוָה עִמָּכֶם ("the LORD be with you") and יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה ("the LORD bless you") — a greeting and a response, each naming God, exchanged between an employer and his workers in a field. **After a chapter in which the divine name appeared mostly in complaint, it reappears as a routine courtesy at work.** That tonal fact is worth a sentence in any sermon on this passage. `[I]`

**2:8–13 is careful and formal on both sides.** `[T]` He calls her בִּתִּי ("my daughter"); she calls him אֲדֹנִי ("my lord") and herself שִׁפְחָה ("maidservant") twice. She is on the ground, having fallen on her face. **Nothing in the register is romantic, and reading it so is the unit's commonest sermon error.**

**2:14 is the warmest moment in the book so far** — and it is almost entirely conveyed by three verbs in sequence: she ate, was satisfied, had some left. `[T]`

**2:15–16 is brisk and conspiratorial.** `[T]` Short clauses, an emphatic construction, two prohibitions. **It is the tone of a man giving instructions he does not intend to explain.**

## Tool 10 — Repetition

**לָקַט ("glean"), seven times in sixteen verses.** `[T]` The chapter's leading word; the full distribution is under Tool 7. **It is spoken by Ruth (2:2, 2:7), by the narrator (2:3), and by Boaz (2:8, 2:15, 2:16)** — every party in the scene uses it.

**קָצַר ("reap") / הַקּוֹצְרִים ("the reapers"), seven verses: 2:3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 14.** `[T]` The reapers are the fixed background of the whole unit — Ruth is behind them (2:3, 2:7), Boaz greets them (2:4), the foreman is over them (2:5, 2:6), she is to follow them (2:9), and she finally sits beside them (2:14). **Her position relative to the reapers is the chapter's quiet index of her standing**, moving from *behind* to *beside*. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate* — the sequence is `[T]`, the significance is a reading.

**נַעַר / נַעֲרָה ("young man" / "young woman"), across 2:5, 6, 8, 9, 15.** `[T]` Boaz has young men and young women on his staff; Ruth is called a נַעֲרָה by the foreman (2:5, 2:6) and told to stay with the נְעָרוֹת (2:8) while the נְעָרִים are ordered not to touch her (2:9). **The vocabulary of a working household, used to place her inside it.**

**שָׂדֶה ("field"), in 2:2, 3 (twice), 6, 8, 9.** `[T]` And it is the same word the book has used of "the fields of Moab" (1:1, 1:2, 1:6, 1:22). **Ruth has moved from one שָׂדֶה to another**, and the book marks it with one noun. *Confidence: moderate–high*; the distribution is `[T]`, the contrast is `[I]`.

**אַחַר / אַחֲרֵי ("after"), in 2:2, 3, 7, 9, 11.** `[T]` Four of the five are Ruth following someone — the reapers, the young women. **The fifth, 2:11, is Boaz describing how she came "after" nobody: she left her father and mother and went to a people she did not know.**

**The divine name, three times: 2:4 (twice), 2:12 (twice).** `[T]` Four occurrences in two verses — **one pair in a workplace greeting, one pair in a blessing.** No character complains about God in this unit.

## Tool 11 — Quotation and Allusion

No quotation. Four contacts, and the Triad is run on the strongest.

### The gleaning law (2:15–16) — the unit's principal finding

**Move 1 — source context.** Leviticus 19:9–10 sits in the Holiness Code's chapter of neighbour-law, a few verses before "love your neighbour as yourself" (19:18). `[T]` Leviticus 23:22 repeats the provision inside the festival calendar, attached to the Feast of Weeks. **Both state the law as a prohibition on the owner** — do not reap to the edge, do not glean your own field — **and both give the beneficiaries as "the poor and the sojourner".** Deuteronomy 24:19 states the positive version (the forgotten sheaf is for the sojourner, orphan and widow) and attaches a promise: "that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands".

**Move 2 — book usage.** Does Ruth use Leviticus elsewhere? **Yes, and in the same chapter.** `[T]` The firstfruits law of Leviticus 23:10–15 supplies both of this unit's rare agricultural words: **עֹמֶר** (the barley sheaf, 8 verses canon-wide, four of them Leviticus 23) and **קָלִי** (roasted grain, 5 verses, one of them Leviticus 23:14). **Leviticus 23 therefore contributes the gleaning law, the sheaf and the roasted grain — three separate lexical contacts into one chapter of Ruth.** *Confidence: high* on each count. **This makes Leviticus a live source for the book in the overview's sense**, which the current map does not record.

**Move 3 — OT-to-OT.** Who else narrates the gleaning law being kept? **Essentially nobody.** `[T]` The law is legislated three times and, outside Ruth 2, the canon contains no scene of a landowner complying with it. The only other narrative use of the sheaf-word in a poverty setting is **Job 24:10**, where the point is the opposite: the destitute "carry the sheaf" while going hungry. *Confidence: moderate–high*; this is an argument from a verified eight-verse distribution rather than an exhaustive survey of narrative, and is stated as such.

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Four, and three of them are strong.

- **כָּנָף, 2:12 → 3:9.** `[T]` The book's only two uses. Boaz prays she has come to take refuge under YHWH's wings; Ruth asks him to spread his wing over her, which is to ask him to be the answer to his own prayer. *Confidence: high* on the verbal link (two verses in the book, verified); *moderate* on design, pending a baseline.
- **עָזַב, 1:16 → 2:11 → 2:16 → 2:20.** `[T]` Ruth will not leave Naomi; she left her parents; Boaz orders grain left; Naomi blesses the God who has not left off his kindness. **Four verses, one verb, and the fourth reframes the first three.** *High* on the chain.
- **דָּבַק, 1:14 → 2:8.** `[T]` She clung to Naomi; Boaz tells her to cling to the young women — **giving her a household to cling to in the place of the one she chose.** *High* on the verbal link.
- **רֵיקָם 1:21 → שָׂבַע/יָתַר 2:14.** `[I]` Conceptual, **not verbal**: and this must be said plainly, because the near-miss is tempting. **Boaz's word for "full" at 2:12 (שְׁלֵמָה) is not Naomi's word for "full" at 1:21 (מְלֵאָה)**; מָלֵא occurs in Ruth once only, at 1:21. *So there is no verbal echo at 2:12 of Naomi's complaint*, and a sermon that claims one is claiming something the lemma index denies. **The answer to רֵיקָם is given in 2:14's two verbs and in 3:17's repetition of רֵיקָם itself — not in 2:12.**

**Verdict on the gleaning law.** **Confirmed, high confidence, as a deliberate use of the statute.** The three-verse collocation at 2:16 is as narrow as this kind of evidence gets, the rivals are the law itself, and Leviticus 23 contributes two further rare words to the same chapter. **What is claimed: the scene is constructed on the gleaning and firstfruits legislation and expects the reader to know it.** What is not claimed: a direction of literary dependence between Ruth and any particular one of the three legal texts.

### The Psalter's refuge idiom (2:12) — moderate, register only

**Move 1 only**, per the Triad's rule for moderate-confidence contacts. Psalm 36:8, 57:2, 61:5 and 91:4 all use the wings-and-refuge image of YHWH's protection of the one who trusts him. `[T]` **Five verses in the canon hold the pair, four of them Psalms** — and, per Tool 7, **Psalm 91:4 is measurably the nearest**, sharing the ordered phrase as well as the verb, though with different spelling. Rated **moderate as register**; **no source identified**, since a single nearest rival in a family of four is not a demonstration of dependence. `[I]` **Boaz's piety is the Psalter's piety, which is a fact about his vocabulary.**

### Qohelet's "chance" (2:3) — moderate, register only

**Move 1 only.** Ecclesiastes 2:14–15 uses the cognate construction to argue that the same מִקְרֶה meets wise and fool, which is Qohelet's case against the profit of wisdom. `[T]` **Three verses in the canon hold the construction; two are these.** Rated **moderate as register**, with the irony flagged as inference. **No claim that Ruth's narrator is answering Qohelet** — the direction and even the fact of contact are unestablished, and both books are in the Writings without a fixed order between them.

### Exodus 16 (the manna chapter) — a proposal, capped, and queued

**What the measurement actually shows.** `[T]` A rare-pair density scan of Ruth 2 against all 859 chapters of the Hebrew Bible of ten verses or more ranks **Exodus 16 first**, and the shared vocabulary is the manna narrative's own: **לָקַט ("gather"), שָׂבַע ("be satisfied"), יָתַר ("leave over"), לֶחֶם ("bread"), בֹּקֶר ("morning"), שָׂדֶה ("field").** A chapter-level test tightens it: **the three verbs לָקַט, שָׂבַע and יָתַר stand together within a single chapter in exactly two chapters of the Hebrew Bible — Exodus 16 and Ruth 2.**

**Why it is nonetheless capped at moderate and claimed as nothing.** `[I]` Four reasons, and they are the gate's reasons. The individual lemmas are common (34, 93, 101, 189, 277 and 296 verses). No single verse unites them, so the contact exists only at chapter scale, where the comparison is between a 36-verse chapter and a 23-verse one. **The link that would have been decisive is a homograph: the manna-omer is a different lemma from Ruth's sheaf-omer, as shown under Tool 7** — so the apparent third pillar collapses on inspection. And a pattern claim of this kind is **barred from rising above moderate until a chance baseline has been run**, which has not been done: two chapters out of 929 is suggestive, but the words are the ordinary vocabulary of gathering and eating food, and no null distribution has been computed. **Stated as a proposal, tagged `[I]`, and named below as the next claim audit's first target for this book.**

## Tool 12 — Genre

Hebrew narrative prose, in its most characteristic mode: **a scene whose theology is carried entirely by arrangement and dialogue.** `[T]`

**What the genre licenses.** The narrator's refusal to say "God arranged this" at 2:3 is a convention, not an admission. `[I]` Hebrew narrative habitually states the human surface and leaves the divine agency to the reader who has been given the facts — which is why 2:1 precedes 2:3. **The reader is expected to do the work the text declines to do.**

**What the genre forbids.** Reading 2:8–13 as courtship. `[I]` The register is employer to labourer, with kinship terms (בִּתִּי) and deference formulas (אֲדֹנִי, שִׁפְחָה) doing exactly what such formulas do in Hebrew narrative — marking relative standing. **Chapter 3 introduces the marriage question; chapter 2 does not, and importing it flattens the one thing chapter 2 is about, which is provision without obligation.**

**What the embedded legal vocabulary licenses.** A scene built on three statutes is making a claim about law-keeping, not only about character. `[I]` **The genre of the chapter is narrative; the substance of its argument is legal.**

## Tool 13 — Copycat

**Boaz is imitable, and this is one of the places in the book where the copycat question has a straight answer.** `[I]` He does four things, each of them `[T]` in the text: he keeps the statute (2:16); he exceeds it, at cost to his own harvest (2:15–16); he protects someone with no claim on him (2:9); and he arranges the benefit so that she does not know (2:15–16). **The fourth is the one a sermon usually leaves out and the one most worth preaching.**

**Ruth is imitable in a narrower way.** `[T]` She asks, she goes, she works from morning, she accepts food, and she claims nothing — twice declining a status she might have claimed (2:10, 2:13). *Confidence: moderate*; her industry is `[T]` from 2:7 and 2:17, her motives are not stated.

**Naomi is not imitable here.** `[T]` She sends Ruth out, says two words, and stays home. The text passes no judgement, and neither should the sermon. `[I]`

**The copycat trap specific to this passage.** Turning the chapter into an exhortation to generosity in which God does not act. `[I]` **The scene has a providence at 2:3 and a prayer at 2:12, and a sermon that preaches only Boaz's example has preached the half of the chapter that the narrator took trouble to frame with the other half.**

## Tool 14 — Bible Timeline

**Where this sits.** Judges period, pre-monarchy, at the barley harvest in Bethlehem. `[T]`

**What is in force.** The Sinai legislation, specifically the gleaning and firstfruits laws — and this unit is the canon's only narrative of their observance. `[T]`

**What has not happened.** No king, no temple, no central sanctuary in view. `[T]` **The law is kept here by a private landowner in a provincial town with nobody watching**, which is the condition the book is interested in. `[I]`

**Where it points.** Forward to David, through the genealogy at 4:18–22. `[T]` **The man who keeps the gleaning law generously is the great-grandfather of the king**, which is the book's own way of connecting ordinary obedience to the royal line. *Confidence: high* on the genealogy; the causal reading of it is `[I]`.

## Tool 15 — Who Am I?

**The reader's position is the one the narrator built at 2:1: knowing more than the characters.** `[I]` The reader knows there is a kinsman while Ruth picks a field at random, and knows about the order in 2:16 which Ruth never learns. **The chapter is designed to be read by someone who can see the providence from outside it.**

**That position has a cost and the passage means it to.** `[I]` The reader cannot un-know 2:1, and so cannot feel 2:3 as Ruth felt it. **The one thing the reader is not permitted is Ruth's experience of the morning**, which was of hard work and an uncertain outcome.

**Where the reader is placed morally:** among the people with fields. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate.* The scene's commands are all addressed to the owner and his staff, not to the gleaner. **The chapter's imperatives are for the people who have the grain.**

## Tool 16 — So What?

**(i) Providence is shown working through a statute, a season and a man's discretion.** `[T]` No miracle occurs. The food comes from a law that was already on the books, a harvest that was already ripe, and a decision Boaz did not have to make. **A congregation that can only recognise God's action when it is unusual will not be able to read this chapter.**

**(ii) The law's floor is not the law's ceiling.** `[T]` Boaz obeys Leviticus at 2:16 and then goes past it — ordering grain pulled from the bundles, which no statute requires. **The measure of his righteousness is what he does above the minimum, in private, for a foreigner.**

**(iii) The one being helped is not asked to describe herself correctly first.** `[T]` Ruth calls herself נָכְרִיָּה, a foreigner — the one category the gleaning law does not cover — and Boaz corrects neither her theology nor her status. He feeds her and gives orders about her. `[I]` **Help arrives before the paperwork is right.**

## Extension — Original Languages

Lexis is under Tool 7. Four grammatical and textual points remain.

**The ketiv/qere at 2:1 — and it is the only one in the chapter.** `[T]` A scan of all 23 verses of Ruth 2 for unpointed forms returns **exactly one: מידע at 2:1.** The BHS apparatus gives the ketiv as **מְיֻדָּע** (a pual participle of יָדַע, "one known, an acquaintance") and the qere as **מוֹדַע** ("kinsman"), noting that many manuscripts read with the qere. `[T]` **The word on which Boaz's whole relation to Naomi depends is a reading tradition**, and both readings come from the root "to know". The Greek has ἀνὴρ γνώριμος, "a man known" — which follows the ketiv's sense. `[T]` **Nothing in the chapter turns on which is read**, since both yield a relative known to the family; but a preacher who says "the Hebrew says *kinsman*" should know that this is the qere, as BHS prints it, and that the consonants are the participle.

**שֹׁל־תָּשֹׁלּוּ at 2:16 — infinitive absolute with finite verb.** `[T]` The standard Hebrew construction for emphasis, and both versions render it adverbially. **Boaz does not say "pull out some"; he says "pulling out, you shall pull out".**

**וְהָלַכְתְּ and the second-person feminine forms of 2:8–9.** `[T]` Boaz's speech is addressed throughout in the feminine singular, with several forms carrying the archaic or dialectal final yod (תִדְבָּקִין at 2:8). The apparatus does not query them. **A morphological feature, not a textual problem**, and nothing in the sense depends on it.

**The cognate accusative at 2:3.** `[T]` וַיִּקֶר מִקְרֶהָ — the verb קָרָה with its own derived noun as subject. **English cannot do it without sounding odd**: "her chance chanced". Both versions use "she happened to come", which is accurate and silent about the figure.

## Extension — Textual Variants

**The one place a variant bears on the meaning: the end of 2:7.** `[T]`

MT reads זֶה שִׁבְתָּהּ הַבַּיִת מְעָט. The BHS apparatus records two notes on the verse: the Greek has ἑσπέρας ("evening"), and for the final clause the Greek has **οὐ κατέπαυσεν ἐν τῷ ἀγρῷ** — "she did not rest in the field" — which the apparatus retroverts as **לֹא שָׁבְתָה בַשָּׂדֶה.** `[T]` The Rahlfs text confirms it: *καὶ ἕως ἑσπέρας, οὐ κατέπαυσεν ἐν τῷ ἀγρῷ μικρόν.*

**Three differences, and the triage.**

| Difference | MT | Greek | Category |
|---|---|---|---|
| the verbal form | שִׁבְתָּהּ, read from יָשַׁב ("sit") | read from שָׁבַת ("rest") | **a Hebrew homograph resolved the other way** |
| the noun | הַבַּיִת ("the house") | בַשָּׂדֶה ("in the field") | possible substantive variant |
| polarity | positive | negative ("she did not") | follows from the first two |

**Verdict.** The first difference is category 1 — the same consonants admit two roots, and the Greek chose the other one. `[I]` The second looks like a genuine variant, and the apparatus treats it as one. **The resulting sense is almost opposite:** MT has the foreman mention that she took a short rest indoors; the Greek has him report that she did not rest at all. *Confidence: high* that the divergence is real and material; **uncertain which reading is original, and I will not adjudicate it** — this is the apparatus's territory and the apparatus does not resolve it either.

**A pattern across two digs, worth naming.** `[T]` This is the **second** crux in two consecutive units where the Greek resolves a Hebrew homograph against the reading the Hebrew tradition and the English versions take: at 1:21, עָנָה read as "afflict" rather than "testify"; here, שִׁבְתָּהּ read as "rest" rather than "sit". **Two instances is not a pattern, and it is not claimed as one** — but it is a useful warning that this book's hard places tend to be homographs rather than corruptions. `[I]`

**One further divergence, observed in the Rahlfs text and not in the apparatus.** `[T]` At 2:13 MT has **וְאָנֹכִי לֹא אֶהְיֶה** כְּאַחַת שִׁפְחֹתֶיךָ ("though I am **not** like one of your maidservants"); Rahlfs has *καὶ ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ ἔσομαι ὡς μία τῶν παιδισκῶν σου* — "and behold, I **shall be** as one of your maidservants", **with no negative.** The sense shifts from self-deprecation to acceptance of a place. The BHS apparatus carries no note here, so this is my own observation from the export and is reported as such, without a judgement on its origin. **The pulpit and study texts both follow MT.**

**Everything else in the chapter's apparatus is immaterial to preaching:** 2:6 omitted in the Syriac, 2:16 omitted in the Syriac, minor orthographic notes at 2:18–23. `[T]`

## Extension — Historical Background

**Gleaning as a system.** The harvester cut the standing grain and bound it; what fell was left. `[S]` A gleaner worked behind the reapers, bending for individual stalks, and the yield depended on how carelessly the reapers worked. **This is why 2:16 is generous in a way that costs money**: pulling grain deliberately from the bound bundles transfers harvested crop, not spillage. *Confidence: high* on the economics; **the detail that this is what צֶבֶת means is limited by the word's being a hapax.** `[T]`

**An ephah of barley (2:17).** Outside this unit, but it calibrates it: a substantial amount for one day's gleaning, on any of the proposed values for the measure. `[S]` **The surplus at 2:14 and the load at 2:17 are the chapter's evidence that Boaz's instruction worked.**

**A woman alone in a field.** Boaz's order that the young men not touch her (2:9) and his warning against going to another field (2:8) presuppose a real risk. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate–high*; the text states the instruction, and the inference that it answers a danger is the natural one. **The protection is part of the provision, and the narrator mentions it before the food.**

**Barley before wheat.** 2:23 has her continuing to the end of the wheat harvest, so the chapter opens at the start of a season of several weeks. `[T]`

## Extension — Original Audience

**What the first hearers knew that a modern congregation does not.** The statute. `[I]` An audience that had heard Leviticus 19 read would recognise 2:16 as the law being quoted, and would recognise the *gap* between "leave the gleanings" and "pull grain out of the bundles for her". **The generosity is legible only to someone who knows the floor.** *Confidence: high* that the law was known; the precise recognition is an inference.

**What they would have noticed about the labels.** `[I]` The law says "the poor and the sojourner". **Ruth is called the Moabitess twice in this unit (2:2, 2:6) and calls herself a foreigner once (2:10), and the law's own words for her are never used.** An audience holding Deuteronomy 23:3 in mind would feel the problem that the chapter does not state: the gleaning law provides for the גֵּר, and nobody in the scene calls her one. *Confidence: moderate* on the audience's discomfort; **high on the vocabulary facts**, which are measured under Tool 7.

**What they would have expected at 2:3.** Not providence, on a first reading. `[I]` The narrator's word is "chance", and the disclosure at 2:1 is two verses old. **A first-time hearer is being invited to feel the coincidence and then to distrust the word for it.**

## Extension — Biblical Theology

**The gleaning law as a theology of land.** The laws in Leviticus 19 and 23 and Deuteronomy 24 rest on the premise that the harvest is not wholly the owner's. `[S]` **Boaz's conduct is therefore not charity but restraint — an acknowledgement that part of his field was never his.** *Confidence: moderate*; the premise is the law's, stated there, and its application to Boaz's motive is an inference.

**The refuge-under-wings thread.** Measured under Tool 7: a Psalter idiom, four rivals, all Psalms. `[T]` **Boaz prays that a Moabite has come under the wings of the God of Israel, and chapter 3 will have her ask him to be the means of it.** The thread runs from a prayer about God to a request to a man, and the book does not treat the second as a reduction of the first. `[I]`

**The "full wages" thread.** מַשְׂכֹּרֶת in Genesis 29 and 31 is wages owed and withheld within a family. `[T]` **Ruth's wages are prayed for by the kinsman who will end up paying them**, at 4:9–10. *Confidence: moderate* on the connection as a thread; the lexical facts are high.

**Food as the sign of covenant faithfulness.** 1:6 (the LORD gave bread), 2:14 (she ate and was satisfied), 2:18 (the surplus reaches Naomi), 4:13 (the LORD gave conception). `[T]` **The book's four divine or near-divine gifts are all things to eat or a child**, and this unit holds the middle two.

## Extension — Difficult Verses

**2:7's unreadable clause.** Treated under Textual Variants. **The honest pastoral position is that the foreman's last remark cannot be translated with confidence, and nothing in the chapter depends on it.** `[I]` A preacher who builds a point on Ruth's diligence from "she has continued from morning until now" is on solid ground (that part is clear); one who builds on "she rested only a little" or "she sat in the house" is building on the disputed clause.

**Is Boaz's instruction at 2:16 deceptive?** `[I]` He arranges for grain to reach her in a way she will read as good gleaning. **The text neither flags this nor hides it from the reader**, and the concealment is from Ruth, not from the audience. *My reading:* it is the generosity of someone protecting another's dignity, and the narrator shows it approvingly by showing it at all. *Confidence: moderate* — the approval is inferred from the arrangement, since the narrator passes no verdict on Boaz anywhere.

**Does Boaz's prayer at 2:12 make him look passive — praying for what he could provide?** `[I]` The objection is anachronistic but worth answering, because congregations feel it. **At 2:12 he does not yet know what chapter 3 will ask of him**, and at 2:16 he is already providing in the only currency he has that day. *Low confidence* on anything about his state of mind; the text reports his words and his orders and nothing of his intentions.

**The hard question the chapter raises and does not answer:** why does Naomi, who knows about Boaz (2:20), say nothing at 2:2? `[T]` The narrator tells the reader at 2:1 and lets Naomi reveal her knowledge only after the grain comes home. **The text does not explain the silence**, and filling it in — with bitterness, with shame, with strategy — is reconstruction. *Flagged, not resolved.*

## Extension — Christological Reading

**The type leg, and it is stronger here than anywhere in the book so far.** `[I]`

Four features of Boaz's conduct in this unit are `[T]` in the text, and together they form the pattern: **he keeps the law; he exceeds it at his own expense; he acts for someone with no legal claim on him; and he conceals the arrangement from its beneficiary.** The fourth makes the figure more than a benefactor — **the provision is designed to look to Ruth like the result of her own morning's work.**

**Where the trajectory runs.** `[I]` To a redeemer who keeps the law, bears its cost himself, acts for people with no claim, and arranges their good before they know of it. **The structure of the type is the concealment as much as the generosity.** *Stated as a trajectory reading, not as a claim about the author's intent.*

**The moralism trap, which is the live danger.** `[I]` This chapter is a sermon on generosity waiting to happen, and the sermon is not wrong — Tool 13 says Boaz is imitable. **But a sermon that stops at "be like Boaz" will have preached a chapter framed at 2:1 and 2:3 by a providence that nobody in the scene can see, and will have made the hearers the heroes of a passage whose point is that the help came from outside.** The guard is the narrator's own: **preach 2:1 before 2:16.**

**What this passage will not bear.** Boaz as a Christ-cipher at every point. `[T]` He is a landowner with a workforce, he asks who Ruth is because he does not know, and he needs a foreman to tell him. **The overview's trap 5 applies: the typology is in the pattern of his conduct, not in his person at every detail.**

## Preaching Pitfalls

**1. Preaching the chapter as a lesson in generosity with the frame removed.** `[I]` The commonest error available here. **2:1 and 2:3 are the narrator's work, and they say that the help was arranged before the helper decided anything.** A sermon on Boaz's kindness that never mentions the kinsman-disclosure or the word "chance" has preached half a chapter.

**2. Reading 2:8–13 as romance.** `[T]` The register is employer and labourer with kinship and deference formulas. **Chapter 3 raises marriage. Chapter 2 does not**, and importing it costs the chapter its actual subject.

**3. Claiming a verbal echo at 2:12 of Naomi's "empty".** `[T]` Boaz's "full" is a different Hebrew word from Naomi's, and מָלֵא occurs once in the book. **The reversal is real and is located at 2:14 and 3:17 — say it there, where the words are.**

**4. Saying "the Hebrew says *kinsman*" at 2:1.** `[T]` That is the qere, as BHS prints it. The consonants are a passive participle, "one known". **Nothing hangs on it, which is exactly why the claim should not be made with more confidence than the text has.**

**5. Building a point on the end of 2:7.** `[T]` The clause is genuinely obscure and the versions disagree because the Hebrew is hard, not because one of them is careless. **"She worked from morning until now" is safe; anything resting on the rest of the sentence is not.**

**6. Preaching Exodus 16 into this chapter.** `[I]` The temptation is strong — gather, be satisfied, leave over, bread, morning — and the density scan ranks Exodus 16 first of 859. **But the omer that would clinch it is a different Hebrew word, and no baseline has been run.** A congregation told that Ruth 2 is "a new manna" has been given a claim the measurements do not support. **This is the overview's trap 7 in its purest form.**

**7. Treating the gleaning law as charity.** `[S]` The statute restrains the owner rather than obliging him to give; the harvest's margin was never his. **"Boaz was generous" is true and incomplete; "Boaz acknowledged that part of his field belonged to the poor, and then gave more" is the chapter.**

**8. Making Ruth's self-description the chapter's problem to be fixed.** `[T]` She calls herself a foreigner, and nobody corrects her. **The chapter's answer to her status is grain, not a category.**

Until then it is a proposal. **The next claim audit's first target for this book.**

## Text-First Declaration

The Hebrew of Ruth 2 was read from the corpus before any English version and before any secondary source. Every count, chain and collocation in this report was made against the **Westminster Leningrad Codex** lemma index, **verified by lemma and at each individual reference**, never by pointed surface form and never by English gloss; the corpus-wide verse total (23,213) was printed and checked before any canon-wide count was made, per the method fault recorded in the claim audit at v1.1.

**Two faults were caught during this run and both are the documented kind.**

The first was a **silent zero from a homograph suffix**. A search for the 2:10 wordplay and for the four-word Hannah set returned *nothing* because the key `5869` does not match the index's `5869 a` for עַיִן ("eye"). Re-run on a suffix-aware key, the wordplay resolves to **one verse in the canon** and the Hannah set to **three**. Every reportable count in this report was then re-derived on a single consistent suffix-aware key, with a positive control asserting that the key matches a verse the lemma is known to occupy before any result was used. **This is the fault the method log names — the lemma field's homograph suffixes make a naive equality test return a silent zero — and it produced two false absences here before it was caught.**

The second was a **false positive waiting to happen**: the density scan ranks Exodus 16 first of 859 chapters against Ruth 2, and עֹמֶר appears in both. **The two are different lemmas** — Ruth's sheaf (8 verses, all gleaning or firstfruits contexts) and Exodus 16's dry measure (6 verses, all in that chapter). Reading the word behind the hit, as the gate requires, removed the strongest-looking pillar of a claim that is now carried only as a capped proposal.

Every absence claim passed a **positive control run on the identical search**, per the correction recorded in the 4:1–12 report at v1.1: each of the six gleaning-law status words was searched again against a verse it is known to occupy, and all six controls returned it. One intended control — for a "resident alien" term at Leviticus 25:35 — **failed**, so that word was dropped from the report rather than reported as an absence. The claim that שָׂבַע occurs once in Ruth, and that גּוּר occurs once, were each controlled the same way.
