# Ruth claim audit 2 — claims file for blind auditor 2: DESIGN CLAIMS

You are testing ten claims about **patterns, devices and structures** in the book of Ruth. **You have not been told why anyone believes them and you should not try to find out.**

## The rule that governs this whole file

**A design claim is capped at *moderate* until a chance baseline has been run, and a superlative is barred unless it was measured.** A design claim is any claim about a *pattern* rather than a single reference: a structure (chiasm, frame, inclusio built from vocabulary), a "live source", a "register", a programme of allusion, a wordplay used as a device, a "most-used source".

**Your central task for each claim is therefore to compute a null, not to assess plausibility.** A claim that merely *looks* strong and has no null is to be reported as unbaselined, whatever its appeal. The baseline asks: *would this pattern occur about this often by chance?* Build it in whichever of these ways fits the claim:

- rank the claimed partner against every chapter or window of comparable size;
- compare the claimed pairing of two sections against every other pairing of sections of the same sizes;
- reshuffle verses into pseudo-sections of the same size, many times, and see where the real value falls in the resulting distribution;
- enumerate every configuration of the same shape in a comparable corpus and count how many exhibit the claimed arrangement.

**State the null you ran, the distribution it produced, and where the observed value falls in it.** "Above chance" without a distribution is not a baseline.

## D1 — the sit/return wordplay at 4:1–3
**Claim:** Ruth 4:1–3 deliberately plays the root *yšb* ("sit") against the root *šwb* ("return"), as a device.
**Baseline needed:** how often do these two roots co-occur within a three-verse window across the Hebrew Bible, and is the Ruth 4 instance distinctive in density or arrangement?

## D2 — Deuteronomy 19 as a register for Ruth 4
**Claim:** Deuteronomy 19 is a live register behind Ruth 4. It ranks second of 859 chapters on a rare-pair density scan against Ruth 4, and holds Deuteronomy's only two occurrences of the lemma "redeemer".
**Baseline needed:** a null for that density rank. Does Deuteronomy 19's density against Ruth 4 clear the band produced by reshuffled pseudo-chapters of the same size? Verify the "only two occurrences in Deuteronomy" claim separately.

## D3 — a 1 Kings 19:19–21 leave-taking parallel at Ruth 1:8–16
**Claim:** Ruth 1:8–16 is patterned on Elijah's call of Elisha. The measured anchor offered is that "kiss" with "father" and "mother" stands in one verse of the canon, 1 Kings 19:20.
**Baseline needed:** verify the anchor; then test whether the wider contact between Ruth 1 and 1 Kings 19 exceeds chance for chapters of those sizes.

## D4 — a kiss/weep chiasm around Ruth 1:10–13
**Claim:** Ruth 1:8–14 is arranged chiastically around 1:10–13, with kissing and weeping as the outer elements.
**Baseline needed:** a chance baseline for the arrangement. How often does a passage of this length in Hebrew narrative produce an apparently chiastic distribution of two repeated motifs?

## D5 — the mirrored divine names at Ruth 1:20–21
**Claim:** Ruth 1:20–21 is designed, with four divine names in a mirrored order (Shaddai · YHWH · YHWH · Shaddai).
**Baseline needed:** enumerate **every** two-verse window in the Hebrew Bible containing exactly four divine names, and count how many of those windows show a mirrored (ABBA) sequence. That gives the chance rate directly. Verify the Ruth name-order first.

## D6 — Job as an authorial programme in Ruth
**Claim:** Ruth draws on Job as a programme, not merely sharing idiom. Offered in support: "be bitter" with Shaddai stands in two verses of the canon (Job 27:2, Ruth 1:20); Shaddai with "answer" in three (Job 31:35, 40:2, Ruth 1:21); Shaddai stands in 48 verses, 31 in Job.
**Baseline needed:** test Ruth's whole vocabulary against Job as a book and against each of Job's chapters, with a null. A density scan reportedly ranks Job 2 only thirtieth of 859 against Ruth 1 — check that, and decide whether anything beyond the Shaddai verses connects the two books.

## D7 — Exodus 16 (the manna chapter) against Ruth 2  **[test this one first]**
**Claim:** Ruth 2 is patterned on the manna narrative. Offered in support: a rare-pair density scan ranks Exodus 16 first of 859 chapters against Ruth 2; and the three lemmas "glean/gather", "be satisfied" and "leave over" co-occur within a single chapter in exactly two chapters of the Hebrew Bible, Exodus 16 and Ruth 2.
**Baseline needed:** the decisive one. Those three lemmas stand in roughly 34, 93 and 101 verses respectively. **Compute how often three lemmas of comparable individual frequency co-occur within one chapter, by repeated random draws of lemma triples matched for frequency.** If two chapters out of 929 is about what chance gives for such a triple, the claim is idiom. Also note whether any single verse unites the three, and check whether a fourth apparent link, the word "omer", is the same lemma in both chapters.

## D8 — the open-and-close frame of Ruth 2:1–16
**Claim:** Ruth 2:1–16 is deliberately framed: it opens with information withheld from the characters (2:1, an aside to the reader) and closes with generosity withheld from its recipient (2:15–16, spoken after Ruth has returned to work), with the dialogue in the middle.
**Baseline needed:** a chance baseline for the arrangement. Is an aside-then-dialogue-then-private-instruction shape distinctive, or the ordinary shape of a Hebrew narrative scene involving an employer?

## D9 — Leviticus as the book's most-used legal source
**Claim:** Leviticus is the legal book Ruth draws on most. Offered in support: "glean" with "leave" in three verses (Lev 19:10, Lev 23:22, Ruth 2:16); the lemma "sheaf" with four of its eight verses in Lev 23; "roasted grain" with one of five in Lev 23:14; and the lemma "right of redemption" with nine of thirteen verses in Lev 25.
**Baseline needed:** a null that accounts for book size and vocabulary overlap. Leviticus is long and its legal vocabulary is agricultural; so is Ruth's subject matter. **Compare Ruth's measured contact with Leviticus against its contact with Deuteronomy, Exodus and Numbers, normalised for length**, and say whether Leviticus's lead survives normalisation.

## D10 — a five-fold closing correspondence between Ruth 4:13–17 and chapter 1
**Claim:** Ruth 4:13–17 was built as a systematic answer to chapter 1. Five correspondences are offered: the verb "bear" (1:12 impossibility → nine uses in ch. 4); the noun "child" (1:5 → 4:16, the book's only two); the root *šwb* (1:21 hiphil → 4:15 hiphil participle, the book's last use); the verb form *tiqreʾnah* (1:20, 1:21 → twice at 4:17, the book's four occurrences); and the town's question at 1:19 answered by the naming at 4:17.
**Baseline needed:** how often does the closing scene of a short Hebrew narrative re-use its opening's distinctive vocabulary to this degree? **Build the null from comparable short narratives** — e.g. Jonah, Esther, and self-contained narrative blocks of similar length — measuring vocabulary shared between each one's first and last scenes, and see where Ruth falls. Verify the five individual measurements first; they may be right while the pattern claim is not.

## Claims raised by the previous auditor. Test these too, on the same terms.

## D11 — Ruth qualifies "field" by region only in Moab
**Claim:** the noun שָׂדֶה stands in 13 verses of Ruth; it is qualified by "Moab" in seven instances across six verses, and the Bethlehem field is always bare or indefinite — there is no "field of Bethlehem" or "field of Judah".
**Baseline needed:** how often do short narratives attach a regional qualifier to שָׂדֶה at all, and is bare "the field" simply the default once a scene's location is established? A frequency control over comparable narrative chapters.

## D12 — Ruth never reuses a root for "leave / be left"
**Claim:** the book uses four distinct, non-overlapping lemmas for the idea: 7604 niphal (1:3, 1:5), `5800 a` (1:16, 2:11, 2:16, 2:20), 3498 (2:14, 2:18), `7673 a` (4:14).
**Baseline needed:** the rate at which narratives of about 85 verses reuse one root for this idea. Four lemmas for four distinguishable senses may be ordinary Hebrew rather than design.

## D13 — "call" is confined to the book's two naming scenes
**Claim:** קָרָא 7121 stands in five verses of Ruth — 1:20, 1:21 and 4:11, 4:14, 4:17 (twice) — with none in chapters 2–3.
**Baseline needed:** the expected distribution of a lemma occurring in roughly 735 verses of the canon across an 85-verse book. A two-chapter gap may well be chance; compute the probability directly.

## D14 — both narrator reports of inner state fall in the threshing-floor scene
**Claim:** the narrator states a character's feeling only at 3:7 ("his heart was glad") and 3:8 ("the man was startled"), both in one scene.
**Baseline needed:** a count of narrator inner-state reports per chapter in comparable narrative. Two instances cannot be shown to cluster without one.
