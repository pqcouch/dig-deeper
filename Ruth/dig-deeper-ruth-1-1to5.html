The Passage

Headline Findings

  1. The opening sentence is not a conceptual echo of the patriarchal famine-migrations; it is their wording. The ordered clause וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ (“and there was a famine in the land”) stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Gen 12:10, Gen 26:1 and Ruth 1:1 (WLC, plain and skeletal searches) [T]; and רָעָב (“famine”) with גּוּר (“to sojourn”) in one verse stands in seven — Gen 12:10; Gen 47:4; 2 Kgs 8:1; Jer 42:17; Jer 42:22; Jer 44:12; Ruth 1:1 [T]. Surfaced by: Author’s Purpose, Context, Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion, Bible Timeline. High confidence on the wording. This corrects the sweep, which rated the link moderate on the ground that it was “carried by shape rather than wording” and that the shared vocabulary was “common”; it is neither shape-only nor common.

  2. The marriage at 1:4 is reported in the canon’s rare idiom, and the book’s own Israelite marriage is reported in the ordinary one. Ruth 1:4 has וַיִּשְׂאוּ לָהֶם נָשִׁים (“they took up wives for themselves”) — נָשָׂא (“to lift, carry, take”) with אִשָּׁה. In the marriage sense that collocation stands in six verses: Judg 21:23; Ruth 1:4; Ezra 10:44; 2 Chr 11:21; 13:21; 24:3 [T], against 116 verses holding the ordinary לָקַח (“to take”) with אִשָּׁה [T]. Ruth 4:13 uses the ordinary verb: וַיִּקַּח בֹּעַז אֶת־רוּת (“so Boaz took Ruth”) [T]. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Translations, Quotation/Allusion Move 4. High confidence on the distribution; moderate that the contrast is designed.

  3. These five verses are the only stretch of the book in which God is neither named nor spoken to, and the vocabulary that carries the book begins the verse after the last death. No divine name or epithet stands in vv.1–5 [T]; YHWH is first named at 1:6, and there as something Naomi heard [T]. The in-law vocabulary on which the whole story runs — חָמוֹת (“mother-in-law”), 10 occurrences, and כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”), 7 — has not one occurrence in this unit, and its first is at 1:6, the verse immediately following [T]. Surfaced by: Narrator’s Comment, Vocabulary, Repetition, Move 4. High confidence.

  4. The study text preserves a two-word chain that the pulpit text breaks — and here the usual direction is reversed. At 1:5 the Hebrew drops בֵּן (“son”), which it has used at 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3, and substitutes יְלָדֶיהָ (“her children”); the noun יֶלֶד stands in two verses of the book, 1:5 and 4:16, where Naomi takes הַיֶּלֶד (“the child”) into her lap [T]. The NASB95 keeps it (“her two children” → “the child”); the ESV levels it (“her two sons” → “the child”), and so does the NIV84 [T]. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Translations, Move 4. High confidence. The sweep’s fifth headline — that for this book the pulpit text is the more transparent of the two at five load-bearing points — does not hold in this unit, and the preacher should know it.

  5. The naming word is concentrated here and nowhere else in the chapter, and chapter 4’s business is raising the names this unit buries. שֵׁם (“name”) occurs 14 times in the book, in 9 verses; five of those occurrences stand in 1:2 and 1:4, and they are the only five in chapter 1 [T]. The word next appears at 2:1 (Boaz), and its work in chapter 4 is לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת (“to raise up the name of the dead”, 4:5, 4:10) [T]. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Move 4, Author’s Purpose. High confidence on the count; moderate–high on the reading.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level. Taken from the overview rather than re-derived: to show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and to give Israel David as the fruit [I: overview]. The keynotes are שׁוּב (“to return”) and then גֹּאֵל (“redeemer”).

Passage level. This unit’s contribution is to establish the deficit the rest of the book exists to make good, and to establish it in two currencies at once: persons and name. In five verses the narrator introduces a household of five, names all five, and removes three of them. He also expends the whole of chapter 1’s supply of שֵׁם (“name”) doing it — five of the book’s fourteen occurrences, all in vv.2 and 4 [T]. The unit is therefore not merely backstory; it is the setting of the account that chapter 4 settles, and it is set in the very vocabulary chapter 4 uses to settle it (לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת, “to raise up the name of the dead”, 4:5, 4:10) [T].

A second, quieter contribution: the unit establishes that this story will be told without divine commentary. The narrator has the means to editorialise and uses them later — he reports Boaz’s inner state at 3:7, explains an obsolete custom at 4:7, and attributes the conception to YHWH in his own voice at 4:13 [T]. Here he does none of it. Three men die and he offers no cause, no verdict and no theology [T]. That restraint is the author’s first move and it governs how the book must be preached.

Choice and pacing. For narrative, Tool 1 asks what the author included and where he hit the accelerator. He has compressed roughly a decade and three deaths into five verses, giving each man’s death a single clause, while he will later spend eighteen verses on one night at a threshing floor (3:1–18) [T]. The ratio is the argument: the losses are the premise, not the subject.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. Nothing in this book precedes the unit, so Question 1 is answered canonically rather than internally — and this is the point at which the sweep stopped at a generality (“a famine-and-migration pattern the patriarchs already ran”) that the corpus turns into a measured claim.

The preceding movement is the canon’s famine-migration script, and Ruth 1:1 does not merely resemble it. It reproduces its opening clause. וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 12:10 (Abram goes down to Egypt), Gen 26:1 (Isaac goes to Gerar) and Ruth 1:1, verified plain and skeletal in the WLC [T]. More than that: Gen 26:1 carries both of Ruth 1:1’s opening elements — the famine clause and a בִּימֵי dating formula, מִלְּבַד הָרָעָב הָרִאשׁוֹן אֲשֶׁר הָיָה בִּימֵי אַבְרָהָם (“besides the previous famine that had occurred in the days of Abraham”) [T].

What the preceding movement has therefore set up is a pattern with a divine ruling attached to it. At Gen 26:2–3, immediately after the clause Ruth reuses, YHWH appears to Isaac and says אַל־תֵּרֵד מִצְרָיְמָה (“do not go down to Egypt”), שְׁכֹן בָּאָרֶץ (“stay in the land”), גּוּר בָּאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת (“sojourn in this land”) and I will be with you and bless you [T]. The patriarchal script thus contains, at its second instance, an instruction to sojourn inside the land rather than outside it. Elimelech does the opposite: he goes לָגוּר (“to sojourn”) outside it [T].

This sharpens the unit’s difficulty without resolving it, and the honest reason is also in the measured set. Of the seven verses holding רָעָב with גּוּר, the company runs in both directions: Gen 12:10 and Gen 47:4 are patriarchal precedent with no condemnation at the point of departure; 2 Kgs 8:1 is a prophet positively commanding the move — Elisha tells the Shunammite קוּמִי וּלְכִי … וְגוּרִי בַּאֲשֶׁר תָּגוּרִי (“arise and go… and sojourn wherever you can sojourn”), because YHWH has called for a famine [T]; and Jer 42:17, 42:22 and 44:12 are judgement oracles in which precisely those who set their faces to go to Egypt לָגוּר שָׁם (“to sojourn there”) die by sword and famine [T]. The idiom itself passes no verdict. Four of its six non-Ruth instances treat the move as legitimate or commanded; three treat it as fatal disobedience. High confidence on the distribution; the evaluative question stays open, which is where the text leaves it.

Necessity answer — why here, and not elsewhere. Two reasons the text supplies.

First, the book is a reversal and a reversal must be measured from a floor. Naomi will say at 1:21 אֲנִי מְלֵאָה הָלַכְתִּי וְרֵיקָם הֱשִׁיבַנִי יְהוָה (“I went out full, and the LORD has brought me back empty”) [T]. That sentence requires a narrated going-out, and these are the only verses that narrate it. Note the tension, which is a finding in its own right: the narrator’s account of the going-out does not describe fullness. He describes a famine. Naomi’s “full” is her own word about her household — husband and two sons — not the narrator’s word about her circumstances [I]. A preacher who reads 1:21 as straightforwardly accurate reporting has not read 1:1.

Second, the book will close by raising a name, and a name cannot be raised that has not first been given and lost. The unit gives five names and extinguishes three [T]; 4:5 and 4:10 take up the business of raising the name of the dead, and 4:10 is the first place in the book that says which son Ruth had married [T]. The unit exists here because it is the deposit against which chapter 4 draws.

Implication for the passage. The unit is not scene-setting that a sermon may summarise in a sentence. It is the book’s balance sheet, written in the book’s own vocabulary, and the sermon’s weight should fall on what is owed rather than on how the debt was incurred — about which the text is silent.

General contextual notes

Immediately before: nothing — the book opens here. Immediately after: 1:6–22, Naomi’s turn for Bethlehem, the parting on the road, Ruth’s refusal, and the arrival at Bethlehem renamed in her own mouth as מָרָא (“Mara”, “bitter”, 1:20). The join is tight and lexical: 1:6 supplies the book’s first כִּי (“for, because”), its first naming of YHWH, its first כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”), and its first לֶחֶם (“bread”) as a common noun [T]. Four firsts in one verse, all of them things this unit withholds.

Section: the first of the arc map’s seven movements, “Emptied” (1:1–5) [S: overview]. Book: four chapters of Hebrew prose narrative closing with a genealogy.

Read out of context? Rarely — the unit is not a stock text. The commoner fault is the reverse: it is skipped, and the sermon starts at 1:6 or 1:16, with the result that Naomi’s lament is preached without the ledger it refers to.

3. Structure

A single accelerating paragraph in five movements, with a disjunctive seam at v.2 and a near-repeated cadence at vv.3 and 5.

Verse Movement Function
1 Era, famine, departure Sets the clock, the pressure and the direction of travel; four participants, none named
2 The names Disjunctive waw (וְשֵׁם, “and the name of”) breaks the narrative chain to supply the register; arrival and settling
3 The first death Elimelech dies; וַתִּשָּׁאֵר (“and she was left”) with two sons
4 The marriages Two Moabite wives named; ten years pass
5 The two deaths Mahlon and Chilion die; וַתִּשָּׁאֵר (“and she was left”) with no one

Device: a two-beat cadence, not a chiasm. Verses 3 and 5 run the same two-clause shape — a death-verb from מוּת, then וַתִּשָּׁאֵר (“and she was left”) with a count of what remains [T]:

v.3   וַיָּמָת אֱלִימֶלֶךְ …        וַתִּשָּׁאֵר הִיא וּשְׁנֵי בָנֶיהָ
      "and Elimelech died"          "and she was left, she and her two sons"

v.5   וַיָּמוּתוּ גַם־שְׁנֵיהֶם …   וַתִּשָּׁאֵר הָאִשָּׁה מִשְּׁנֵי יְלָדֶיהָ וּמֵאִישָׁהּ
      "and both of them died too"   "and the woman was left of her two children and of her husband"

The second beat differs from the first in three measurable ways, and each is a loss: she is no longer הִיא (“she”, by her own name’s antecedent) but הָאִשָּׁה (“the woman”); the preposition turns from the accompanying waw (“she and her two sons”) to privative מִן (“left of” — that is, deprived of); and the sons have become יְלָדִים (“children”) [T]. The cadence is the structure: the same sentence, run twice, emptying as it goes. High confidence on the three changes; moderate–high that the repetition is deliberate.

Where the modern unit boundary comes from. The unit 1:1–5 is a modern division, and worth saying so plainly. The WLC reading file carries exactly one paragraph marker in the whole of Ruth — a פ (petuchah) after 4:17 [T]. There is no setumah or petuchah at 1:5/1:6 as Leningrad prints it, so the Masoretic paragraphing of this manuscript does not mark a break where every modern edition and every preaching plan does. The ESV sets a paragraph break after v.5; the NASB95 runs on [T]. The division is a sound literary judgement — the deaths close, the rising begins at וַתָּקָם (“and she arose”, 1:6) — but it is the editors’ and ours, not the manuscript’s [I].

[unchecked — apparatus spread] This is a statement about one manuscript tradition: the corpus holds Leningrad only, and it prints paragraph divisions without variants, so it cannot report how widely this paragraphing is attested. 4. Linking Words

For narrative, the significant finding here is an absence that the text itself makes visible, and it is worth stating because it is unusual.

There is not one causal, conditional, purposive or adversative connector in these five verses. No כִּי (“for, because”), no לָכֵן (“therefore”), no אִם (“if”), no אַךְ or אוּלָם (“but”). The unit runs on the plain waw-consecutive (wayyiqtol) chain — וַיְהִי … וַיְהִי … וַיֵּלֶךְ … וַיָּבֹאוּ … וַיִּהְיוּ … וַיָּמָת … וַתִּשָּׁאֵר … וַיִּשְׂאוּ … וַיֵּשְׁבוּ … וַיָּמוּתוּ … וַתִּשָּׁאֵר — with one disjunctive break [T].

The pattern that makes the absence visible is the book’s own usage immediately afterwards: 1:6 carries two כִּי clauses in a single verse — כִּי שָׁמְעָה (“for she had heard”) and כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה (“that YHWH had visited”) — and chapter 1 goes on to reason with כִּי at 1:10, 1:12 (twice), 1:13 (twice), 1:17, 1:18 and 1:20 [T]. So the narrator can and does supply reasons; he withholds them for exactly as long as the deaths last. Moderate confidence on the design (per the pattern-break rule, an absence claim is capped there); high on the datum.

The three connectors that are present:

Marker Verse Function
Disjunctive וְשֵׁם (“and the name of”) 1:2 Steps out of the narrative chain to supply the register of names. Hebrew’s standard device for background information; the English “The name of the man was…” reproduces it adequately
גַּם (“also, too”) 1:5 Additive, and the unit’s one evaluative word. “Both of them died too” — the particle links the sons’ deaths to the father’s and so does the arithmetic the narrator declines to comment on [T]
כְּ of approximation on עֶשֶׂר (“ten”) 1:4 כְּעֶשֶׂר שָׁנִים, “about ten years”. The narrator is precise about names and vague about time

→ The one place arrow notation helps: death of father →and also→ death of both sons →privative→ the woman left of everything. That is the whole logical content of the unit, and all of it is carried by one particle and one preposition.

6. Narrator’s Comment

No authorial intrusion of any kind occurs in these five verses [T]. There is no fulfilment formula, no editorial aside, no decoder explanation, no theological verdict, and no report of any character’s inner state.

This is admissible as a finding rather than a guess because the book establishes the pattern the unit breaks. The same narrator:

  • reports an inner state the reader could not otherwise know — וַיִּיטַב לִבּוֹ (“and his heart was merry”, of Boaz, 3:7) [T];
  • steps outside the action to explain an obsolete custom — וְזֹאת לְפָנִים בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל (“now this was the custom in former times in Israel”, 4:7) [T];
  • states a divine act in his own voice, once in eighty-five verses — וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן (“and the LORD enabled her to conceive”, 4:13) [T].

So the silence in vv.1–5 is a choice by a narrator demonstrably willing to speak. Moderate confidence on the significance, which is the ceiling the pattern-break rule sets for an absence claim; high on the three cited instances.

What the silence withholds, specifically. Four verdicts a reader wants and does not get: whether the famine was judgement; whether leaving Bethlehem was sin; whether the Moabite marriages were lawful; whether the three deaths were punishment. On all four the narrator is mute [T].

Book-overview note. This confirms the sweep’s first headline — that the narrator attributes exactly one act to God in his own voice, at 4:13 — and supplies its negative pole: the book opens by declining to attribute three deaths to anyone. The claim audit’s discard of a “narratorial cluster” as a design claim (R28) does not touch this, which is an observation about one unit against a cited pattern, not a claim about a distribution [S: audit].

(a) The marked verb at 1:4 — נָשָׂא, not לָקַח

The unit’s sharpest lexical fact, and new to this run.

Ruth 1:4 reports the marriages as וַיִּשְׂאוּ לָהֶם נָשִׁים מֹאֲבִיּוֹת (“and they took up for themselves Moabite wives”) — the verb נָשָׂא (“to lift, carry, bear, take”), not the ordinary לָקַח (“to take”).

(b) שָׁאַר (“to be left, remain”) — the unit’s own word

The niphal וַתִּשָּׁאֵר (“and she was left”) at 1:3 and 1:5. שָׁאַר occurs twice in the book, and both occurrences are in this unit [T] — the only lemma in Ruth whose entire presence falls inside these five verses, and it is the verb of the book’s problem. High confidence on the count.

Two notes. First, the root is the Hebrew Bible’s remnant word — the שְׁאָר and שְׁאֵרִית of the prophets — so the unit describes Naomi in the vocabulary normally used of a surviving remainder of Israel [I], which a reader of the Writings arrives already carrying (see Original Audience Reception). Moderate confidence: the sense here is plainly the plain one, “left over”, and nothing in the unit activates the theological register beyond the word itself. Second, the word has no answer later in the book: nothing is ever said to be “left” again, and 4:14’s blessing uses a different verb — אֲשֶׁר לֹא הִשְׁבִּית לָךְ גֹּאֵל (“who has not left you without a redeemer”) is from שָׁבַת (“to cease”) [T]. The remnant language is not picked up; the reversal is narrated in other words.

(c) יֶלֶד (“child”) for בֵּן (“son”) at 1:5

The substitution is exact and mechanical. בֵּן stands at 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3 and then stops; 1:5, naming the same two individuals at the moment of their death, has מִשְּׁנֵי יְלָדֶיהָ (“of her two children”) [T]. בֵּן returns in Naomi’s own mouth at 1:11–12 (הַעוֹד־לִי בָנִים בְּמֵעַי, “have I yet sons in my womb?”) and then at 4:13, 4:15 and 4:17 [T]; יֶלֶד stands twice only — 1:5 and 4:16 [T].

Why the switch matters: בֵּן is the relational-legal word, the son who carries a name and an inheritance; יֶלֶד is the born one, the child of a mother’s body. At the point where the two men cease to be heirs they are named as what Naomi has borne and lost [I]. And the word waits, unused, until 4:16, where Naomi takes הַיֶּלֶד into her lap [T]. High confidence on the chain; moderate–high on the reading. See Tool 8 — the pulpit text does not carry it.

(d) גּוּר (“to sojourn”) — a hapax in the book

לָגוּר at 1:1 is the only occurrence of גּוּר in Ruth [T]. The Israelite is the sojourner, in Moab; the Moabitess who comes to Israel is never called a sojourner. She calls herself נָכְרִיָּה (“foreigner”, 2:10) [T], and Boaz describes what she has left rather than what she now is (2:11) [T]. The irony is measurable and quiet: the gleaning law whose vocabulary the book reuses provides for the triad הַגֵּר הַיָּתוֹם וְהָאַלְמָנָה (“the sojourner, the orphan and the widow”, Deut 24:19–21) [S: overview, R18], and Ruth is placed in that provision as the widow, not as the sojourner [I]. Moderate confidence.

(i) 1:1 — the opening clause. Category 2, minor. BHS records at 1:1: 𝔊* reads ἐν τῷ κρίνειν = בִּשְׁפֹט, and 𝔖 omits שׁפט [T]. Moderate–high confidence on that diagnosis; high that the omission is real in Swete’s text. Preaching implication: none, and it should not be mentioned from the pulpit. 9. Tone and Feel

Register: the obituary notice. Flat, fast, administrative. The unit reads like a register entry — era, place, names, marriages, deaths, duration — and its coldness is the device.

What carries it:

  • Pacing. A decade and three deaths in five verses; one clause per death [T]. The narrator will later give eighteen verses to a single night (3:1–18). The compression is not haste; it is the refusal to dwell.
  • Register of names. Verse 2 is a list. Five names in two verses, with the same formula (וְשֵׁם, “and the name of”) five times [T]. The effect is documentary, and it makes the deaths in vv.3 and 5 land on named people rather than on figures.
  • The repeated cadence. וַתִּשָּׁאֵר twice, in the same position of the same sentence-shape, with less left each time (Tool 3) [T].
  • The one particle of feeling. גַּם (“too”) at 1:5 [T]. Everything the narrator declines to say about the accumulation is in that one syllable.
  • No sensory detail whatever. No weather, no landscape, no dialogue, no tears. Chapter 1 will have all four from v.9 onwards (kissing, weeping, raised voices) [T]; this unit has none.

Soundtrack. No music. The closest thing is a clock. If anything, a single low sustained note — and the right instruction to a reader is to read it without expression, because the narrator does.

The preacher’s note. The emotional architecture of the sermon should honour this. Do not open warmly; do not supply the pathos the narrator withholds. A congregation that feels the flatness of these verses will feel 1:20–21 properly when Naomi finally supplies the feeling herself, and 4:16 properly when the child is laid in her lap without comment.

No paronomasia claim is made. The one sound-feature worth naming is in the names, and it is handled at Tool 7g with an uncertain flag.

Live sources for this book, carried as elevated priors [S: overview v1.4, as amended by the claim audit]: Genesis — live on six verified contacts, with the “programme” reading discarded (R20); Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 25 — the redemption and name-raising laws, never cited; Deuteronomy 24:19–21 — the gleaning provision (R18); Judges 17 — one verbal contact at 1:1, the “block” reading withdrawn (R19); Proverbs 31:10–31 ↔︎ Ruth 3 — the relation retained, the canonical adjacency withdrawn outright (L1). 1 Samuel is the densest partner book per chapter (R20).

Plain statement for this unit. There is no citation formula and no quoted line [T]. Everything below is verbal or name-based contact, derived first from the unit’s own vocabulary and then tested; the candidates the overview and sweep already named were tested afterwards, not instead.

Gen 12:10; Gen 26:1 → Ruth 1:1 (high confidence on the wording; moderate on what the author intends by it) [T] for the datum, [I] for intentionality

The strongest external contact in the unit, and new to this run as a verbal claim.

Source context. Gen 12:10 stands immediately after the call and the promise (12:1–3) and the first altars (12:7–8): וַיְהִי רָעָב בָּאָרֶץ וַיֵּרֶד אַבְרָם מִצְרַיְמָה לָגוּר שָׁם כִּי־כָבֵד הָרָעָב בָּאָרֶץ (“and there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there, for the famine was severe in the land”) [T]. What follows is the wife-endangerment episode (12:11–20) — the sojourn goes badly, and the narrator records the consequence without moralising about the departure. Gen 26:1 opens Isaac’s cycle: the same clause, plus the backward reference to “the famine in the days of Abraham”, and Isaac goes to Abimelech at Gerar [T]. And then, at 26:2–3, YHWH appears and rules on the question: אַל־תֵּרֵד מִצְרָיְמָה (“do not go down to Egypt”), שְׁכֹן בָּאָרֶץ (“stay in the land”), גּוּר בָּאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת (“sojourn in this land”), with the promise of presence, blessing and the oath sworn to Abraham attached [T]. The function of the cited clause within its own passage is therefore the same both times: it is the trigger that sets a patriarch moving, and in Genesis 26 it is immediately followed by a divine restriction on where the moving may go.

Book usage (Move 2). Genesis is a live source for Ruth on six verified contacts, five of them at the seams of 2:11 and chapter 4 [S: overview, R20]. This is the sixth seam and the earliest: the book’s first sentence. Three of the overview’s Genesis contacts are concentrated in the patriarchal-promise material (12:1 at Ruth 2:11; 24:27 at 2:20; 35:16–20 and 48:7 at 4:11), and Ruth 2:11’s contact — land, birthplace, go, father, shared with Gen 12:1 in two verses only — stands eleven verses after the clause Ruth 1:1 reuses from Gen 12:10 [T]. So the book reaches into the same few lines of Genesis 12 twice, once at its opening and once at Boaz’s summary of Ruth. That is a genuine Move 2 finding and it was not available before this run. Moderate confidence that the two uses are coordinated; high on each wording separately.

OT-to-OT (Move 3). Gen 12:10 and Gen 26:1 are already in deliberate conversation inside Genesis: 26:1 cross-references 12:10 explicitly (“besides the previous famine that had occurred in the days of Abraham”) [T], and the Isaac cycle is narrated throughout as a re-run of the Abraham cycle with a divine adjustment. Ruth 1:1 does not have to put them in conversation; it joins one that Genesis itself opened. And the wider seven-verse set adds a third party already in canonical dialogue with Genesis: Jer 42:17 and 44:12 apply the going-to-Egypt-to-sojourn script as a judgement formula, which is the Genesis script read as a warning [T].

What it adds. Three things, in order of confidence. (1) Ruth’s first sentence places this household inside the canon’s patriarchal famine script, verbally, before it says anything about anyone [T]. (2) The reader who knows the script knows it comes with a ruling attached and a mixed record — commanded at 2 Kgs 8:1, fatal at Jer 42–44, left unjudged at Gen 12:10 [T]. (3) Therefore the narrator’s silence on whether Elimelech sinned is not a gap in the account; it is the script running without its ruling, and the reader is left holding the question the text declines to answer [I]. Moderate confidence on (3), and it is the most preachable of the three.

The ordered phrase מִבֵּית לֶחֶם יְהוּדָה (“from Bethlehem of Judah”) stands in eight verses of the Hebrew Bible — Judg 17:7, 17:8, 17:9, 19:1, 19:18, 1 Sam 17:12, Ruth 1:1, Ruth 1:2 [T]; with לָגוּר (“to sojourn”) in the ordered sequence, in exactly two: Judg 17:8 and Ruth 1:1 [T], plain and skeletal. Source context, with the narrative read. Judges 17 introduces the Levite of the Danite appendix. A young man of Bethlehem in Judah, of the family of Judah, himself a Levite, sojourning (17:7); he leaves the city to sojourn wherever he can find a place and arrives at the house of Micah in the hill country of Ephraim (17:8); he tells Micah the same thing in his own words (17:9); Micah hires him at ten pieces of silver a year, clothing and keep, to be “a father and a priest” (17:10); and Micah concludes עַתָּה יָדַעְתִּי כִּי־יֵיטִיב יְהוָה לִי כִּי הָיָה־לִי הַלֵּוִי לְכֹהֵן (“now I know that the LORD will do me good, because the Levite has become my priest”, 17:13) [T]. The chapter’s function in its own book is to open the account of how a private shrine became the cult of Dan, under the refrain that there was no king in Israel and every man did as he saw fit [T].

Book usage (Move 2). Judges 17 is a live source at exactly one point and no other: the claim audit withdrew the “Judges 17–19 block” and showed that suppressing the Bethlehem formula drops Judges 17 from 18th of 859 chapters to 247th [S: audit, R19]. So Move 2 returns a deliberate nil beyond this verse: the book does not draw on Judges 17 again.

OT-to-OT (Move 3). Judges 17 and Genesis 12/26 are not in canonical conversation with each other in any way that the text shows; no pairing is claimed. The only relation worth naming is that both supply Ruth 1:1 with half of its sentence — the Genesis clause for the famine and the Judges clause for the departure — which is a fact about Ruth, not about them [I].

What it adds. The one clause in the canon most like Ruth 1:1 belongs to the story of a Bethlehemite who left home to sojourn and became the hinge of an idolatrous cult. The contact is verbal and the evaluation is not. Ruth reuses a sentence; she does not reuse a verdict, and nothing in Ruth 1:1 marks the departure as the Levite’s was marked. What it supplies is the era’s texture — the period in which leaving Bethlehem to find somewhere better is the kind of thing that happens — which is what Ruth 1:1’s own dating formula also supplies [I]. Moderate–high on the contact, moderate on the texture reading, and nothing above that.

12. Genre

Hebrew prose narrative, and within it a recognisable sub-form: the introductory notice — era, pressure, protagonist, household register, and a summary of elapsed time. The reading rules are the ordinary ones for Hebrew narrative: the wayyiqtol chain carries the main line and the disjunctive waw at v.2 carries background; the events are presented as having happened; the narrator’s silences are deliberate; characters’ statements are not the narrator’s verdicts.

One rule earns its place here. Because the unit is a notice rather than a scene, its proportions carry no evaluative weight: the brevity of a death-clause is not a measure of the narrator’s indifference, and the ten years compressed into four words are not a judgement on how long they stayed. The temptation to read the pacing as moral commentary is a genre error [I].

Brief and uncontested.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits. The narrated events fall in the judges’ period, which v.1 states [T]: after the conquest, before the monarchy — the era whose refrain in Judges is that there was no king in Israel. The household is named with a theophoric name meaning “my God is king” [I], and the book will close on David, named four generations later and without a royal title (4:17, 22) [T].

Where the book sits — canonical position. Ruth stands in the Ketuvim, fourth in the sequence BHS prints [T]; English Bibles and the Greek tradition place it between Judges and 1 Samuel [S]. Codex Leningradensis itself puts Chronicles first among the Writings, though Ruth keeps the same neighbours either way [S].

The adjacency argument is withdrawn and must not be preached. The Proverbs→Ruth sequence that the toolkit’s own reference material (and earlier drafts of this project’s overview) treated as meaningful is the later arrangement. Baba Batra 14b places Ruth first among the Writings, before Psalms; Campbell states the consensus that the head-of-the-Writings position antedates the move into the Megillot, which Webb dates between the sixth and ninth centuries AD; and Campbell suggests the Leningrad placement may itself have been prompted by the אֵשֶׁת חַיִל link at Ruth 3:11, which would make the lexical link the cause of the adjacency rather than evidence of a design [S: overview v1.4, Logos L1]. The אֵשֶׁת חַיִל relation stands at moderate–high; the adjacency stands at nil.

What the position does imply, which is enough. Ruth is read as a Writing, not as an appendix to Judges. Its first clause fixes the setting in the judges’ period without shelving the book there [I]. In the English and Greek order the same unit reads as the hinge out of Judges into Samuel — and that reading is available and not the Hebrew canon’s own [S].

Canonical and compositional order. The events fall in the judges’ period [T]; the Hebrew canon files the book among the Writings [T]; the English and Greek canons file it after Judges [S]. The direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15 is open, the BHS apparatus itself cross-referring the two at 4:18 [T]. Both orders reported; neither preferred.

Where the reader sits. This side of the cross. What has happened in between that materially changes the reading: the line this unit nearly extinguishes produced David, and through David the Christ whom Matthew’s genealogy names along with Perez, Boaz, Obed, Jesse — and Ruth herself, one of four women in that list [S]. A famine in the house of bread, emptied of its men, is on the far side of a long chain whose end is the one who called himself the bread of life [I].

How that reframes it. It does not make the unit’s silences speak. It means that the reader of 1:1–5 knows something Naomi cannot: that this is the beginning of a line, not the end of a household. That knowledge is the book’s gift to its reader and is the only comfort available in these five verses — and it is comfort the characters do not have, which the sermon should not pretend they do.

15. Who Am I?

Short, and the answer is not a hero.

  • Naomi is the figure the reader is positioned with, and the position is one of loss without explanation. She is not a model (she does nothing here) and not a warning (she is not blamed). She is the one to whom things happen, and the reader stands where she stands — with a ledger and no commentary [I].
  • Elimelech is neither type nor example. The strong temptation to make him a cautionary figure requires the verdict the narrator withholds (Tools 6, 13).
  • The sons are not characters; they are names and deaths. Chilion is never resolved (Move 4).
  • Orpah and Ruth are, in this unit, objects. Reading Ruth’s later loyalty back into 1:4 flattens the book’s own pacing, which withholds her characterisation for nine more verses [T].
  • There is no type of Christ in this unit. Boaz, the book’s redeemer figure, has not appeared; he enters at 2:1 [T]. The Christological material here is of a different kind — need, not type. See the Christological Reading extension.

The misidentification to name. The instinct to self-insert as the one who “stayed faithful” or “left too soon” makes this unit about the reader’s decisions, when the unit is about what a household lost and what God has not yet said. The honest identification is with the position, not with a character: this is what it is to be left, and to be given no reason.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. Not yet an action. The unit asks the reader to hold a loss without an explanation and to keep reading. The narrator’s refusal to interpret (Tool 6) is itself the demand: he will not let the reader settle the question of blame, and he will not let the reader out of the room either. The one thing he requires is that the deficit be counted accurately, which is why he counts it twice (Tool 3) [I].

Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.

Domain Content
Worldview God is not absent from these verses; he is unmentioned. The difference matters and the book will prove it, naming him first at 1:6 and acting in his own voice at 4:13 [T]. A providence that is real and unnarrated is the book’s premise, and a Christian’s ordinary experience of one
Behaviour — stop Explaining other people’s bereavements. Supplying God’s reasons on his behalf. Reading a famine, a death, or a decade of nothing as a verdict on the person it happened to
Behaviour — start Counting losses honestly, as the narrator does — naming what is gone rather than reaching past it. Staying with the people who are “left” (1:3, 1:5) for longer than a sentence
Motivation Not stoicism. The reader can hold the silence because the reader knows the end of the book, and beyond it the end of the line — which is grace already given, not duty newly imposed [I]

Four audiences.

Audience What this unit says
For me My instinct in the face of someone else’s loss is to explain it. This narrator, who knew more than I do, declined. The speck-and-plank question here is whether my theology of providence is a way of not sitting down
For a Christian friend For someone in an unexplained loss — bereavement, a decade that went nowhere — this unit is permission to count it accurately and to refuse the premature verdict, their own included
For the church We are a congregation that includes people who are “left”. The unit’s register — flat, exact, unhurried — is a model for how to speak to them, and its silence is a rebuke to our fluency
For an unbeliever The Bible’s picture of life is not sunnier than theirs. Five verses of famine, migration and three funerals, with no God mentioned, is where this book starts. What it claims is not that such things do not happen but that they are not the end of the account

Prayer in response.

  • Sorry for the explanations I have offered on your behalf.
  • Thank you that you were at work in these five verses where you are not named.
  • Please give me the patience to sit where Naomi sat without hurrying anyone out of it.
  • Mercy on those in this congregation who are counting what is left.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • The wayyiqtol chain is unbroken but for one disjunction. Eleven consecutive waw-consecutive verbs carry vv.1–5, with the single disjunctive וְשֵׁם at 1:2 stepping out to supply the register [T]. Hebrew’s standard foreground/background device, and the only structural signal the unit gives.
  • Privative מִן at 1:5. מִשְּׁנֵי יְלָדֶיהָ וּמֵאִישָׁהּ — “left of” in the sense of deprived of, against the accompanying waw of 1:3 (“she and her two sons”) [T]. The whole change in her situation is carried by a preposition. Both English versions render it with “without” or “bereft of” and the force survives; the contrast with v.3’s construction does not.
  • Doubled וַיְהִי at 1:1. וַיְהִי בִּימֵי … וַיְהִי רָעָב — the formula twice in one verse, the first temporal and the second existential [T]. וַיְהִי בִּימֵי as an ordered phrase stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 14:1; Isa 7:1; Jer 1:3; Ruth 1:1; Esth 1:1 [T]. Four of the five date by a named ruler (Amraphel, Ahaz, Jehoiakim, Ahasuerus); Ruth alone dates by an office with no name in it [T]. High confidence on the distribution; moderate that the omission is pointed — though the book’s own closing name (David, 4:17, 22) and its protagonist’s name (“my God is king”) make the reading attractive [I].
  • Approximation. כְּעֶשֶׂר שָׁנִים (“about ten years”) — the preposition כְּ on a numeral [T]. The ordered phrase stands in Ruth 1:4 alone in the Hebrew Bible [T]. A small thing, verified: the book’s one duration is also its one approximation.

Wordplay and sound-features

Transliteration is given only where the sound is the point.

  • שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים (šəp̄ōṭ haššōp̄ṭîm) — the repeated Š-P-Ṭ consonants in adjacent words, verb then agent [T]. The sound carries the sense of an era defined by an activity rather than by a person. High on the device; it is a standard Hebrew construction, not a pun.
  • בֵּית לֶחֶם with רָעָב in the same clause [T] — the place-name’s second element is the word for bread, and the clause before it says there was none. The sound is doing the work, which is why no translation carries it: a congregation hears “Bethlehem” and “famine” as unrelated words. Moderate as authorial design, which is the ceiling a name-pun can reach.
  • The personal names — handled at Tool 7g. Three of the five are uncertain and flagged as such.

Recommended verification

  • The senses of מַחְלוֹן, כִּלְיוֹן, עָרְפָּה and רוּת (Tool 7g). A lexicon (HALOT, NIDOTTE) or a commentary that works the Hebrew names would settle how much weight each will bear. The corpus cannot: it gives the forms and their distribution and nothing about etymology.
  • Whether the נָשָׂא marriage idiom is treated in the literature as a late-Hebrew marker, and whether any commentator reads Ruth 1:4 against Ezra 10:44. This is the unit’s one genuinely new claim and the one most worth a Logos pass (see Open Questions).

Textual Variants

Category 2 only. The Hebrew/Greek divergences in this unit are classified under the three-way triage at Tool 8; the translation-loss items (the יֶלֶד levelling, the reordering at 1:5) belong there and not here. Three substantive items, all minor, none affecting preaching.

1:1 — the temporal formula

  • The issue: MT has בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים (“in the days when the judges judged”); both Greek editions have ἐν τῷ κρίνειν τοὺς κριτάς, with no equivalent of “in the days of”. BHS records 𝔊* = בִּשְׁפֹט, and 𝔖 omitting שׁפט [T].
  • Manuscript evidence: BHS apparatus at Cp 1, 1 [a–a]; confirmed in Swete and in the Rahlfs export.
  • Most likely original: the fuller Hebrew. The Greek infinitive construction already carries the temporal sense, so the shorter reading is economically explained as translation; and MT’s doubled formula is the harder reading. Moderate confidence.
  • Preaching implication: none. Worth the preacher’s private note only because the clause carries Headline 1.

1:1 — “two” before “sons”

  • The issue: MT וּשְׁנֵי בָנָיו (“and his two sons”); 𝔊 and 𝔖 omit “two” here, while supplying δύο at 1:2, 1:3 and 1:5 [T].
  • Manuscript evidence: BHS apparatus at Cp 1, 1 ᵇ; confirmed in Swete and Rahlfs.
  • Most likely original: MT. The Greek most plausibly trims a redundancy at first mention. Moderate confidence.
  • Preaching implication: none. The “two” count at Tool 10 is a count in the Hebrew and the report says so.

1:2 — the clause naming Naomi

  • The issue: Swete omits καὶ ὄνομα τῇ γυναικὶ αὐτοῦ Νωεμείν, so that Vaticanus names the man and the two sons but not the wife [T]. Rahlfs has the clause [T]. BHS records nothing at 1:2 [T].
  • Manuscript evidence: Swete’s text (Vaticanus-based) against Rahlfs 1979. The corpus holds Swete as its observation layer; the Rahlfs export is the citation of record. No wider collation is available here.
  • Most likely original: the longer reading. Three successive clauses opening καὶ ὄνομα make haplography by homoioteleuton the obvious explanation of the omission, and nothing suggests a shorter Hebrew. Moderate–high confidence on the diagnosis; high that the omission stands in Swete.
  • Preaching implication: none, and it should not be mentioned.
  • Method implication: a second instance of the class the claim audit flagged at 3:11 (R22) — a Greek reading BHS does not carry. It was visible only because this run opened Swete, which the overview’s colophon records as not opened. Logged for the overview’s Finalise pass.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period (narrated): the judges’ era, as v.1 states [T] — a tribal confederacy with no central monarchy, local and charismatic leadership, and recurrent pressure from neighbouring peoples [S].
  • Time period (composition): not stated by the book [T]. A monarchic date and a post-exilic date are both argued and neither is resolved here [S: overview]. The narrator’s explanation of an obsolete custom at 4:7 shows a distance from the events [T], and the book’s close on David’s name shows it reached its present form after David was known [I].
  • Location: Bethlehem in Judah, about five miles south of Jerusalem, in the Judahite hill country — rain-fed agriculture, barley and wheat, vulnerable to a failed season [S]. Moab lies east across the Dead Sea on the plateau above the Arnon; the “fields of Moab” are its arable tableland [S]. The journey is some fifty miles and involves descending to the rift and climbing out of it [S]. Moderate confidence on the distances; the geography is not in dispute.
  • Political context: no king in Israel [T: Judges' refrain]; Moab an independent neighbour with a long record of hostility in the canon’s own telling [S].

What the original audience knew without being told

Six items, each bearing on how these five verses read.

  1. What a famine meant in a rain-fed hill economy. Not scarcity but a failed harvest and no stored surplus, with no option but to move toward water or toward someone else’s grain [S]. The decision at 1:1 is not imprudence; it is the standard response, which is why the canon has a script for it (Tool 11).
  2. What sojourning cost. A גֵּר (“resident alien”) had no land-right, no clan protection and no standing in the local assembly; he lived on the goodwill of a host community [S]. Elimelech does not emigrate to prosperity; he accepts dependency. Moderate–high confidence.
  3. What Moab was. Not merely a foreign country but the canon’s standing example of a near neighbour with a hostile record and a disreputable origin [S]. The book never explains it, and the narrator’s reticence assumes a reader who needs no explanation [I] — which is itself evidence about the audience.
  4. What a widow without sons faced. Not grief alone but the loss of economic standing, legal representation and any claim on land. A widow with sons has a future through them; a widow without has neither income nor protection [S]. This is the content of וַתִּשָּׁאֵר הָאִשָּׁה at 1:5, and the original hearer supplied it instantly.
  5. What the loss of a name meant. A household’s name attached to its land and was carried by its sons; a line extinguished lost both [S]. The five occurrences of שֵׁם in vv.2 and 4 and the business of raising a name in chapter 4 belong to the same institution [T] for the words, [S] for the institution.
  6. That ten years without children was a verdict in itself. The narrator’s “about ten years” with no mention of offspring is, for the original hearer, the statement that the line had already failed before the men died [I]. Moderate confidence, and worth noting because a modern reader reads the ten years as duration and the ancient reader read it as barrenness.

What this changes about how we read

  • The unit is heavier than its length. Items 4, 5 and 6 mean that by the end of v.5 the original audience has heard not a sad story but a terminal one: no man, no son, no heir, no claim, and a decade of evidence that there will be none. The sermon’s weight should match that, not the five verses’ word-count.
  • The flight to Moab is not the scandal a modern sermon makes of it. Items 1 and 2 are what the first hearers brought, and they make the departure ordinary and costly rather than faithless. The scandal the text does flag — by reporting it in a marked idiom — is at 1:4, not 1:1 [T], and even there it flags without judging.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: Ketuvim. The reader of the Writings arrives having read the Torah and the Prophets through to Malachi — post-exilic, knowing David, knowing how the monarchy ended, living with promises not resolved [S].
  • What the canonical reader knows: the patriarchal famine-migrations and their mixed outcomes, including the divine restriction at Gen 26:2–3 [T]; the judges’ era and its refrain; Moab’s record; the gleaning and redemption laws; and the end of the story — that David came of this household [S]. That last item is decisive for this unit: the canonical reader cannot read 1:1–5 as open-ended, and the book counts on it.
  • Canonical register: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah and Chronicles are the neighbours [S]. Two bear on this unit. Lamentations supplies the register of a bereaved woman counting what is left; Ezra–Nehemiah supplies the only canonical company for the idiom at 1:4 [T]. The adjacency to Proverbs supplies nothing — the sequence is centuries later than the book [S: Logos L1].

The first hearers

  • Who they were: Israelites who knew Torah, the land, the agricultural year and the institutions of widowhood and redemption. The narrative-world audience is Bethlehem; the compositional audience is contested; the canonical audience is the reader of the Writings [S: overview], and interpretation defaults to the last.
  • Their situation: living with promises partly kept. For a post-exilic reader, a household driven out of the land by famine and emptied in a foreign country is not a remote story.
  • What they brought: the six items above; a script for famine-flight with no settled ruling attached; and an instinct to ask of any such story whose fault, which the narrator refuses to satisfy.

Where the passage fits their world

Three things they would have heard that we do not naturally pick up:

  1. The terminal arithmetic of v.5 (items 4–6 above). Where we hear bereavement, they heard the end of a house.
  2. The marked verb at 1:4. A hearer whose ear knew the idiom would notice that this is not how the Bible usually says “married” [I]. Moderate confidence — the distribution is certain, what an ancient ear made of it is not.
  3. The absence of God’s name. Hebrew narrative of this kind normally has YHWH in it somewhere within a few verses. Five verses of famine, migration and three deaths with no divine name is an audible silence to someone used to the convention [I]. Moderate confidence.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: the Moabite marriages, reported without comment [T]. Whatever the law’s bearing, the first hearer expected the narrator to say something, and he does not.
  • Surprising: that the man’s name is “my God is king” and the era is the one with no king [T]; and that the woman named “pleasant” is the one left with nothing.
  • Comforting: nothing in this unit. That is not an oversight; the comfort is withheld until 1:6, and the unit’s job is to make the withholding felt.
  • Disturbing: the possibility — never confirmed, never denied — that the three deaths are a verdict [T]. The hearer who assumes they are has to live with the narrator’s refusal to say so; the hearer who assumes they are not has the same problem.
  • Familiar: the famine-flight script, and the household register. The form of vv.1–2 is the form of a dozen introductions in the canon.

What we bring that they didn’t

  • Therapeutic expectation. We expect a narrator to help us feel the loss. This one declines, and we read the decline as coldness rather than as discipline.
  • Individualism. We read “left without her two sons and her husband” as private grief. They heard an economic and legal collapse of a household.
  • A modern map. We hear “Moab” as a place name; they heard a people with a history.
  • Impatience with unexplained suffering. Our theodicies are quicker than theirs, and the pull to resolve vv.3 and 5 is ours more than theirs.

Implication for the sermon

Recovering the terminal arithmetic (items 4–6) and the audible silence (item 3) changes the sermon’s centre of gravity. The emphasis falls not on Elimelech’s decision — which the text does not judge and which the original hearers would have found unremarkable — but on what it is to be left, counted exactly, with God unmentioned. That is the passage’s own emphasis, and it is the one a modern sermon most often replaces.

Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, selected by textual signal and purpose connection; a third is named and declined.

(1) Land, famine and the loss of inheritance

  • Where this passage sits: at the point where a Judahite household leaves the land of promise because the land will not feed it, and loses in a foreign country the sons through whom its portion of that land would have passed [T].
  • What comes before: the land promised to Abraham and his seed (Gen 12:7; 26:3, where the promise is attached to the very command to sojourn in this land) [T]; the conquest and the apportioning of inheritance; the covenant terms in which famine stands among the consequences of unfaithfulness [S]. The canon has supplied both a famine-flight script and, at Gen 26:2–3, a divine restriction on it (Tool 11).
  • What comes after: Naomi’s field, sold and standing unredeemed until 4:3–9 [T]; the redemption vocabulary of Lev 25 that the book uses and never cites [S: overview]; the exile, in which the loss of land becomes the nation’s own story; and the reframing of the inheritance toward a heavenly country (Heb 11:16) [S].
  • What this contributes: the unit’s losses are not only domestic. A household outside the land with no sons has lost its stake in the promise, and the book’s resolution is a land transaction as much as a marriage (4:3–10) [T]. Preaching the unit as bereavement alone misses half of what is gone. Confidence: high that the theme is textually signalled (famine, land, sojourn, field, and the field’s redemption in ch. 4); moderate on the covenant-curse frame, which the text never invokes.

(2) Seed, offspring, and the preservation of a line

  • Where this passage sits: at a near-extinction. Two sons, ten years, no children, then both dead [T]. The line that will produce David is reduced to a widow and a Moabitess.
  • What comes before: the promised seed of Gen 3:15 and the long chain of threatened lines — Sarah’s barrenness, Rebekah’s, Tamar’s levir crisis in Gen 38, which Ruth 4:12 names explicitly [T].
  • What comes after: the genealogy at 4:18–22, which runs Perez to David and uses the Pentateuch’s own וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת heading [S: sweep, L8]; the Davidic covenant; and Matthew’s genealogy, which names Perez, Boaz, Obed and Jesse, and Ruth among its four women [S].
  • What this contributes: it tells the reader what kind of jeopardy this is. The deaths at 1:3 and 1:5 are not only a woman’s grief; they are a threat to the line, and the reader of the Writings knows which line [I]. Confidence: high, on the strength of the בֵּן/יֶלֶד chains running from this unit to 4:13–17 and of 4:12’s explicit naming of Gen 38.

How the two relate

(c) Climactic convergence. They do not run in parallel and neither merely illuminates the other: they meet in a single transaction. At the gate, Boaz acquires the field and the widow in one act, and the stated purpose is לְהָקִים שֵׁם־הַמֵּת עַל־נַחֲלָתוֹ (“to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance”, 4:5, 4:10) — name, seed and land in one clause [T]. This unit opens both accounts at once: it removes the sons and strands the field. That is why the two themes are selected and the rest are not.

Declined

Exodus / new exodus. The sweep surfaced פָּקַד (“to visit”) at 1:6 as exodus vocabulary and the claim audit has since withdrawn the novelty claim: the vocabulary is standard, and no commentator argues that Ruth deliberately evokes the exodus [S: audit, L7]. The clause lies outside this unit in any case. Not entered.

Christological Reading

Type of Christological connection

Contrast (need answered) and trajectory. Not typology, and not Christophany. The four typology tests are not met because there is no figure, institution or event in this unit to bear a type: the redeemer has not appeared, no priesthood or kingship is in view, and the unit’s content is loss. The tests are therefore not scored; the honest category is contrast, with the trajectory running through the book’s close rather than through these verses.

How the passage points to Christ

  • By stating a need it cannot meet. A widow outside the land with no sons, no heir, no claim and no explanation is a need, and the unit supplies nothing toward it — no deliverer, no promise, no divine word [T]. The whole of the book’s answer lies ahead, and beyond the book the greater Redeemer who takes on the debt of those who have no claim [I].
  • By placing the need in the line. The household that nearly ends here is the household of David (4:17, 22) [T], and so of the Christ whom Matthew’s genealogy traces through it [S]. The unit is the narrowest point of a line the canon has been following since Gen 3:15 — and the narrowing is the point: the line is preserved not by its own strength but by a providence the text does not even name [I].
  • By the shape of the reversal it sets up. What Naomi loses is counted precisely (two children, one husband, a name, a field’s heirs). What she receives at 4:16 is one child, in her lap, with the same word she lost them by [T]. The pattern — exact loss answered by a given child — is the pattern the gospel completes, and the lexical chain is in the text rather than imposed on it.

Trajectory

  • What the passage anticipates or longs for: a redeemer with a claim, and a word from God. It receives neither in these five verses.
  • What Christ fulfils: the redeemer category the book opens at 2:20 and closes at 4:14, taken up at cost by one who had no obligation [I]; and the silence broken — the God who is unmentioned in Ruth 1:1–5 has, in these last days, spoken [S: Heb 1:1–2].
  • What still remains: the unit’s own experience. Christians are still left, still uninformed, still counting. The already/not-yet here is pastoral rather than cosmic: the explanation withheld from Naomi is still largely withheld from us, and what has changed is not that we are told but that we have been given a name to hold [I].

Moralism check

  • The “be like X” temptation: there is no X. Which is precisely why this unit tempts to the negative form — “don’t be like Elimelech” — and that is the moralism to guard against here. It requires a verdict the narrator withholds (Tools 6, 13) and converts a passage about being left into a passage about making better decisions.
  • The gospel grounding: the kindness in this book is God’s before it is anyone’s — he is the one who gives bread (1:6, on Naomi’s report) and conception (4:13, in the narrator’s own voice) [T]. The unit’s silence is not God’s absence from it; the book proves that, and the cross settles it.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the hero of this unit is nobody. That is the correct answer and it should be preached as such: the passage has no agent except death, and the only actor who could change it has not yet been named. A sermon that supplies a human hero here has supplied something the text pointedly lacks.

Confidence: high that the unit functions as need-without-answer and that the trajectory runs through 4:13–17 to David; moderate on any reading that makes the unit itself Christologically freighted. It is not. It is the hole the gospel fills, and that is enough.

Difficult / Contested Verses

Three live issues, and a fourth named and dismissed.

1:1 — Was leaving Bethlehem for Moab a sin?

  • Category: ethical / apologetic; and the unit’s principal interpretive crux.
  • The difficulty: a Judahite leaves the land of promise for a foreign country under covenant pressure, and the whole family dies there. Jewish and Christian interpreters have read the deaths as consequence; the text says nothing.
  • Common mishandlings: treating the deaths as the narrator’s verdict on the departure (they are narrated without comment, Tool 6); or the opposite, insisting the move was blameless in order to protect Naomi’s later lament from an easy answer.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the clause is a trigger, not an evaluation. It sets the household moving so the narrative can begin, in the canon’s standard idiom for doing so (Tool 11).
  • The main interpretive options: (a) culpable flight from the land — the deaths as consequence; (b) ordinary and legitimate famine response — the deaths as the book’s unexplained affliction; (c) deliberately undecided — the narrator withholding the verdict as part of his method.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the lexical evidence now constrains the options and does not close them. The clause Ruth 1:1 reuses stands in three verses, and the second (Gen 26:1) is immediately followed by a divine instruction to sojourn inside the land [T] — which tells against (b) being obviously right. But the wider seven-verse famine-and-sojourn set contains 2 Kgs 8:1, where a prophet commands exactly this move [T] — which tells against (a) being obviously right. And the narrator who elsewhere supplies verdicts supplies none here [T] — which tells for (c). Moderate–high confidence that (c) is the text’s own posture; the material for (a) and (b) is real and should be shown rather than suppressed.
  • Suggested handling: address it briefly and early, name the two readings, and say that the text declines to choose — then say why that matters, since the book’s whole method is to withhold the verdict and let the story answer. Preach the silence; do not fill it, and do not pretend it is not there.

1:4 — The Moabite marriages

  • Category: ethical / doctrinal; and a pastoral landmine in any congregation with intermarriage in it.
  • The difficulty: two Israelite men marry Moabite women and the narrator passes no comment; Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] excludes the Moabite from the assembly; and this book’s heroine is one of the two.
  • Common mishandlings: importing Deut 23:3 as though the book cited it (it does not, and Ruth lacks the law’s “assembly” term entirely [T]); reading the deaths of vv.5 as punishment for the marriages; or running the passage into a modern intermarriage debate the book never opens.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the verse supplies the two women the story needs, and it does so in a marked idiom (Tool 7a). It is an introduction, not an indictment.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the marked verb is real and measured — six verses against 116, with the only comparable narrative instances at Judg 21:23 and Ezra 10:44, both irregular marriages [T]. So the text does appear to mark the marriages as irregular, and it conspicuously does not condemn them. Both halves must be preached. The verse that does the marking is also the verse that introduces the woman whom Boaz will call an אֵשֶׁת חַיִל and the elders will bless beside Rachel and Leah [T]. Moderate confidence on the marking; high that no condemnation is present.
  • Suggested handling: name the irregularity, name the silence, and let the book’s own trajectory answer it — the Moabitess marked here is blessed at 4:11 and named in David’s line at 4:17. Do not open the modern debate from this verse.

1:3, 1:5 — Three deaths with no explanation

  • Category: pastoral; and the place where a sermon on this unit will land hardest.
  • The difficulty: a congregation contains people who have had losses they were given no reason for, and this unit is a list of three.
  • Common mishandlings: supplying the reason (either “sin” or “sovereignty, and here is how it was for the best”); or moving on quickly to 1:6 because the unit is “introductory”.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the narrator’s restraint is the finding, and it is measured against his willingness to speak elsewhere (Tool 6) [T]. The text models the refusal to explain another person’s bereavement.
  • Suggested handling: this is the unit’s pastoral centre and should be given time rather than hurried. The honest comfort available is the reader’s, not Naomi’s — we know the end of the book — and the sermon should be clear that this is what it is offering.

Named and dismissed

The deaths as covenant curse. A sermon can be built on reading the famine and the deaths through Deuteronomy’s covenant sanctions. The book never invokes them, uses none of their vocabulary in this unit, and the narrator passes no verdict [T]. The reading is available as canonical reflection and should be labelled as such if used at all; it is not this text’s claim.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: making Elimelech the villain

  • What it looks like: “Elimelech took his family out of the place of blessing, and look what happened. Don’t leave Bethlehem when the bread runs out.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it requires a verdict the narrator withholds, and he withholds it pointedly — the same narrator reports Boaz’s inner state (3:7), explains a dead custom (4:7) and names a divine act (4:13) [T]. The canon’s own famine-flight script is also no help to the sermon: it is commanded by a prophet at 2 Kgs 8:1 and left unjudged at Gen 12:10 [T]. The original hearers would have found the move ordinary and costly (Historical Background).
  • The corrective: name the question, show both readings, and say that the text declines to choose. Then preach what the text does say: a household was emptied, and nobody in the passage says why. The silence is the author’s method and the sermon should reproduce it rather than resolve it.

Pitfall: skipping the unit, or summarising it in a sentence

  • What it looks like: “Chapter 1 opens with the sad backstory — a famine, a move to Moab, three deaths — and then in verse 6 Naomi decides to go home.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it discards the book’s balance sheet. The five occurrences of שֵׁם in vv.2 and 4 are the only five in the chapter and the direct deposit against 4:5 and 4:10 [T]; the יֶלֶד at 1:5 is one half of a two-verse chain whose other half is the child in Naomi’s lap [T]; and 1:21’s “I went out full” is unintelligible without 1:1–5, which describes a famine rather than fullness. A congregation taken past this unit at speed cannot hear chapter 4.
  • The corrective: preach it as a unit in its own right, and make the counting explicit. What is lost here is what is restored there, in the same words.

Pitfall: preaching the ESV’s “sons” and losing the chain

  • What it looks like: the sermon on 4:16 says “and Naomi received a child” with no reference back, because the pulpit text at 1:5 said “sons”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the Hebrew changes nouns at 1:5 — יְלָדֶיהָ, not בָּנֶיהָ — and uses the same noun at 4:16 and nowhere else in the book [T]. The NASB95 keeps it; the ESV and NIV84 level it [T].
  • The corrective: supply the link in your own words from the pulpit: the word for what she lost in verse 5 is the word for what she is given at the end. And know that the NIV84 goes further, replacing “the woman” at 1:5 with “Naomi” [T] — so if that is the pulpit text, the anonymity point must be supplied too.

Pitfall: importing the Moabite law, or the modern intermarriage debate

  • What it looks like: a sermon that reads 1:4 through Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] and treats the sons’ deaths as its enforcement; or one that uses the verse to adjudicate marrying outside the faith.
  • Why it’s wrong: the book never cites the law, and Ruth lacks its defining term “assembly” altogether [T]. And the verse that marks the marriages as irregular is the verse that introduces the woman the elders bless beside Rachel and Leah (4:11) and whose son is David’s grandfather (4:17) [T].
  • The corrective: report what the text does — mark the irregularity by its verb, and pass no sentence. Then let the book answer it, which it does, at the gate and in the genealogy.

Pitfall: supplying the pathos the narrator withholds

  • What it looks like: an opening that imagines Naomi’s grief at the graveside, the dust of Moab, the long walk home — warmth and colour poured into five verses that contain none.
  • Why it’s wrong: the unit has no sensory detail, no dialogue and no tears, while chapter 1 from v.9 has all three [T]. The flatness is a device (Tone and Feel); filling it in spends, in the first three minutes, the emotional credit the text saves for 1:20–21 and 4:16.
  • The corrective: read it plainly and let it be cold. A congregation that feels the chill of these verses will feel “call me Mara” when it comes.

Pitfall: preaching a pattern that has not been baselined

  • What it looks like: “ten years, ten elders, ten names” as the book’s numerical design; or the brothers’ names made load-bearing (“Sickness and Consumption — the narrator is telling us these men were doomed”).
  • Why it’s wrong: the claim audit baselined the numerals and withdrew the design reading — two is this book’s number, nine occurrences against ten’s two, and five of those nine are in this unit [S: audit, R1; T for the figures]. And the narrator who glosses Ichabod’s name (1 Sam 4:21) glosses none of these; the brothers’ etymologies are uncertain (Tool 7g).
  • The corrective: preach Naomi’s name, which 1:20 glosses in the text, and Elimelech’s, which v.1 supplies the foil for. Leave the rest as what they are: suggestive and unverified.
