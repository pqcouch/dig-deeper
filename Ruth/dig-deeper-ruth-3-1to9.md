## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools converging, each *high confidence* on its lexical base.

1. **The passage keeps the forbidden idiom and the covenant idiom lexically apart, and the separation is deliberate enough to preach.** `[T]` for the distribution; `[I]` for the design. To *uncover* a כָּנָף ("skirt, wing") is the Deuteronomic incest formula — גָּלָה with כָּנָף stands in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1] and 27:20, both "he shall not uncover his father's skirt." Ruth uncovers (גִּלִּית, 3:4; וַתְּגַל, 3:7) the **מַרְגְּלֹת**, the place of the feet; and she asks Boaz to **spread** (פָּרַשׂ) his כָּנָף. The narrator never lets the forbidden verb and the loaded noun meet. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion, Original Languages, Difficult Verses.
2. **3:9 hands Boaz his own blessing back as a claim.** `[T]` כָּנָף occurs twice in the book — 2:12, where Boaz prays that Ruth has come to take refuge under YHWH's wings, and 3:9, where Ruth asks Boaz to spread his wing over her. And "spread a wing over" (פָּרַשׂ + כָּנָף + עַל) stands in nine verses of the Hebrew Bible, of which five are cherubim over the ark and two are eagles; **Ezekiel 16:8 is the only other place where it is a covenant act toward a person.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Quotation/Allusion (full Triad below), the internal-echo check, Translations.
3. **One root does double duty: it names Boaz as kinsman, then carries the chapter's concealment.** `[T]` for the lexical facts; the frequency claim is withdrawn (*v1.1 — claim audit, R24*). Boaz is introduced at 2:1 by a word from יָדַע: the WLC prints the ketiv מידע unpointed there, and BHS at 2:1 records qere מוֹדַע against ketiv מְיֻדָּע. He is then called מֹדַעְתָּנוּ ("our kinsman", 3:2) — a **hapax legomenon in the Hebrew Bible**, whose masculine counterpart מוֹדַע stands elsewhere only at Prov 7:4. The same root then runs through chapter 3 in the sense of knowing and not being known: "do not make yourself **known**" (3:3), "you shall **know** the place" (3:4), "all the gate of my people **know**" (3:11), "before one could **recognise** another" (3:14), "until you **know** how the matter falls" (3:18). **What the audit withdrew:** calling יָדַע "the chapter's leading root" because five of its eight occurrences fall here. Nineteen of Ruth's eighty-six recurring lemmas are at least as chapter-bound, several far more so, so the concentration carries no information. Two unique or near-unique kinship nouns from the root that the chapter then uses for concealment — that is the finding. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Original Languages.
4. **יָטַב binds Naomi's purpose to Boaz's heart and then to Boaz's verdict, and every English version breaks it.** `[T]` Three occurrences in the book, all in this chapter: "that it may be **well** with you" (3:1), "his heart was **merry**" (3:7), "you have made your last kindness **better**" (3:10). Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Structure, Translations.
5. **The night Naomi dismissed as impossible in 1:12 is the night of this chapter.** `[T]` לַיְלָה ("night") occurs four times in the book: 1:12, where Naomi says that even if she had a husband *tonight* and bore sons it would be no use; then 3:2, 3:8 and 3:13. Surfaced by: the internal-echo check, Repetition, Context.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Two things have been established and one has been exhausted. Established: the category "redeemer" (2:20, the first of twenty-one occurrences of גָּאַל in the book, none earlier) `[T]`, and Boaz's disposition, demonstrated in three extra-legal orders in the field (2:9, 15, 16) `[T]`. Exhausted: the gleaning provision. 2:23 closes "until the end of the barley harvest and the wheat harvest" `[T]` — the grain is finished, and the law of Leviticus 19:9 has given these women everything it has to give.

**Necessity answer.** This passage exists here because **the lawful provision has expired and nothing in the law obliges anyone to do more.** Gleaning is a right; what Ruth asks for at 3:9 is not. The sequence is therefore not optional: had the request come while the harvest was running, it would have been a shortcut round a working provision; coming after 2:23, it is the only move left. Position also explains why Naomi, silent through most of chapter 2, drives this scene: she is the one who named the redeemer category at 2:20, and she is the one who now uses it `[I]`.

**Implication for this passage.** Read in position, 3:1–9 is not a romantic initiative but a legal approach made at the only moment and in the only manner available to two women with no standing. That reframes the night: the secrecy (3:3, 3:14) is not the furtiveness of an affair but the discretion of a petition that would ruin both parties if it were misread `[I]`, *moderate–high confidence*. And it explains the ground-clause at the end of Ruth's speech — כִּי גֹאֵל אָתָּה, "for you are a redeemer" (3:9) — which is the only reason she offers.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 4 confirms and sharpens this: the request at 3:9 is verbally the return of Boaz's own blessing from 2:12, so the positional logic is also a verbal logic. Ruth is not inventing a claim; she is quoting the man who told her where refuge was to be found and asking him to be the place.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** To show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and so to give Israel David as the fruit `[I]`. The overview's microscript, confirmed independently across the sweep.

**Passage-level.** This unit's contribution is to put the book's central request into a human mouth at the point of maximum risk. Everything to this point has been given to Ruth and Naomi — bread, grain, protection. Here, for the only time in the book, the initiative is theirs, and the author's purpose in the unit is to show that the initiative is *lawful in substance and dangerous in form* `[I]`. The narrator takes unusual care over it: he supplies Boaz's inner state (3:7), he adopts Boaz's point of view at the moment of discovery (3:8), and he has Boaz pass an explicit verdict of kindness over the night in the very next verse (3:10) `[T]`. An author indifferent to how the scene would be read would not have done any of those three things.

The purpose is served, in other words, by a narrative strategy of *controlled ambiguity*: the scene is told so that the reader feels the risk and is given, at every point where it matters, the evidence to resolve it rightly `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above — recorded there in full, as the phase table directs.*

**Immediately before (2:17–23).** Ruth's ephah of barley, Naomi's discovery of whose field it was, her blessing of YHWH "who has not withdrawn his kindness to the living and to the dead", her naming of Boaz as "one of our redeemers", and the closing notice that Ruth stayed with Boaz's young women to the end of both harvests and "lived with her mother-in-law" `[T]`. Three things carry forward: the redeemer category, Naomi's restored speech about YHWH, and the end of the grain.

**Immediately after (3:10–18).** Boaz's blessing and his verdict that this is Ruth's *last kindness*, greater than the first; his promise; the obstacle of a nearer redeemer; the oath; the six measures; and Naomi's "wait" `[T]`. The unit's boundary at v.9 is therefore a seam in the dialogue, not in the scene.

**On the boundary.** The overview's division at 3:9/3:10 is defensible — v.9 ends Ruth's speech and v.10 begins Boaz's, and the preacher gains a unit that ends on the request rather than on its answer, which is homiletically strong. But it should be chosen knowingly: **the tighter literary unit is 3:1–13**, request and reply, since Boaz's "I will do for you all that you ask" (3:11) answers Naomi's "he will tell you what you shall do" (3:4) across the seam, and the verdict of 3:10 is the narrator's own man resolving the ambiguity the scene has created `[T]`. A sermon on 3:1–9 that does not at least quote 3:10 leaves the congregation holding the risk with no verdict on it. *Recommendation:* preach 3:1–9 and read through v.13.

**Wider section.** Chapter 3 is the third of the book's seven movements in the overview's arc, and the only one set at night `[T]`.

**Is the passage read out of context?** Routinely — as romance, and as a template for female initiative or for sexual restraint. The immediate context corrects both: the scene is bracketed by a mother-in-law's plan (3:1) and a kinsman's legal verdict (3:10), and the request's stated ground is a legal status (3:9).

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 3:1–2 | Naomi's proposal | Names the goal (מָנוֹחַ, "rest"), the man, the occasion and the night |
| 3:3–4 | Naomi's five instructions | Wash, anoint, dress, go down, uncover-and-lie — then "he will tell you" |
| 3:5–6 | Ruth's compliance | One line of assent, one line of execution; the narrator compresses |
| 3:7 | Boaz, and Ruth's approach | Boaz eats, drinks, is content, lies down; Ruth comes secretly, uncovers, lies down |
| 3:8 | The discovery | Midnight; the man startles; the viewpoint shifts to his |
| 3:9 | The exchange | "Who are you?" — and a self-identification that becomes a request with a legal ground |

**Device: instruction and execution, with one gap.** Naomi gives five imperatives in vv.3–4 and Ruth performs them in vv.6–7, in order and almost in the same words — the narrator even repeats וַתְּגַל מַרְגְּלֹתָיו ("she uncovered the place of his feet") from וְגִלִּית מַרְגְּלֹתָיו `[T]`. The structural point is what Naomi did *not* instruct. Naomi's plan ended "and **he** will tell you what you shall do" (3:4). At 3:9, Ruth speaks first `[T]`.

That gap is the structure's whole argument, and it should be stated carefully. The text does not say Ruth disobeyed, and it does not say why she spoke. It says she was asked a question and answered it with more than the question required `[T]`. The reading that she went beyond her instructions is `[I]` at *moderate–high* confidence — the departure is visible in the text; the motive is not, and the text supplies none.

A second device was proposed here and has been withdrawn (*v1.1 — claim audit, R25*): **descent and ascent**. Ruth "goes down" (יָרַד) to the threshing floor at 3:3 and 3:6, the only two occurrences of that verb in the book, and at 4:1 Boaz "goes up" (עָלָה) to the gate, the only occurrence of that one `[T]`. The distribution is right; the inversion is not a finding. Two occurrences beside one occurrence is the modal shape of a lemma here — 194 of Ruth's 308 distinct lemmas occur once or twice, 63% of its vocabulary — so the pattern has no statistical content; עָלָה with שַׁעַר ("gate") stands in seventeen verses of the Hebrew Bible, an ordinary idiom; and the book's two great journeys, to Moab and back, use neither verb. Both are doing plain topography: the floor is outside and below the town, the gate is in it. *(v1.2 — Logos round 1, L5: a correction to the audit.)* The audit added that the consonantal text does not deliver a descending Ruth at all, because the ketiv at 3:3 is first person (BHS: qere וְיָרַדְתְּ, ketiv ‑תִּי). **That inference does not survive the grammars.** The ‑תִּי is an archaic second-feminine-singular perfect, not a first person: Brotzman notes the direction of change runs from the archaic ketiv to the normalised qere (*Old Testament Textual Criticism*, 150) `[S]`, and Driver's parallels are Micah 4:13 and frequent forms in Jeremiah and Ezekiel (via Wilch, Concordia Commentary, 19) `[S]`. **No commentator surveyed prefers reading it as first person.** So the descent is Ruth's on either reading, and nothing here needs the qere argued first. What survives is a rarity and no more: יָרַד with גֹּרֶן ("threshing floor") in one verse stands in Ruth 3:3 and 3:6 only, in the whole Hebrew Bible.

### 4. Linking Words

Hebrew narrative carries its logic in verb forms and a few particles rather than in a chain of connectors, so this tool is short but not empty.

- **וְעַתָּה ("and now", 3:2)** — the pivot from the goal to the plan. Naomi states the end (rest) and then, with "and now", the means. The same particle opens Boaz's two clauses in the next unit (3:11, 3:12), the second introducing the obstacle `[T]`.
- **הֲלֹא ("is it not…?", 3:1, 3:2)** — twice, in successive verses, each a rhetorical question expecting assent: "shall I not seek rest for you?", "is not Boaz our relative?" Naomi builds her plan on two propositions Ruth cannot refuse `[T]`. Both English versions keep them.
- **אֲשֶׁר ("that, which")** — the subordinating workhorse: the rest "that it may be well with you" (3:1), the man "with whose maids you were" (3:2), the place "where he lies" (3:4), "all that you say" (3:5), "all that her mother-in-law commanded" (3:6).
- **The waw-consecutive chain of vv.6–8** — וַתֵּרֶד … וַתַּעַשׂ … וַיֹּאכַל … וַיָּבֹא … וַתָּבֹא … וַתְּגַל … וַתִּשְׁכָּב … וַיֶּחֱרַד … וַיִּלָּפֵת: fourteen sequential verbs across three verses `[T]`. The narrative accelerates precisely where a modern narrator would slow down, and that pace is itself a decision (see Tool 9).
- **וְהִנֵּה ("and behold", 3:8)** — a disjunction breaking the sequence. It is not a logical connector but a *perception* marker, and it is the hinge of the unit: the reader is put inside Boaz's eyes (see Tool 6).
- **כִּי ("for, because", 3:9)** — the load-bearing connector of the passage. Ruth's request is grounded, and the ground is not need, kindness or affection but status: *for you are a redeemer* `[T]`. ←*for*← reversed into a therefore: "You are a redeemer; therefore spread your wing over your maidservant." That is the clause a sermon should be built on.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as Hebrew poetic parallelism** — this is prose narrative with no bicola.

The tool is not wholly idle, though, because the unit's prose is built on **correspondence rather than parallelism**: Naomi's five instructions (3:3–4) and Ruth's execution (3:6–7) stand in matched sequence, with one clause repeated word for word (uncovering the place of his feet) `[T]`. Where Hebrew poetry would use a second colon to interpret the first, this narrative uses an execution to interpret an instruction — and the single divergence between them (Ruth's speech at 3:9) carries the weight a broken parallelism would carry in a psalm. Noted here and worked under Structure.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Three authorial intrusions, all in three verses, and they are the interpretive key to the scene.

**(a) 3:7 — "and his heart was merry" (וַיִּיטַב לִבּוֹ).** Inside-character information the reader could not otherwise have `[T]`. This is the same narrator who tells us nothing about Ruth's motives, Naomi's calculation, or Boaz's feelings anywhere else in the book. He tells us this because the reader needs to know Boaz's state before the approach — and the phrase is a settled contentment after food and drink, not intoxication; the text says nothing about wine beyond "drank" `[T]`. A sermon that makes Boaz drunk has added a word. *High confidence.*

**(b) 3:8 — "and behold, a woman lying at the place of his feet."** The narrator switches to Boaz's viewpoint. הִנֵּה ("behold") after a verb of waking is a perception marker: we are told not what was the case but what *he* found `[T]`. The effect is that the reader discovers Ruth at the moment Boaz does, and therefore shares his alarm rather than observing it `[I]`, *moderate–high*. It also means that the one description of Ruth's position in the whole scene is given as Boaz's perception, not as the narrator's independent report — which is worth knowing before building anything on the posture.

**(c) 3:6 — "and did according to all that her mother-in-law had commanded her."** An editorial summary standing in for the action `[T]`. It is a verdict of obedience, placed *before* the night rather than after it, and it is the narrator's own. Compare 2:23's closing note that she lived with her mother-in-law: this narrator uses short compliance-notices to pass judgement without comment `[I]`, *moderate*.

**The cumulative point, restated (*v1.1 — claim audit, R28*).** Three of the four are the narrator's: Boaz's contentment before the approach (3:7), Ruth's obedience before the uncovering (3:6), and Boaz's perspective at the discovery (3:8). **The fourth is not.** Ruth 3:10 opens וַיֹּאמֶר ("and he said"), so the verdict of חֶסֶד is Boaz's own direct speech, not a narratorial aside, and it must be preached as his word.

**The claim that these interventions suspend the book's reticence is withdrawn.** Tested across the whole book, the distribution is flat: counting every narratorial aside outside direct speech gives four in chapter 1, five in chapter 2, four in chapter 3 and four in chapter 4 — about one device every five verses throughout — and the narratorial הִנֵּה ("behold") falls exactly once in each of chapters 2, 3 and 4. The book's two heaviest interventions are elsewhere: a whole verse of antiquarian explanation of the sandal custom at 4:7, and "YHWH gave her conception" at 4:13, the only narratorial act of God in the book. Chapter 3 is not unusually glossed, because the book is not reticent.

**What carries the weight instead.** The internal argument against reading the scene as sexually compromised may no longer run through a cluster of interventions. It runs through what the text says, which is firmer ground: the lexical separation of the forbidden idiom from the covenant idiom (Headline 1); Ruth's request at 3:9, which asks for the spreading of a כָּנָף and so speaks the vocabulary of covenant rather than of seduction; Boaz's own verdict at 3:10, now correctly attributed, that this kindness is greater than the first; and 3:14's care that she not be seen, which presupposes nothing to conceal beyond the meeting itself. Three of the four observations above still serve that reading — they simply do not serve it as a designed cluster.

### 7. Vocabulary

Seven words carry this passage. The proper-noun inventory first, as the tool directs: **Naomi, Boaz, Ruth** — and nothing else. No place name, no gentilic, no number. For a book whose other units are thick with Bethlehem, Judah, Moab, Ephrathah, Perez and Tamar, the absence is itself notable: this scene happens in the dark, at an unnamed floor, with three people and no geography `[T]`. No canonical freight to triage; the names' own hyperlinks (unit 1's work) are not activated here.

**מָנוֹחַ ("resting place, security"), 3:1** `[T]`
Naomi's stated goal. The word is a *cognate* of מְנוּחָה at 1:9, where she wished her daughters-in-law would "find rest, each in the house of her husband" — different word, same root `[T]`, and the row must say *cognate*, not identical. The pair is the book's clearest statement that Naomi's plan in chapter 3 is the pursuit of what she had prayed for and abandoned in chapter 1. *High confidence* on the lexical relation; *moderate* that the cognate is deliberate rather than idiomatic.

**מֹדַעְתָּנוּ ("our relative"), 3:2** `[T]`
A noun from the root יָדַע ("to know"), and the lemma occurs once in the book `[T]`. It matters because Boaz was introduced at 2:1 by a word from the same root — the WLC prints the ketiv there unpointed, מידע, parsed as a passive participle of יָדַע, "one known, an acquaintance", with the qere מוֹדַע, "kinsman" (BHS apparatus) `[T]`. So the kinship term that makes the whole plot possible is built from the root that the chapter then uses five times for knowing and not being known. *High confidence* on the morphology and the distribution; the wordplay reading is `[I]` at *moderate* — one root doing duty in two semantic fields is ordinary Hebrew, and the hard rules on sound-based claims require corroboration before more is claimed. The corroboration available is the distribution itself: five uses of the root in one chapter, in the concealment sense, around a kinship term from the same root.

**מַרְגְּלֹת ("the place of the feet"), 3:4, 3:7, 3:8 — and 3:14** `[T]`
Not "feet" (רַגְלַיִם) but a place-noun: the *spot at* the feet. The word stands in **five verses of the Hebrew Bible — four of them in this chapter, and Dan 10:6** `[T]`, verified by lemma. *High confidence.* Two things follow. First, the word is effectively Ruth's, which means the narrator reached for an unusual term four times rather than using the common one. Second, and more important for preaching: a place-noun describes *where Ruth lay*, not *what she uncovered of Boaz*. The euphemistic reading of "feet" — well attested elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, where רֶגֶל can be a euphemism `[S]` — has to travel through a word that denotes a location, and that is a longer journey than the English "his feet" suggests. *Moderate confidence* that the choice of word is itself a distancing move `[I]`; the lexical facts are `[T]` and *high*.

**כָּנָף ("wing, skirt, corner of a garment"), 3:9** `[T]`
The passage's pivot, and the book's: two occurrences, 2:12 and 3:9 `[T]`. The noun covers both a bird's wing and the hem of a garment, so the request is simultaneously a marriage proposal (spread your skirt over me) and a claim on the divine protection Boaz had invoked (be the wings you said I had come to) `[I]`, *moderate–high*. See Tool 11 for the full allusion work and Headline Finding 1 for the lexical separation the text maintains.

**אָמָה ("maidservant"), 3:9 twice** `[T]`
Ruth calls herself this twice in one verse `[T]`. At 2:13 she had called herself שִׁפְחָה, twice in that verse `[T]`. The change of word is real; its direction is not demonstrable from Ruth. Whether אָמָה ranks above or below שִׁפְחָה is a lexicon question and the lexica are not unanimous `[S]`. *Low–moderate confidence* — recommend HALOT or NIDOTTE, and see Open Questions. What *is* `[T]` is that she identifies herself by a servile term in the same breath as making a legal claim, which is the speech's rhetorical shape: no status, and a right `[I]`.

**גֹּאֵל ("redeemer"), 3:9** `[T]`
The ground of the request. One of twenty-one occurrences in the book, none before 2:20, seven in this chapter `[T]`. Ruth does not ask Boaz to be kind; she tells him what he is. See Tool 16.

**בַּלָּט ("secretly, softly"), 3:7, with זָרָה ("winnow"), 3:2; לָפַת ("turn, twist"), 3:8; חָרַד ("tremble, be startled"), 3:8; חֲצִי ("half"), 3:8** `[T]`
Five words, each occurring **once in the book**, all of them in vv.2–8 `[T]`, verified by lemma. *High confidence* on the distribution. The narrator's vocabulary goes unusual exactly where the scene goes delicate — see Tool 9. לָפַת is rare canonically too: three verses, Judg 16:29, Job 6:18, Ruth 3:8 `[T]`, and its sense here is genuinely uncertain (Open Questions).

### 8. Translations

The pulpit text is the ESV, the study text the NASB95, and for this passage **the pulpit text is the more transparent at the point that matters most**. That is unusual and the preacher should know it.

**3:9 — the decisive divergence.** NASB95 "spread your **covering** over your maid, for you are a **close relative**"; ESV "Spread your **wings** over your **servant**, for you are a **redeemer**." The Hebrew כָּנָף is the same word as 2:12's "under whose wings you have come to take refuge" `[T]`. **The ESV congregation can hear Ruth asking Boaz to become the wings Boaz himself invoked; the NASB95 reader hears a blanket and a relative.** Both renderings are defensible as English — the garment sense is almost certainly the primary one in the request — but only one of them lets the book's central echo be heard. *Preach from the ESV here, and say that the word means both.*

**3:1 — "security" against "rest".** NASB95 "shall I not seek **security** for you"; ESV "should I not seek **rest** for you". The Hebrew מָנוֹחַ is cognate with 1:9's מְנוּחָה, which both versions render "rest" `[T]`. The ESV preserves the link, the NASB95 breaks it. The ESV slightly over-levels, since the words are cognate rather than identical; on balance it serves the congregation better, and the preacher should say "rest" at both points while noting the forms differ.

**3:3 — "your best clothes" against "your cloak".** The Hebrew is שִׂמְלֹתַיִךְ, "your garments/mantle" — the ketiv is singular, the qere plural (Textual Variants below) `[T]`. NASB95's "best" is supplied; nothing in the Hebrew says best `[T]`. ESV's "cloak" is closer, and also quietly anticipates 3:15's מִטְפַּחַת (a different word, the cloak that carries the barley). Neither version is wrong; the NASB95 adds an interpretation — that Ruth dressed up — which a sermon should not lean on.

**3:8 — "bent forward" against "turned over".** *(v1.2 — Logos round 1, L4: the lexica settle this.)* The niphal means to twist or bend oneself, to turn oneself back to see (NIDOTTE 2:810; Gesenius 441) `[S]`, so both English renderings are in range and **the ESV's "turned over" is the nearer of the two**; neither congregation is misled. The verb is Boaz's own movement on waking, and it says nothing about contact. The verb is לָפַת, three verses in the canon, and the two renderings are guesses in different directions `[T]`. Flag it; don't build on it.

**3:4 and 3:7 — "his feet".** Both versions have "his feet" for מַרְגְּלֹתָיו, which is a place-noun ("the place of his feet") `[T]`. Both therefore make the object of the uncovering Boaz's body, where the Hebrew makes it a location. Neither congregation will hear the distinction, and it is the distinction on which Headline Finding 1 turns. **This is the one place in the unit where the preacher must correct both versions.**

**3:2 — "kinsman" / "relative".** Both lose the root-link to 2:1 and to the chapter's five uses of "know" `[T]`. Unavoidable in English; worth one sentence in the pulpit.

**Ancient versions check.** The trigger is met at 3:9: the moderns agree that the request concerns a garment, and the rendering carries exegetical weight. **Rahlfs has περιβαλεῖς τὸ πτερύγιόν σου ἐπὶ τὴν δούλην σου** — πτερύγιον is a diminutive of πτέρυξ ("wing") used for the hem or corner of a garment, so **the Greek preserves the same double sense as the Hebrew**, and preserves it with the same word it uses at 2:12 (ὑπὸ τὰς πτέρυγας αὐτοῦ) `[T]`. No split between the ancient and modern traditions on the sense; but the Greek is a witness *against* the NASB95's flattening, since it kept the wing-word where the NASB95 did not. *High confidence.* At 3:7 Rahlfs has ἠγαθύνθη ἡ καρδία αὐτοῦ, matching the Hebrew idiom exactly and preserving the יָטַב root-link with 3:1 (ἀγαθόν) and 3:10 (ἠγάθυνας) — **so the Greek carries the chapter's יָטַב chain that both English versions break** `[T]`. *Moderate–high confidence*; worth checking against a Greek lexicon before it is preached as a point.

**Pulpit divergence note.** Declared pulpit text: ESV. It diverges from the NASB95 at four points in this unit (3:1, 3:3, 3:8, 3:9) and is closer to the Hebrew at three of them. At the fourth — "his feet" — both are equally distant, and that is the one to correct aloud.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Dominant register: held breath.** Not romance, not dread, but suspended risk `[I]`.

What carries it:

- **Pacing.** Fourteen sequential verbs across vv.6–8 `[T]`, with no description, no interiority, and no dialogue until v.9. The narrator accelerates through the approach — the opposite of what a romance would do. Then at v.8 the clock stops: "it happened in the middle of the night", and the verse is given to one man waking.
- **Darkness as the controlling condition.** Night at 3:2, midnight at 3:8, and in the following unit "before one could recognise another" at 3:14 `[T]`. Nothing in the scene is seen; everything is felt or said.
- **A vocabulary that goes strange where the scene goes delicate.** Five words occur once in the whole book, and all five are in vv.2–8 — winnow, secretly, startle, twist, half `[T]`. *High confidence* on the distribution. The narrator is reaching for words he does not otherwise use, in the seven verses where he most needs precision `[I]`, *moderate*.
- **Silence.** Between Ruth's "all that you say I will do" (3:5) and Boaz's "Who are you?" (3:9) nobody speaks `[T]`. Three verses of action in total silence, in the dark.
- **The question itself.** מִי־אָתּ — two words, and the shortest speech in the book `[T]`. It is frightened, not amorous.

**Tonal shift.** The unit turns at 3:8 from narrated action to perceived presence, and the register changes with it: before 3:8 the reader is above the scene watching it unfold; after 3:8 the reader is inside it, with Boaz, not knowing who is there `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

**Soundtrack.** A single low sustained note, and then nothing. A preacher who plays this scene warm has misread it; a preacher who plays it lurid has misread it worse. It is quiet and it is dangerous and it resolves into a legal claim.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

No quotation formula and no marked citation anywhere in Ruth `[T]`, so the work here is echo and idiom. The elevated-prior analysis from Phase 0.5 applies: Genesis, Leviticus 25, Deuteronomy 25 and Proverbs are the book's confirmed live sources, and Ezekiel is cold — it appears nowhere else in Ruth `[T]`. A cold source needs a distinctive match to carry weight. The one here has it.

#### Ezekiel 16:8 → Ruth 3:9 *(moderate–high confidence — distinctive idiom, direction of dependence unknown)*

The full Triad, as the significance rule requires.

*Source context.* Ezekiel 16 is the long allegory of Jerusalem as a foundling. YHWH finds her exposed and bloodied at birth, cast out in a field with no one to pity her (16:4–5); he says "Live!" and she grows (16:6–7); and then at 16:8 he passes by again: "I passed by you and saw you, and behold, your time was the time of love. **And I spread my wing over you and covered your nakedness**, and I swore to you and entered into a covenant with you, declares the Lord GOD, and you became mine." What immediately follows is washing, anointing and clothing (16:9–13) — and what follows *that* is the whole chapter's turn into indictment, as she uses the gifts to prostitute herself (16:15 onwards). The function of 16:8 in its own passage is the *covenant-making moment*: the single verse in which the relationship is constituted, before the chapter becomes an accusation. Its emotional register is tender and it is placed to make the betrayal that follows unbearable.

*Book usage (Move 2).* **Nil, and the nil matters.** Ezekiel appears nowhere else in Ruth — not in this sweep's ten units, not in the overview's intertextual map except at this one row `[T]`. So there is no pattern of use to appeal to, and the row cannot borrow strength from a live-source prior. It stands or falls on the idiom.

*OT-to-OT (Move 3).* It stands, because Ezekiel 16:8 is itself already in conversation with the Torah's sexual-boundary law, and Ruth 3:9 taps that conversation rather than creating it. Three measured facts:
- **To uncover a כָּנָף is the Deuteronomic incest formula.** גָּלָה with כָּנָף stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1] and 27:20, both "he shall not uncover his father's כָּנָף" `[T]`. Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1] pairs it with "a man shall not take his father's wife", which is Lev 18:8's prohibition restated — and Lev 18:8 uses the standard formula, "the nakedness of your father's wife you shall not uncover" `[T]`.
- **To uncover nakedness (גָּלָה + עֶרְוָה) is that formula's standing shape**: 28 verses, the Leviticus 18 and 20 codes `[T]`.
- **To *cover* nakedness (כָּסָה + עֶרְוָה) is rare — four verses**: Gen 9:23, Exod 28:42, **Ezek 16:8**, Hos 2:9 [Heb 2:11] `[T]`.

So Ezekiel 16:8 performs a deliberate inversion of the Levitical idiom: where the law says *do not uncover nakedness*, YHWH *covers* it, and he does so by spreading a כָּנָף. The marriage-covenant idiom is constructed, in Ezekiel, out of the negation of the incest formula. *High confidence* on all three distributions; *moderate–high* that Ezekiel is consciously inverting the formula.

*Rarity and rivals, as the gate requires for any rating above moderate.* **פָּרַשׂ + כָּנָף + עַל ("spread a wing over") stands in nine verses of the Hebrew Bible**: Exod 25:20; 37:9; 1 Kgs 8:7; 2 Chr 3:13; 5:8 — all five of the cherubim spreading wings over the ark or mercy seat; Deut 32:11 and Jer 49:22 — both eagles; **Ezek 16:8** and **Ruth 3:9** `[T]`. Nine verses is just under the idiom threshold, so the phrase alone would cap at moderate. But the rival analysis is what decides it: seven of the nine are cherubim or birds, and **Ezekiel 16:8 is the only other verse in the Hebrew Bible where a person spreads a כָּנָף over another person** `[T]`. There is no closer rival and no equal one. *That* is what lifts the row to moderate–high on the lexical case — a correction, upward, to the overview's flat *moderate*.

What the row still cannot have is a *direction*. Ruth's date is contested `[S]`, so whether Ruth draws on Ezekiel, Ezekiel on Ruth, or both on a shared idiom is undecidable from the text, and no design claim may be built on it. The overview's cap on that question should stand.

*What it adds.* Ruth's request is phrased in the Hebrew Bible's one other idiom for a person taking another person into covenant by spreading a garment-wing over them — an idiom built by inverting the incest formula. Three consequences for preaching. First, the request is **covenantal in form**, not merely marital and certainly not merely sexual: in Ezekiel the spreading is followed by an oath and a covenant, and in Ruth it is followed by an oath (3:13) and a legal transaction (4:1–12) `[T]`. Second, the one canonical analogue has **YHWH as the one who spreads**, which is why Ruth's asking it of Boaz, having been told at 2:12 that she had come under YHWH's wings, is theologically loaded rather than merely clever `[I]`, *moderate–high*. Third, and pastorally decisive: the idiom Ruth uses is the one Scripture uses for *covering* nakedness, not uncovering it.

#### Deuteronomy 23:1 and 27:20 → the idiom Ruth 3:4–9 does *not* use *(high confidence on the lexical facts; the avoidance reading is `[I]`, moderate–high)*

*Source context.* Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1] closes the sequence of sexual prohibitions that opens Deuteronomy's laws on the assembly: "A man shall not take his father's wife, and he shall not uncover his father's כָּנָף." Deut 27:20 puts the same offence under a curse from Mount Ebal. The two are the only verses in the Hebrew Bible that combine גָּלָה with כָּנָף `[T]`.

*What it adds.* Ruth's night contains an uncovering and a כָּנָף, and the text **never puts them together**. She uncovers the מַרְגְּלֹת (3:4, 3:7) — a place-noun, not a body or a garment; and the כָּנָף is what she asks Boaz to *spread* (3:9) `[T]`. Had the narrator written "she uncovered his כָּנָף", he would have written the Deuteronomic transgression verbatim. He did not. *High confidence* on the facts; the inference that the separation is deliberate is `[I]` at *moderate–high*, and it is strengthened by the narrator's four other interventions around the scene (Tool 6). This is the single most useful thing the dig has to offer a preacher who is nervous about chapter 3.

#### 2 Samuel 12:20 → Ruth 3:3 *(moderate confidence — shared sequence)*

*Source context.* David, having fasted and wept while his child was dying, learns the child is dead, rises, **washes, anoints himself and changes his clothes**, and goes to worship and then to eat (2 Sam 12:20). The sequence marks the *end of mourning*. Washing with anointing stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — 2 Sam 12:20; Ezek 16:9; Ruth 3:3 — and with the garment added, two: 2 Sam 12:20 and Ruth 3:3 `[T]`.

*Book usage:* no earlier use of Samuel in Ruth. *OT-to-OT:* no pairing in this passage.

*What it adds.* Naomi is dressing Ruth to stop being a widow `[I]`. The three-act sequence is what an Israelite did when mourning ended, and that reading is more useful and better grounded than reading the washing and anointing as seduction — for which the text supplies nothing. *Moderate confidence*; the Ezekiel 16:9 member of the set belongs to the row above, where the washing *follows* the covenant rather than preceding the request.

#### Internal echo (Move 4) — required, and the richest part of this tool

*Answers earlier material:*
- **2:12 → 3:9.** Boaz's blessing returned as a claim; the only two occurrences of כָּנָף in the book `[T]`. *High* on the chain, *moderate–high* on the reading. This is the book's hinge.
- **1:9 → 3:1.** Naomi's wish that her daughters-in-law find מְנוּחָה becomes her plan to seek מָנוֹחַ for Ruth — cognate, not identical `[T]`. *High* on the relation.
- **2:20 → 3:9.** The redeemer category, named by Naomi, now used by Ruth as the ground of a request `[T]`. *High.*
- **1:12 → 3:2, 3:8.** Naomi's dismissed hypothetical night; לַיְלָה four times in the book `[T]`. *High* on the chain, *moderate* on the reading — but the reading is striking: the woman who said a night could do her no good sends another woman into one.
- **2:1 → 3:2.** Boaz introduced by a word from יָדַע; called "our relative" from the same root `[T]`. *High* on the morphology.
- **2:13 → 3:9.** שִׁפְחָה becomes אָמָה `[T]`. *Low–moderate* on the direction.

*Planted for later:*
- **גָּלָה (3:4, 3:7) → 4:4**, where Boaz says "I will uncover your ear" — three occurrences in the book, and both English versions render the third as "tell", so the link from the night scene to the legal scene is invisible in English `[T]`. *High* on the chain, *moderate* that it is designed — "uncover the ear" is itself a set phrase, standing in thirteen verses of the canon, six of them in 1–2 Samuel `[T]`.
- **יָרַד (3:3, 3:6) → עָלָה (4:1)** — down to the floor, up to the gate; the only occurrences of either verb in the book `[T]`. **The inversion is withdrawn as design** (*v1.1 — claim audit, R25*): the distribution is modal for this book, the ascent is a seventeen-verse idiom, and the 3:3 ketiv is first person. Recorded as a distribution only.
- **יָטַב (3:1, 3:7) → 3:10** `[T]`. *High.*
- **The oath pattern** — Ruth's request is answered by Boaz's oath at 3:13 (חַי־יְהוָה), the book's only oath by YHWH apart from Ruth's own at 1:17 `[T]`. *Moderate–high*; and in Ezek 16:8 the spreading of the wing is likewise followed by an oath.
- **אָמָה (3:9) → 4:5, 4:10**, where Boaz "acquires" Ruth: the woman who called herself a maidservant is transferred by a commercial verb `[T]`. *Moderate* — the vocabulary is not shared, the trajectory is.

*Addressee-differentiation check:* not applicable — Ruth's opening unit names two daughters-in-law, and Orpah's thread is closed at 1:14–15 rather than resolved later `[T]`.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew prose narrative, third-person, anonymous narrator; within it, two embedded speech-units (Naomi's instruction, Ruth's request) and one minimal dialogue. Reading rules are the ordinary narrative ones: it happened, it is theologically shaped, the narrator's comments are authoritative where characters' are not, and the Copycat and Who-Am-I tools must run before any application.

One genre question is worth raising because it is frequently asserted: **is this a betrothal type-scene?** The classic Hebrew betrothal type-scene — a journey to a foreign place, a meeting at a well, a drawing of water, news carried home, a betrothal — is a recognised convention `[S]` (Gen 24; Gen 29; Exod 2). **The claim audit tested it and discarded it** (*v1.1 — R27*). Against Ruth 2, Genesis 24 ranks 29th of 859 chapters on rare shared vocabulary at K=5 and 7th at K=10, clearing its null; **Genesis 29 ranks 219th, and Exodus 2 ranks 288th with zero rare shared vocabulary at all** — a nil result with the positive controls passing in the same run. Two of the three exemplars register nothing, so there is no convention here being varied. ### 14. Bible Timeline

**Canonical position (Phase 0.55).** Ruth stands fourth in the Ketuvim as BHS prints them — Psalms, Job, Proverbs, **Ruth**, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles `[T]`. It is a Writing. The English and Greek placement after Judges is a later arrangement `[S]`, and the book's own opening clause fixes the *events* in the judges' period without filing the *book* there. Extracted from the overview rather than re-derived, as the phase directs.

What that changes for this passage: the canonical reader of the Writings has read the Prophets through Malachi and arrives post-exilic — knowing David, knowing how the monarchy ended, living with promises unresolved `[S]`. Such a reader meets a childless Moabite widow asking an Israelite landowner to spread his garment over her, and already knows that the line being negotiated in the dark runs to David and past him into ruin and hope `[I]`, *moderate*.

**Timeline position of the events.** Judges period — after the conquest, before the monarchy; before temple, before king, before exile. What has happened *since*, that materially changes how a Christian reads it: the monarchy this night produces, its failure, the exile, and the coming of the son of David in whose genealogy Ruth is named (Matt 1:5) `[S]`. The redeemer category the scene turns on is the category the canon develops to Christ.

**Where the reader is.** This side of the cross, with the redemption Boaz's act prefigures accomplished rather than promised. The practical effect on this passage is that the Christian does not read it looking for a redeemer, but recognising one.

**Timeline-driven misreading to avoid.** Treating the levirate-adjacent duty, or the sandal custom of 4:7, or the uncovering gesture as a practice to be recovered. These are provisions of a kinship-and-land economy fulfilled and set aside in Christ `[I]`.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** That the reader should see a destitute foreigner stake everything on what a redeemer *is*, and should see the request honoured `[I]`. The passage is not asking for imitation of a technique; it is establishing that the redeemer's obligation is a thing a person with no standing may invoke.

**Stage 2 — into life.**

*Worldview.* The passage works against two instincts at once. Against self-reliance: Ruth's one sentence contains no appeal to her merit or her need, only to Boaz's status `[T]`. Against passivity: she asks, at cost and at risk, and the book's turn depends on her asking. The Christian analogue is not a technique of prayer but a ground of it — we ask on the basis of what Christ is, not what we are. The motive is supplied by the text itself: *for you are a redeemer*.

*Behaviour — stop.* Stop grounding requests to God in your own record. The shape of 3:9 is status-then-request, and it is the reverse of the way most of us pray.

*Behaviour — start.* Ask on the ground of the redeemer's obligation. And, at the level the passage most obviously commends: act beyond what duty requires, as Boaz already has in chapter 2 and as he will again at the gate — the book's kindness is consistently a kindness that exceeds the law `[T]`.

*Motivation, not bare imperative.* The kindness in this book is God's before it is anyone's (1:6; 2:20), and the only act the narrator attributes to God in his own voice is the conception at 4:13 `[T]`. The call to act in חֶסֶד rests on a חֶסֶד received.

**Four audiences.**
- *For me:* On what ground did I last ask God for something? If the honest answer is my own faithfulness, 3:9 is a rebuke and a relief.
- *For a Christian friend:* The friend who believes they have forfeited the right to ask — because of their history, their failure, their outsider status — is in Ruth's position exactly, and 3:9 is the sentence to give them. Her ground is not her record; she is a Moabite widow with no record worth citing.
- *For the church community:* A church that reads this chapter only as a cautionary tale about propriety will miss that its own standing before God has the shape of Ruth's request. And a church with outsiders in it should notice who is asking and who is asked.
- *For an unbeliever:* The passage is about a claim made on someone else's obligation — which is the structure of the gospel, and the opposite of the earning most people assume Christianity requires.

**Prayer.** Lord, we have no standing of our own to plead. Spread your covering over us, for you are the redeemer. We ask on the ground of what you are, not what we have done.

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**

- **The ketiv forms of 3:3–4 end in ־תִּי.** The WLC prints שמלתך, וירדתי and ושכבתי unpointed, and the qere supplies שִׂמְלֹתַיִךְ, וְיָרַדְתְּ, וְשָׁכַבְתְּ (BHS apparatus at 3:3, with 3:4 cross-referring to it) `[T]`. Read as they stand, two of Naomi's instructions are first person — "and *I* shall go down… and *I* shall lie down". The standard explanation is an **archaic second-feminine-singular ending in ־תִּי**, attested elsewhere (Jer 2:33; 3:4–5 among them) `[S]`, which the Masoretes normalised. *(v1.2 — Logos round 1, L5: answered, and upgraded to high.)* The archaic second-feminine-singular explanation is the settled one: Brotzman has the change running archaic ketiv → normalised qere (*OT Textual Criticism*, 150), and Driver's parallels are Micah 4:13 with frequent forms in Jeremiah and Ezekiel (via Wilch, Concordia, 19) `[S]`. **Campbell adds something this report did not see:** all nine archaic verb forms in Ruth stand in the speeches of **Boaz and Naomi**, which suggests the storyteller used them to mark the seniority of those two characters — and the forms at 3:3 and 3:4 are placed to round off two series of verbs (Anchor Bible 7:25) `[S]`, *moderate–high*. If that holds, the ketiv is a characterisation device, not an antiquarian survival, and it belongs with the book's other ways of distinguishing voices. Either way nothing doctrinal turns on it — but a reader comparing a Hebrew text with an English one will see no trace of it, and three ketiv in two verses is a high local density.
- **The infinitive absolute is absent here**, which is worth noting only because it was present at 2:16 (שֹׁל־תָּשֹׁלּוּ, "you shall surely pull out") `[T]`. Naomi's instructions are unemphatic in form.
- **וְהִנֵּה at 3:8** is a presentative, not a conjunction — it introduces perceived content and shifts the deictic centre to Boaz (Tool 6).
- **The waw-consecutive chain of vv.6–8** is unbroken: fourteen forms, no disjunctive clause, no circumstantial material `[T]`. Hebrew narrative's default, deployed for speed.
- **3:9's nominal clause** אָנֹכִי רוּת אֲמָתֶךָ — pronoun, name, apposition — is maximally formal for a two-word question. She gives her name and her status before she asks `[T]`.

**Wordplay and sound.**

The root **יָדַע** supplies both the kinship term (3:2, and 2:1's ketiv) and the chapter's five verbs of knowing and concealment `[T]`. The hard rules on sound-based claims apply: this is not a consonant reversal and not a taxonomy activation, but one root operating in two semantic fields, which is ordinary in Hebrew. What raises it above the ordinary is the density — five uses in one chapter, clustered on concealment, around a kinship noun from the same root. Reported at *moderate*, and it may not be rated higher without a commentary's support. No transliteration is given, since the sound is not the argument; the root is.

**Recommended verification.** The אָמָה / שִׁפְחָה ranking; the sense of לָפַת at 3:8; the archaic ־תִּי ending; whether the Greek πτερύγιον at 3:9 carries the garment sense as clearly as the Hebrew. All four are lexicon or grammar questions, and all four are listed in Open Questions.

### Textual Variants

Three items, two of them minor and one worth a sentence in the pulpit. All are category 2 of the triage (variation inside the Hebrew tradition, or a divergence of *Vorlage*) except where noted, and each names its witness as BHS prints it rather than appealing to "the Masoretic text".

**3:3 and 3:4 — the ־תִּי ketiv forms.** As Original Languages above. BHS at 3:3 records that a few manuscripts read with the qere לֹתַיִךְ and that the ketiv, with many manuscripts, has לָתֵךְ; and for the second word, that the qere with many manuscripts has וְיָרַדְתְּ against a ketiv in ־תִּי, with 3:4 cross-referring `[T]`. **Preaching implication: none.** Acknowledge if asked; build nothing on it. *Moderate confidence* that the archaic-ending explanation is correct.

**3:5 — a qere with no ketiv.** BHS records that many manuscripts read with the qere אֵלַי ("to me"), and that the Greek reads with the ketiv — that is, without it `[T]`. The WLC prints vowel points with no consonants at this point, the classic "read but not written" phenomenon, and the same thing happens at 3:17 `[T]`. So Ruth's assent is either "all that you say **to me** I will do" or "all that you say I will do". **Preaching implication: negligible**, though it is a nice illustration of how the reading tradition and the consonantal text can differ. *High confidence* on the data; the feature is a reading tradition and not authorial.

**3:9 — "who are you?"** BHS notes that the form אָ֑תְּ stands thus in Leningrad, with many manuscripts and printed editions reading אָתְּ `[T]`. Orthographic; nothing turns on it. Named here only because the gate requires apparatus findings to name their witness, and because this is a Leningrad-specific form.

**No substantive variant affects the meaning of this passage.** The two places in Ruth where a variant genuinely changes the sense — 4:5's ketiv/qere and 4:14's "his name" against the Greek's "your name" — are both outside this unit.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.**
- **Time period:** the judges' era for the events; a later, probably post-exilic vantage for the telling, though the date is contested `[S]`.
- **Location:** a threshing floor outside Bethlehem — a flat, exposed, communal space, usually on raised ground to catch the wind, where grain was trodden and then winnowed `[S]`. It is named four times in the chapter and the town is not named at all `[T]`.
- **Occasion:** the end of the barley harvest (2:23), which is when winnowing is done and when the owner would sleep beside his heap to guard it `[S]`, *moderate confidence* — the guarding motive is a reconstruction, though 3:7's "he went to lie down at the end of the heap of grain" fits it exactly `[T]`.

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

1. **Why a man sleeps on a threshing floor.** Grain at the floor is portable wealth, unwalled and unwatched. Boaz is not camping romantically; he is guarding a harvest `[I]`, *moderate*.
2. **Why winnowing happens at night.** The evening breeze is what separates chaff from grain `[S]`. The timing is agricultural, not atmospheric — the narrator did not choose night for mood.
3. **What a threshing floor was associated with.** *(v1.2 — Logos round 1, L11: this claim is overstated and is withdrawn as background.)* The threshing floor's attested functions are agricultural and administrative — processing and distributing grain, public gatherings, harvest celebrations (IVP Bible Background Commentary) `[S]`. The sexual-licence association rests almost entirely on one disputed reading of **Hosea 9:1**, and Alice Keefe argues that the cultic reading is an interpretive tradition rather than unambiguous textual support, the floor being as plausibly a centre of harvest economics and legal adjudication, with the "fornication" language carrying the sense of treacherous profiteering; she observes that confident assertions about licentious Canaanite fertility cults are "repeated over and over in copy-cat fashion in one commentary to the next" (*Woman's Body and the Social Body in Hosea*, JSOTSup 338:43–44, 136–137) `[S]`. **A sermon may not use the threshing floor to insinuate what the text does not say.** The original wording of this item ran: Floors were also places of cultic irregularity and of prostitution in prophetic indictment `[S]`, *moderate confidence* — Hosea 9:1 is the usual citation. If that association was live, the original audience would have felt the risk of the scene more sharply than a modern reader does, and the narrator's four interventions (Tool 6) are the more pointed for it.
4. **What a garment-corner meant.** The כָּנָף was the part of the garment that carried identity and legal significance — tassels were commanded on it (Num 15:38) `[T]`, and a corner of cloth could seal a transaction `[S]`. Spreading it over a woman as a claim is therefore a legible legal-social gesture, not an invented symbol `[S]`, *moderate*.
5. **What a redeemer was for.** Land in Israel was inalienable in principle; a kinsman's duty was to keep a family's land and name in the family (Lev 25) `[T]` for the law, `[S]` for its working. The audience heard "you are a redeemer" as a claim on a defined obligation.
6. **How exposed an unattached foreign woman was.** 2:9 and 2:22 have already established the risk in daylight `[T]`. At night, at a floor, it is greater.

**What this changes about how we read.** Two things. First, **the risk in the scene is social and legal, not primarily moral**: discovery would have destroyed Ruth's standing and compromised Boaz's, which is exactly what 3:14's "let it not be known that the woman came to the threshing floor" says `[T]`. Second, **the gesture Ruth asks for is a recognised one.** A modern reader supplies either romance or impropriety because the legal vocabulary is invisible; the first audience had the vocabulary.

*Confidence note.* The agricultural and legal background above is well attested; the threshing-floor-as-place-of-irregularity association and the guarding motive are reconstructions at *moderate*, and the specifics of garment-corner law are weaker again. None of the exegetical findings above depends on them.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.**
- **Canonical section:** Ketuvim. The reader has the Torah and the whole Prophets behind them, and the Prophets end with Malachi — so the Writings are read from a post-exilic vantage, with the monarchy over and the promises unresolved `[S]`.
- **What the canonical reader knows:** the redemption and name-raising laws (Lev 25; Deut 25); the Deuteronomic prohibition on uncovering a father's כָּנָף (Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1]); Moab's status (Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4]); Tamar and Judah (Gen 38); Rachel's death at Ephrath (Gen 35:19); and David.
- **Canonical register:** Job, Psalms and Proverbs are the immediate neighbours. The שַׁדַּי of Naomi's lament points to Job first; the אֵשֶׁת חַיִל of 3:11 to Proverbs; the "restorer of life" of 4:15 to Psalm 19 and Lamentations. For this unit the register matters mainly in that a Writings reader is used to wisdom's interest in the well-placed word and the risky act `[I]`, *moderate*.

**The first hearers.**
- **Who they were:** Israelites who knew the law and the land system, and for whom Moab was a name with freight.
- **Their situation:** living with a Davidic promise whose historical vehicle had failed.
- **What they brought:** the legal vocabulary, the agricultural calendar, the honour-shame calculus of a woman's night-time movement, and the Deuteronomic uncovering prohibition.

**Where the passage fits their world.** They would have recognised the washing-anointing-dressing sequence as the end of mourning (2 Sam 12:20) rather than as adornment for seduction; they would have heard "spread your כָּנָף over your maidservant" as a formal claim; and they would have registered the narrator's careful avoidance of "uncover the כָּנָף" because the phrase was in their law `[I]`, *moderate–high* on the first two, *moderate* on the third.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** that the woman speaks first, and that what she asks for is marriage on legal grounds in the middle of the night. Also that she is a Moabite doing it.
- **Surprising:** that the man's response, when it comes (3:10), is a blessing and a verdict of kindness rather than an accusation.
- **Comforting:** that the redeemer's obligation is a thing the powerless may invoke, and that it is honoured.
- **Disturbing:** that the household's whole future turns on a night that could have gone differently, and that the narrator lets the reader feel it.
- **Familiar:** the floor, the harvest, the night breeze, the kinship duty.

**What we bring that they didn't.** Romantic-love marriage, which converts a legal approach into a courtship. Individualism, which makes the scene about Ruth's self-actualisation rather than a household's survival. Modern privacy, which cannot see why discovery would be catastrophic. And a suspicion of euphemism that reads the scene for what it might be hiding rather than for what it says.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge.

**1. Redemption / kinsman-redeemer.**
- *Where this passage sits:* at the point where the category, named at 2:20, is first *invoked* by the one who needs it. The law stands behind it (Lev 25) `[T]`; the costly public discharge lies ahead (4:1–12) `[T]`.
- *What comes before:* the exodus as the paradigm redemption, where God redeems a people with no claim; the Levitical provision that keeps land and name in a family.
- *What comes after:* the prophetic use of גֹּאֵל for YHWH himself (Isaiah's "your Redeemer"), and the NT's ἀπολύτρωσις.
- *What it contributes here:* Ruth's ground-clause makes the request a claim on an obligation rather than an appeal to goodwill — which is the logic the whole doctrine of redemption runs on `[I]`. *High confidence* on the theme's presence; the textual signal is the word itself.

**2. Covering / covenant protection.**
- *Where this passage sits:* at the one place in the Hebrew Bible, besides Ezek 16:8, where a person is asked to spread a כָּנָף over another `[T]`.
- *What comes before:* God clothing Adam and Eve (Gen 3:21); the cherubim's wings over the mercy seat (Exod 25:20); the eagle over its young (Deut 32:11); the Psalter's refuge-under-wings idiom (five verses, 2:12 among them) `[T]`.
- *What comes after:* Ezekiel 16:8's covenant-making; and Christ's gathering image — "as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings" `[S]`.
- *What it contributes here:* the request is covenantal in form, and the idiom belongs to the family in which God covers what the law forbids uncovering `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

**Confluence — and it is the climactic kind.** These two themes are not running in parallel here; they **meet in one clause**. "Spread your wing over your maidservant, **for you are a redeemer**" (3:9) puts the covering idiom and the redemption category in a single sentence, with the second grounding the first `[T]`. The protection is asked for *on the basis of* the legal obligation — which is precisely the structure the canon develops: God covers because God has bound himself to redeem. *High confidence* that the convergence is in the text; it is in one verse, and it is the verse the passage exists to reach.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: typology, through an established theological category — with a trajectory component.**

**The four tests, scored.**

1. **Theological-category test — passes.** The connection runs through *redemption* and the office of גֹּאֵל, not through an incidental detail. This is the test that matters most here, because it is what disqualifies the cloak, the barley, the feet and the hour of the night from carrying any typological weight.
2. **NT-precedent test — passes in the weaker form.** The NT does not read Boaz as a type of Christ anywhere. It does name Ruth and Boaz in Messiah's genealogy (Matt 1:5) `[S]`, and it applies the redemption category to Christ pervasively. So: no precedent for *this figure*, strong precedent for *this kind of connection*. Score it as "strong", not "strongest".
3. **Escalation test — passes.** Boaz redeems one widow's field and one dead man's name, at a cost he could afford, and only after a nearer redeemer declines. Christ redeems at infinite cost, as the nearer kinsman who does *not* decline. The antitype exceeds the type in every direction, which is what a genuine type does.
4. **Authorial-pattern test — passes.** The book itself raises and sustains the expectation: it introduces the category at 2:20, repeats גָּאַל twenty-one times, structures its climax around a redemption transaction, and ends by tying the redeemed household to David `[T]`. The pattern is in the text, not read back into it.

**Score: all four — *high confidence* typology.** This is unusually clean, and the reason is that the book did the work.

**How this passage points to Christ.**

Not by analogy of plot but by the shape of the request. At 3:9 a person with no standing asks for covering on the ground of another's obligation, and the ground is sufficient. That is the structure of the gospel appeal, and the passage states it in one clause. Christ is the redeemer who is also the covering — the one whose obligation is self-assumed and whose spreading of protection is the covenant itself `[I]`.

Two particulars are worth the preacher's weight:
- **Boaz does not create his obligation; he discovers it is claimed.** He is asleep when the claim is laid, and startled when he finds it. The initiative in this scene is Ruth's, and the obligation is prior to both of them. In the antitype the obligation is self-assumed, which is where the type is exceeded `[I]`, *moderate–high*.
- **The nearer redeemer complicates it, and that is not a flaw in the type.** Boaz cannot act until a man with a prior right declines (3:12) `[T]`. Christ has no prior claimant, and the contrast is instructive rather than embarrassing.

**Trajectory.**
- *What the passage anticipates:* a redeemer who will act at cost for someone with no claim.
- *What Christ fulfils:* that, and without the contingency — no nearer redeemer, no refusal, no risk that the obligation goes undischarged.
- *What remains (already / not yet):* the covering is given; the household's full restoration, in the book, waits for a birth, and in the canon for a return.

**Moralism check.**
- *The "be like X" temptation here:* three of them. Be loyal like Ruth; be shrewd like Naomi; be chaste like Boaz. All three are available from the text and none is its argument `[I]`.
- *The gospel grounding:* the kindness in this book is God's before it is anyone's (1:6; 2:20), and the only act the narrator attributes to God in his own voice is the conception at 4:13 `[T]`. Every human kindness in Ruth, this one included, is response.
- *Christ as hero, not just example:* the subject of the sentence Ruth speaks is not Ruth. "**You** are a redeemer" — the clause makes the other party the agent. A sermon that keeps the grammar keeps the gospel.

**What must not be allegorised.** The cloak is not the righteousness of Christ; the six measures (next unit) are not a sacramental figure; the feet are not anything. The overview's trap 3 is live and this is where it bites. The type runs through the category, and the category is in the text twenty-one times.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 3:4, 3:7 — the uncovering, and whether the scene is sexual

- **Category:** pastoral, with an ethical edge.
- **The difficulty:** Ruth, at night, in secret, uncovers a sleeping man and lies down at his feet. A modern hearer — and some commentators — will ask whether a sexual encounter is being described or hinted at.
- **Common misreadings:** (a) that the scene is a seduction, successful or attempted; (b) that it is innocent to the point of being sexless and the preacher may skip the question; (c) that "feet" is simply a euphemism for genitals and the matter is settled.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the uncovering is the *last* of five instructions whose first four are unremarkable, and it is followed immediately by "and he will tell you what you shall do" (3:4) — the instruction's own expectation is that the man will *speak*, not act `[T]`. In the scene's own logic the gesture is a signal, not a consummation.
- **Two supports the claim audit and Logos round 1 have added** (*v1.2*). First, **the ambiguity in לָפַת at 3:8 is not in the Hebrew.** The niphal is reflexive — Boaz twisted or turned himself on waking (NIDOTTE 2:810; Gesenius 441) — and the passive sense "he was clung to" that makes the verse suggestive is a rabbinic midrashic reinterpretation the grammar does not support `[S]`, *high confidence*. Second, **the threshing-floor background that is usually wheeled in has been withdrawn** (Historical Background above): its attested functions are agricultural, administrative and judicial, and the sexual-licence association rests on one contested reading of Hosea 9:1 `[S]`. So two of the three props of the sexual reading — a suggestive verb and a disreputable location — are not props at all, and the third, רֶגֶל as euphemism, is addressed below.

- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** Four facts, and they do not all point the same way, which is why the question is real.
  1. The object of the uncovering is **מַרְגְּלֹת**, a place-noun, "the place of the feet" — not רַגְלַיִם and not a body `[T]`, *high confidence*. Five verses in the canon, four of them here.
  2. רֶגֶל ("foot") *can* be a euphemism elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible `[S]`, *moderate confidence* — but מַרְגְּלֹת is a different word, and the euphemistic reading has to travel through a term that denotes a location.
  3. The text **never pairs** גָּלָה with כָּנָף, which is the Deuteronomic incest formula and stands in exactly two verses (Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1]; 27:20) `[T]`, *high confidence*. The narrator had the phrase available and used the forbidden verb only of the feet-place and the loaded noun only of the spreading.
  4. The narrator intervenes four times around the scene — Boaz's contentment (3:7), Ruth's obedience (3:6), Boaz's viewpoint (3:8), and in the next verse Boaz's verdict that this was חֶסֶד and that Ruth had not gone after young men (3:10) `[T]`, *high confidence*. The last of these is the book's own man passing a verdict of chastity on the night.
- **Suggested handling.** **Address it briefly and early, then move on.** Name the risk the text itself names (3:14), state that the narrator answers the question in 3:10, and give the lexical point — the text keeps the forbidden idiom and the covenant idiom apart. Do not build the sermon on the ambiguity, and do not pretend it is absent: a congregation that has read chapter 3 in a modern translation has felt it, and will trust a preacher who says so.
- **The oversimplification to avoid:** flattening the scene into "it was all perfectly proper, nothing to see here". The text itself treats it as dangerous — that is what 3:14 is for — and the danger is part of what makes Ruth's action חֶסֶד rather than mere compliance.

#### 3:9 — the commercial and proprietary edge of the request

- **Category:** ethical / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** Ruth asks a man to spread a garment over her and calls herself his אָמָה — a slave-term — twice in one sentence. Chapter 4 will have Boaz "acquire" her with the same verb used of the field.
- **Rhetorical function:** the self-designation is the rhetorical floor from which the claim is made — no status, and a right `[T]`.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the Hebrew does not soften any of it, and the text's own frame is that a dead man's name is being discharged at a living man's expense `[T]`. The vocabulary of the book's redemption is property vocabulary because the institution was a property institution.
- **Suggested handling:** name the discomfort rather than smoothing it. Smoothing it costs the costliness, and the costliness is where the typology lives.

#### 3:1 — Naomi's plan, and whether it was right

- **Category:** doctrinal/ethical, and mostly manufactured.
- **The difficulty:** did Naomi act in faith or in manipulation?
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the text passes no verdict `[T]`. It reports the plan, reports its execution with an approving compliance-notice (3:6), and lets Boaz characterise the *outcome* as kindness (3:10) — not the plan as strategy.
- **Suggested handling:** sidestep with a note. A preacher who adjudicates has supplied what the narrator withheld, and the sermon gains nothing by it.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: preaching the scene as romance

- **What it looks like:** "Ruth made herself beautiful and went to the man she loved"; "waiting for your Boaz".
- **Why it's wrong:** Naomi drives the scene from her own emptiness (3:1); the washing-and-dressing sequence is the end-of-mourning sequence (2 Sam 12:20) `[T]`; the request's stated ground is a legal status (3:9); and Boaz's own verdict is that the action was חֶסֶד to the dead rather than attraction to him (3:10) `[T]`. The word "love" does not occur in the passage.
- **The corrective:** preach a legal approach made at the only moment available to two women with no standing, and let the risk be real.

### Pitfall: allegorising the threshing floor

- **What it looks like:** the cloak as the righteousness of Christ; the feet as submission; the grain-heap as provision; the night as the dark night of the soul.
- **Why it's wrong:** the typology runs through the *redeemer category* — which the book states twenty-one times and which passes all four typology tests — not through the staging. Allegorising the details is the move the Christological extension names as the characteristic failure, and it is the overview's trap 3.
- **The corrective:** preach גֹּאֵל. Everything the passage gives Christologically is in the last clause of 3:9, and it is enough.

### Pitfall: resolving or dodging the sexual question

- **What it looks like:** either a knowing aside that "we all know what 'feet' means", or a brisk assurance that nothing improper occurred, with no evidence offered.
- **Why it's wrong:** the first overreads a place-noun; the second asks the congregation to take the preacher's word for something the text actually argues. The text's own evidence is specific and better than either: the object of the uncovering is a location; the Deuteronomic formula is carefully not used; and Boaz passes a verdict in 3:10.
- **The corrective:** take two minutes, give the evidence, and move to the request. A congregation trusts a preacher who has noticed what they noticed.

### Pitfall: making Ruth's initiative the point

- **What it looks like:** "Ruth took a risk — God honours those who step out in faith."
- **Why it's wrong:** it converts a request grounded in someone else's obligation into a lesson about the asker's boldness, which reverses the grammar of 3:9. The subject of Ruth's clause is Boaz: "**you** are a redeemer."
- **The corrective:** keep the grammar. The sermon's energy should fall where the sentence's does.

### Pitfall: preaching 3:1–9 without reading to 3:13

- **What it looks like:** ending the sermon on the request for dramatic effect.
- **Why it's wrong:** the unit boundary at v.9 is a seam in a dialogue. Boaz's verdict (3:10), his promise (3:11), the obstacle (3:12) and the oath (3:13) are the reply the scene exists to elicit — and 3:10 is the narrator's resolution of the ambiguity the sermon will have just raised `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach 3:1–9 and read through v.13. Leaving the congregation holding the risk with no verdict on it is a pastoral error, not a cliffhanger.

### Pitfall: assuming the pulpit text has flattened the Hebrew

- **What it looks like:** correcting the ESV at 3:9 on the assumption that a literal version would be closer.
- **Why it's wrong:** here the ESV is the *more* transparent — "spread your wings… for you are a redeemer" preserves the book's central echo, where the NASB95's "covering… close relative" breaks it `[T]`. The Greek agrees with the ESV's instinct, using the same wing-word at 3:9 as at 2:12.
- **The corrective:** build on the ESV in this chapter. The one place to correct both versions is "his feet" for the place-noun.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **Does אָמָה (3:9) rank above שִׁפְחָה (2:13)? — Answered as a live dispute** (*v1.2 — Logos round 1, L3*). **For the distinction:** Hamlin takes them as "female slave" and "household servant", so that calling herself אָמָה implies eligibility to become a wife (International Theological Commentary, 45); Younger argues the socially higher term is "doubtless her attempt to suggest that she is within the class of women who might be married" (NIV Application Commentary, 580–581). **Against it:** Campbell holds that whatever legal distinction once existed, there is none in Ruth, the storyteller using synonyms in parallel situations for rhetorical effect (Anchor Bible 7:123); and Fowl names the decisive counter-example — **Abigail uses both terms of herself in 1 Sam 25:23–35** (Brazos, 237). So the upgrade reading is the majority but it is not secure, and the counter-example is a single passage anyone can check. Preach it, if at all, as a probable nuance and not as the point. Original wording of this question: Ruth changes her self-designation between the two scenes `[T]` and the lexica are not unanimous on the relative status `[S]`. *Low–moderate.* A lexicon question — HALOT, NIDOTTE, or TDOT — and the answer changes how the speech's rhetoric is described, not what it claims.
2. **What does לָפַת mean at 3:8? — Answered** (*v1.2 — Logos round 1, L4*). The qal at Judg 16:29 is "grasp, take hold"; the niphal, which is the form here, is "twist, bend, turn" — of a body, or of a caravan changing direction (NIDOTTE 2:810), and Gesenius gives "to bend oneself", "to turn oneself back to see" (*Hebrew and Chaldee Lexicon*, 441) `[S]`. **The niphal is reflexive: Boaz turned himself.** The verb describes his waking movement, not contact between them. Decisively for the preacher: the ambiguity people exploit here is **not** in the Hebrew but in the rabbinic midrash, which reads the niphal as a passive — "he was clung to" — a sense the grammar does not support (NIDOTTE 2:810) `[S]`. *High confidence.* Original wording of this question: Three verses in the canon (Judg 16:29; Job 6:18; Ruth 3:8) `[T]`; NASB95 "bent forward", ESV "turned over", and the renderings guess in different directions. *Uncertain.* Worth a lexicon and a commentary.
3. **Is the ־תִּי of the 3:3–4 ketiv an archaic second-feminine singular? — Answered, yes** (*v1.2 — Logos round 1, L5*). See Original Languages above: Brotzman, Driver via Wilch, and Campbell, who adds that all nine archaic forms in the book fall in the speeches of Boaz and Naomi. No commentator surveyed reads it as a first person. This also corrects the claim audit's R25 reasoning. Original wording of this question: The standard explanation `[S]`, *moderate confidence*, and settleable from a grammar in a line.
4. **Does the Greek πτερύγιον at 3:9 carry the garment sense as clearly as כָּנָף?** It is a diminutive of "wing" used of a hem `[S]`, and if so the Greek preserves the double sense the NASB95 loses. *Moderate–high*, but it rests on my reading of the Greek lexicon rather than on a checked entry.
5. **Ruth's date — which the Ezekiel 16:8 row depends on for direction.** Contested `[S]`. The lexical case for the row is now stronger than the overview had it (nine verses, no equal rival), but no direction of dependence and therefore no design claim may be built on it. *Unresolvable from the text.*
6. **Was the threshing floor associated with cultic irregularity or prostitution in the period?** Hosea 9:1 is the usual citation `[S]`, *moderate*. If so, the scene's risk is sharper and the narrator's interventions more pointed. A background commentary would settle how well attested this is.
7. **Is the betrothal type-scene a real frame here, with the well displaced into the field (2:9)?** **Answered — no** (*v1.1 — claim audit, R27*). Exodus 2 shares no rare vocabulary with Ruth 2 and Genesis 29 ranks 219th of 859; only Genesis 24 registers, and it registers as a single lexical contact at 2:9. Closed.
8. **Where should the preaching unit end — 3:9, 3:13 or 3:18?** An exegetical judgement rather than an uncertainty: the tighter literary unit is 3:1–13 `[I]`. Recorded so the series decision is made knowingly.

**None of these affects the Headline Findings**, each of which rests on a measured distribution in the WLC rather than on a contested reading. Items 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 are exactly the kind a Logos pass would close in minutes, and a pass on them is the recommended next step before this reaches a pulpit.

## Book-Overview Tensions

**One correction, two extensions, no contradictions.**

**Correction — the Ezekiel 16:8 row should be raised, and its cap kept.** The overview rates the row *moderate* and declines to go higher. On the rarity count and rival search now done, the **lexical** case is stronger than that: "spread a wing over" stands in nine verses of the Hebrew Bible, seven of which are cherubim or eagles, leaving **Ezek 16:8 as the only verse where a person spreads a כָּנָף over another person** `[T]`. There is no equal or closer rival, which is the gate's own test for a rating above moderate. The row should read *moderate–high on the idiom*. **But the overview's cap is right where it matters:** the direction of dependence remains unknown because Ruth's date is contested, so nothing structural may be built on it, and the overview should say both things rather than one.

**Extension 1 — the overview's echo table should gain the Deuteronomy 23:1 non-use.** That גָּלָה with כָּנָף stands in exactly two verses, both the Deuteronomic incest prohibition, and that Ruth uses the verb only of the מַרְגְּלֹת and the noun only of the spreading, is the strongest single piece of evidence in the book for how the narrator handles this scene — and it is an argument from an idiom *not* used, which is admissible here because the pattern that makes the absence visible can be cited `[T]`.

**Extension 2 — the overview's intertextual map should record the Ezek 16:8 / Lev 18 / Deut 22:30 [Heb 23:1] conversation.** The covering-of-nakedness idiom stands in four verses and the uncovering formula in twenty-eight `[T]`; Ezekiel 16:8 is constructed by inverting the law, and Ruth 3:9 taps that inversion. The overview has the Ezekiel row but not the Torah leg of it, which is what gives the row its force.

Confirmed without change: the overview's wings chain (2:12 → 3:9) and its confidence; the cognate relation between 1:9 and 3:1; the 2 Sam 12:20 row at *moderate*; the שִׁפְחָה/אָמָה question as unresolved; trap 1 and trap 3 as the live traps for this unit; and the ⭐ HIGH weighting, which this dig vindicates — the unit carries more unaudited material per verse than any other in the book.

Against the **sweep**, this dig confirms all five of its findings for the unit and adds the four-fold מַרְגְּלֹת rarity, the Deuteronomic non-use, the Move 3 chain, the five hapax-in-Ruth words and the יָדַע kinship nouns. Two additions it originally made have since been withdrawn by the claim audit (*v1.1*): the descent/ascent pair as design (R25) and the יָדַע concentration as a frequency finding (R24). The sweep's one claim this dig *narrows*: the sweep called the departure from Naomi's instructions "Ruth tells *him*" at *moderate–high*; on closer work the departure is `[T]` and the motive is unsupplied, so the inference should be stated as the structure's argument rather than as Ruth's intention.
