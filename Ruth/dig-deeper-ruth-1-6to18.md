## Headline Findings

1. **The passage is saturated by one verb, and it is the book's first keyword: nine occurrences of שׁוּב ("return") in thirteen verses, out of fifteen in the whole book.** `[T]` At 1:6 (Naomi returns), 1:7 (to return to Judah), 1:8 (go, *return*), 1:10 (we will return with you), 1:11 and 1:12 (return, my daughters), 1:15 twice (your sister-in-law has *returned*; *return* after her), and 1:16 (to turn back from following you). *High confidence.* **The word is contested throughout: Naomi commands it four times, the daughters-in-law refuse it once, Orpah performs it, and Ruth refuses it in the one sentence where she uses it.** The chapter is an argument about what "going back" means, conducted in one verb.

2. **Ruth's oath at 1:17 uses a formula found nowhere else outside Samuel–Kings, and in her exact wording nowhere else at all.** `[T]` The ordered phrase כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה לִי וְכֹה יֹסִיף ("thus may the LORD do to me, and thus may he add") matches at **Ruth 1:17 and no other verse of the Hebrew Bible**, plain and skeletal. The shorter self-imprecatory formula כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה stands in thirteen verses on a plain pass — eleven of them in 1 Samuel, 2 Samuel and 1–2 Kings, plus Jeremiah 5:13 and this verse. *High confidence.* **A Moabite widow swears in the idiom of Israel's kings, generals and queens** — Saul, Jonathan, David, Abner, Jezebel — and she is the only person outside that block to use it.

3. **The levirate vocabulary enters the book here, and only here, inside the argument that levirate remedy is impossible.** `[T]` יְבָמָה ("sister-in-law") stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Deuteronomy 25:7, Deuteronomy 25:9 and **Ruth 1:15, where it occurs twice**. The masculine יָבָם ("husband's brother") stands at Deuteronomy 25:5, 25:7 and Genesis 38:8, and Ruth never uses it. *High confidence.* Naomi's speech at 1:11–13 is levirate reasoning — have I sons in my womb, would you wait for them — and her conclusion is that the mechanism cannot work. **The book states the legal remedy's name at the moment it declares the remedy unavailable, and the word never appears again**, including in the legal scene of chapter 4, which is conducted in the language of redemption instead.

4. **Two kisses and two weepings frame the argument, and their order inverts.** `[T]` נָשַׁק ("kiss") and בָּכָה ("weep") each occur exactly twice in Ruth, both times in this passage: at 1:9 Naomi kisses and *then* they weep; at 1:14 they weep and *then* Orpah kisses. *High confidence.* The inversion brackets Naomi's central speech (1:10–13), and the second kiss is a parting where the first was a blessing. **The structure puts the argument inside an embrace that comes apart.**

5. **Genesis 2:24's verb-pair is distributed across this chapter and the next, and it cannot be found by a verse-against-verse search.** `[T]` עָזַב ("leave") with דָּבַק ("cling") in one verse stands in **exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Genesis 2:24**. Ruth has both: דָּבְקָה ("she clung") at 1:14 and לְעָזְבֵךְ ("to leave you") at 1:16, with the leaving of a father and a mother supplied at 2:11, where עָזַב with אָב and אֵם stands in four verses of the canon — Genesis 2:24, 1 Kings 19:20, Psalm 27:10 and Ruth 2:11. *High confidence* on the distribution; *moderate* that the pairing is designed. **What Genesis says of a man and his wife, Ruth says of a woman and her mother-in-law** — and says it in two halves, in the wrong order.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 1:1–5 has done three things. It has emptied Naomi — the narrator twice uses שָׁאַר ("be left"), at 1:3 of the husband's death and at 1:5 of the sons', so that she is twice described as what remains `[T]`. It has passed **no verdict**: the text does not say the family sinned by going to Moab, and does not say the deaths were judgement `[T]`. And at 1:4 it has named **two** Moabite wives, Orpah and Ruth, in that order — a differentiated opening.

**Necessity answer.** The famine is over (1:6), so a return is possible; but the book's problem was never the famine, it was the three graves, and a geographical return does not touch it. **This passage exists to convert a journey into a covenant, and to reduce two daughters-in-law to one.** Neither could happen earlier: before 1:5 there are husbands and no decision to make, and after 1:18 the decision is made and the book moves to Bethlehem.

**The addressee-differentiation check, which the opening requires.** The book opens by naming two distinct parties, so the check asks whether a later passage resolves one of them specifically. It does, and in the same verse: **1:14 resolves both, in a single line, by what each does with the same gesture** — Orpah kisses, Ruth clings `[T]`. Orpah is never mentioned again after 1:15. *High confidence.* The pair named at 1:4 exists in order to be separated here.

**Implication.** The passage should not be preached as Ruth's heroism against Orpah's failure. Naomi tells them *both* to go, three times, and the narrator passes verdict on neither. The text's own interest is in what survives the dismissals, not in ranking the two women — and what survives is one sentence of covenant language from the wrong nationality.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens this. The visitation formula of 1:6 is the only act attributed to YHWH in the narrator's voice before 4:13 `[T]`, so the movement is framed by two divine acts — bread at the start and conception at the end — with everything between carried by human kindness. That is the book's shape, and this passage supplies its first term.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** Ruth tells how YHWH's חֶסֶד ("covenant-kindness") turns an Israelite widow's emptiness into the line that produces David, through people who pay for it.

**Passage-level.** Two things, and the second is the one usually missed. First, to establish that Ruth's attachment is **covenantal rather than sentimental** — it is sworn, by YHWH, with a self-imprecation, and it concerns people, God, death and burial, in that order. Second, to establish that it was **resisted**. Naomi does not recruit Ruth; she tries three times to send her away, and the third time she offers Orpah's example as the reasonable course. The author wants a reader who knows that the central relationship of the book was argued against by the person who benefits from it `[I]`, *high confidence*.

**What the passage declines to do.** It does not explain Ruth's motive. The text gives her oath and no reasoning behind it, and the narrator — who elsewhere tells us Boaz's heart was merry (3:7) — says nothing about hers `[T]`. A sermon that supplies the motive is supplying what the author withheld.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above.* Preceding movement: 1:1–5 has emptied Naomi, passed no verdict, and named two Moabite wives. Necessity: a geographical return does not touch a bereavement, so this passage converts the journey into a covenant and reduces two daughters-in-law to one. The addressee-differentiation check resolves both parties at 1:14.

**Immediately before (1:1–5).** Famine, migration, marriage, three deaths in five verses. The narrative accelerates through ten years (1:4) and then stops. The vocabulary that carries into this passage: כַּלָּה ("daughter-in-law"), שָׂדֶה ("field") of Moab, and the names.

**Immediately after (1:19–22).** The arrival at Bethlehem, the town stirred, and Naomi's renaming speech — אַל־תִּקְרֶאנָה לִי נָעֳמִי קְרֶאןָ לִי מָרָא ("do not call me Naomi, call me Mara"). **The link is lexical and immediate:** the root מָרַר ("be bitter") occurs twice in Ruth, at 1:13 and 1:20 `[T]`. Naomi's private word to her daughters-in-law becomes her public name five verses later. *High confidence.*

**Section.** This is the heart of the book's second movement (1:6–22), the road back. Its boundaries are clean: 1:6 begins with וַתָּקָם ("then she arose"), and 1:18's וַתֶּחְדַּל ("and she ceased") closes the dialogue before 1:19 resumes the journey.

**Book.** Of the four movements, this is the only one in which Naomi speaks at length, and the only one set outside Bethlehem's fields and gate.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:6–7 | **Setting out** | Narrative only, no speech. The hearing, the rising, the road. |
| 1:8–9 | **First dismissal** | "Go, return" + a double blessing (kindness; rest in a husband's house), sealed with a kiss. |
| 1:10 | **Joint refusal** | One sentence, spoken by both. |
| 1:11–13 | **Second dismissal** | The argument from impossibility — the longest speech in the book's first chapter. |
| 1:14 | **The division** | Weeping; Orpah kisses, Ruth clings. The two are separated without a word of dialogue. |
| 1:15 | **Third dismissal** | Shortest of the three, and the only one that names a precedent to follow. |
| 1:16–17 | **The oath** | Ruth's reply: five clauses, then a self-imprecation. |
| 1:18 | **Silence** | Naomi stops speaking. |

**The binding device is a threefold dismissal with escalating answers, closed by silence** `[T]`. Naomi says "return" at 1:8, at 1:11, at 1:12 and at 1:15 — four imperatives across three speeches — and each speech is answered more decisively than the last: words (1:10), then action (1:14), then an oath (1:16–17). *High confidence.*

**Inside it sits a chiasm of gesture** `[T]`: kiss (1:9) — weep (1:9) — [Naomi's argument, 1:10–13] — weep (1:14) — kiss (1:14). The two outer gestures are the same verbs in reverse order, and the second kiss is a parting where the first was a blessing. *High confidence* on the distribution; *moderate* that the inversion is designed, **unbaselined** and queued.

**What the structure is not.** It is not a simple two-character contrast with Orpah as foil. Orpah speaks only in 1:10, and then jointly with Ruth. The text gives her no words of her own at all `[T]`.

### 4. Linking Words

- **כִּי ("for, because, that") — eight occurrences** in thirteen verses `[T]`, which is heavy. It grounds the departure (1:6, "for she had heard"), the refusal (1:10, an emphatic "no, but"), the argument (1:12 twice, 1:13 twice), and the oath (1:16, 1:17). **Naomi's case is built entirely of reasons**; Ruth's reply gives one כִּי and then stops reasoning and starts swearing.
- **הֲ … הֲ (1:13)** — two rhetorical questions in one verse, הֲלָהֵן … הֲלָהֵן ("would you therefore …? would you therefore …?") `[T]`. Both expect "no". This is the hinge of Naomi's argument.
- **אַל ("no, do not") — 1:13 and 1:16** `[T]`. Naomi's אַל בְּנֹתַי ("no, my daughters") is answered by Ruth's אַל־תִּפְגְּעִי־בִי ("do not urge me"). **The same negative particle opens the last word of each speaker's case**, which is the passage's sharpest piece of verbal mirroring.
- **גַּם … וְגַם (1:12)** — "even if … and even if", stacking an impossible hypothesis on an impossible hypothesis.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as formal Hebrew poetic parallelism** — this is narrative prose with reported speech.

Ruth's oath (1:16–17) is nonetheless built in **paired clauses**, and the pairing is the point `[T]`:

> where you go, I will go
> and where you lodge, I will lodge
> your people, my people
> and your God, my God
> where you die, I will die
> and there I will be buried

Six clauses in three pairs, each pair tighter than the last: the first pair is verbs of travel, the second drops the verb entirely (the Hebrew of עַמֵּךְ עַמִּי וֵאלֹהַיִךְ אֱלֹהָי has no verb at all), the third is death and burial `[T]`. **The grammar contracts as the commitment deepens**, and the verbless centre is the theological claim. *High confidence* on the structure; *moderate* that the contraction is deliberate.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

The narrator is unusually quiet here, and the silences are the finding.

**(a) No verdict on Orpah.** She kisses and goes, and the narrator says nothing about it `[T]`. Contrast Genesis 25:34, where a single clause ("so Esau despised his birthright") reframes an episode. Here there is no such clause. *High confidence* that the absence is deliberate, since this narrator supplies verdicts elsewhere (3:7; 4:13).

**(b) No motive for Ruth.** The narrator reports דָּבְקָה בָּהּ ("she clung to her") and then lets Ruth speak for herself `[T]`.

**(c) The one interpretive word is given to Naomi's perception, not the narrator's.** At 1:18 וַתֵּרֶא כִּי־מִתְאַמֶּצֶת הִיא ("when she saw that she was determined") — the assessment is Naomi's seeing, reported `[T]`. The narrator tells us what Naomi concluded, not what was the case.

**(d) The Greek supplies a narration the Hebrew withholds.** At 1:14 Rahlfs adds καὶ ἐπέστρεψεν εἰς τὸν λαὸν αὐτῆς ("and she returned to her people") after Orpah's kiss, and BHS records it `[T]`. **In the Hebrew, the reader learns that Orpah went back only because Naomi says so at 1:15.** That is a real difference in narration, and it is taken up under Textual Variants.

### 7. Vocabulary

**שׁוּב ("return") — nine occurrences, the book's first keyword.** See Headline 1. The distribution across speakers is the finding: Naomi uses it as an imperative four times (1:8, 1:11, 1:12, 1:15), the daughters-in-law use it once as a refusal (1:10, "we will return *with you*"), it describes Orpah once (1:15), and Ruth uses it once, to refuse it (1:16, "to turn back from following you") `[T]`. *High confidence.* **The same word means "go home to Moab" in Naomi's mouth and "abandon you" in Ruth's.**

**יְבָמָה ("sister-in-law") — twice in 1:15, and nowhere else in Ruth.** Three verses in the Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 25:7, 25:9, Ruth 1:15 `[T]`. *High confidence.* Note the **sense is not Deuteronomy's**: there the word is the widow who claims the duty from her brother-in-law; here it is the relation between the two widowed sisters-in-law. The term is the institution's term, used of a relationship the institution does not cover — which is precisely Naomi's point. See Headline 3.

**חֶסֶד ("covenant-kindness") — 1:8, the first of three in the book** (1:8, 2:20, 3:10) `[T]`. *High confidence.* All three are in characters' speech and none is the narrator's. Naomi asks YHWH to do to them what they have done to the dead and to her — **so the book's governing term is first used of two Moabite women, and first used as a description of something already done, not something hoped for.**

**מְנוּחָה ("rest", 1:9).** Twenty-two verses in the canon, so not rare. The finding is internal: it is cognate with מָנוֹחַ at 3:1, where Naomi says she will seek rest for Ruth `[T]`. The two are different lemmas and the report says *cognate*, not identical. **What Naomi prays for in a Moabite husband's house at 1:9 she sets out to find in Bethlehem at 3:1.** *High confidence* on the link; the two nouns are formally distinct and a reader of English sees one word.

**עָגַן (1:13) — a hapax legomenon in the Hebrew Bible.** One verse, this one `[T]`. *High confidence.* Rendered "refrain from marrying" (both versions); the ancient versions guess differently, Rahlfs offering κατασχεθήσεσθε ("would you be held back"). **The climax of Naomi's argument rests on a word that occurs once in Scripture**, which is worth knowing before preaching a confident paraphrase of it.

**דָּבַק ("cling", 1:14).** Fifty-two verses in the canon, so the verb alone is not rare. What is rare is its pairing: עָזַב ("leave") with דָּבַק in one verse stands in **exactly one verse, Genesis 2:24** `[T]`. See Headline 5 and Tool 11. *High confidence* on the count.

**Proper-noun inventory (Tool 7c).** Naomi, Orpah, Ruth, Moab, Judah, YHWH. Routine: Moab and Judah, established at 1:1–2 with no fresh freight here. **Orpah** is a candidate and the check returns little: the name occurs twice in the canon, both in Ruth (1:4, 1:14), and proposed etymologies are speculative — recorded as *uncertain* and not built on. **Naomi** is load-bearing but its canonical freight is supplied by the book itself at 1:20, outside this passage.

### 8. Translations

**1:13 — the divergence that changes Naomi's meaning.** The Hebrew is כִּי־מַר־לִי מְאֹד מִכֶּם. NASB95: "for it is **harder for me than for you**". ESV: "for it is exceedingly bitter to me **for your sake**" `[T]`. The preposition מִן can be comparative ("more than you") or causal ("because of you"), and the two readings give different women: one who ranks her grief above theirs, and one whose grief is on their account. **The ancient versions side with the NASB95** — Rahlfs has ἐπικράνθη μοι ὑπὲρ ὑμᾶς, comparative `[T]`. *Moderate confidence* that the comparative is right; the point for the preacher is that the ESV in the pew has made a choice, and a sympathetic reading of Naomi partly depends on it.

**1:11–12 — where the NASB95 keeps the chain and the ESV breaks it.** The Hebrew has שֹׁבְנָה ("return") at 1:8, 1:11 and 1:12, the same imperative each time. NASB95 renders all three "Return"; **ESV renders 1:8 "return" and 1:11–12 "Turn back"** `[T]`. *High confidence.* This is the reverse of the book-wide pattern, where the ESV is the more transparent: here **the study text preserves the keyword and the pulpit text splits it**, so the ESV congregation hears four "returns" where the Hebrew has six in Naomi's mouth. The preacher using the ESV should say the word is one word.

**1:16 — the same loss, mirrored.** NASB95 has "turn back from following you" for לָשׁוּב, where ESV has "to return from following you" `[T]`. So each version breaks the שׁוּב chain once, at a different point; neither congregation hears it whole. **Nobody hears Ruth refusing the exact verb Naomi commanded.**

**1:14 — a loss no English version can avoid.** דָּבְקָה ("she clung") carries the Genesis 2:24 verb; "clung" is a good rendering and the allusion still cannot be heard without the Hebrew, because the English of Genesis 2:24 is "hold fast" (ESV) or "be joined" (NASB95) `[T]`. This is translation loss, not a translation fault — category 1 of the triage.

**Pulpit divergence note.** The ESV is the declared pulpit text. **At 1:11–12 it is the weaker of the two**, splitting the chapter's keyword; at 1:13 it takes the less-supported reading of a genuine ambiguity. This is the first passage in this project's Ruth work where the NASB95 is the more transparent at more points than the ESV, and it is worth recording against the sweep's book-wide finding, which runs the other way.

**Ancient versions check.** The trigger is met at 1:13 (both moderns carry exegetical weight and they disagree — so strictly the trigger's first condition fails, but the check is run anyway because the ancient evidence resolves the disagreement). Rahlfs supports the comparative, as above. **A second, larger divergence stands at 1:14 and is category 2**: the Greek plus "and she returned to her people", treated under Textual Variants. At 1:15 Rahlfs renders יְבָמָה with ἡ σύννυμφός σου ("your fellow bride"), a term for the co-sister-in-law rather than the levirate widow — **the Greek translator read the word in the same non-Deuteronomic sense this report does** `[T]`, *moderate–high*, which is independent support for the reading at Tool 7.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The register is grief under argument.** Two scenes of loud public weeping (1:9, 1:14) bracket a speech that is almost forensic in its construction — a hypothesis, two rhetorical questions, a conclusion. **Naomi does not weep in this passage; the daughters-in-law do** `[T]`. Her own grief surfaces only as a conclusion from evidence: "the hand of the LORD has gone out against me."

Devices carrying it:
- **The idiom of loud mourning.** וַתִּשֶּׂאנָה קוֹלָן וַתִּבְכֶּינָה ("they lifted up their voices and wept"), twice `[T]`. This is public, audible grief, not private tears.
- **Impossible hypotheses.** 1:12's "even if I had a husband tonight and bore sons" asks the hearer to picture something it then withdraws `[T]`.
- **Accumulating negation.** אַל ("no"), לְבִלְתִּי ("not to"), הֲלָהֵן ("would you therefore?") — Naomi's speech is built of refusals.
- **The silence at the end.** וַתֶּחְדַּל לְדַבֵּר ("she ceased speaking") `[T]`. The passage does not end in agreement; it ends in a woman giving up the argument.

**The tonal shift is at 1:16, and it is total.** From Naomi's dense, reasoned, negative Hebrew to Ruth's short paired clauses, five of them without a conjunction between the halves. **A sermon should let the register change audibly**, because the text does.

**Soundtrack.** Two voices and a long pause: keening, then a cross-examination, then one unaccompanied line.

**Four words have both their occurrences in the book inside this passage** — נָשַׁק, בָּכָה, יְבָמָה `[T]`, and the pairs are structurally placed (Tool 3). *High confidence.*

**The pairing that matters most is שׁוּב against יָלַךְ.** Nine "returns" and seven "goes" in thirteen verses, and at 1:8 and 1:12 Naomi puts them together as a single command — לֵכְנָה שֹּׁבְנָה ("go, return") and שֹׁבְנָה … לֵכְןָ ("return … go") `[T]`. **Ruth's oath takes the two verbs apart**: אֶל־אֲשֶׁר תֵּלְכִי אֵלֵךְ ("where you go, I will go") keeps הָלַךְ and refuses שׁוּב in the same sentence (1:16). *High confidence* on the distribution; the reading that she is dismantling Naomi's phrase is `[I]`, *moderate*.

**Idea-level repetition: three dismissals, four imperatives to return, and three answers.** See Tool 3.

**Near-repetition.** 1:9's אִשָּׁה בֵּית אִישָׁהּ ("each in the house of her husband") against 1:8's אִשָּׁה לְבֵית אִמָּהּ ("each to her mother's house") `[T]` — the same construction twice in two verses, with the destination changed from mother to husband. Naomi's two blessings are a return to origin and a move to a new household, and she asks for both in consecutive breaths.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources at elevated attention:** Genesis, Deuteronomy 25, Samuel. **Candidates derived independently from this passage's own vocabulary before any source was consulted:** the visitation formula; the oath formula; the leave/cling pair; the levirate term; "her mother's house"; the kiss-and-depart scene.

#### Exodus 4:31 → Ruth 1:6 *(moderate–high on wording; the allusion reading capped)*

*Source context.* Exodus 4:29–31 closes the commissioning sequence. Moses and Aaron gather the elders, Aaron speaks the words, the signs are done, and the people believe: וַיִּשְׁמְעוּ כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה אֶת־בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל ("and they heard that YHWH had visited the sons of Israel"), and they bow and worship. The verse is the hinge between commission and confrontation, and its emotional register is relief after long silence.

*Book usage.* No earlier use in Ruth; this is the book's first external contact.

*What the measurements give.* The clause כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה אֶת as an ordered phrase stands in **three verses** — Exodus 4:31, 1 Samuel 2:21 and Ruth 1:6 `[S: audit]`, a count the claim audit established and this dig does not re-derive. Extending it by one word, כִּי־פָקַד יְהוָה אֶת־עַמּוֹ ("that YHWH had visited **his people**"), returns **Ruth 1:6 alone** `[T]`. *High confidence* on the counts.

*What it adds, and the caution.* The vocabulary is the vocabulary of divine visitation after a long silence, and in Exodus it precedes an exodus. **But Logos round 1 established that no commentator argues Ruth deliberately evokes the exodus here** — the tradition treats פָּקַד as shared covenantal idiom, grouping Ruth 1:6 with Genesis 21:1 and 50:24–25 `[S: Logos]`. The honest position: the wording is a measured three-verse contact, the allusion reading is a possibility the wording permits and the literature does not support, and it should be preached as the former. *Moderate–high* on wording; *uncertain* on deliberate allusion.

#### Genesis 2:24 → Ruth 1:14 with 1:16 and 2:11 *(moderate–high)*

*Source context.* Genesis 2:24 is the narrator's comment on the making of the woman: עַל־כֵּן יַעֲזָב־אִישׁ אֶת־אָבִיו וְאֶת־אִמּוֹ וְדָבַק בְּאִשְׁתּוֹ ("therefore a man shall leave his father and his mother and cling to his wife"). It is a general statement, not a command to a character, and it is the canon's foundational account of a kinship bond created by choice rather than birth.

*What the measurements give.* עָזַב with דָּבַק in one verse stands in **exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Genesis 2:24** `[T]`. Ruth has both verbs but never in one verse: דָּבְקָה at 1:14, לְעָזְבֵךְ at 1:16. And עָזַב with אָב ("father") and אֵם ("mother") in one verse stands in four verses — Genesis 2:24, 1 Kings 19:20, Psalm 27:10 and **Ruth 2:11** `[T]`. *High confidence* on both counts.

*Why a scan cannot see this.* The contact is spread across two verses in Ruth 1 and completed in Ruth 2, so a rare-pair scan working verse against verse is blind to it — the known limitation of that instrument. It was found by taking the passage's own vocabulary to the canon, which is what independent candidate-finding is for.

*What it adds.* **The canon's one text about leaving and clinging is reused for a woman and her mother-in-law**, with the clinging first and the leaving refused. Ruth does not leave her father and mother to cling to a husband; she clings to Naomi, refuses to leave her, and is commended at 2:11 for having left her father and mother. *Moderate–high* that the pairing is in view; *moderate* on how far the marriage register should be pressed, and the book-overview's trap 2 is the relevant warning.

#### The oath formula → Ruth 1:17 *(high confidence)*

*Source context.* The self-imprecatory oath כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה … וְכֹה יֹסִיף ("thus may God do … and thus may he add") belongs to Israel's narrative of kingship and conflict. Its users: Eli to Samuel (1 Sam 3:17); Saul twice (1 Sam 14:44; and the formula at 20:13 in Jonathan's mouth); David (1 Sam 25:22); Abner (2 Sam 3:9); David again (2 Sam 3:35; 19:14 [Eng 19:13]); Solomon (1 Kgs 2:23); Jezebel (1 Kgs 19:2); Ben-hadad (1 Kgs 20:10); Jehoram (2 Kgs 6:31).

*What the measurements give.* The full ordered phrase in Ruth's wording — כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה לִי וְכֹה יֹסִיף — matches **Ruth 1:17 and no other verse**, plain and skeletal `[T]`. The shorter כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה returns thirteen verses on the plain pass: eleven in 1 Samuel, 2 Samuel and 1–2 Kings, plus Jeremiah 5:13 and Ruth 1:17 `[T]`. *High confidence.* (The skeletal pass balloons to forty-five, because the reduced form collides with the common verb; the plain pass is the right instrument here and the skeletal noise is recorded rather than used.)

*Book usage.* No other oath in Ruth. Boaz swears at 3:13 with חַי־יְהוָה ("as YHWH lives"), a different formula `[T]`.

*What it adds, and the one distinctive feature.* **Ruth is the only speaker outside the Samuel–Kings block to use the formula** — and she is the only one to swear it by **YHWH** in the first person about herself where most users swear it against another. For a book that ends on David's name, a Moabite woman swearing in the idiom of the house of Saul and the house of David is a real anticipation `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

#### Genesis 38:11 → Ruth 1:8 *(moderate — narrative parallel, not a verbal citation)*

*Source context.* Genesis 38:11: Judah says to Tamar his כַּלָּה ("daughter-in-law") שְׁבִי אַלְמָנָה בֵית־אָבִיךְ ("remain a widow in your father's house") until Shelah grows up — intending no such thing — and Tamar goes and dwells in her father's house. The move is a bereaved parent-in-law disposing of a widowed daughter-in-law by sending her to her parental home.

*What the measurements give, and what they do not.* The two verses share כַּלָּה and the sending-home, **but not the verb**: Genesis has יָשַׁב ("remain, dwell"), Ruth has שׁוּב ("return") `[T]`. The ordered phrase אִשָּׁה לְבֵית אִמָּהּ ("each to her mother's house") returns **Ruth 1:8 alone** `[T]`. And "mother's house" rather than "father's house" is itself a choice: בַּיִת with אֵם in one verse stands in seventeen verses, among them Genesis 24:28 (Rebekah running to her mother's house in the betrothal scene) and Song of Songs 3:4 and 8:2 `[T]`. *Moderate* on the canonical register.

*What it adds.* Naomi makes Judah's move and makes it honestly — she means it, where he did not. And the book will name Judah and Tamar in its closing blessing (4:12), where the audit rates the Genesis 38 citation *high* `[S: audit]`. **The book opens by repeating the gesture of the story it ends by invoking.** *Moderate*, as a narrative parallel; the wording does not carry it and this report does not claim it does.

#### 1 Kings 19:19–21 → Ruth 1:8–16 *(moderate — narrative pattern, unbaselined)*

Elisha leaves the oxen, runs after Elijah, asks אֶשְּׁקָה־נָּא לְאָבִי וּלְאִמִּי ("let me kiss my father and my mother") and says וְאֵלְכָה אַחֲרֶיךָ ("and I will go after you"); Elijah answers לֵךְ שׁוּב ("go, return"). The elements shared with this passage: a leave-taking kiss, a declaration of following, and the imperative pair "go, return" — which Naomi uses at 1:8 as לֵכְנָה שֹּׁבְנָה and inverts at 1:12 `[T]`. One measured anchor: נָשַׁק ("kiss") with אָב and אֵם in one verse stands in **exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible, 1 Kings 19:20** `[T]`, and that verse is also one of the four carrying Ruth 2:11's leave-father-and-mother set.

**This is reported at *moderate* and no higher, as a narrative-pattern contact.** No null has been run; the shared vocabulary is partly commonplace; and the direction of dependence is undecidable. It is queued as a design claim for the next audit, under the discipline this book's audit established.

- **1:4's naming of Orpah and Ruth.** Resolved at 1:14, both of them, in one verse (Phase 0.6) `[T]`, *high*.

*Planted for later:*

- **מְנוּחָה ("rest"), 1:9 → the cognate מָנוֹחַ at 3:1**, where Naomi seeks for Ruth what she here prayed a Moabite husband would give `[T]`, *high*.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew narrative with extended reported speech; the proportion of dialogue to narration is the highest in the book. Reading rules: the speeches characterise their speakers and are not the narrator's verdict; Naomi's theology at 1:13 is Naomi's, reported accurately and not endorsed.

One sub-genre is embedded: **the oath** at 1:17, which has a fixed form and legal force. Reading it as poetry, or as a wedding vow, mistakes its genre — it is a self-imprecation of the kind sworn by kings and generals (Tool 11).

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** The judges' period, in Moab and on the road to Judah. Before the monarchy, before the temple, before the exile.

**Canonical position.** Ketuvim (Phase 0.55). **The canonical reader knows what a Moabite was**, having read Deuteronomy 23:3 [Heb 23:4], and knows that Israel's later history included both the Davidic covenant and its loss. A Moabite swearing "your God my God" reads differently to someone who has read the Prophets through to the end.

**What has happened since, that changes the reading.** The category "Moabite" has been dissolved in Christ (Ephesians 2:11–13 argues exactly this move, from "strangers to the covenants of promise" to "brought near"). The passage's drama — whether an outsider may attach herself to YHWH's people — has an answer this side of the cross that the text itself does not yet have.

**The tool's warning.** Do not read Ruth's oath as a conversion formula to be reproduced. The text gives no ritual, no circumcision analogue, no priest; what it gives is a sworn attachment to a people and their God, and the book then spends three chapters on whether Israel will receive her.

---

### 15. Who Am I?

- **Ruth** is, in this passage, **neither type nor simple example**. The redeemer typology of the book runs through Boaz, not her. What she is, is the recipient of nothing yet — she attaches herself to a woman with no prospects, and the attachment is not reciprocated with any promise.
- **Naomi** is the **character the congregation most resembles**: bereaved, correct about her circumstances, wrong about nothing in particular, and convinced God is against her.
- **Orpah** is a **foil and nothing worse**. She exists so the reader sees that Ruth's choice was a choice.
- **Where this is commonly misread as "me":** the preacher who makes Ruth the model disciple and invites the congregation to be like her. The passage will bear some of that, but the oath is sworn to a person, not to God, and its content is a people and a burial plot. **Flattening it into a general commitment to God is the trap the overview names.**

---

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author is seeking.** That the reader should see a covenant attachment made by an outsider, against advice, with nothing promised in return — and should hold that together with Naomi's verdict that God's hand has gone out against her, without resolving either prematurely.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

*Worldview.* Three things the passage asks. **Covenant loyalty can be sworn where nothing is owed.** Ruth has no legal obligation to Naomi; the one legal category available, levirate, Naomi has just declared void. **God's providence is not visible to the people inside it.** Naomi reads her circumstances accurately and concludes that YHWH is against her — at the exact moment the book's reversal has begun with the bread of 1:6. **And the outsider may belong.** The book does not argue this; it narrates it, and lets a Moabite swear by YHWH in Israel's royal oath-formula.

*Behaviour.* Stop reading 1:16–17 as a wedding text. Start asking where loyalty is owed to people who can offer nothing — which is the shape of the attachment the text actually describes.

*Motivation, and the guard against moralism.* The passage gives no command and no motive. Ruth's reason is withheld (Tool 1). **The gospel grounding is not in Ruth's resolve but in what the narrator has quietly said at 1:6** — YHWH had visited his people to give them bread, which is the only thing in this passage that God is said to have done, and neither woman knows what it will come to mean.

*Four audiences.* **For me:** am I, like Naomi, reading an accurate account of my circumstances into a verdict about God? **For a Christian friend:** the one who has concluded that God's hand is against them, and who is right about the facts. **For the church:** we are more often the people a Ruth attaches herself to than we are Ruth. **For an unbeliever:** an outsider with no claim says "your people shall be my people, and your God, my God" — and the book's whole remaining argument is whether that will be honoured.

*Prayer.* For those who are right about their circumstances and wrong about God; for loyalty where nothing is owed; and thanks that the bread had already been given before anybody knew.

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**עָגַן (1:13) — a hapax legomenon.** One occurrence in the Hebrew Bible `[T]`. *High confidence* on the count. The niphal תֵּעָגֵנָה is usually glossed "shut yourselves up, refrain", and both English versions render it "refrain from marrying"; Rahlfs guesses κατασχεθήσεσθε ("be held back"). **A lexicon is worth consulting before the verse is paraphrased confidently from the pulpit**, because the confident English conceals a word nobody can check against another occurrence. *Uncertain — consider verifying.*

**הֲלָהֵן (1:13) — twice, and disputed.** The form is usually taken as interrogative הֲ plus לָהֵן ("therefore"), an Aramaic-flavoured particle `[S]`, which is one of the items sometimes adduced in dating arguments for the book. **This report takes no position on the date**, and records the form because the preacher should know that the verse's two rhetorical questions hang on a particle whose analysis is contested. *Uncertain.*

**מִתְאַמֶּצֶת (1:18) — hithpael of אָמַץ, "showing herself determined."** The root occurs in forty-one verses of the canon, so it is not rare; the reflexive stem here is the point, and it is the narrator's one word for Ruth's state `[T]`. Note that the assessment is routed through Naomi's seeing (Tool 6).

**The verbless clause at the centre of the oath.** עַמֵּךְ עַמִּי וֵאלֹהַיִךְ אֱלֹהָי ("your people my people, and your God my God") contains **no verb at all** `[T]`. Hebrew can do this; the effect is a statement of identity rather than of intention, and it sits exactly at the centre of the six clauses (Tool 5). *High confidence* on the grammar; *moderate* on the rhetorical reading.

**The oath's apodosis is suppressed.** כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה יְהוָה לִי וְכֹה יֹסִיף ("thus may YHWH do to me and thus may he add") never says *what* he may do `[T]`. That is the form's convention throughout Samuel–Kings — the curse is gestured at and left unstated. Worth naming from the pulpit, because the English "and worse" (NASB95) or "and more also" (ESV) can sound like a vague intensifier rather than a deliberately unspoken self-curse.

### Textual Variants

#### 1:14 — the Greek plus · **category 2, and the most interesting variant in the chapter**

- **The issue.** After וַתִּשַּׁק עָרְפָּה לַחֲמוֹתָהּ ("and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law"), Rahlfs adds καὶ ἐπέστρεψεν εἰς τὸν λαὸν αὐτῆς ("and she returned to her people"). BHS records the plus and reconstructs a possible Hebrew behind it, וַתָּשָׁב אֶל־עַמָּהּ `[T]`.
- **What turns on it.** In the Greek, the narrator tells us Orpah went back. **In the Hebrew, nobody tells us** — the reader learns it from Naomi's mouth at 1:15, "your sister-in-law has gone back". That is a real difference in who vouches for the information, and it bears on Tool 6: the Hebrew narrator's refusal to report Orpah's departure is of a piece with his refusal to judge it.
- **Position.** The shorter Hebrew is likelier original, on the standard preference for the harder and shorter reading, and the plus looks like a translator or a scribe supplying the obvious. *Moderate confidence.* But the possibility that the Greek reflects a fuller *Vorlage* is not excluded, and this report does not resolve it further.
- **Preaching implication.** Small but real: do not say "the narrator tells us Orpah went home". He does not. Naomi does.

#### 1:8 — the ketiv יעשה · **category 2, trivial**

- **The issue.** The WLC prints יעשה unpointed. BHS: the Greek and the Vulgate read with the qere יַעַשׂ ("may he do") `[T]`.
- **Implication.** None for sense; a jussive against an imperfect. Do not mention in a sermon.

#### 1:13 — the preposition, not a variant

מִכֶּם is comparative or causal, and the versions divide (Tool 8). **This is a translation question, not a textual one**, and it is recorded here only so it is not mistaken for one.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** The territory of Moab, east of the Dead Sea, and the road west to Judah. A journey of perhaps fifty miles with a descent to the Jordan and a climb to the Judean hills.

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

- **What a widow without sons faced.** No independent legal standing, no inheritance of her own, and dependence on a male relative's willingness. This is the background to every line of Naomi's argument, and it is why the argument is about sons rather than about affection.
- **What Moab meant.** Not merely foreign: a neighbour with a hostile history and an explicit exclusion in Deuteronomy 23:3 [Heb 23:4]. The audience hears "Moabitess" as a legal problem, not as exoticism.
- **That returning to "her mother's house" meant returning to the marriage market.** The phrase's canonical company is betrothal material — Rebekah at Genesis 24:28, the Song of Songs twice (Tool 11). Naomi is not telling them to go home to be looked after; she is telling them to go home to be married again, which is exactly what 1:9 then prays for.
- **How an oath worked.** A self-imprecation invoked the deity as the enforcer. Ruth is not expressing strength of feeling; she is making herself legally liable before YHWH.

**What this changes about how we read.** Naomi's three dismissals are not emotional self-pity; they are a responsible older woman doing the only generous thing available to her. **She has nothing to offer them and says so**, and under the law of the day she is right. *Confidence:* the institutional background is well attested `[S]`, *high*; the specific social expectations around a Moabite widow in Israel are less well evidenced and are not pressed.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Writings. The canonical reader arrives knowing the Torah and the whole of the Prophets — so they know Deuteronomy's exclusion of Moab, they know the oath formula from Samuel and Kings, and they know the house of David rose and fell.

**The first hearers.** Israelites for whom kinship and land were the structures of survival, hearing a story in which a Moabite attaches herself to a destitute Israelite widow by an oath sworn in Israel's own royal idiom.

**Where the passage fits their world.**
- They would have followed Naomi's legal reasoning at 1:11–13 without help, and would have recognised that she names the levirate remedy in order to rule it out.
- They would have heard the oath formula of 1:17 as belonging to kings and commanders, not to foreign widows.
- They would have known that "return to your mother's house" meant remarriage, and that Naomi's blessing in 1:9 was a real kindness, not a brush-off.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** a Moabite swearing by YHWH, in the first person, with a self-curse.
- **Surprising:** that Naomi argues *against* the loyalty the book is about, three times.
- **Comforting:** 1:6 — the LORD had visited his people to give them bread. The first thing God does in this book is feed people.
- **Disturbing:** 1:13. Naomi states that the hand of YHWH has gone out against her, and the narrator lets it stand.

**What we bring that they didn't.** Romantic friendship, which turns a legal and covenantal attachment into an emotional one. Individual religious choice, which makes "your God my God" sound like a private decision rather than the attaching of a person to a people. And a reflex to defend God's reputation, which makes Naomi's verdict at 1:13 feel like something to correct rather than to report.

**Implication for the sermon.** The thing to recover is that **Naomi is not wrong about her circumstances**. Preach her as mistaken and the passage loses its nerve; preach her as accurate and unresolved, with the bread of 1:6 already given and unnoticed, and it does its own work.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**Covenant loyalty (חֶסֶד) extended by the unobligated.** The book's governing term enters at 1:8, in Naomi's mouth, describing what two Moabite women have already done. The theme's shape in Ruth is that חֶסֶד is shown by those who need not show it — which is what makes it the right category for what God does.

**The inclusion of the outsider.** Not argued here, enacted. The claim "your people my people, and your God my God" is made by a member of the one nation Deuteronomy names for exclusion, and the book neither defends it nor flags it. The Torah's own trajectory — the alien who sojourns, the stranger in the gate — is being pressed to its limit.

**Rest (מְנוּחָה / מָנוֹחַ).** Introduced here as a prayer for a Moabite husband's house (1:9) and taken up at 3:1 as Naomi's own project. The canonical register of the cognate at 3:1 is bleak — the dove finding no rest (Genesis 8:9), the curse of no rest among the nations (Deuteronomy 28:65), Judah finding no rest in exile (Lamentations 1:3) `[T]`. The theme runs from a prayer, through a scheme, to a Redeemer.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: trajectory and contrast. Typology is *not* claimed here**, and the four tests say why.

Running the tests on a proposed "Ruth as a type of Christ" reading, which preachers do attempt from this passage:
1. **Theological-category test — fails.** The proposed connection runs through *loyalty* and *self-giving*, which are virtues, not established theological categories like priesthood, kingship, sacrifice or redemption. The book's own typological category is the redeemer, and it attaches to Boaz.
2. **NT-precedent test — fails.** The NT names Ruth once, in Matthew's genealogy (Matthew 1:5) `[S]`, and makes nothing typological of her conduct.
3. **Escalation test — not applicable** once the first two fail.
4. **Authorial-pattern test — fails.** The text raises no unresolved expectation about Ruth that Christ answers; the expectation it raises is about a redeemer, and chapter 4 answers it.

**Score: passes none. Reclassify.** What the passage does carry:

**Trajectory.** The line that produces David is secured, humanly speaking, by this oath. *What the passage anticipates:* a people enlarged by an outsider's attachment, and a redeemer not yet in view. *What Christ fulfils:* the dissolution of the category that makes Ruth's oath remarkable (Ephesians 2:11–13), and the arrival of the Redeemer the book's remaining chapters reach toward.

**Contrast.** Naomi's verdict at 1:13 — the hand of YHWH gone out against her — is the passage's deepest need, and nothing in the passage answers it. It is answered at 4:14–15 by the women of Bethlehem, and ultimately by a hand that went out against someone else.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Be a Ruth — be loyal, be committed, don't be an Orpah." This is the standard sermon on this text and it is three-quarters of the way to works-righteousness. It also requires condemning Orpah, which the narrator declines to do.
- **The gospel grounding:** the only divine act in the passage is the giving of bread at 1:6, before anyone has done anything.
- **Christ as hero, not just example:** the passage's hero, if it has one, is not Ruth but the God who had already visited his people while Naomi was concluding he had not.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 1:13 — "the hand of the LORD has gone out against me"

- **Category:** pastoral and doctrinal, and the overview's trap 4.
- **The difficulty:** Naomi attributes her bereavements directly to YHWH, and the narrator neither endorses nor corrects her `[T]`.
- **Common misreadings:** (a) correcting her theology on the spot — "of course God wasn't against her"; (b) endorsing it as the book's teaching about providence.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** She is right that YHWH is sovereign over what has happened; the text gives no other agent. She is wrong about what it means, and the book — not the preacher — supplies the correction, at 4:14–17, in the mouths of the women who told her at 1:19 that she had come back. **The right pulpit move is to let her say it**, and to point out that 1:6 has already contradicted her conclusion without her knowing it.

#### 1:16–17 — the wedding-text problem

- **Category:** popular misuse; the overview's trap 2.
- **The difficulty:** the most quoted lines in the book are routinely read at weddings.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The words are spoken by a widowed daughter-in-law to a widowed mother-in-law who has three times told her to go away; they include a burial clause and a self-curse; and the oath formula belongs to kings and commanders. **None of that makes them unusable at a wedding**, but a preacher who uses them there should know he is transferring an oath of covenant loyalty out of its situation, and should say so.

#### Orpah

- **Category:** pastoral, and a landmine.
- **The difficulty:** sermons routinely make Orpah the negative example.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** She obeys a three-times-repeated instruction from her mother-in-law, weeps twice, and kisses her goodbye. **The narrator passes no verdict and the Hebrew does not even report her departure** (Textual Variants). A congregation contains people who went home; preaching Orpah as the failure will land on them, and the text gives no warrant for it.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: making Orpah the villain

- **What it looks like:** "Two women faced the same choice. One went back to her gods. Which will you be?"
- **Why it's wrong:** Orpah does what Naomi three times told her to do. The narrator passes no verdict on her, and the Hebrew does not even report her departure — Naomi reports it. Condemning her requires a judgement the text declines to make, and it will land badly on anyone in the congregation who went home.
- **The corrective:** preach the division of 1:14 as the text gives it — two women, the same gesture, different outcomes, no verdict. Ruth's choice is remarkable because it was a real choice, which Orpah's existence establishes.

### Pitfall: 1:16–17 as a wedding text

- **What it looks like:** the lines read at a marriage service as a model of mutual commitment.
- **Why it's wrong:** it is an oath sworn by a destitute widow to another widow who has just told her to leave, it contains a burial clause, and its formula is the self-imprecation of kings and generals in Samuel–Kings. The commitment is unilateral and nothing is promised in return.
- **The corrective:** if the lines are used at a wedding, say where they come from. In a sermon on Ruth, preach them as covenant loyalty sworn where nothing is owed — which is both stranger and more useful than romantic commitment.

### Pitfall: correcting Naomi before the book does

- **What it looks like:** "Naomi was wrong — God wasn't against her, he was working it all out."
- **Why it's wrong:** she is right that YHWH is behind what has happened; the text names no other agent. The narrator lets her verdict stand, and the book's answer comes at 4:14–17 from the women of Bethlehem, not from a corrective aside.
- **The corrective:** let her say it. Then point at 1:6, where the bread has already been given, and note that she does not know what it will come to mean. That is the passage's own structure and it preaches.

### Pitfall: supplying Ruth's motive

- **What it looks like:** "Ruth had come to believe in the God of Israel through Naomi's witness."
- **Why it's wrong:** the text gives no motive. This narrator reports inner states when he wants to (3:7), and here reports none.
- **The corrective:** preach the oath, which is what we are given, and say plainly that the reason is withheld. The withholding is itself a finding.

### Pitfall: preaching an unbaselined pattern

- **What it looks like:** building a point on the 1 Kings 19 parallel, or on the chiasm of kiss and weep, as though either were established.
- **Why it's wrong:** both are flagged in this report as unbaselined, and this book's claim audit tested eight pattern claims and all eight failed.
- **The corrective:** use the measured contacts — the oath formula, the levirate term, the Genesis 2:24 pair. They are the ones that will hold.
