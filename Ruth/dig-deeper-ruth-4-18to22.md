## Headline Findings

1. **The book's single paragraph division falls at the head of this unit, and four solo digs were needed to see how unusual that is.** The WLC carries **exactly one paragraph marker in the whole of Ruth — a petuchah after 4:17** `[T]`. The 1:1–5, 2:17–23 and 3:10–18 runs each reported, as a limitation on their structural findings, that Leningrad marks no break at their boundaries — none at 1:5/1:6, none anywhere in chapter 2, none anywhere in chapter 3 `[S: the three digs]`. **This is the one seam in eighty-five verses that the manuscript itself marks**, and it sets off the genealogy from the story. Surfaced by: Structure, Context, Genre, Author's Purpose. *High confidence* on the datum. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` still applies — this is Leningrad, and paragraphing is a reading tradition, not authorial `[S]` — but here the caveat limits a positive finding rather than excusing an absence.

2. **The book's last ten verses run one root through three voices, and the study text flattens the last into the one before it.** יָלַד occurs in 4:13 and 4:15 as **qal with Ruth as subject** (וַתֵּלֶד, "and she bore"; יְלָדַתּוּ, "she bore him"), at 4:17 as a **passive with no agent at all** (יֻלַּד, "a son has been born"), and nine times in 4:18–22 as **hiphil with nine men as subject** (הוֹלִיד, "fathered") `[T]`, every form checked in the index. **Agency moves from Ruth, to nobody, to a line of men.** And the NASB95 renders all nine hiphils as passives — "to Perez was born Hezron" — using the same English construction it uses for 4:17's genuine passive, so the third stage disappears `[T]`. The ESV's "fathered" keeps it. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Structure, Repetition, Translations, Original Language Observations. *High confidence.* **New to this run.**

3. **Ruth 4:19–22a is verbatim 1 Chronicles 2:10–12, and the two clauses that are Ruth's own are precisely the ones that make this a line from Perez to David.** Four clauses match as ordered phrases, plain: וְרָם הוֹלִיד אֶת־עַמִּינָדָב and וְעַמִּינָדָב הוֹלִיד אֶת־נַחְשׁוֹן (Ruth 4:19, 4:20 = 1 Chr 2:10), וּבֹעַז הוֹלִיד אֶת־עוֹבֵד (Ruth 4:21 = 1 Chr 2:12), and וְעוֹבֵד הוֹלִיד אֶת־יִשָׁי (1 Chr 2:12, matching Ruth 4:22 on the skeletal pass, the difference being Obed's spelling) `[T]`. **Ruth's first clause is unique to Ruth** — פֶּרֶץ הוֹלִיד אֶת־חֶצְרוֹן, where **Chronicles gives Perez two sons, Hezron and Hamul** (1 Chr 2:5) `[T]`. **And Ruth's last clause is unique to Ruth** — וְיִשַׁי הוֹלִיד אֶת־דָּוִד, where **Chronicles abandons the formula at 2:13 and ranks Jesse's seven sons instead** `[T]`. Surfaced by: Structure, Quotation/Allusion, Textual Variants, Bible Timeline. *High confidence* on every reading; **the direction of dependence stays open** `[S: overview]`. **New to this run.**

4. **Matthew names, in exactly this stretch, the three women Ruth's own genealogy omits — including Ruth.** Matthew 1:3–6 covers Perez to David and inserts **Tamar** (ἐκ τῆς Θαμάρ, 1:3), **Rahab** and **Ruth** (ἐκ τῆς Ῥαχάβ… ἐκ τῆς Ῥούθ, 1:5) `[T]`, verified in the SBLGNT. **Ruth 4:18–22 names no woman** `[T]`, and Ruth 4:12 had named Tamar seven verses earlier `[T]`. Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion, Vocabulary (the proper-noun inventory), Christological Reading, Who Am I?. *High confidence* on the data; *moderate–high* on the reading. **This is the sharpest available form of the sweep's pattern-break absence, and it is new.**

5. **BHS's conjecture at 4:19 is answered by the Greek itself.** BHS proposes reading אֲרָם for MT's רָם, citing the Greek and Matthew 1:3–4 `[T]`. But **both printed Greek editions of 1 Chronicles 2:9 carry a doublet** — ὁ Ῥάμ *and* Ἀράμ listed as two of Hezron's sons, where the Hebrew has one `[T]`. So **Ἀράμ is a Greek-side alternative transliteration of רָם, not a witness to a Hebrew אֲרָם** `[I]`. And both printed Greek editions of **Ruth** 4:19 read Ἀρράν, not Ἀράμ `[T]`. Surfaced by: Translations, Textual Variants, Quotation/Allusion. *Moderate–high confidence* — the doublet is certain, the inference from it is short, and the honest limit is that no critical apparatus to Chronicles is in the corpus. **New to this run, and it bears on an item BHS puts in the margin of the book's last page.**

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** Taken from the overview rather than re-derived: to show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and to give Israel David as the fruit `[I: overview]`.

**Passage level.** This unit's contribution is to convert a story into a claim about a line, and to do it in a form that cannot be read as anything else. The narrative has already said the thing twice — "a son has been born to Naomi" and "he is the father of Jesse, the father of David" (4:17) `[T]`. **The genealogy does not add information; it changes genre** `[I]`. A story about kindness could be an edifying episode; a list of begettings from Perez to David is a genealogical assertion, and the manuscript sets it apart as such with the book's only paragraph marker `[T]`.

A second contribution, and the one the sweep did not see. **The unit completes a grammatical movement that runs across the book's last ten verses.** The root יָלַד appears in the qal with Ruth as subject (4:13, 4:15), in the passive with no subject at all (4:17), and in the hiphil nine times with nine men as subject (4:18–22) `[T]`. The story's climax gives the bearing to Ruth; the neighbours' naming removes the agent entirely; the genealogy assigns it to a male line. **The book does not choose between these; it prints all three in sequence, and the sequence is the argument** — the child is Ruth's, and nobody's, and the line's `[I]`. *Moderate–high confidence* on the reading; the forms are certain.

**Choice and pacing.** The narrator has spent eighty verses on about a year and five verses on roughly four centuries `[T]`. That is the sharpest acceleration in the book, and it is the mirror of its opening, where a decade and three deaths were compressed into five verses `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 4:13–17 has done three things this unit requires.

*First, it has produced the child and attributed the conception to YHWH in the narrator's own voice* — וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן (4:13), **the only act the narrator ascribes to God in his own voice in eighty-five verses** `[T]`. *Second, it has named the child and placed him* — "they named him Obed; he is the father of Jesse, the father of David" (4:17) `[T]`. *Third, it has ended the story on David's name without explanation* `[T]`, and a reader of the Writings knows whose name that is `[S]`.

So the narrative has already asserted the genealogical point. **The question Phase 0.6 has to answer is therefore not what the genealogy adds but why a book that has already said it says it again in another form.**

**Necessity answer — why here, and not elsewhere.** Three reasons the text supplies.

First, **a claim about a line is proved by a list, not by a story** `[I]`. 4:17's "he is the father of Jesse, the father of David" is a two-step assertion by the narrator; 4:18–22 is a nine-step formal chain anchored in a name the Torah knows and the Chronicler's list repeats verbatim (Tool 11). The second is a different kind of statement, and the manuscript's paragraph marker says the tradition read it as one `[T]`.

Second, **the heading reaches back past the whole book.** וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת פָּרֶץ begins not from Elimelech, not from Boaz, but from **Perez** — the name Ruth 4:12 had just put in the elders' mouths, "like the house of Perez, whom Tamar bore to Judah" `[T]`. The genealogy therefore starts where the blessing of seven verses earlier ended, and it starts outside the book's own story altogether, in Genesis 38 `[I]`. **The unit exists here because the book's claim is not about a household but about a line that was already in jeopardy two hundred years before Elimelech left Bethlehem.**

Third, **the last word has to be David.** 4:17 ends on David and 4:22 ends on David `[T]` — the book's final word in both its narrative and its list. 1:1 set the story בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים, "in the days when the judges judged", a clause unique in the Hebrew Bible and one that dates by an office with no name in it `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`. **A period named by the absence of a king is answered by a king's name, twice** `[I]`. *Moderate confidence* — the inference is short and the data are certain.

**Implication for the passage.** The genealogy is not an appendix and it is not a postscript by a later hand as far as this report can tell from the text. **It is the book's second ending, in a second genre**, and it is the only part of the book the manuscript sets off as a unit. A sermon that stops at 4:17 has stopped before the book's own last word on what the story was for.

#### General contextual notes

**Immediately before:** 4:13–17, the birth, the naming, and the women's blessing. **Immediately after:** nothing — the book ends `[T]`. In BHS order the next book is Song of Songs, and no lexical handoff is claimed `[S: overview]`; in the English and Greek order the next book is 1 Samuel, where Jesse is introduced as an Ephrathite of Bethlehem in Judah (1 Sam 17:12), a triple occurring in only two verses of the Hebrew Bible, the other being Ruth 1:2 `[S: overview; 1:1–5 dig]`.

**Section:** the arc map's seventh and last movement, "The line" `[S: overview]`.

**The boundary here is the one boundary in the book that is not editorial.** The overview divides 4:13–17 / 4:18–22 and directs that they be preached together `[S: overview]`. **That division coincides exactly with the manuscript's petuchah** `[T]` — which is not true of any other preaching-unit boundary in the book (Tool 3). So the overview's division is right, and its direction to preach the two together is a homiletic judgement rather than a literary one: **the text marks a seam here and the overview proposes to preach across it.** That is defensible — the genealogy is thin on its own — but it should be a decision taken knowingly.

**Read out of context?** The unit is more often skipped than misread. Where it is read, 4:22 is quoted as a devotional flourish about God's providence reaching to David, with the four centuries and the schematic compression (Tool 14) passed over.

### 3. Structure

A heading and nine begettings in one invariable formula.

| Verse | Content | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 17b (**פ**) | The manuscript's paragraph marker | **The book's only structural division as Leningrad prints it** `[T]` |
| 18a | וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת פָּרֶץ | The heading, in the Pentateuch's own formula |
| 18b–22 | Nine × *X* הוֹלִיד אֶת־*Y* | The chain: Perez → Hezron → Ram → Amminadab → Nahshon → Salmah → Boaz → Obed → Jesse → David |

**Device: an unvaried formula, and the unvariedness is the finding.**

**Nine identical constructions, with no expansion anywhere** `[T]`. There is no "and he lived *n* years", no comment, no aside, no mother named, no event attached. **This matters because it is the ground on which the claim audit withdrew the book's "ten" design** `[S: audit, R1]`: Genesis 5 marks its seventh from Adam with an expanded entry, and **Ruth's list gives Boaz's seventh position no formal marking of any kind** `[T]`. The flatness is uniform.

**The two irregularities are both orthographic, and both are in the names.**

- **שַׂלְמָה at 4:20 against שַׂלְמוֹן at 4:21** `[T]` — the same ancestor, two forms, in consecutive verses, and the WLC's index files them as two different entries `[S: sweep]`. **1 Chronicles 2:11 gives a third form, שַׂלְמָא** `[T]`.
- **עוֹבֵד at 4:21 against עֹבֵד at 4:22** `[T]` — plene and defective in consecutive verses. **New to this run**, and found because an ordered-phrase search for Ruth 4:22a matched 1 Chr 2:12 only on the skeletal pass (Declaration).

So **two of the ten names are spelled two ways each, inside three verses** `[T]`. *High confidence* on the data. The reading is `[I]` and modest: a formally invariable list carries two orthographic wobbles, which tells against the list having been composed as a tightly controlled artefact and is consistent with its having been copied from, or alongside, another list `[I]`. *Moderate*, and no stronger — spelling variation of names is common.

**No chiasm, inclusio or sandwich is claimed.** The one framing observation worth making is handled under Move 4: **the unit, like the book's opening five verses, contains no divine name** `[T]`.

### 4. Linking Words

**Effectively none, and the absence is total enough to be worth stating.**

The unit contains **no causal, conditional, purposive, adversative or inferential connector of any kind** `[T]`. Its entire syntactic furniture is the conjunctive waw on each new subject (וְחֶצְרוֹן, וְרָם, וְעַמִּינָדָב…) and the object marker אֶת before each son `[T]`. There is not one כִּי, not one לָכֵן, not one אִם.

**The pattern that makes this admissible as a finding is the book's own, and it has been established twice already.** The 1:1–5 dig found the same total absence in the book's opening five verses, against two כִּי clauses in 1:6 alone and nine more in chapter 1 `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`. **So the book's first unit and its last unit are the two places where the narrator reasons about nothing** `[T]`. *Moderate confidence* on the significance — the ceiling the pattern-break rule sets for an absence claim — and *high* on the datum.

→ The only logical content in the unit is sequence: *this one, then that one, then that one*, nine times. **A genealogy's argument is its order**, and order is carried here by nothing but the waw.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**N/A in the strict sense — and the N/A is itself the observation.**

There is no authorial intrusion in these five verses because **there is no narrative for the narrator to intrude into** `[T]`. The unit is a list; it has no action to interpret, no character whose inner state could be reported, and no event on which a verdict could be passed.

What is worth recording is the **contrast with the verse immediately before the paragraph marker.** 4:13 carries the one divine act the narrator states in his own voice in the whole book — וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה לָהּ הֵרָיוֹן `[T]` — and 4:17 carries the neighbours' naming and the narrator's own two-step placement of the child `[T]`. **Then the marker, and then five verses in which the narrator says nothing about anything** `[T]`. The book's most theologically explicit sentence and its most formally silent unit are eight verses apart, with the manuscript's only division between them `[I]`. *Moderate confidence* on the significance.

The claim audit's discard of a "narratorial cluster" as a design claim (R28) stands and is not reopened `[S: audit]`.

### 7. Vocabulary

#### Proper-noun inventory (run before triage)

**Ten personal names**, and nothing else: פָּרֶץ, חֶצְרוֹן, רָם, עַמִּינָדָב, נַחְשׁוֹן, שַׂלְמָה/שַׂלְמוֹן, בֹּעַז, עוֹבֵד/עֹבֵד, יִשַׁי, דָּוִד `[T]`. **No place name, no gentilic, no number, and no divine name** `[T]`. **And no woman** `[T]` — see Tool 11.

Triage: **Perez** gets the full name-hyperlink check, being the heading's name and the one the canon itself glosses. **Nahshon** gets the check because he is datable and the dating is a finding. **David** gets one line: the book's last word, twice (4:17, 4:22) `[T]`, and the name on which the whole list converges. **Boaz** needs no check here — the book has supplied his dossier. **Hezron, Ram, Amminadab, Salmah, Obed, Jesse** are inventory: no canonical freight beyond their place in this and the Chronicles list, and no outstanding word attaches to any of them. **The outstanding-word check returns nil for all ten.**

#### (a) הוֹלִיד — nine hiphils, and the three-voice movement

**The unit's central lexical fact, and the spine of Headline 2.**

יָלַד appears in the book's last ten verses in **three different stems**, every form checked in the WLC index `[T]`:

| Verse | Form | Stem | Subject |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4:13 | וַתֵּלֶד | **qal** | Ruth — "and she bore a son" |
| 4:15 | יְלָדַתּוּ | **qal** | Ruth — "she has borne him" (in the women's mouths) |
| 4:17 | יֻלַּד | **passive (pual)** | **none** — "a son has been born to Naomi" |
| 4:18–22 | הוֹלִיד ×9 | **hiphil** | nine men — "X fathered Y" |

*High confidence* on all twelve forms.

**What this is, and what it is not.** It is not a wordplay and it is not a chain in the usual sense: it is one root carried through the three voices Hebrew has for the same event, in sequence, at the book's close `[T]`. The hiphil of יָלַד is the standard genealogical form and carries no special emphasis on its own `[S]`; **what carries the finding is the sequence and the company** — a qal with a woman as subject, a passive with no subject, and then nine hiphils with men `[I]`. *Moderate–high* on the reading.

**And the pulpit consequence is real**: see Tool 8. The NASB95 converts all nine hiphils into passives, so that 4:17 ("a son has been born") and 4:18 ("to Perez was born") read as the same construction in English when they are two different stems in Hebrew `[T]`.

**A limit, stated.** This is an observation about the whole of 4:13–22, not about 4:18–22 alone, and it is only visible once both units are in view. The overview's direction to preach 4:13–17 and 4:18–22 together `[S: overview]` is therefore supported by this finding, which is one argument for crossing the manuscript's own seam.

#### (b) תּוֹלְדוֹת at 4:18 — the Pentateuch's heading, differently spelled

**The claim audit's R6 row, independently reproduced and confirmed in every particular.**

**תּוֹלְדוֹת occurs 39 times in 39 verses of the Hebrew Bible: Genesis 13, Numbers 13, 1 Chronicles 9, Exodus 3, and Ruth 1** `[T]`. *High confidence* — reproduced here by lemma, matching the sweep exactly.

**The ordered phrase וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת matches at Ruth 4:18 alone in the plain pass; the skeletal pass adds eleven verses — Gen 2:4; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10; 11:27; 25:12; 25:19; 36:1; 36:9; 37:2 and Num 3:1** `[T]`. **The difference between the passes is spelling, not wording: Genesis spells the noun defectively (תּוֹלְדֹת) and Ruth spells it plene (תּוֹלְדוֹת)** `[T]`. *High confidence.*

**This is also the one place in four digs where the skeletal pass has done exactly the job it exists for.** The three preceding runs each caught it producing false positives — אַחֲרֵי collapsing into אַחֵר, כְּאַפִּי into כְּאֵיפָה, מִי אַתָּה into מִי אַתְּ, and רֹקֵם into רֵיקָם `[S: the three digs]`. **Here the pass recovers a genuine plene/defective variation of the same word and the result is a real finding.** Worth recording so that the queued amendment is not read as a case against the pass.

**The row must say *the same formula, differently spelled*, and not "verbatim"** — the audit's own correction, and it holds `[S: audit, R6]`. The audit also withdrew the stronger form of the claim ("the only book outside the Torah to use it"), since 1 Chr 1:29 has אֵלֶּה תֹּלְדוֹתָם as a heading and 1 Chronicles has nine occurrences of the noun `[S: audit, R6]`. **What is distinctive to Ruth is the combination of וְאֵלֶּה with the fully plene noun and a named ancestor** `[S: audit]`.

**And Campbell's observation, which the sweep did not have, is the strongest form of the finding** — in Genesis no genealogy of this type ever functions as a *colophon* to a story, so Ruth's use of the formula is structurally unique and its significance is hard to assess `[S: audit, L8]`. That is an honest place to leave it.

#### (c) פָּרֶץ — the one name the canon glosses

The name-hyperlink check, with the source narrative read.

**Perez's first canonical occurrence is his birth, and the text supplies the etymology.** Gen 38:29: as the midwife's thread is drawn back, "behold, his brother came out, and she said, **מַה־פָּרַצְתָּ** ('what a breach you have made!') — and he called his name **פָּרֶץ**" `[T]`. The name is a wordplay on פָּרַץ, "to break through, breach", and **the narrator of Genesis makes it himself** `[T]`.

**Two things follow.**

**(i) The genealogy's head is the one name in the list whose meaning the canon states.** None of the other nine is glossed anywhere `[T]`, and the 1:1–5 dig found that the book's own personal names — Elimelech, Naomi, Mahlon, Chilion, Orpah, Ruth — are glossed only in Naomi's case, at 1:20 `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`. *High confidence* on the asymmetry.

**(ii) The story the name carries is the one the elders had just cited.** Ruth 4:12: "like the house of **Perez**, whom **Tamar** bore to **Judah**" `[T]` — the book's one explicit naming of a prior narrative. So the heading at 4:18 picks up, as its starting point, the only story the book openly cites `[I]`. *Moderate–high confidence*: the citation at 4:12 is `[T]`, and that 4:18 picks it up deliberately is a short inference from adjacency and from the name.

**What the source narrative contributes.** Genesis 38 is a levirate story that goes wrong and is put right irregularly: a widow denied the levir's duty, a disguise, a transaction at a roadside, twins, and a line continued by a route nobody would have chosen `[T]`. **Ruth 4:18 begins its line from the child of that episode** `[I]`. *Moderate–high* — the overview rates the Genesis 38 link *high* for the story being invoked and *moderate* for any thematic parallel `[S: overview, row 5]`, and nothing here changes those ratings.

#### (d) נַחְשׁוֹן — the name that dates the list

**Nahshon occurs in nine verses of the Hebrew Bible: Exod 6:23; Num 1:7; 2:3; 7:12; 7:17; 10:14; Ruth 4:20; 1 Chr 2:10; 2:11** `[T]`. *High confidence.*

**Six of the nine are wilderness-generation texts in which he is the chief of Judah.** Num 1:7, "for Judah, Nahshon the son of Amminadab" `[T]`; Num 2:3 and 10:14, Judah's standard-bearer on the march; Num 7:12 and 7:17, the first of the twelve tribal leaders to bring his offering at the tabernacle's dedication `[T]`. Exod 6:23 makes him Aaron's brother-in-law `[T]`.

**So the list carries one datable anchor, and the dating produces a finding.** Nahshon belongs to the exodus generation; Boaz is his grandson in this list (Nahshon → Salmah → Boaz) `[T]`; and Boaz lives in the judges' period, which 1:1 names `[T]`. **Two generations therefore span the conquest and the whole judges era** `[I]`.

**That makes the list demonstrably schematic rather than complete**, and it is the honest basis on which to resist pressing its arithmetic. *Moderate–high confidence*: the anchor is `[T]`, the length of the judges' period is a chronological question the text does not settle `[S]`, and the conclusion — that generations are omitted — follows on any reckoning. **This is also the strongest available argument against reading design into the count of ten** (Tool 14 and R1): a list that omits generations is not counting them.

#### (e) The two double-spelled names

Reported under Tool 3 and verified there: **שַׂלְמָה / שַׂלְמוֹן** at 4:20 / 4:21, with **שַׂלְמָא** as a third form at 1 Chr 2:11 `[T]`; and **עוֹבֵד / עֹבֵד** at 4:21 / 4:22, plene and defective `[T]`.

**All three English versions level both.** "Salmon" twice and "Obed" twice in NASB95, ESV and NIV84 `[T]`, so no congregation in any of the three will hear either `[T]`. Nothing exegetical turns on it; it is reported because it is the unit's only formal irregularity and because the Obed pair is new.

#### (f) What is absent, and why it is admissible

**No divine name, no place name, no number, no woman** `[T]`.

Three of those four are unremarkable in a genealogy. **The fourth is a pattern-break absence of the admissible kind, and the pattern is the book's own, established seven verses earlier.** At 4:11–12 the elders ground the house's future on three named women — Rachel, Leah and Tamar — and the narrative has run on two more, Naomi and Ruth `[T]`. **The genealogy that follows names none of the five** `[T]`. The hard gate is met: the pattern is citable, at 4:11–12, in the immediately preceding unit `[T]`. *Moderate confidence*, which is the ceiling the rule sets for an absence claim, and the reading — that the list is deliberately formal where the blessing was personal — is `[I]` and no higher `[S: sweep, finding 5]`.

**And the comparison that makes it sharp is in the New Testament, not the Old** — see Tool 11, Headline 4.

**The absent divine name is a different kind of observation and belongs to Move 4**, because its pair is at the other end of the book.

**The 4:19 item deserves setting out, because BHS puts a conjecture in the margin of the book's last page.**

- **The issue.** MT reads רָם ("Ram"). Both printed Greek editions of **Ruth** read Ἀρράν `[T]`. BHS reports that the majority Greek witness to Ruth reads Ἀράμ, and **proposes emending the Hebrew to אֲרָם, citing Matthew 1:3–4** `[T]`. Matthew does read Ἀράμ `[T]`, verified in the SBLGNT.
- **The evidence that answers it.** **Both printed Greek editions of 1 Chronicles 2:9 carry a doublet**: Swete has "ὁ Ἰραμεὴλ καὶ ὁ **Ῥὰμ** καὶ ὁ Χαβὲλ καὶ **Ἀράμ**" and Rahlfs "ὁ Ιραμεηλ καὶ ὁ **Ραμ** καὶ ὁ Χαλεβ καὶ **Αραμ**" — **four sons where the Hebrew has three (Jerahmeel, Ram, Chelubai), with Ῥάμ and Ἀράμ both standing** `[T]`. Supporting this: the **Hebrew** tradition reads רָם at Ruth 4:19, 1 Chr 2:9 and 1 Chr 2:10 `[T]`, so there is no Hebrew witness to the longer name at all in either list.

The conclusion is that **BHS's conjecture rests on a Greek form whose own tradition shows it to be a variant transliteration** — which is an argument against the emendation, not a demonstration that it is impossible. **Recommend a library pass before the finding is relied on**, and it is queued.
- **Preaching implication: none directly**, and the preacher should not raise it. **The useful point is the one that stands regardless**: Matthew's list says Ἀράμ, and that is what the New Testament's genealogy of Christ carries (category 3).

**No KJV comparison is offered**; no KJV export is in the corpus.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Register: the register of a public record. Deliberately without feeling.**

The unit has no adjectives, no adverbs, no verbs but one, and no clause that is not the same clause `[T]`. After a chapter containing a legal transaction, a wedding, a birth, a women's blessing and a child in a grandmother's lap, the book ends in the voice of a register office.

What carries it:

- **Nine identical formulas with no expansion** `[T]`. The flatness is total, and it is the strongest evidence against the design readings the audit withdrew (Tool 3).
- **The paragraph marker.** The manuscript's one division falls immediately before this, so the tonal shift is marked in the text as transmitted `[T]`.
- **The absence of every connective** (Tool 4) `[T]`. Nothing is explained, because a list does not explain.
- **The absence of the divine name** `[T]`, in a unit that follows eight verses after the only sentence in the book where the narrator says God did something.
- **Two names wobbling in their spelling** (Tool 7e) `[T]` — the one place the formality is imperfect.

**Soundtrack.** None. If anything, the sound of a page being read aloud without inflection. The right instruction is to read it flat and let the last word land.

**The preacher's note, and it matters for this unit more than most.** **The temptation is to supply warmth the text refuses, and the text refuses it on purpose** — it has just spent four verses on the women of the neighbourhood and a child in Naomi's lap `[T]`. The genealogy's coldness is the book's way of saying that the claim does not depend on how the story made anyone feel. **Read it flat; the last word is "David", and it will do its own work.**

**The Pentateuchal toledot formula → Ruth 4:18** *(high confidence on the formula; the significance honestly unresolved)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* The formula heads the Pentateuch's structural divisions: the heavens and the earth (Gen 2:4), Noah (6:9), Noah's sons (10:1), Shem (11:10), **Terah (11:27)**, Ishmael (25:12), **Isaac (25:19)**, Esau (36:1, 36:9), Jacob (37:2), and Aaron and Moses (Num 3:1) `[T]`. Its function in Genesis is to open a section and to hand the narrative from one generation to the next.

**Two of the eleven are the specific models**, because they alone continue as Ruth does — *heading, then the same name repeated, then "fathered"*: **Gen 11:27** (וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדֹת תֶּרַח תֶּרַח הוֹלִיד אֶת־אַבְרָם) and **Gen 25:19** `[S: audit, R6]`. Ruth 4:18 has exactly that shape `[T]`.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Genesis is used by **name** at 4:12 (Perez, Tamar, Judah) and 4:11 (Rachel, Leah, Ephrathah), by **formula** here, and by **wording** at 1:1 (the famine clause, three verses canon-wide), 2:11 (Gen 12:1, two verses) and 2:20 (Gen 24:27, two verses) `[S: the digs and the overview]`. **This is the sixth and last Genesis contact, and it is the only one that is formal rather than lexical.** The sweep was right that it is the hardest evidence the book offers for Genesis as a live source `[S: sweep]`.

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* The toledot formula is already a structuring device across Genesis and into Numbers before Ruth reaches it `[T]`; Ruth does not put it in conversation with anything, it adopts it.

*What it adds, with the significance left where the evidence leaves it.* The book ends by taking the Pentateuch's own section-heading and applying it to a line that runs to David `[T]`. **What that means is genuinely hard to assess**, and Campbell's observation is the strongest form of the difficulty: **in Genesis no genealogy of this type ever functions as a colophon to a story** `[S: audit, L8]`. So Ruth's use is structurally unique, and this report does not resolve what it implies. *High* on the formula; **the significance is an open question and is listed as one.**

### 12. Genre

**A genealogy embedded as a colophon — and the genre question here is not routine, because this is the one place in the book where the genre changes.**

The reading rules for a genealogy: it is **theological architecture, not merely data** — note who is in, who is out, where it begins, where it ends, and what is emphasised `[S]`. Applied here, every one of those produces something: it begins outside the book (Perez), it ends on David, it includes one of the book's own characters and excludes the other six, and it emphasises nothing at all, having no expansion anywhere `[T]`.

**Two rules earn their place.**

**First, a schematic genealogy is not a chronology.** Nahshon's datable position makes the omission of generations demonstrable (Tool 7d) `[T]`. **A sermon that counts the ten names as ten generations from Perez to David has read a register as a census.**

**Second, and this is the unusual one: the genre change is itself the book's argument** (Tool 1). Hebrew narrative does not normally end in a list, and **Campbell's point is that in Genesis no genealogy of this type ever functions as a colophon to a story** `[S: audit, L8]`. So the genre shift at 4:18 is not a convention being followed but, as far as the canon shows, a convention being used in a way the canon does not otherwise use it. **That is a genre observation with real weight, and its significance is the open question listed below.**

### 13. Copycat

**N/A — there are no narrative characters in this unit to imitate or not.**

The one line worth adding, because it is the pitfall this unit most often produces: **the names are not examples.** Nine of the ten do nothing in the list but father a son, and the tenth does nothing at all `[T]`. **A sermon that supplies character material for Hezron, Ram, Amminadab or Salmah is inventing it** — the canon says nothing about any of them except that Nahshon led Judah in the wilderness (Tool 7d) `[T]`.

The three characters this book has spent four chapters on — Naomi, Ruth and Boaz — are either absent from the list or present in it without comment (Tool 7f) `[T]`.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** The list spans from **Perez**, in the patriarchal period (Gen 38), to **David**, at the founding of the monarchy `[T]` — on any chronology something over four centuries, through the descent into Egypt, the exodus, the conquest and the whole judges era.

**And the list is demonstrably schematic.** Nahshon is the Judahite chief of the wilderness generation, named six times in Exodus and Numbers `[T]`; Boaz is his grandson in this list `[T]`; Boaz lives in the judges' period `[T]`. **Two generations therefore cover the conquest and the judges, which no reckoning of that period can accommodate** `[I]`. *Moderate–high confidence.* Generations are omitted, as they are in other biblical genealogies `[S]`, and **this is the firmest argument against pressing the count** (R1).

**Where the book sits — canonical position, and this unit is where it tells.**

Ruth stands in the **Ketuvim**, fourth in the sequence BHS prints `[T]`; English Bibles and the Greek tradition place it between Judges and 1 Samuel `[S]`. **The Proverbs-adjacency argument is withdrawn and may not be preached** `[S: Logos L1]`.

**Three things about canonical position bear specifically on this unit.**

*First, the handoff is real in one order and absent in the other.* In the English and Greek sequence the genealogy hands the reader to 1 Samuel and to Jesse the Ephrathite `[S: overview]`. In BHS order it hands the reader to Song of Songs, and no link is claimed `[S: overview]`. **The genealogy reads as a hinge in one arrangement and as a terminus in the other**, and a preacher should know which he is standing in.

*Second, the parallel list is in the book that closes the canon.* BHS ends with Chronicles `[T]`, whose second chapter carries this list verbatim through its middle (Tool 11). **One honest caveat the corpus itself flags: Codex Leningradensis puts Chronicles *first* among the Writings, so "Chronicles closes the canon" rests on the BHS and Talmudic sequence rather than on L** `[S: _texts README]`. Nothing in this report depends on it.

*Third, the direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15 is the book's standing open question* `[T]`, and it is reported open (Tool 11).

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross — and this is the unit where the timeline is least optional, because **the list is reused in the New Testament** (Tool 11, Headline 4). What has happened in between is that the line this genealogy traces to David continued to Christ, and that the Gospel which traces it names Ruth in it `[S]`.

**How that reframes the reading.** It stops the genealogy being an appendix. **The book's last five verses are the ones the New Testament picks up**, and the reader who knows Matthew 1 reads Ruth 4:18–22 as the middle of a longer list rather than the end of a short one `[I]`.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Short, and the honest answer is "nobody in this unit".**

- **There is no character here to identify with.** Ten names, nine verbs, no action `[T]`.
- **Boaz** appears as one link, without epithet — not as the redeemer the book has spent two chapters establishing `[T]`. **That is itself worth preaching**: the man whose costly act the book exists to narrate is, in the list, a name between Salmon and Obed.
- **Ruth** is absent `[T]`. The woman the book is named after does not appear in the book's last word on what the story was for.
- **David** is the name everything converges on, and he is not yet born `[T]`.
- **The reader's position** is the one the unit actually constructs: **someone looking at a list and able to see where it goes.** Naomi cannot see 4:22 from 1:21; the reader can `[I]`. **That is the identification the unit offers — not with a figure in the text but with the vantage point the text has just given.**

**The misidentification to name.** Finding oneself in the list — "I am a link in a chain, part of God's bigger story" — is warm, available, and not what the unit says. **The unit does not say that the links matter; it says the line arrived.** The nine men who father are not characterised at all, and making them an encouragement about ordinary faithfulness supplies content the text withholds (Tool 13).

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** To accept that the story just told was not an edifying episode but a genealogical claim — and to see the name it arrives at `[I]`. The unit asks for no behaviour. It asks the reader to finish the book.

**Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.**

| Domain | Content |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | Providence in this book is retrospective and visible in lists. Nothing in 1:1–5 or 1:21 could have shown Naomi where this was going; the genealogy shows the reader, and it mentions God in neither place `[T]`. **A God who is unnamed at both ends of the book and acts once in the narrator's own voice in the middle (4:13) is the God this book teaches** `[I]` |
| **Behaviour — start** | Finishing the book. More practically: reading the lists. The one piece of this book that the New Testament picks up is the part most often skipped (Tool 11) |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Supplying character material the text withholds (Tool 13), and pressing the arithmetic of a schematic list (Tool 14) |
| **Motivation** | Not "you are part of a bigger story", which the unit does not say. **The ground is that the line arrived** — and for the Christian reader, that it continued past David to the one whose own genealogy names Ruth `[I]` |

**Four audiences.**

| Audience | What this unit says |
|---|---|
| **For me** | The part of the story I would have cut is the part the Gospel keeps. And the figure I most admire in this book appears here as a name in a list with nothing said about him |
| **For a Christian friend** | For someone who cannot see where their situation is going: the honest comfort is not that they will see it, but that Naomi never did either, and the book's last word was settled before she stopped grieving |

| **For an unbeliever** | The claim this book makes is checkable in kind: a named line, from a named ancestor, to a named king — repeated verbatim in another book of the same canon and picked up again in the New Testament. Whatever else is true of it, it is not a vague story with a moral |

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **The hiphil is the point, and the NASB95 removes it.** הוֹלִיד is causative and active: the subject *causes* the birth `[T]`. Rendering it "to X was born Y" is a legitimate English idiom and it converts an active clause into a passive one nine times `[T]`. See Tool 8.
- **Three stems of one root in ten verses** — qal (4:13, 4:15), pual (4:17), hiphil ×9 (4:18–22) `[T]`, every form checked in the index. **The pual at 4:17 is the only passive of יָלַד in the book** `[T]`.
- **The object marker אֶת nine times** `[T]`. Each son is grammatically the object of his father's action; there is no clause in the unit in which a son is a subject before his own turn comes.
- **Not one subordinating particle, and not one finite verb other than הוֹלִיד** `[T]`. The unit's whole grammar is: conjunction, subject, verb, object marker, object — nine times.
- **The heading's apposition.** וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדוֹת פָּרֶץ **פֶּרֶץ הוֹלִיד** — the name is repeated immediately after the heading rather than resumed by a pronoun `[T]`, which is the shape of Gen 11:27 and Gen 25:19 and of no other toledot heading `[S: audit, R6]`.

#### Wordplay and sound-features

- **No paronomasia claim is made.** The repetition check fires on הוֹלִיד alone, and there is no other lexical word in the unit for it to play against (Tool 10).
- **The one name-wordplay in view is Perez / פָּרַץ, and it is Genesis's** (Gen 38:29) `[T]`, not Ruth's — Ruth repeats the name without glossing it `[T]`.
- **The consonant-reversal and taxonomy-activation checks were run and both return nil.**

#### Recommended verification

- **The Ἀράμ / רָם question** (Tool 8). The corpus has gone as far as it can: the Greek doublet at 1 Chr 2:9 in both printed editions, and no Hebrew witness to the longer name in either list. **A critical apparatus to Chronicles would say how the doublet arose and how widely it is attested**, and no such apparatus is in the corpus. **Queued; the finding is rated *moderate–high* and should not be relied on beyond that until checked.**
- **Whether anyone besides Campbell has pressed the colophon point** (Tool 12). The significance of a toledot heading used as a colophon is this unit's one genuinely open question, and the library is where it would be settled.

### Textual Variants

**Four apparatus items, all set out with evidence and positions in Tool 8's table.** Two are orthographic and carry nothing; one is a cross-reference rather than a variant; **one is substantive and is answered.**

#### 4:19 — BHS's conjecture, and the Greek evidence against it

- **The issue.** MT רָם. **BHS proposes reading אֲרָם**, citing the Greek and Matt 1:3–4 `[T]`.
- **Manuscript evidence.** Both printed Greek editions of **Ruth** read Ἀρράν `[T]`; BHS reports the majority Greek witness to Ruth as Ἀράμ `[T]`; both printed Greek editions of **1 Chronicles 2:10** read Ἀράμ `[T]`; **and both carry a doublet at 1 Chr 2:9, listing Ῥάμ and Ἀράμ as separate sons of Hezron where the Hebrew has one** `[T]`. **The Hebrew reads רָם at Ruth 4:19, 1 Chr 2:9 and 1 Chr 2:10** `[T]`.
- **Most likely original: MT.** The Greek preserves two transliterations of one Hebrew name and has set them side by side as alternatives; Ἀράμ is therefore a Greek-side form rather than a witness to a Hebrew אֲרָם `[I]`. **No Hebrew witness to the longer name exists in either list.** *Moderate–high confidence.*
- **Honest limit.** No critical apparatus to Chronicles is in the corpus, so this run cannot establish how the doublet arose. The conclusion is an argument against the emendation, not a demonstration of its impossibility. **Recommend a library pass; queued.**
- **Preaching implication: none, and it should not be raised.** The point that stands is category 3: **Matthew's list reads Ἀράμ, and that is the form the New Testament's genealogy carries** — a fact about how the NT reads the OT, and not to be set aside on the ground that the Hebrew is original.

#### 4:21 — the Hebrew tradition levelling its own irregularity

- **The issue.** MT has שַׂלְמוֹן at 4:21 against שַׂלְמָה at 4:20; **BHS records a few manuscripts reading שלמה at 4:21**, levelling the two `[T]`.
- **Most likely original:** MT's unlevelled text, which is the harder reading and which the levelling manuscripts are most simply explained as smoothing. *Moderate–high.*
- **Worth recording** because it is the fourth time in four digs that **the Masoretic reading has been the harder one and the divergent witnesses have smoothed it** — see the standing observation below.

#### Nothing else

**No category-1 item arises in this unit** — there is no literary feature here for a translation to lose except the hiphil's voice, which is reported under Tool 8 as a translation question rather than a divergence between texts. **One category-3 item arises, the first in four digs** (Matthew's reuse, Tool 11).

#### The standing cross-unit observation, updated and still capped

The 3:10–18 dig recorded that across three units the Masoretic reading had been the harder one at every substantive divergence, with the divergent witnesses smoothing `[S: 3:10–18 dig]`. **This unit adds a fourth instance** — the levelling of שַׂלְמוֹן to שַׂלְמָה at 4:21 `[T]`.

**It remains an observation and not a finding, and the cap stands.** Four units, no baseline, and "the harder reading is original" is a text-critical canon being applied rather than a result being derived. **Flagged for the macro-synthesis, where cross-pericope claims belong; queued as N14; not preachable.**

**And the homograph distribution, for completeness: this unit adds none** `[T]`. The run stopped at three (1:21, 2:7, 2:23), which is weaker evidence than a continuing one `[S: 3:10–18 dig]`.

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time period (narrated span).** Perez to David: from the patriarchal period to the eve of the monarchy, something over four centuries on any reckoning `[S]`, *moderate confidence*, and the list covers it in nine steps `[T]`.
- **Time period (composition).** Not stated `[T]`. The genealogy's agreement with 1 Chronicles 2 has been used in both directions in the dating debate `[S]`, and nothing here resolves it.
- **Location.** None named `[T]`. The unit is the only part of the book with no geography in it.

#### What the original audience knew without being told

Five items.

1. **What a toledot heading was for.** A reader of the Torah had met the formula eleven times and knew it opened a section and handed the story on `[T]`. **He would also have known it never closed one** `[S: audit, L8]`, which is why its use here would have registered as unusual rather than conventional `[I]`. *Moderate confidence.*
2. **That genealogies select.** Hebrew genealogies routinely skip generations and are shaped to make a point `[S]`, *moderate–high*. The first hearers did not read ten names as ten generations, and **Nahshon's position would have told them so** (Tool 7d) `[I]`.
3. **Who Nahshon was.** The chief of Judah who brought the first offering at the tabernacle's dedication (Num 7:12) `[T]` — a name from the centre of the Torah's narrative, not an obscure link `[S]`, *moderate–high*. **His presence anchors the list in the exodus generation for a hearer who knew Numbers.**
4. **Who Perez was, and that his birth was irregular.** Genesis 38 is a story about a levirate duty refused and a line continued by a route nobody would have chosen `[T]`. The elders had just cited it at 4:12 `[T]`. **A hearer therefore heard the list begin from an irregular birth and end at the king** `[I]`. *Moderate–high.*
5. **What David's name meant at the end of a scroll.** For the canonical reader of the Writings — post-exilic, knowing how the monarchy ended `[S]` — the last word of this book is not merely a king's name but a promise whose fulfilment had visibly failed `[I]`. *Moderate confidence*, and it is the item that most changes the unit's weight.

#### What this changes about how we read

- **Items 2 and 3 together disarm the arithmetic.** A modern reader counts ten names and looks for a pattern; the original audience knew the form selects, and had a datable name in front of them that proved it `[I]`.
- **Items 4 and 5 give the list its two ends.** It begins at an irregular birth and ends at a name that, by the time the Writings were being read as a collection, carried an unresolved promise `[I]`. **That is a better frame for preaching the unit than either "God's providence" in the abstract or "ten generations to David".**

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section:** **Ketuvim**. The reader arrives having read the Torah and the whole of the Prophets through to Malachi — post-exilic, knowing David, knowing how the monarchy ended, living with promises unresolved `[S]`.
- **What the canonical reader knows that bears on this unit:** the toledot formula and its eleven Pentateuchal uses `[T]`; Genesis 38 `[T]`; Nahshon and the wilderness census `[T]`; the Judahite registers at Gen 46:12 and Num 26:21 `[T]`; **and the line's outcome, which he has read in Samuel and Kings** `[S]`.
- **Canonical register:** **Chronicles is live here and nowhere else in the book** `[T]` — the one Writings neighbour that carries this material.

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were:** Israelites who knew the Torah's genealogical conventions and the names in this list.
- **Their situation:** living after the monarchy's end, with a Davidic promise that had not been fulfilled in the way its terms suggested `[S]`.
- **What they brought:** the five background items above, and the habit of reading a list as a claim rather than as data `[I]`.

#### Where the passage fits their world

Three things they would have heard that we do not naturally pick up:

1. **The oddity of the form** (item 1). We hear a conventional genealogy; they heard a Torah section-heading used to close a story.
2. **The irregularity at the head of the list** (item 4). We hear "Perez"; they heard Genesis 38.
3. **The weight of the last word** (item 5). We hear "David" as a triumphant close; a post-exilic reader heard a name with a question attached `[I]`. *Moderate confidence*, and it is the reception finding that most repays a sermon.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking:** nothing. The unit is the least shocking five verses in the book, and that is its character.
- **Surprising:** that the book ends in a list at all; and that **Ruth is not in it** `[T]`.
- **Comforting:** the arrival. The line got there, and the reader can see it `[T]`.
- **Disturbing:** for a reader after the exile, the last word. The line arrived at David and the house of David had fallen `[I]`.
- **Familiar:** everything else. The formula, the names, the register form.

#### What we bring that they didn't

- **Impatience with genealogies**, which we skip and they read.
- **A chronological instinct**, which makes us count generations the form does not intend (item 2).
- **A completed canon**, which lets us read 4:22 forward to Matthew 1 — a reading they could not have and we should not pretend they did.
- **A preference for stories over lists**, which is exactly the preference the book's ending refuses `[I]`.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes; one named and declined.

#### (1) Seed, and the preservation of a line

- **Where this passage sits:** at the point where the line is listed rather than narrated `[T]`. The book has shown one generation nearly lost (1:3–5) and one given (4:13); the genealogy places both inside a chain of nine.
- **What comes before:** the promised seed of Gen 3:15; the repeated jeopardy of the patriarchal lines; **Genesis 38, from which this list begins** `[T]`; the Judahite registers of Gen 46:12 and Num 26:21 `[T]`.
- **What comes after:** the Davidic covenant; the line's apparent failure at the exile; **and Matthew 1, which carries this same stretch and names Ruth in it** `[T]`.
- **What this contributes:** **the theme's form, not just its content.** The book's claim about the seed is made as a list, in the Pentateuch's own heading, from an irregular birth to a king. **Confidence:** *high* that the theme is textually signalled; *moderate–high* on the reading.

#### (2) Kingship, and a period named by its absence

- **Where this passage sits:** at the answer to the book's first clause. 1:1's בִּימֵי שְׁפֹט הַשֹּׁפְטִים dates the story by an office with no name in it, and **four of the five canonical occurrences of וַיְהִי בִּימֵי date by a named ruler** `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`. The book ends on a king's name, twice `[T]`.
- **What comes before:** the Judges refrain that there was no king in Israel `[S]`; the patriarchal promise that kings would come from Abraham `[S]`; **and the name Elimelech, "my God is king", borne by a man who left Bethlehem in the era with no king** `[S: 1:1–5 dig]`.
- **What comes after:** Samuel, the monarchy, the covenant with David, the failure of his house, and the New Testament's claim that the line reached its king `[S]`.
- **What this contributes:** the book's frame. **A period dated by the absence of a king is closed by a king's name, with nothing in between to explain the connection** `[I]`. **Confidence:** *moderate–high* — the two data are certain and the inference is short.

#### How the two relate

**(c) Climactic convergence.** They meet in the unit's last word. **The seed-line and the kingship theme are the same list read twice** — as a chain of fathers (seed) and as an arrival at David (kingship) — and the genealogy's form makes the two inseparable: it is a seed-list whose only distinctive feature is where it stops `[I]`. *Moderate–high.* **Remove the last clause and it is a Judahite register like Chronicles'; the last clause is what makes it the book's argument** (Tool 11, Headline 3).

#### Declined

**Covenant.** The unit contains no covenant vocabulary and no divine name `[T]`. The Davidic covenant is canonically downstream and belongs to the trajectory rather than to this text's own participation. Not entered.

### Christological Reading

#### Type of Christological connection

**Trajectory, and — uniquely in this book — a connection the New Testament makes explicitly with this very text.**

The three preceding solo digs worked the **redeemer** typology, which passes all four tests at *high confidence* on the strength of the book's own construction of the category `[S: 3:1–9 dig]`. **That typology is not this unit's business.** Boaz appears here as one name among nine, with no epithet and no act `[T]`.

What this unit contributes is different in kind, and stronger in one respect: **the New Testament reuses this list.** Matthew 1:3–6 covers Perez to David in the same ten names `[T]`, and the genealogy of Christ therefore runs through the text under exegesis. The four typology tests are not the right instrument for that — **it is not a type but a continuation**, and it is as direct as a canonical connection gets.

#### How the passage points to Christ

- **By being picked up.** The list is not an illustration of something Christ fulfils; **it is the first ten names of the list Matthew gives** `[T]`. *High confidence* on the datum.
- **By stopping where it stops.** The book ends on David, with a promise that the canon then follows into failure and the New Testament into fulfilment `[I]`. **The genealogy's incompleteness is its forward motion** — it is a list that demonstrably goes on.
- **By beginning where it begins.** Perez, the irregular birth of Genesis 38, whom Ruth 4:12 had just named `[T]`. **The line to Christ is traced in this book from a levirate duty refused and a line continued by a route nobody would have chosen** `[I]`. *Moderate–high.*
- **By what Matthew adds back.** The three women Matthew names in this stretch — Tamar, Rahab, Ruth `[T]` — are the ones Ruth's own list omits. **The Gospel's version of this genealogy makes explicit what the book's narrative had shown and its list had not: that the line ran through foreign women and irregular providences** `[I]`. *Moderate–high*, and it is the unit's richest Christological material.

#### Trajectory

- **What the passage anticipates:** David, and the house whose promise the canon carries forward.
- **What Christ fulfils:** the line itself. Not a pattern the list exhibits but the list's own continuation `[S]`.
- **What still remains:** nothing of the list. **The already/not-yet here is unusually thin, and that is the point** — this is the one part of the book where the canonical answer is simply given.

#### Moralism check

- **The "be like X" temptation is almost absent**, there being no character to imitate (Tool 13). **Its substitute is the one to watch: "you are part of the story too"** — warm, common in sermons on genealogies, and not what the unit says. The nine fathers are uncharacterised; the unit's claim is about arrival, not participation.
- **The gospel grounding:** the line arrived, and nobody inside the book could see that it would `[I]`. The only divine act the narrator states in his own voice is eight verses earlier (4:13) `[T]`, and this unit mentions God not at all.
- **Christ as hero, not just example:** here the hero is a name at the end of a list, and beyond it a name at the end of a longer one. **The right sermon lets the list arrive rather than extracting a lesson from the links.**

**Confidence:** *high* that the New Testament reuses this text and that the trajectory is direct; *moderate–high* on the reading of the three women; and the redeemer typology is inherited from the earlier units rather than re-grounded here.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

Three live issues, and one named and dismissed.

#### 4:18–22 — is the genealogy original to the book?

- **Category:** apologetic / doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** a hearer who reads a study Bible will meet the view that 4:18–22 is a later appendix.
- **Common mishandlings:** asserting its originality as though the text settled it; or conceding it is secondary and therefore preaching it as less than Scripture.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **the text itself gives evidence on both sides and settles neither.** For integration: 4:18 picks up 4:12's "house of Perez" `[T]`; the whole last ten verses run one root through three voices (Tool 7a) `[T]`; and 4:17 and 4:22 both end on David `[T]`. For distinctness: the manuscript marks a break here and nowhere else `[T]`; the unit has no divine name and no connectives `[T]`; and **Campbell notes the near-universal critical view that the genealogy is an appendix whose style matches P — while also observing that in Genesis no such genealogy ever functions as a colophon, so Ruth's use is unique and its significance hard to assess** `[S: audit, L8]`. *Moderate confidence* that the question is genuinely open on the evidence available here.
- **Suggested handling:** acknowledge briefly if the congregation is likely to meet it; do not make it the sermon. **The canonical text has the genealogy, the manuscript tradition sets it apart, and the New Testament picks it up** — all three are preachable facts.

#### The ten names, and the temptation to find a pattern

- **Category:** weaponised, in the mild sense that it is the sermon everyone preaches.
- **The difficulty:** ten names, with Boaz seventh and David tenth, invites a numerical design — and the overview's wider "ten" pattern (ten years at 1:4, ten elders at 4:2) invites it more.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **the claim audit baselined every numeral in the book and withdrew the design** `[S: audit, R1]`. **Two** is the book's commonest numeral at nine occurrences, eight of them in chapter 1; **ten** stands at two, tying with **six** and one above **seven** `[T]`. **The text never says "ten" of the genealogy** `[T]`, so the count is the reader's. **Boaz's seventh position carries no formal marking** — nine identical formulas, no expansion anywhere, unlike Genesis 5, which marks its seventh from Adam `[T]`. **And the list omits generations** (Tool 7d) `[T]`, so it is not counting them.
- **Suggested handling:** **do not preach it.** If the congregation raises it, the honest answer is that the numbers are there and the design is not demonstrable.

#### Why is Ruth not in the genealogy?

- **Category:** pastoral, and increasingly the question a congregation will actually ask.
- **The difficulty:** the book is named after her, exists to explain her place in the line, and leaves her out of its own list.
- **Common mishandlings:** treating it as a grievance against the text; or dismissing the question as anachronistic.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** two things, and both should be said. **(a)** The form is a line-of-descent register and such registers name fathers — **Chronicles does the same with the same material** `[T]`. **(b)** The absence is nonetheless a pattern-break of the admissible kind, because **the immediately preceding unit grounded the house's future on three named women** (4:11–12) `[T]`. **And (c), which answers it canonically: Matthew's genealogy of the same stretch names her** `[T]`.
- **Suggested handling:** answer it, in that order, and let Matthew 1:5 do the work. **This is the best route from this unit to Christ** and it starts from a question the congregation already has.

#### Named and dismissed

**The Ἀράμ / רָם variant and BHS's conjecture.** Fully worked at Tool 8 and Textual Variants. **Nothing in the sermon depends on it and it should not be raised** — a congregation told that BHS wants to emend the Hebrew of the book's last page will take away only the emendation.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: skipping it

- **What it looks like:** a series on Ruth that ends at 4:17, with the genealogy read aloud and not preached, or not read at all.
- **Why it's wrong:** the book does not end at 4:17, and **the manuscript tradition sets this unit apart as the only unit in eighty-five verses with a paragraph of its own** `[T]`. It is the book's second ending, in a second genre (Tool 1), and it is **the part of Ruth the New Testament picks up** `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach it with 4:13–17, as the overview directs — and say what it is for. The story could have been an edifying episode; the list makes it a claim.

### Pitfall: preaching the ten names as a pattern

- **What it looks like:** "Ten names, Boaz seventh, David tenth — the structure is telling us something"; and the wider "ten years, ten elders, ten names".
- **Why it's wrong:** **the audit baselined every numeral in the book and withdrew the design** `[S: audit, R1]`. Two is this book's number at nine occurrences; ten stands at two, tying with six. **The text never says "ten"**, Boaz's position carries no formal marking in nine identical formulas, **and the list omits generations** `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach the two ends of the list — an irregular birth and a king's name — not its arithmetic.

### Pitfall: supplying character for the links

- **What it looks like:** a sermon that finds in Hezron, Ram or Salmah an encouragement about ordinary faithfulness; or the modern form, "you are a link in the chain too".
- **Why it's wrong:** **the canon says nothing about any of them** except that Nahshon led Judah in the wilderness `[T]`. Nine of the ten do nothing in the list but father a son. **The unit does not say that the links matter; it says the line arrived** (Tool 15).
- **The corrective:** let the list be a list. The encouragement the unit actually offers is that nobody inside the book could see where it was going, and it got there anyway.

### Pitfall: reading the ten names as ten generations

- **What it looks like:** a chronology drawn from the list, or a sermon illustration about four hundred years in ten steps.
- **Why it's wrong:** **Nahshon is datable** — the chief of Judah in the wilderness, named six times in Exodus and Numbers `[T]` — and he is Boaz's grandfather in this list `[T]`. **Two generations cannot cover the conquest and the judges** `[I]`. Hebrew genealogies select `[S]`.
- **The corrective:** say the list is schematic, which is what a genealogy is, and move on.

### Pitfall: preaching the NASB95's passives as the Hebrew

- **What it looks like:** a sermon built on "to Perez was born Hezron" — the line as a series of things that happened to people.
- **Why it's wrong:** **the Hebrew is active nine times over** — the hiphil of יָלַד, "X fathered Y" `[T]`. And the NASB95's passive uses the same English construction as 4:17's genuine passive, so the movement from Ruth bearing, to a birth with no agent, to nine men fathering is invisible `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** read 4:18–22 from the ESV, or supply the active voice. Then 4:13, 4:17 and 4:18 are three different sentences, which is what they are in Hebrew.

### Pitfall: treating Ruth's absence as a problem to defend or a grievance to air

- **What it looks like:** either an apology for the text's patriarchy, or a brisk "that's just how genealogies work".
- **Why it's wrong:** both halves are true and neither is the whole. The form names fathers, and **Chronicles does the same with the same material** `[T]`; **and the immediately preceding unit had grounded the house's future on three named women** `[T]`, which makes the absence noticeable rather than invisible.
- **The corrective:** answer the question, then turn to **Matthew 1:5**, where the same stretch of the same list names Ruth `[T]`. It is the best route from this unit to Christ, and it begins from a question the congregation already has.
