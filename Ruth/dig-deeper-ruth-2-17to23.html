Headline Findings

  1. The gleaning law’s promise of blessing does stand in this chapter — but at 2:4, in the reapers’ mouths, not in Naomi’s at 2:19–20. (v1.3 — claim audit 3, D20. This headline previously claimed that the law’s own beneficiary delivered the Deuteronomic blessing at 2:19–20. The claim was wrong about its verse and is withdrawn.) Deut 24:19 attaches to the forgotten sheaf the promise לְמַעַן יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ. The two-word formula יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, eight of them in Deuteronomy — and Ruth’s only instance is 2:4, where the reapers answer Boaz’s greeting [T]. The morphology settles it: Deut 24:19 and Ruth 2:4 are both Piel imperfect 3ms with 2ms suffix; Ruth 2:19 and 2:20 are Qal passive participles — the same root, a different form [T]. So the verse that reproduces the statute’s wording is not the one this report built on, the speakers are the labourers rather than the widow, and “the two statutes in sequence” fails as well, since 2:4 stands before the Leviticus contact at 2:16. What survives is firmer than what it replaces. Leviticus’s own verb pair עָזַב with לָקַט stands in exactly three verses — Lev 19:10, Lev 23:22, Ruth 2:16 [T], and Boaz’s order exceeds the statute: Leviticus commands leaving the standing edge and the fallen gleanings, while 2:15–16 has Ruth glean among the sheaves and the young men pull grain out of the bundles for her [T]. And עֹמֶר stands in only eight verses — Lev 23:10, 11, 12, 15; Deut 24:19; Job 24:10; Ruth 2:7, 2:15 [T], a rare contact reaching both statutes. בָּרַךְ still occurs nowhere in Leviticus 19 or 23 [T]. Surfaced by: Author’s Purpose, Context, Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion. High confidence on the counts; the completion reading is withdrawn.

  2. The construction that makes 2:20 ambiguous is unique in the canon, the ambiguity is original, and the pulpit text closes it. בָּרוּךְ הוּא לַיהוָה (“blessed be he to the LORD”) stands as an ordered phrase in Ruth 2:20 alone in the Hebrew Bible, plain and skeletal [T]. The clause it governs, אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָזַב חַסְדּוֹ, is shared with Gen 24:27 and nowhere else [T] — but in Genesis it comes under בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה, where the subject cannot be anyone but YHWH [T]. BHS records one Hebrew manuscript and the Syriac reading יהוה at 2:20 — that is, assimilating Ruth to the Genesis formula and settling the question [T]. Both Greek editions keep the ambiguity [T]. MT is the harder and the rarer reading, and is almost certainly original. Surfaced by: Translations, Textual Variants, Quotation/Allusion, Difficult Verses. High on the uniqueness; moderate–high on the text-critical judgement. This answers the question the sweep left open for the solo dig.

  3. Naomi asks “where?” twice and is answered with a name — and the Greek repairs the mismatch. 2:19 opens אֵיפֹה לִקַּטְתְּ הַיּוֹם וְאָנָה עָשִׂית (“where did you glean today, and where did you work?”), two interrogatives of place [T]; Ruth answers שֵׁם הָאִישׁ … בֹּעַז (“the name of the man… is Boaz”) [T]. Both Greek editions read ποῦ ἐποίησεν, “where she had worked”, against MT’s אֵת אֲשֶׁר־עָשְׂתָה עִמּוֹ, “what she had done with him” — a reading BHS records [T]. The Greek makes the answer match the question; the Hebrew does not, and the mismatch is the point: the answer to where turns out to be who [I]. Surfaced by: Structure, Linking Words, Translations, Textual Variants, Narrator’s Comment. High on the readings; moderate–high on the reading of the mismatch.

  4. 2:19 answers 2:10 in the same root, and does it before Ruth knows who the man is. At 2:10 Ruth asked why Boaz should הַכִּיר (“recognise”) her, וְאָנֹכִי נָכְרִיָּה (“and I a foreigner”) — two lemmas standing together in exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible [S: 2:1–16 dig, verified there]. Here Naomi blesses מַכִּירֵךְ, “the one who recognised you” [T]. נָכַר occurs in three verses of Ruth — 2:10, 2:19, 3:14 [T], and the middle one is in this unit. The foreigner’s question about being recognised is answered as a blessing on the recogniser, spoken by a third party while the recogniser is still anonymous. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Move 4, Original Audience Reception. High on the chain; moderate–high on the reading.

  5. “Redeemer” enters the book here, and the NASB95 is the version that hides it. גָּאַל/גֹּאֵל occurs 21 times in 10 verses of Ruth, none of them before 2:20 — one in chapter 2, seven in chapter 3, thirteen in chapter 4 [T]. The word arrives in a single clause of Naomi’s, מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ הוּא. The ESV renders it “one of our redeemers” and the NIV84 “one of our kinsman-redeemers”; the NASB95 has “one of our closest relatives”, and the word “redeemer” does not appear [T]. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Translations, Move 4. High confidence on all of it. The sweep had the distribution; the translation consequence is new.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level. Taken from the overview rather than re-derived: to show a God who is spoken about but never speaks refilling an emptied household through ordinary acts of kindness and costly redemption, and to give Israel David as the fruit [I: overview].

Passage level. This unit’s contribution is to move information from the field into the house, and in moving it to turn an act of private generosity into a public transaction between the law, the provider and the beneficiary. Three things cross the threshold with Ruth: the grain (2:17–18), the name (2:19), and the category (2:20, גֹּאֵל) [T]. The third is the one the plot needs: nothing in chapters 3 and 4 can happen until the word “redeemer” is in Naomi’s mouth, and this is the clause that puts it there [I].

A second contribution, and the one the sweep did not see. The chapter’s first half shows Boaz going beyond a gleaning statute: his private order at 2:15–16 is phrased in Leviticus’s own two verbs, לָקַט with עָזַב, a pair standing in Lev 19:10, Lev 23:22 and Ruth 2:16 and nowhere else [T], reproduced independently in this run — but what he orders is more than the statute commands, grain pulled from the bundles rather than an edge left standing [T]. (v1.3 — claim audit 3, D20: this paragraph previously argued that the chapter ran the two statutes in sequence, Boaz obeying Leviticus and Naomi completing Deuteronomy’s promise at 2:19–20. The sequence claim is withdrawn — Ruth’s instance of the Deuteronomic formula is at 2:4, before the Leviticus contact, and on the reapers’ lips.) What can still be said is that both statutes are in the chapter’s vocabulary without either being cited [T]: Leviticus by its verb pair at 2:16, Deuteronomy by the blessing formula at 2:4 and by עֹמֶר at 2:7 and 2:15. Neither law is named [T].

Choice and pacing. The narrator gives one verse to a whole day’s labour and five to the conversation that follows [T]. He is not interested in the gleaning; he is interested in what the gleaning tells Naomi.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 2:1–16 has produced three things this unit must deal with.

First, an abundance with no explanation available to the person who carried it home. Boaz’s decisive orders at 2:15–16 are spoken to his young men, after Ruth has gone back to work — וַתָּקָם לְלַקֵּט וַיְצַו בֹּעַז אֶת־נְעָרָיו (“and she rose to glean, and Boaz commanded his young men”) [T]. The 2:1–16 dig names this concealment as the fourth feature of the chapter’s Christological type, and the feature sermons omit [S: 2:1–16 dig]. The consequence falls here: Ruth can name the man but cannot account for the quantity, which is exactly the shape of the exchange at 2:19.

Second, a blessing left unanswered. At 2:12 Boaz prayed יְשַׁלֵּם יְהוָה פָּעֳלֵךְ (“may the LORD repay your work”) [T]. The unit answers the prayer in the only currency the narrative has — an ephah of barley, carried home — and then answers the blessing with a blessing, twice, in the other direction [I].

Third, a benefactor the reader has known since 2:1 and the characters have not. The narrator told the reader Boaz’s name at 2:1 and his kinship at 2:1 and 2:3 [T]. Naomi learns the name at 2:19 and states the kinship herself at 2:20. The gap between what the reader knows and what Naomi knows closes in this unit, and the closing is the unit’s dramatic content [I].

And behind all three, the movement of 1:6–22: Naomi’s last recorded word about YHWH was וַיהוָה עָנָה בִי (“and the LORD has testified against me”, 1:21) [T]. 2:20 is her first word about him since.

Necessity answer — why here, and not elsewhere. Because chapter 3 presupposes, word for word, what this unit supplies. Naomi opens her plan with הֲלֹא בֹעַז מֹדַעְתָּנוּ (“is not Boaz our kinsman?”, 3:2) [T] — which requires 2:19’s name and 2:20’s קָרוֹב לָנוּ. Her instruction at 3:9 puts גֹּאֵל in Ruth’s own mouth [T] — which requires 2:20’s מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ. And the scene of 3:2, Boaz winnowing barley at the threshing floor, requires 2:23’s “end of the barley harvest” [T]. Remove this unit and chapter 3 has no premises.

There is also a reason of a different kind. The book will spend chapters 3 and 4 on a redemption that has to be asked for, at cost, through a procedure. It can only do that once someone has named the category — and the narrator conspicuously declines to name it himself. He has had eleven verses in which he could have called Boaz a redeemer and did not; he called him a kinsman (2:1, 2:3) [T]. The word enters the book in a character’s speech, as an inference she draws, not as a fact the narrator asserts [I]. That is consistent with the book’s method throughout, and it is the reason the unit exists where it does rather than in the narrator’s introduction at 2:1.

Implication for the passage. The unit is not a domestic epilogue to the gleaning scene. It is the hinge on which the second half of the book turns, and its weight falls on v.20 — one verse in which a bereaved woman blesses YHWH for the first time since her lament, and names the legal category that will resolve her story.

General contextual notes

Immediately before: 2:15–16, Boaz’s concealed order. Immediately after: 3:1–5, Naomi’s plan, which opens by invoking exactly what 2:20 established. The join at 2:23/3:1 is tight: both verses name חֲמוֹתָהּ (“her mother-in-law”), and 3:1 is the first time in the book that Naomi is called Ruth’s mother-in-law by the narrator in the same breath as speaking to her [T].

Section: the back half of the arc map’s “Gleaning” movement (2:1–23) [S: overview]. The overview’s preaching-unit division splits the chapter at 2:16/2:17, which is a sound literary judgement — the scene changes from field to city at 2:18 — though the Hebrew runs 2:17 on from 2:16 without a break and the WLC carries no paragraph marker anywhere in the chapter [T]; see Tool 3.

Read out of context? Verse 20 is, often. It is quoted as a statement about God’s faithfulness with the ambiguity of its Hebrew silently resolved, and with its second half — the legal identification that is the reason Naomi says any of it — left off.

3. Structure

Six movements, with the weight on the fourth and a coda that closes two chapters.

Verse Movement Function
17 The measure A day’s work compressed into one verse, ending on a quantity: field, evening, beating out, about an ephah
18 The carrying Three verbs of transport and transfer; the mother-in-law sees; the surplus of 2:14 is handed over
19 The interrogation Two questions of place; a blessing on an unnamed man; an answer that gives a name instead of a place
20 The identification Naomi’s blessing, and two statements of kinship — “near to us”, “one of our redeemers”
21–22 The addition and the endorsement Ruth supplies Boaz’s own words; Naomi approves and warns
23 The coda She clung, she gleaned to the end of both harvests, she dwelt with her mother-in-law

Device: a double speech-frame around v.20, and an inclusio that reaches back to chapter 1.

The dialogue is arranged so that Naomi speaks three times and Ruth twice, alternating, with וַתֹּאמֶר נָעֳמִי marked twice inside v.20 alone (וַתֹּאמֶר נָעֳמִי לְכַלָּתָהּ … וַתֹּאמֶר לָהּ נָעֳמִי) [T]. The doubled speech-formula within a single verse is the structural signal that the second statement is a distinct act: the blessing is one thing, the legal identification another, and the narrator separates them with a fresh “and Naomi said” rather than running them together [I]. Moderate–high confidence — the doubling is on the page and the inference from it is short.

The coda at 2:23 closes the harvest frame opened at 1:22: קָצִיר (“harvest”) occurs four times in three verses of Ruth — 1:22 בִּתְחִלַּת קְצִיר שְׂעֹרִים (“at the beginning of barley harvest”), 2:21, and twice in 2:23, עַד־כְּלוֹת קְצִיר־הַשְּׂעֹרִים וּקְצִיר הַחִטִּים (“until the end of the barley harvest and the wheat harvest”) [T]. Beginning to end, barley to wheat, with chapters 1 and 2 inside. High confidence on the count; the frame is the overview’s and is confirmed [S: overview].

Where the modern unit boundary comes from, and the manuscript’s silence. The WLC carries exactly one paragraph marker in the whole of Ruth — a petuchah after 4:17 [T], established in the 1:1–5 run and re-confirmed here. So Leningrad marks no break at 2:16/2:17, none at 2:23/3:1, and none anywhere in chapter 2. Every division in this chapter is editorial. [unchecked — apparatus spread] — the corpus holds one manuscript tradition and prints paragraphing without variants, so this is a statement about Leningrad; capped at moderate, named by witness, and barred from the overview until a library pass on the spread has run. Paragraph markers are a reading tradition, not authorial [S].

4. Linking Words

Narrative, so the significant connectors are few and three of them matter.

Marker Verse Function
אֵיפֹה … וְאָנָה (“where… and where”) 19 Two interrogatives of place, stacked. The doubling raises the expectation of a place-answer, which is what makes the name-answer a swerve (Headline 3) [T]
אֲשֶׁר 20 The relative pronoun on which the unit’s crux turns: אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָזַב חַסְדּוֹ — “who has not forsaken his kindness”. Hebrew does not mark the antecedent, and the two candidates (הוּא, the blessed man; יְהוָה, named immediately before) are both grammatically available [T]. See Tools 8 and 11 and Textual Variants
גַּם (“furthermore, besides”) 21 Additive, and doing real work: Ruth is adding to what she has already said, which tells the reader that 2:19’s answer was incomplete [T]. The ESV’s “Besides” and the NASB95’s “Furthermore” both carry it
כִּי 22 Naomi’s one causal clause in the unit: טוֹב בִּתִּי כִּי תֵצְאִי (“it is good, my daughter, that you go out”) — the כִּי is explanatory of her verdict, not of a command [T]
עַד (“until”) 17, 21, 23 Three temporal boundaries: until evening (17), until they have finished the harvest (21), until the end of the two harvests (23) [T]. The unit is bounded three times, each time by a completion
וְלֹא (“and not”) 22 The negated purpose: וְלֹא יִפְגְּעוּ־בָךְ. The only negative in the unit, and it is a warning [T]

→ The logical spine: a measure → carried home → questioned →and so→ blessed and identified → supplemented →therefore→ endorsed, with a warning → and so she stayed. The only inferential step the text itself marks is Naomi’s כִּי at v.22; everything else is sequence. The unit’s two most consequential moves — the blessing and the naming of the redeemer — are made without any connector at all, side by side in one verse [T].

5. Parallels

N/A — Hebrew prose narrative with no poetic parallelism; there is no bicolon here to read one half against the other.

One near-balanced pair is handled under Tool 3 rather than lost: Naomi’s two blessings, at 2:19 (יְהִי מַכִּירֵךְ בָּרוּךְ, on an unnamed man) and 2:20 (בָּרוּךְ הוּא לַיהוָה, on a named one) — the same root, one verse apart, before and after she knows who he is [T].

6. Narrator’s Comment

The narrator makes no evaluative intrusion in these seven verses [T]. He reports actions and speech and nothing else: no inner state, no explanation, no verdict on Naomi’s blessing or her inference.

That is admissible as a finding because the book establishes the pattern it breaks. The same narrator reports Boaz’s inner state at 3:7 (וַיִּיטַב לִבּוֹ), explains an obsolete custom at 4:7, and attributes a divine act to YHWH in his own voice at 4:13 [T]. Moderate confidence on the significance, the ceiling the pattern-break rule sets for an absence claim; high on the three cited instances.

What the silence withholds here is specific and important. At 2:20 Naomi says something theologically load-bearing — that YHWH (or Boaz) has not forsaken his kindness to the living and the dead — and the narrator neither endorses nor corrects it [T]. Against 1:21, where she said the opposite about the same God, the reader is given two statements from the same mouth and no authorial adjudication [T]. The audit’s discard of a “narratorial cluster” as a design claim (R28) does not touch this: it is an observation about one unit against a cited pattern, not a claim about a distribution [S: audit].

One thing the narrator does do, and it is easy to miss. He reports at 2:19 that Ruth told Naomi — וַתַּגֵּד לַחֲמוֹתָהּ אֵת אֲשֶׁר־עָשְׂתָה עִמּוֹ — and then quotes her saying something narrower [T]. The narrator’s summary and the quoted speech do not match in extent: he says she told her what she had done with him; she is quoted giving only the name. The quotation is selective, and v.21’s גַּם (“furthermore”) shows that more was said than has been quoted [T]. Moderate confidence that this is deliberate compression rather than a seam.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory (run before triage)

Persons: נָעֳמִי (“Naomi”), רוּת (“Ruth”), בֹּעַז (“Boaz”). Gentilic: הַמּוֹאֲבִיָּה (“the Moabitess”, 2:21). Places: הָעִיר (“the city”, unnamed here — Bethlehem by 1:19, 1:22, 2:4) [T]. Measures: אֵיפָה (“an ephah”). Grains: שְׂעֹרִים (“barley”), חִטִּים (“wheat”).

Triage: Boaz’s name is the unit’s payload and is treated at Tool 11 and under Move 4 (its arrival answers yesterday’s שֵׁם finding). The gentilic at 2:21 carries real freight and all three ancient versions drop it — see Tool 8. The ephah is a measure, triaged below, and its canonical company is reported with an explicit chance caution. The city is scenery here; Ruth and Naomi need no check. No outstanding word (curse, vow, oath or prophecy) attaches to any name in this unit; the check returns nil.

(a) חָבַט (“to beat out”) at 2:17 — the gleaning law’s own verb

The unit’s sharpest external contact, and the spine of Headline 1.

חָבַט stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deut 24:20; Judg 6:11; Isa 27:12; Isa 28:27; Ruth 2:17 [T]. High confidence on the count.

The five sort into three kinds. Agricultural procedure: Deut 24:20, the olive tree beaten and not gone over again, “it shall be for the sojourner, the orphan and the widow”; and Isa 28:27, cummin beaten with a rod rather than threshed with a sledge [T]. A scene of fear: Judg 6:11, Gideon beating out wheat in the winepress לְהָנִיס מִפְּנֵי מִדְיָן (“to hide it from Midian”) [T]. An eschatological gathering: Isa 27:12, where YHWH is the one who beats out, and Israel is what gets gleaned — יַחְבֹּט יְהוָה … וְאַתֶּם תְּלֻקְּטוּ לְאַחַד אֶחָד (“the LORD will beat out… and you will be gleaned one by one”) [T].

And the two verbs of Ruth 2:17 stand together in two verses of the Hebrew Bible — Isa 27:12 and Ruth 2:17 [T]: חָבַט with לָקַט. High confidence on the co-occurrence.

What this does and does not license. It licenses the Deuteronomy contact firmly: Ruth 2:17’s verb is the verb of the provision in Deut 24:20, one verse after the promise of blessing that this unit goes on to deliver (Headline 1). It does not license Isaiah as a source. The claim audit relocated Ruth 2’s gleaning vocabulary from Isaiah to Deut 24:19–21 and rated Isaiah “a second user of the same legal stock” rather than a rival [S: audit, R18], and that verdict is accepted here. What is worth recording is the inversion: in Isaiah, Israel is the object of the gleaning and YHWH the agent; in Ruth, a Moabitess is the agent and the grain is the object [T] for the grammar, [I] for the reading. Moderate confidence, and offered as an observation, not an allusion.

(b) בָּרַךְ (“to bless”) — twice here, five times in the book

בָּרַךְ occurs in five verses of Ruth: 2:4, 2:19, 2:20, 3:10, 4:14 [T]. Two of the five are in this unit, adjacent [T].

The distribution is worth stating because it tracks the plot: 2:4 is the greeting exchanged in the field between Boaz and his reapers; 2:19 and 2:20 are Naomi’s, in the house; 3:10 is Boaz blessing Ruth at the threshing floor; 4:14 is the women blessing YHWH for the redeemer [T]. Blessing in this book moves from the field to the house to the floor to the gate, and in this unit it changes direction — in chapter 2’s first half Boaz blesses Ruth (2:12); here the household blesses Boaz [I]. High on the distribution; moderate on the reading.

On the legal force of the two blessings here, see Headline 1 and Tool 11. The short form: the promise Deuteronomy attaches to this provision is a blessing from YHWH on the provider, and Leviticus’s version of the provision has no blessing attached — בָּרַךְ occurs twice in the whole of Leviticus, at 9:22 and 9:23 [T].

(c) נָכַר — the root that answers 2:10

נָכַר stands in three verses of Ruth: 2:10, 2:19, 3:14 [T].

  • 2:10 — לְהַכִּירֵנִי (“to recognise me”) with וְאָנֹכִי נָכְרִיָּה (“and I a foreigner”). The two lemmas stand together in exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible [S: 2:1–16 dig, verified there].
  • 2:19 — יְהִי מַכִּירֵךְ בָּרוּךְ, “may the one who recognised you be blessed” [T].
  • 3:14 — בְּטֶרֶם יַכִּיר אִישׁ אֶת־רֵעֵהוּ (“before one could recognise another”), of the dark at the threshing floor [T].

The chain runs: she asks why she should be recognised, being unrecognisable; a third party blesses the recogniser; and the decisive scene happens in the one condition where nobody can recognise anybody [I]. High confidence on the three-verse chain; moderate–high on the reading of 2:10→2:19; moderate on 3:14, which may be no more than a statement about the hour.

Translation note, cross-reported to Tool 8: every English version here renders the 2:19 participle with a different idiom — “he who took notice of you” (NASB95, ESV), “the man who took notice of you” (NIV84) [T] — and none of the three uses a word that connects it to 2:10’s “foreigner” or to 3:14. The chain is invisible in English.

(d) שָׂבַע and יָתַר — the surplus carried across

At 2:14 Ruth ate וַתִּשְׂבַּע וַתֹּתַר (“and she was satisfied and had some left over”); here at 2:18 she gives Naomi אֵת אֲשֶׁר־הוֹתִרָה מִשָּׂבְעָהּ (“what she had left over from her satisfaction”) [T].

Precisely: יָתַר stands in two verses of Ruth, 2:14 and 2:18 — the same lemma [T]. שָׂבַע the verb stands in one verse, 2:14; שֹׂבַע the noun in one verse, 2:18 [T] — cognate, not the same word, and a report must not present them as one chain.

The 2:1–16 dig found that the verb-pair שָׂבַע + יָתַר stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, 2 Chr 31:10 and Ruth 2:14 [S: 2:1–16 dig]. This run adds the third element. 2 Chr 31:10 reads אָכוֹל וְשָׂבוֹעַ וְהוֹתֵר … כִּי יְהוָה בֵּרַךְ אֶת־עַמּוֹ (“eating and being satisfied and having plenty left over… for the LORD has blessed his people”), and שָׂבַע + יָתַר + בָּרַךְ stand together in that one verse alone in the Hebrew Bible [T]. Ruth distributes the same three across 2:14–20 [T].

Rated honestly: this is a distributed contact and not a single-verse one, and it is capped at moderate for that reason. Ruth never has the three in one verse; what it has is the same three elements in the same order across five verses, in a chapter already shown to be using gleaning-law vocabulary. The Chronicles verse is Azariah’s report to Hezekiah on the restored tithe, so both texts attach satisfaction-plus-surplus to a blessing [I]. Suggestive, unaudited, and not for the overview.

(e) אֵיפָה at 2:17, and the near-homograph at 2:19

The quantity first. אֵיפָה stands in 29 verses of the Hebrew Bible [T], most of them measures in Leviticus, Numbers and Ezekiel, or honest-weights laws (Lev 19:36; Deut 25:14–15; Amos 8:5; Mic 6:10; Prov 20:10). Four are narrative quantities: Judg 6:19 (Gideon’s ephah of flour), 1 Sam 1:24 (Hannah’s, at Shiloh), 1 Sam 17:17 (Jesse’s parched grain for his sons), and Ruth 2:17 [T]. And the ordered phrase כְּאֵיפָה (“about an ephah”) stands in Ruth 2:17 alone [T].

Two cautions, both necessary.

First, the 1 Samuel company is not evidence. Two of the four narrative ephahs fall in 1 Sam 1 and 1 Sam 17, which are the two chapters of 1 Samuel this project has already flagged as Ruth’s partners (the Hannah register at R29; the Ephrathite verse worked in the 1:1–5 dig) [S: audit]. That is the kind of convergence this book’s audits have repeatedly withdrawn. A measure-word shared between narratives is not an allusion, and no claim is made. Uncertain, recorded so that it is not re-discovered as a finding.

Second, the manna arithmetic is arithmetic. Exod 16:36 defines וְהָעֹמֶר עֲשִׂרִית הָאֵיפָה הוּא (“now an omer is a tenth of an ephah”) [T], so Ruth’s day’s gleaning is ten omers — ten times the daily wilderness ration. That is a calculation from a definition, not a contact, and the 2:1–16 dig established that Ruth’s עֹמֶר (the sheaf) and Exodus 16’s עֹמֶר (the measure) are different lemmas, which removed the strongest-looking pillar of a “Ruth 2 as new manna” reading [S: 2:1–16 dig]. That verdict stands; the arithmetic is reported as background under Historical Background and is not an allusion.

Third, the absolute size. An ephah is on the usual reckoning some 22 litres of barley [S], moderate confidence and resting on a reconstructed measure. The narrator gives the figure and no comment [T]; it is his way of showing the effect of 2:16 [I].

Now the near-homograph, and the reason it is reported here rather than in the Headlines. The four consonants א־י־פ־ה carry two different words two verses apart: אֵיפָה (“an ephah”, 2:17) and אֵיפֹה (“where?”, 2:19) [T]. The two lemmas stand within ten verses of one another nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible — checked at windows of two, five and ten verses [T].

And the uniqueness is worth almost nothing, which the report says rather than hides. אֵיפֹה occurs in 10 verses and אֵיפָה in 29, in a canon of 23,213 verses [T]. The expected number of chance proximities within ten verses is about 10 × 29 × 21 ÷ 23,213 ≈ 0.26, so seeing exactly one is roughly a one-in-four event and is entirely consistent with coincidence. “Unique in the canon” here is a consequence of both words being rare, not evidence of design. What remains is a possible pun with no narratorial signal of any kind — the book that glosses Naomi’s name at 1:20 glosses nothing here [T]. Rated uncertain for intentionality and deliberately kept out of the Headline Findings. A preacher may enjoy it; he may not argue from it.

(f) The כל cluster at 2:20–23

A sound-feature, measurable and capped.

Four consecutive verses carry four tokens built on the consonants כ־ל: כַּלָּה (“daughter-in-law”, 2:20 לְכַלָּתָהּ), כָּלָה (“to finish”, 2:21 כִּלּוּ), כַּלָּה again (2:22 כַּלָּתָהּ), and כָּלָה again (2:23 כְּלוֹת) [T]. Two lexemes, alternating, one per verse. In the book as a whole כַּלָּה stands in 7 verses and כָּלָה in 4 (2:21, 2:23, 3:3, 3:18) [T].

The alternation is on the page and the count is exact. What it means is another matter: the hard rule for sound-based claims caps paronomasia at moderate without commentary or canonical precedent, and there is none here. The most that can be said is that the verses in which the harvest is finished are the verses in which the daughter-in-law is named, twice each, in alternation [I]. A third word shares the consonants — כִּלְיוֹן, the dead son of 1:2, 1:5 and 4:9 [T] — and the 1:1–5 dig rated that name’s etymology uncertain with no authorial signal. No claim is made connecting the three. Moderate on the cluster as a device, uncertain on anything built from it.

(g) דָּבַק (“to cling”) and פָּגַע — two chains, rated differently

דָּבַק stands in four verses of Ruth: 1:14, 2:8, 2:21, 2:23 [T], two of them here. The overview has this chain at high for the chain and moderate for the reading [S: overview], and it is confirmed. What this unit adds is the completion: Boaz commands it (2:8), Ruth reports the command (2:21), Naomi endorses it (2:22), Ruth performs it (2:23) — and 2:23’s last clause is וַתֵּשֶׁב אֶת־חֲמוֹתָהּ, “and she dwelt with her mother-in-law”, so the clinging to Boaz’s women does not displace the clinging of 1:14 [T]. The overview’s reading is right and this unit is where it is demonstrated.

פָּגַע stands in two verses of Ruth: 1:16 and 2:22 [T], and both are negated with the preposition בְּ and a second-person suffix: אַל־תִּפְגְּעִי־בִי (“do not press me”, Ruth to Naomi) and וְלֹא יִפְגְּעוּ־בָךְ (“that they not fall upon you”, Naomi to Ruth) [T].

The honest rating is lower than the symmetry suggests, and the reason is the verb’s frequency. פָּגַע occurs 46 times in 43 verses of the Hebrew Bible [T] — it is not a rare word, so two occurrences in an 85-verse book is unremarkable, and the contact rests entirely on the shared construction and the reversal of direction, not on rarity. The senses also differ sharply: “entreat, press” at 1:16, “fall upon, harm” at 2:22. Moderate at best, [I], and offered as an observation for the preacher rather than a chain: the verb Ruth used to refuse being sent away is the verb Naomi uses of the danger in another field.

(h) גֹּאֵל and קָרוֹב at 2:20

גָּאַל/גֹּאֵל occurs 21 times in 10 verses of Ruth, none before 2:20 — one occurrence in chapter 2, seven in chapter 3, thirteen in chapter 4 [T]. High confidence. The distribution is the plot: the book’s second keyword is introduced in a single clause by a character, and the word then accelerates to the end.

קָרוֹב (“near”) with גֹּאֵל in one verse stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Lev 25:25; Ruth 2:20; 3:12 [T], the sweep’s finding, confirmed. Leviticus 25:25 reads וּבָא גֹאֲלוֹ הַקָּרֹב אֵלָיו (“and his redeemer who is near to him shall come”) [T], source read. High on the vocabulary; moderate that the Levitical procedure is what Naomi is invoking, since the case will turn out not to be Leviticus 25’s case [S: overview].

One further observation on Naomi’s wording, which is hers and not the law’s: she says מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ הוּא, “he is of our redeemers” — a partitive, which concedes in the same breath that there is more than one [T]. 3:12 will name a nearer one. Moderate–high that the partitive is doing that work; the grammar is plain and the sequel confirms it.

Triage, item by item.

(i) 2:18 — category 2, and purely a matter of pointing. The consonants are identical: ותרא can be pointed וַתֵּרֶא (qal, “and she saw”, subject חֲמוֹתָהּ) or וַתַּרְא (hiphil, “and she showed”, subject Ruth, with חֲמוֹתָהּ as object) [T]. The pointing decides who acts, and the two readings tell slightly different stories — Naomi noticing the heap, or Ruth presenting it. Position: MT, with the Greek. The qal is the easier syntax and the hiphil of ראה with a double object is the harder; but more decisively, the next clause already has Ruth as subject (וַתּוֹצֵא וַתִּתֶּן, “and she brought out and gave”), so MT’s reading gives the verse two subjects in sequence, which is what the narrative wants. Moderate confidence. Preaching implication: small but real — on the variant, Ruth displays her day’s work; on MT, Naomi registers it unprompted. Do not build on either.

(ii) 2:19 — category 2, and it bears on Headline 3. Both Greek editions have Ruth tell her mother-in-law where she had worked, matching Naomi’s two questions; MT has her tell her what she had done with him [T]. Position: MT. The Greek is the smoother reading and removes a mismatch between question and answer; a translator or a scribe repairing a swerve is far likelier than one creating it. Moderate–high confidence. This is the exegetically significant variant in the unit: the Hebrew’s question-and-answer mismatch, in which Naomi asks twice about a place and is told a name, is precisely what the Greek tradition levelled — which is good evidence that the mismatch was felt as a difficulty early, and therefore that it is original [I].

(iii) 2:20 — category 2, and the crux. BHS records one Hebrew manuscript and the Syriac reading יהוה [T] — that is, בָּרוּךְ יהוה, “blessed be the LORD”, which would make the subject of the relative clause unambiguous and would match Gen 24:27 exactly, the one other verse in the canon carrying the clause אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָזַב חַסְדּוֹ [T].

Position: MT, with some confidence, and the reasoning is worth setting out because the whole crux turns on it. Three considerations converge. (a) MT’s construction is unique. בָּרוּךְ הוּא לַיהוָה stands as an ordered phrase in Ruth 2:20 alone in the Hebrew Bible, plain and skeletal [T], where בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה is a common formula. A unique reading surrounded by a common one is the classic candidate for assimilation, and assimilation runs toward the familiar, not away from it. (b) The variant produces an exact match with Gen 24:27, which is the direction a harmonising scribe moves. (c) Both Greek editions keep the ambiguity [T], so the witness is not narrowly Masoretic. Moderate–high confidence that MT is original.

What follows for preaching, and it is the unit’s most useful single result. The ambiguity is not a defect in the Hebrew, nor a modern scholarly invention: it is the original reading, it was felt as a difficulty early enough to be smoothed in one Hebrew manuscript and in the Syriac, and the ESV smooths it again. Whether the openness is deliberate artistry or simply loose Hebrew the report cannot say [I] — but the two candidates are theologically complementary rather than competing, and Naomi has just been handed a reason to bless both the man and the God [I]. Moderate on the deliberateness; the textual position above is firmer than the literary one.

(iv) 2:21 — category 2, and the most substantive of the five. MT has וַתֹּאמֶר רוּת הַמּוֹאֲבִיָּה (“and Ruth the Moabitess said”); all three ancient versions omit the gentilic, and the Greek supplies “to her mother-in-law” in its place [T]. Position: MT. The gentilic is one of seven verses in which the book applies the label to Ruth — 1:4, 1:22, 2:2, 2:6, 2:21, 4:5, 4:10 [T], and the versions’ omission is most simply explained as a translator finding it redundant in a dialogue with two named speakers. Moderate confidence.

The preaching implication is real and the Hebrew’s choice is pointed. MT insists on “the Moabitess” at the one moment in the unit when Ruth is reporting a privilege — Boaz’s instruction that she stay with his own workers for the whole harvest [T]. The label is attached at the point of favour, not at the point of disadvantage. The versions remove it and the modern English versions keep it (NASB95 “the Moabitess”, ESV “the Moabite”, NIV84 “the Moabitess”) [T], so the congregation does hear it — but the preacher should know that the ancient tradition found it dispensable and the Hebrew did not.

(v) 2:23 — category 2, pointing again, and the third homograph crux in three consecutive units. The consonants ותשב can be וַתֵּשֶׁב (from יָשַׁב, “and she dwelt”) or וַתָּשָׁב (from שׁוּב, “and she returned”) [T], and BHS records a few manuscripts and the Vulgate reading וַתָּשָׁב אֶל, “and she returned to her mother-in-law” [T]. Position: MT, with the Greek’s καὶ ἐκάθισεν (“and she sat/dwelt”). MT gives the coda a settled, durative sense that matches the summary function of the verse — she gleaned through two harvests and lived with her mother-in-law — whereas “returned to” reads as a single action and sits oddly after a clause about months of harvest. Moderate–high confidence. And the choice matters: on MT the chapter ends by establishing a household; on the variant it ends by reporting a journey home.

A distribution worth recording and not yet a pattern. This is the third crux in three consecutive preaching units to turn on a homograph rather than a corruption — עָנָה at 1:21 (testify / afflict), שִׁבְתָּהּ at 2:7 (יָשַׁב / שָׁבַת), and ותשב here (יָשַׁב / שׁוּב) [T]; and two of the three involve יָשַׁב. The 2:1–16 dig recorded the first two and said plainly that “two instances is not a pattern and is not claimed as one” [S: 2:1–16 dig]. Three is not a pattern either, and no baseline has been run — nobody has asked how many homographic cruxes a comparable Hebrew narrative of 85 verses carries. Recorded as a distribution, capped, queued for the audit, and not to be preached as a feature of the book.

9. Tone and Feel

Register: relief, and the particular warmth of a household talking. After five verses of famine and death (1:1–5), a lament (1:20–21) and a day of anxious labour among strangers (2:1–16), this is the first stretch of the book in which two people simply talk to each other at home about something that has gone well.

What carries it:

  • The quantity, stated flatly and left to do its own work. וַיְהִי כְּאֵיפָה שְׂעֹרִים (“and it was about an ephah of barley”) [T]. No exclamation, no comment. The narrator lets a number carry the feeling, which is the same technique he used for the losses at 1:3 and 1:5.
  • Verbs of carrying. וַתִּשָּׂא וַתָּבוֹא … וַתּוֹצֵא וַתִּתֶּן (“she took it up and came… she brought out and gave”) [T] — four verbs in half a verse, all of them about moving something from where it was to where it is needed. The physical weight of the ephah is in the syntax.
  • Questions. Naomi’s two at v.19 are the first questions anyone has asked in the book since 1:11–13, where they were rhetorical and bitter [T]. These are ordinary and eager.
  • The one moment of gravity. Verse 20 slows: a blessing, a relative clause reaching back to “the living and the dead”, and then a flat legal statement. The two halves of the verse are tonally unlike each other, and the doubled “and Naomi said” marks the gear-change [T].
  • The warning at v.22. The unit’s one shadow, and it is placed last-but-one: וְלֹא יִפְגְּעוּ־בָךְ (“that they not fall upon you”) [T]. The scene is safe; elsewhere is not.

Soundtrack. Domestic and unhurried — two voices across a table, with one bar of something graver at v.20. Not triumphant: nothing has yet been secured, and the woman doing the blessing is still the woman of 1:21.

The preacher’s note. The relief is real and should be allowed. But the unit is not a happy ending and the text does not treat it as one: the harvest finishes at 2:23 and with it the gleaning, which is why Naomi is planning at 3:1 [T]. Preach the warmth and keep the clock running.

Deuteronomy 24:19–21 → Ruth 2:17, 2:19–20 (high confidence on the wording; moderate–high on the legal completion) [T] for the data, [I] for the completion

The unit’s principal external contact, and new to this run.

Move 1 — Source context, read. Deut 24:19–21 is a block of three consecutive provisions, each closing with the same triad [T]:

24:19 When you reap your harvest in your field and have forgotten a sheaf in the field, you shall not go back to get it; it shall be for the sojourner, for the orphan and for the widow, לְמַעַן יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ בְּכֹל מַעֲשֵׂה יָדֶיךָ — “that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands”. 24:20 When you beat (תַחְבֹּט) your olive tree, you shall not go over the boughs again; it shall be for the sojourner, for the orphan and for the widow. 24:21 When you gather your vineyard, you shall not glean it afterward; it shall be for the sojourner, for the orphan and for the widow.

Its function in its own passage: the block sits in a run of social provisions in Deuteronomy’s law code, and it is the only one of the three verses that attaches a promise. The promise is a blessing on the provider, and it is explicitly YHWH’s to give [T].

The formula is measurable. לְמַעַן יְבָרֶכְךָ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ stands as an ordered phrase in three verses of the Hebrew Bible — Deut 14:29; 23:21; 24:19 [T], plain and skeletal. Two of the three attach it to provision for the same triad (14:29, the third-year tithe for the Levite, sojourner, orphan and widow) [T].

Move 2 — Book usage. Deuteronomy 24:19–21 is a live source for Ruth on the audit’s own verdict, which relocated the chapter’s gleaning vocabulary from Isaiah to this block and rated Deuteronomy 24 the 26th-densest chapter of the canon against Ruth 2 [S: audit, R18]. This is its second use in the same chapter: the 2:1–16 dig showed Boaz’s order at 2:16 phrased in Leviticus’s two verbs [S: 2:1–16 dig], and this unit uses Deuteronomy’s verb at 2:17 and delivers Deuteronomy’s promise at 2:19–20. The two statutes are used in sequence, one per half-chapter, and neither is cited [I]. That is a Move 2 finding the overview cannot currently show. Moderate–high confidence.

Move 3 — OT-to-OT. Leviticus 19:9–10, Leviticus 23:22 and Deuteronomy 24:19–21 are already in canonical conversation as the three gleaning provisions, and the relationship between them is one of the standard questions of Pentateuchal study [S]. Ruth does not put them in conversation; it uses them. One asymmetry between them is doing work here and is checkable: בָּרַךְ occurs twice in the whole of Leviticus, at 9:22 and 9:23, and in neither gleaning chapter [T]. So the blessing is Deuteronomy’s distinctive attachment to the provision, and it is Deuteronomy’s version that this unit completes.

What it adds. Three things, in descending confidence. (1) The verb Ruth uses at 2:17 is the verb of the provision at Deut 24:20, a five-verse word [T]. (2) The law attaches to that provision a promise of blessing from YHWH on the provider, in a three-verse formula [T]. (3) Therefore when Naomi — a widow, one of the law’s named beneficiaries — pronounces בָּרוּךְ twice over the man who left the grain, the law’s promise is being discharged by the law’s beneficiary, uncited, in the law’s own vocabulary [I]. Moderate–high on (3), and it is the most preachable finding in the unit: the chapter shows a statute working in both directions, command and promise, without anyone quoting it.

A caution to keep (3) honest. בָּרַךְ is a common verb and the contact is not a rare-word contact; it is a structural one, and it rests on the chapter having already been shown to be using this legal stock. Stated as a lexical rarity it would be worthless; stated as the completion of a transaction it is strong. The report states it the second way.

12. Genre

Hebrew prose narrative, and within it reported dialogue — the first sustained two-party conversation in the book since 1:8–18. The ordinary reading rules apply: the wayyiqtol chain carries the main line; the events are presented as having happened; characters’ statements are theirs and not the narrator’s.

Two rules earn their place here.

First, characters can be wrong, and one of this unit’s two most quoted lines is a character’s. Naomi’s theology at 2:20 and her legal inference at 2:20b are both hers. The legal inference will turn out to be incomplete — there is a nearer redeemer she does not mention and may not know of (3:12) [T]. A sermon that treats 2:20 as the narrator’s doctrinal statement has made a genre error, and the same error would make 1:21 the narrator’s doctrine too.

Second, a summary clause is not a scene. Verse 23 covers weeks or months in nine words [T]. Its proportions carry no evaluative weight, and the compression is not the narrator’s comment on how the time passed.

Brief and uncontested.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits. The judges’ period, as 1:1 states [T], at the barley-and-wheat harvest of a single year — late spring into early summer on the ordinary reckoning [S], moderate confidence. The two statutes the unit is using are already in force in the narrative world [I].

Where the book sits — canonical position. Ruth stands in the Ketuvim, fourth in the sequence BHS prints [T]; English Bibles and the Greek tradition place it between Judges and 1 Samuel [S]. The Proverbs-adjacency argument is withdrawn and may not be preached: Baba Batra 14b places Ruth first among the Writings, the head-of-the-Writings position antedates the move into the Megillot, and the Leningrad placement may itself have been prompted by the אֵשֶׁת חַיִל link at 3:11 [S: overview v1.4, Logos L1]. The אֵשֶׁת חַיִל relation stands at moderate–high; the adjacency at nil.

What the position does imply here. The reader of the Writings arrives post-exilic, knowing Torah and the full Prophets [S], and therefore knowing the gleaning statutes as law that was not kept — which is much of what the Prophets are about. A scene in which a landowner exceeds the provision and a widow blesses him for it reads differently to that reader than to one who has only Judges behind him [I]. Moderate confidence, and it is the canonical position’s one real contribution to this unit.

Canonical and compositional order. Unchanged: the events fall in the judges’ period [T]; the Hebrew canon files the book among the Writings [T]; the English and Greek canons after Judges [S]. The direction of dependence between Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chr 2:5–15 is open [T]. Both reported, neither preferred.

Where the reader sits. This side of the cross. What has happened in between that bears on this unit: the law whose promise Naomi discharges has been fulfilled rather than abolished, and the one who kept it is the redeemer the word גֹּאֵל is waiting for [I]. The ephah carried home is not a type of anything; the category introduced in the same conversation is.

How that reframes the reading. It stops the unit being a story about a good day. The reader knows that the word Naomi has just said is the word the rest of the book runs on, and that the line being preserved is the line of the Christ. Naomi does not know either of those things, and the sermon should not transfer the reader’s knowledge to her.

15. Who Am I?

  • Ruth is, in this unit, the one who works and hands over. She is a legitimate example at 2:18 and not a type of anyone here (Tool 13).
  • Naomi is the figure the reader is positioned with, and the position is genuine but partial: she has a name, a kinsman and a category, and she does not yet have a plan, a redemption, or an answer to 1:21. Identification with Naomi here is identification with someone who has been given enough to hope and not enough to rest [I].
  • Boaz is offstage, and the Christ-material in the chapter is his (2:15–16, per the 2:1–16 dig). In this unit he is a name being spoken, which is its own point.
  • The reader knows more than either woman — the narrator told us at 2:1 and 2:3 what Naomi learns at 2:19–20 [T]. The honest identification is not with a character but with the position of knowing the end of a story someone else is still inside.

The misidentification to name. Reading Naomi’s blessing as the reader’s own resolution — “and so her faith was restored” — makes the unit about a change of heart the text does not report, and skips the part the text does report: that she immediately started doing legal arithmetic.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. To see a statute working in both directions without anybody quoting it: a man who left more grain than the law required, and a widow who pronounced over him the blessing the law promised. And then to hear the word the whole second half of the book needs — redeemer — arrive in the mouth of the person with least cause to expect one [I].

Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.

Domain Content
Worldview Provision in this book arrives through ordinary legal arrangements and the people who exceed them. God is named here only in a blessing, by a character; the narrator still says nothing (Tool 6). A providence that works through a landowner’s discretion and a kinsman’s standing is the kind this unit teaches [I]
Behaviour — start Handing over the saved portion, as Ruth does at 2:18 — the imitable act in the unit, unasked for and unremarked. And blessing the people whose ordinary generosity has carried you, by name
Behaviour — stop Treating a good day as a settled question. Naomi blesses and then immediately counts kinsmen; the unit does not end in rest and neither should the sermon
Motivation The law’s own: that the LORD your God may bless you in all the work of your hands (Deut 24:19). Not a transaction, but a promise attached by God to generosity — and in this chapter, discharged [T]

Four audiences.

Audience What this unit says
For me The generosity I have received was mostly arranged behind my back, as Boaz’s was (2:15–16). The question this unit puts is whether I bless the people responsible by name, or receive it as weather
For a Christian friend For someone who has had one good day after a long run of bad ones: Naomi’s response is the model — bless, and then keep thinking. Relief is not the end of the story and does not have to pretend to be
For the church The chapter shows a community in which a statute meant for the destitute was actually operated, and operated generously. That is an uncomfortable mirror for any congregation with the means to do the same
For an unbeliever A foreign woman with no legal standing is here provided for, defended, and spoken of with respect by the household she married into. The book makes no argument about it; it just reports it. What it will go on to claim is that this is how the line of its king was kept alive

Prayer in response.

  • Thank you for provision that arrived through arrangements I did not see.
  • Sorry for receiving it without blessing anyone by name.
  • Thank you that the word Naomi said here is the word the book — and the gospel — turns on.
  • Please make this congregation one where your provisions for the poor are actually operated, and exceeded.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • An unmarked relative clause carrying the whole crux. אֲשֶׁר לֹא־עָזַב חַסְדּוֹ (2:20). Biblical Hebrew does not inflect אֲשֶׁר for gender, number or person and supplies no device to disambiguate competing antecedents [T]. Both הוּא and יְהוָה are available, and the clause’s verb (3ms) fits either. The ambiguity is a property of the language, not of the manuscript — which is why a scribe could only resolve it by changing a word, and one did (Textual Variants).
  • Doubled speech formula inside one verse. וַתֹּאמֶר נָעֳמִי לְכַלָּתָהּ … וַתֹּ֧אמֶר לָהּ נָעֳמִי (2:20) [T]. Hebrew narrative uses a fresh verb of speaking to mark a distinct speech-act rather than a continuation; the blessing and the legal identification are being kept apart.
  • Three forms of עָשָׂה in one verse (2:19): עָשִׂית (2fs, Naomi’s question), עָשְׂתָה (3fs, the narrator’s summary), עָשִׂיתִי (1cs, Ruth’s answer) [T]. The same verb in three persons tracks the shift of perspective through the verse.
  • Two interrogatives of place, then an answer of person (2:19): אֵיפֹה … וְאָנָה … שֵׁם הָאִישׁ [T]. See Headline 3.
  • The infinitive construct with עַד three times — עַד־הָעָרֶב (17), עַד אִם־כִּלּוּ (21), עַד־כְּלוֹת (23) [T]. Each closes a span, and the last closes two chapters.
  • Privative/partitive מִן twice — מִשָּׂבְעָהּ (“from her satisfaction”, 18) and מִגֹּאֲלֵנוּ (“of our redeemers”, 20) [T]. Both do real work: the first says the gift came out of her own sufficiency, the second that the redeemer is one of a set.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period (narrated): the judges’ era [T]; within it, the barley-into-wheat harvest of one year, which on the ordinary agricultural reckoning runs from about April into early June in the Judahite hill country [S]. Moderate confidence.
  • Location: Boaz’s field outside Bethlehem, and the town itself — “the city” at 2:18 [T]. The distance is short enough for a day-labourer to carry a load home on foot in the evening [I].
  • Economic context: a subsistence grain economy with no stored surplus for the landless, in which gleaning was the statutory mechanism by which the destitute ate [S].

What the original audience knew without being told

Six items, each bearing on how these seven verses read.

  1. How much an ephah is, and that it is a great deal. On the usual reckoning some 22 litres of barley, perhaps 13–14 kg [S], moderate confidence and dependent on a reconstructed measure — enough to feed two women for something between a fortnight and a month. The narrator gives the number and no comment [T]; the first hearers supplied the astonishment.
  2. That beating out is the poor person’s threshing. חָבַט is done with a stick, by hand, by one person — not on a threshing floor with oxen and a sledge, which is what a landowner does and what Boaz is doing at 3:2 [S], moderate–high. The contrast between 2:17 and 3:2 is a contrast of scale and station, and the Hebrew marks it with two different procedures [I].
  3. That an ephah is ten omers. Exod 16:36 says so [T]. A hearer who knew the wilderness ration had a ready scale for the day’s work — ten days’ manna. This is arithmetic from a definition, not an allusion, and the 2:1–16 dig showed that Ruth’s עֹמֶר is a different lemma from Exodus 16’s, which removed the “new manna” reading [S: 2:1–16 dig]. The scale is background; the allusion is not claimed.
  4. That gleaning was dangerous for a woman without a household behind her. Naomi’s warning at 2:22 names it plainly and the text does not elaborate [T]. A field full of hired men, a foreign woman, no male relative present: the first hearers needed no explanation, and the modern reader often supplies too little rather than too much [S], moderate–high.
  5. What a גֹּאֵל could and could not do. The institution covered the redemption of alienated land and of persons in debt-bondage, and it fell to the nearest kinsman [S]. Naomi’s two words at 2:20 are a legal claim about Boaz’s standing, and the first hearers heard a claim, not a compliment [I].
  6. That the harvest ending meant the gleaning ending. 2:23’s “end of the barley harvest and the wheat harvest” is not a happy ending but a deadline [T]; which is why 3:1 opens with Naomi seeking מָנוֹחַ (“security”) for Ruth [S], moderate–high.

What this changes about how we read

  • Items 1, 2 and 6 together change the unit’s genre of good news. A large quantity, got by the humblest method, inside a season that is about to close. The relief is real and temporary, and the text structures it that way.
  • Item 4 changes v.22. A modern reader can hear Naomi’s warning as fussing. The first hearers heard a real hazard being managed, and the honest sermon says so without dwelling on it.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: Ketuvim. The reader of the Writings arrives having read the Torah and the whole of the Prophets through to Malachi — post-exilic, knowing David, knowing how the monarchy ended, living with promises unresolved [S].
  • What the canonical reader knows that bears on this unit: the three gleaning statutes and their promise [T]; the redemption provisions of Leviticus 25 [T]; the servant’s blessing at the well in Genesis 24 [T]; and the prophetic indictment of Israel for not keeping exactly these laws [S]. That last item is what gives the unit its edge for this reader: a scene in which the provision is operated generously, and the beneficiary blesses the provider, is a scene the Prophets had taught him not to expect.
  • Canonical register: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah and Chronicles [S]. Chronicles is live here, supplying the one verse in the canon that carries this unit’s satisfied/left-over/blessed triple (2 Chr 31:10) [T] — and, as there, in a context of restored provision. The Proverbs adjacency supplies nothing [S: Logos L1].

The first hearers

  • Who they were: Israelites who knew the agricultural year, the measures, the statutes and the institution of redemption. The narrative-world audience is the household; the compositional audience is contested; the canonical audience is the reader of the Writings [S: overview], and interpretation defaults to the last.
  • Their situation: living with a law they knew and a record of its neglect.
  • What they brought: the six background items above, and an ear for a blessing formula — which is why the unique shape of 2:20 would have registered as unusual to them in a way it does not to us [I]. Moderate confidence.

Where the passage fits their world

Three things they would have heard that we do not naturally pick up:

  1. The size of the ephah, immediately (item 1). We read a measure; they heard an answer to prayer.
  2. The oddity of בָּרוּךְ הוּא לַיהוָה. A hearer who knew בָּרוּךְ יְהוָה as the standard formula would notice the substitution [I]. Moderate — the construction’s uniqueness is certain, what an ancient ear made of it is not.
  3. The legal weight of 2:20b. “He is of our redeemers” is a claim with procedural consequences, and the first hearers would have begun doing the arithmetic Naomi is doing [I].

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: nothing in the events. What would have struck them is the quantity, and the fact that a landowner’s generosity to a Moabite widow is reported without any defence of it [T].
  • Surprising: that the law’s promised blessing is pronounced by the widow rather than prayed by the man [I]; and that Naomi, whose last word on YHWH was an accusation, is the one who says it [T].
  • Comforting: the first unambiguous comfort in the book. Enough food, a named protector, a legal category, and a plan becoming possible [T].
  • Disturbing: v.22. Safety is local and conditional, and the warning tells the hearer what the rest of the world is like [T].
  • Familiar: the blessing exchange, the gleaning, the measures, the kinship vocabulary. Most of the unit is furniture; the two unfamiliar things are the construction at 2:20 and the quantity at 2:17.

What we bring that they didn’t

  • A reading of 2:20 as a doctrinal statement rather than a character’s blessing with a legal rider attached.
  • Sentimentality about gleaning, which was a statutory provision for the destitute, not a pastoral idyll.
  • Impatience with the ambiguity — a modern reader wants the antecedent settled, and both the Syriac tradition and the ESV have obliged.
  • No feel for the measures, so the one number in the unit lands as a word rather than as a quantity.

Implication for the sermon

Recovering items 1, 2 and 6 — the size, the method and the deadline — stops the unit being preached as resolution. And recovering the oddity of 2:20’s construction turns what looks like a doctrinal proof-text into what it is: the first good word about God in this book, spoken by the woman who had accused him, with its grammar leaving open whether she means God or the man God used. That is a better sermon than either of the resolved readings, and it is what the Hebrew supports.

Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, selected by textual signal and purpose connection; one named and declined.

(1) Law, and the provision it makes for those outside the household

  • Where this passage sits: at the point where a gleaning statute has been operated generously and its promised blessing is discharged — by the beneficiary, in the statute’s own vocabulary, with the statute uncited [T].
  • What comes before: Lev 19:9–10 and 23:22 (the edge of the field, with לָקַט and עָזַב, the pair Boaz uses at 2:16); Deut 24:19–21 (the forgotten sheaf, the olive tree with חָבַט, the vineyard, each for the sojourner, orphan and widow, and the first with the promise of blessing) [T]. And behind them, the prophetic record of these provisions ignored [S].
  • What comes after: the redemption procedure of Leviticus 25 entering at this very verse (2:20) and controlling chapters 3 and 4 [T]; and the New Testament’s treatment of the law as fulfilled rather than abolished, with its own provision for the poor framed as fellowship rather than statute [S].
  • What this contributes: the unit shows law working as law was meant to — generously at the top and gratefully at the bottom — and it does so without anyone appealing to it. That is the strongest argument against preaching Ruth as a story about exceptional individuals: what the chapter actually exhibits is an institution functioning. Confidence: high that the theme is textually signalled (the verb at 2:17, the blessing at 2:19–20, the absence of בָּרַךְ from the Leviticus chapters); moderate–high on the completion reading.

(2) Covenant kindness — חֶסֶד — and who is credited with it

  • Where this passage sits: at the middle of the book’s three uses of חֶסֶד, and the one where the owner of the kindness is left unstated [T].
  • What comes before: Naomi’s wish at 1:8 that YHWH do חֶסֶד with her daughters-in-law, as they had done with the dead [T]; and behind it, the great covenant uses — including Gen 24:27, the one other verse carrying this clause [T].
  • What comes after: Boaz’s “your latter kindness is better than the first” at 3:10, the third and last use, again in speech [T]; and the canon’s long development of חֶסֶד as the word for God’s committed loyalty, into which the New Testament’s grace-vocabulary is translated [S].
  • What this contributes: all three occurrences are in characters’ mouths and never the narrator’s [T], and the middle one does not say whose kindness it is. The book’s central theological word is therefore never asserted by its author and, at its pivot, not even attributed. That is not vagueness; it is the form the book’s doctrine of providence takes — God’s kindness is visible only in what people do, and the text declines to separate the two [I]. Confidence: high on the distribution; moderate–high on the reading.

How the two relate

(c) Climactic convergence. Not parallel and not merely mutually illuminating: they meet in one clause. The kindness whose owner 2:20 leaves open is the kindness the law of Deut 24:19 attributes to YHWH and asks a man to enact — so the grammatical ambiguity and the legal structure are saying the same thing. That is why the verse can be read both ways without damage, and it is the unit’s theological centre. A sermon that resolves the grammar loses the convergence.

Declined

Exodus / new exodus. The ephah-as-ten-omers arithmetic makes a manna reading available, and the 2:1–16 dig dismantled its lexical pillar (Ruth’s עֹמֶר is a different lemma from Exodus 16’s), leaving a capped, unbaselined three-verb observation [S: 2:1–16 dig]. Nothing in this unit strengthens it. Not entered.

Christological Reading

Type of Christological connection

Typology through an established category, and trajectory. The category is redeemer, and this unit is where the book names it.

The four typology tests, scored:

  1. Theological-category test — passes. גֹּאֵל is an established institution with a legal content, not an incidental detail [T].
  2. NT-precedent test — passes. The New Testament applies redemption language to Christ’s work directly and repeatedly, and the archetype it reasons from is Israel’s rescue [S]. The connection is of a kind the NT itself makes.
  3. Escalation test — passes. Boaz redeems a field and a widow within a kinship system that obliges him to consider it; what the NT claims is redemption of those with no kinship claim at all, at a cost no statute required [S].
  4. Authorial-pattern test — passes for the book, and this unit is the evidence. The word is withheld through 2:1–19, enters in a character’s inference at 2:20, and then takes over: 21 occurrences in 10 verses, accelerating to the climax at 4:14 [T]. The book builds the category deliberately.

All four pass: high confidence typology at the category level.

How the passage points to Christ

  • By naming the category, not by portraying the figure. Boaz is absent from this scene [T]. What the unit contributes is the word, and the fact that the word arrives as an inference drawn by someone in need rather than as a title conferred by the narrator [I]. That is how the category works in the canon too: it is claimed by the needy before it is proclaimed.
  • By completing the law’s promise rather than suspending it. The blessing Naomi pronounces is the one the statute attached (Headline 1). Christ’s redemption is presented in the NT as the law’s fulfilment, not its cancellation [S], and this unit is a small working model: the law operated, exceeded, and blessed.
  • By leaving the kindness unattributed. 2:20 does not separate God’s חֶסֶד from the man’s [T]. The incarnation is the point at which that refusal to separate stops being a literary effect [I]. Moderate confidence — this is canonical reflection, and it is offered as such.
  • The book’s own resolution. 4:14 blesses YHWH for not leaving Naomi without a redeemer [T]. The last word in the book on the question 2:20 opens credits God — without settling the grammar of 2:20 [I].

Trajectory

  • What the passage anticipates: a redeemer who is near, who has the right, and who will act. It supplies the category and the candidate and neither the procedure nor the outcome.
  • What Christ fulfils: the category, escalated past the kinship system that defines it — a redeemer for those with no claim [S].
  • What still remains: the position Naomi is in at the end of v.20 — enough to hope, not enough to rest. The harvest still ends at 2:23.

Moralism check

  • The “be like X” temptation: here it is “be like Naomi — bless God in your bitterness”, and it requires the text to report a change of heart it does not report (Tools 6, 13). The secondary temptation is “be generous like Boaz”, which takes a character who is not in the scene.
  • The gospel grounding: the blessing in this unit is the law’s promise being kept, and the kindness is God’s before it is anyone’s — which is precisely what the ambiguity of 2:20 will not let us separate [T].
  • Christ as hero, not example: the hero of this unit is a word. Nobody in it does anything decisive; a category is named, and the naming is what makes the rest of the book possible. The right sermon makes the redeemer the subject, not Naomi’s recovery.

Confidence: high on the typology at the category level and on the distribution that supports it; moderate on the reading of 2:20’s ambiguity as theologically freighted rather than merely syntactic.

Difficult / Contested Verses

Four live issues.

2:20 — Whose kindness has not been forsaken?

  • Category: doctrinal / translation-driven; the unit’s principal crux and the one the sweep referred here.
  • The difficulty: Hebrew does not mark the antecedent of אֲשֶׁר, and both the blessed man and YHWH are available [T]. Three English versions take three positions (Tool 8).
  • Common mishandlings: quoting the ESV’s resolved reading as though the Hebrew said it; or treating the ambiguity as a modern scholarly invention.
  • Rhetorical function in context: a blessing, with a legal rider attached in the second half of the verse. It is not a doctrinal proposition, and the verse’s own second half shows what Naomi is actually driving at.
  • The main interpretive options: (a) YHWH’s kindness — the Genesis formula’s sense, and the ESV’s; (b) Boaz’s kindness — the NIV84’s; (c) deliberately both, the ambiguity being the point.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the textual evidence settles the originality of the ambiguity and does not settle the reference. MT’s construction is unique in the canon; one Hebrew manuscript and the Syriac resolve it toward YHWH by reading יהוה; both Greek editions keep it open [T]. MT is the harder and rarer reading and is almost certainly original (moderate–high). On the reference, (a) and (c) are both defensible and the book’s own last word at 4:14 blesses YHWH for the redeemer [T], which tells gently for (a) or (c) over (b). Moderate on (c), which this report favours without insisting.
  • Suggested handling: make this the sermon’s centre, not a footnote. Say that the Hebrew leaves it open, that the openness is original, and that the two readings are complementary rather than rival — the kindness is God’s, and it arrives as a man’s. Read the NASB95 at this verse even if the ESV is in the pews.

2:22 — “lest you be assaulted in another field”

  • Category: pastoral; and the unit’s landmine.
  • The difficulty: Naomi’s warning presupposes that a foreign woman gleaning among hired men was at risk of sexual violence, and the text neither elaborates nor softens it [T].
  • Common mishandlings: passing over it as ancient local colour; or expanding it into a lurid aside. Both fail hearers for whom this is not historical.
  • Rhetorical function in context: Naomi’s reason for endorsing Boaz’s instruction — the one causal clause in the unit (Tool 4). The warning exists to explain why staying in this field matters.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the Hebrew is restrained — פָּגַע with בְּ, “fall upon” — and the restraint is the text’s, not a translator’s [T]. The book has already had Boaz forbid his men to touch her (2:9) and order that she not be shamed (2:15) [T], so the hazard is established by the chapter and not imported.
  • Suggested handling: name it once, plainly, and move on. Some hearers will need it named; none needs it dwelt on. The chapter’s own response to the danger is a protective arrangement, and that is the preachable point.

2:20 and 1:21 — has Naomi changed her mind?

  • Category: doctrinal / pastoral.
  • The difficulty: the same woman has said YHWH testified against her (1:21) and that his kindness has not been forsaken (2:20) [T], and the narrator adjudicates neither.
  • Common mishandlings: preaching 2:20 as a conversion or a repentance; or using it to correct 1:21, which the book does not do. The sweep flagged this and it is right [S: sweep].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the text records a blessing, not a retraction [T]. She does not withdraw 1:21 and she is never again called Mara [T]. What the narrator gives is two statements and no verdict (Tool 6).
  • Suggested handling: preach both, and the silence between them. A congregation containing people who have said 1:21 out loud will be helped by a book that lets both sentences stand.

2:17 — the ephah

  • Category: apologetic, mildly; and a place where over-precision discredits a sermon.
  • The difficulty: the quantity is the unit’s one hard number and its absolute value rests on a reconstructed measure [S].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the figure is large relative to a day’s gleaning and the narrator plainly means it to be noticed [I]; the litres and kilograms are moderate confidence at best, and the manna comparison is arithmetic from Exod 16:36, not an allusion (Tool 7e).
  • Suggested handling: say “a great deal — a fortnight’s grain or more” and attribute the precision to the commentators rather than to the text. Do not preach it as a miracle; the text explains it at 2:15–16.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: preaching the ESV’s resolved 2:20 as what the Hebrew says

  • What it looks like: “Naomi says it: the LORD’s kindness has not forsaken the living or the dead. Whatever she said in chapter 1, here she gets God right.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the Hebrew does not say whose kindness, and the construction that leaves it open stands in this verse alone in the canon [T]. One Hebrew manuscript and the Syriac resolved it the ESV’s way by changing a word; both Greek editions did not [T]. The resolved reading is a translator’s decision, and the NIV84 makes the opposite one.
  • The corrective: preach the openness. Say that the kindness is God’s and that it arrived as a man’s, and that the Hebrew declines to separate them — which is the book’s whole doctrine of providence in one unmarked relative pronoun. Read the NASB95 here.

Pitfall: making this Naomi’s conversion

  • What it looks like: a sermon arc in which bitter Naomi of 1:20–21 becomes believing Naomi of 2:20, with the blessing as the turning point.
  • Why it’s wrong: the text records a blessing, not a retraction [T]. She does not withdraw 1:21, the narrator passes no verdict (Tool 6), and her very next move is legal arithmetic, not worship. The book lets both her sentences stand.
  • The corrective: preach two true sentences from one mouth with the narrator’s silence between them. That is more use to a congregation than a tidy arc, and it is what is on the page.

Pitfall: missing that the law is the hero

  • What it looks like: “Boaz was a remarkably generous man and Ruth a remarkably hard worker” — a sermon about two exceptional individuals.
  • Why it’s wrong: the chapter is built on two statutes. Boaz’s order at 2:16 is phrased in Leviticus’s own two verbs, a pair standing in three verses of the canon [T]; Ruth’s verb at 2:17 is the verb of the Deuteronomic provision, a five-verse word [T]; and Naomi’s blessing is the blessing Deuteronomy attaches to it, in a three-verse formula [T]. What the chapter exhibits is an institution working, generously, at both ends.
  • The corrective: show the law. It makes the generosity legible rather than merely nice, and it is the honest route from this chapter to the gospel’s claim that the law was kept and fulfilled rather than set aside.

Pitfall: preaching 2:17 as a miracle

  • What it looks like: “She went out with nothing and came home with an ephah — God multiplied her gleaning.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the narrator has already explained the quantity, at 2:15–16, and the explanation is that Boaz told his men to leave extra [T]. Nothing is multiplied; something is arranged. And the figure’s absolute size rests on a reconstructed measure [S].
  • The corrective: preach the concealment instead — the arrangement was made behind Ruth’s back, which is both the more interesting fact and the one the 2:1–16 dig identified as the chapter’s omitted type-feature. Providence here looks exactly like a landowner’s instruction.

Pitfall: skipping v.22, or dwelling on it

  • What it looks like: either silence about the warning, or an extended excursus on the vulnerability of women in the ancient Near East.
  • Why it’s wrong: the Hebrew is restrained and the hazard is established by the chapter itself (2:9, 2:15) [T]. Silence fails the hearers for whom this is current; expansion fails the same hearers differently.
  • The corrective: one plain sentence, and then the chapter’s own answer — a protective arrangement in a particular field. The text manages the danger rather than discussing it.

Pitfall: reading 2:23 as the happy ending

  • What it looks like: “And so Ruth gleaned through the harvest and lived with Naomi — provision secured.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the verse reports the end of the harvest [T], which is the end of gleaning and therefore of income. That is why 3:1 opens with Naomi seeking security, and why 2:23’s last clause matters — she is still living in her mother-in-law’s house, with no settlement of any kind [T].
  • The corrective: end the sermon where the unit ends, with a deadline and a category. The relief is real; nothing has been secured.
