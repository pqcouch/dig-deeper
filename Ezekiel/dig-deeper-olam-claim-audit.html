Claim Audit: עוֹלָם (“for ever”) in the Torah’s Statutes and Covenants — Ezekiel 37:24–26 and Hebrews 13:20

Passages audited: every verse of the Hebrew Bible in which עוֹלָם (“perpetuity, for ever”) stands within three words of בְּרִית (“covenant”), חֻקָּה (“statute”) or חֹק (“statute, due”), with the Septuagint rendering of each; the co-occurrence of לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם (“throughout your generations”) and its third-person form; Ezekiel 37:24–26, with Ezek 16:59–62 and 46:13–15, as the test case; Hebrews 13:20, with Heb 7:11–28, as the New Testament end. Date: 10 October 2026 · Version: 1.2 (Jenni checked on screen; ISBE withdrawn; TDOT not in library; revisions applied to the paper; Heb 13:20 history of interpretation added) Purpose: to test the footnote on ʿôlām in Torah/tripartite-law-paper.md §5.1 before the paper relies on it, and to answer the question the paper itself leaves open in §7 (item 3): does “everlasting covenant” language in the prophets transfer the Mosaic institutions or replace them? Documents under test: tripartite-law-paper.md §5.1 — chiefly the footnote (“The usual account is that ʿôlām denotes indefinite duration within an order. Its frequent companion לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם, ‘throughout your generations’, points that way”), and the body claims the footnote supports. Secondary documents read at Phase 0.5: Ezekiel/book-overview-ezekiel.md and Ezekiel/dig-deeper-ezekiel-sweep.md (units on chs 16, 34 and 37), as thread-sets only. Primary texts: WLC Hebrew; Swete LXX; SBLGNT. Rahlfs–Hanhart (Logos export) for Ezekiel 37 and 46; the BHS apparatus for Ezekiel (Logos export); NA28 text for Hebrews 13:20 (Logos export). Study text: NASB95. Pulpit text: none. No sermon is in view. Hosting: the real file is in Ezekiel/; a one-line pointer stands in Torah/.

Summary

# Claim tested Verdict Confidence
1 “The usual account is that ʿôlām denotes indefinite duration within an order” (footnote) Confirmed with nuance. The lexica support it, and the text supports it more strongly than the footnote says. But “within an order” is the wrong way to put it. The text measures each ʿôlām by what it qualifies, a lifetime, a line of descent or a people’s generations, and uses the same word without limit of God high on the data; moderate on the inference
2 לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם is ʿôlām’s “frequent companion” (footnote) Confirmed. 13 of its 27 occurrences share a verse with עוֹלָם; with the third-person form, 20 of 38. In 17 of the 32 Torah “perpetual statutes” a generations-phrase stands in the same verse high

These occur only in Genesis 17–Numbers 35, never in Deuteronomy, and they tie the duration to Israel’s line of descent high on the distribution; moderate on the inference

Deuteronomy never uses the formula. But it attaches עוֹלָם to the good that follows keeping all the commandments, straight after the Decalogue (Deut 5:29), and to the revealed torah as a whole (29:28 [29:29]) high on the data
6 Test case: does Ezekiel 37:24–26 transfer or replace? (§7 item 3) Neither, simply. Ezekiel re-cuts the covenant and carries its torah into it, but reshapes its institutions. The continuity runs through the covenant God remembers (Lev 26). The discontinuity is a newly cut covenant, a Davidic shepherd and a new heart. And Ezekiel’s own temple law re-specifies a “perpetual statute” (46:13–15 against Num 28:3–6) moderate (synthetic)
7 Hebrews 13:20 as the New Testament end The best canonical answer to the footnote’s problem, and a different one. Hebrews does not argue that ʿôlām was limited. It sets a later, sworn “for ever” (Ps 110:4) above an earlier “perpetual priesthood” (Exod 40:15). It keeps αἰώνιος (“eternal”) for the realities of the new covenant, six times, and never once uses it of the first. Heb 13:20 is a composite: Isa 63:11 and Zech 9:11 verbally, Ezekiel 34/37 conceptually moderate–high

Net effect on §5.1. The footnote’s conclusion holds, and the corpus gives it better support than the footnote gives itself. One part of its argument fails: the bare pairing of “generations” with ʿôlām proves nothing. The body text’s distinction between “ceremonial” and “moral” holds for the statute formula but not for the Torah’s language of permanence as a whole, because Deuteronomy speaks of perpetuity differently. The audit also answers the paper’s open question in §7. Ezekiel neither transfers the Mosaic institutions unchanged nor replaces them. He re-founds the covenant, carries its torah forward and reshapes its cult. Hebrews then names the principle Ezekiel only enacts: a later word of God may rank above an earlier “for ever”. Recommended redrafting is at the end.

Positional Necessity Check (Phase 0.6)

Q1 — what has the preceding movement set up that §5.1’s footnote must now provide? Section 4 argued that the Torah itself discerns different kinds of commandment. Section 5.1 then concedes the strongest datum against any Christian account: the Torah calls its cultic laws perpetual, not its ethical ones. Having conceded that, the paper owes the reader an account of how “perpetual” laws can end in Christ. Otherwise the Hebrew Roots questioner of §1.1 wins by default. The footnote is where that debt is paid. It is therefore load-bearing, not decorative: the “Against the critics” paragraph and the treatment of circumcision both rest on it.

Q2 — why does the test case belong here? Because the paper’s own question in §7, transfer or replace, can only be answered from inside the canon. If the Hebrew Bible itself shows an ʿôlām institution being re-specified, re-cut or outranked, then the Christian account is not an imposition on the text but a reading of it. Ezekiel 37 is the obvious place to look. It is the one text in which “covenant of peace”, “everlasting covenant”, “my statutes” and “my sanctuary for ever” stand together. Hebrews 13:20 is the one New Testament verse that calls the covenant in Jesus’ blood διαθήκη αἰώνιος (“eternal covenant”). The audit is scoped by that positional frame: the question is not what ʿôlām means in a dictionary but how the canon handles an ʿôlām that ends.

The Census

Scope and method

The census was run on the WLC by lemma, not by surface form, so pointing, prefixes, suffixes, plene and defective spelling, and ketiv forms cannot hide a verse. The window is three words in either direction within one verse. A second run checked the same window across verse boundaries within a chapter. It found only one case, Ezek 37:25 into 37:26, which is already in the list. Wider windows were run as a control: a four-word window adds three verses, which are reported separately. Hebrew versification is used throughout, with English in square brackets where it differs.

Positive controls, all passed: עוֹלָם beside חֻקָּה returns Lev 16:29; beside חֹק returns Lev 6:11; beside בְּרִית returns Gen 17:7. The Septuagint search for διαθήκη (“covenant”) beside αἰώνιος (“eternal”) returns Gen 17:7.

A control that failed, and what it showed. A search for the formula “walk in my statutes, keep and do them” returned a nil at Lev 26:3 and Ezek 37:24, where it is known to stand. The cause is in the WLC’s word index: it files the imperfect of הָלַךְ (“walk”) under a separate entry from its other forms. The search was rerun with both entries. This is a new member of the family of silent failures the house gate lists, and a reason to keep running controls: a suppletive verb has two dictionary entries, and a search on one of them silently loses the other.

Totals (WLC). עוֹלָם occurs 438 times in 413 verses. Within three words of בְּרִית, חֻקָּה or חֹק it stands in 59 verses (67 pairings).

The paper’s 34

The paper’s count of ḥuqqat / ḥoq ʿôlām is reproduced exactly. There are 32 in the Torah, at the references §5.1 lists, plus Jer 5:22 and Ezek 46:14. The phrase search behind the footnote was sound. The paper’s sentence “every one of the Torah occurrences attaches to cultic or priestly law” holds. Two of the 32 need a word of qualification, and §5.1 should give it:

  • Lev 3:17 — “you shall not eat any fat or any blood”. The blood prohibition reappears in the apostolic decree (Acts 15:20, 29), where the companion audit (Acts/dig-deeper-acts-15-claim-audit) finds it carried into the church with the sojourner laws. So one “perpetual statute” crosses the boundary that §5.1 draws. [T] for the wording; [S: audit] for the decree reading.
  • Lev 17:7 — “they shall no longer sacrifice their sacrifices to the goat demons”. It is cultic in form, but its content is the First Word. [T]

Num 15:15 is also worth a line. The perpetual statute there is the parity of Israelite and sojourner: חֻקָּה אַחַת (“one statute”) for both. [T]

Distribution. Every one of the 38 lies between Gen 17:7 and Num 35:29, and so do the two related forms (דֹרֹתֵיכֶם without the preposition at Lev 23:43; the singular-suffixed לְדֹרֹתָיו at Lev 25:30). Deuteronomy has none, and it has no ḥuqqat ʿôlām either. [T]

The pairing without the suffix is a different idiom. דּוֹר (“generation”) stands within three words of עוֹלָם 48 times, in 41 verses. Many of them are said of God, not of any institution: his name, זֶה־שְּׁמִי לְעֹלָם וְזֶה זִכְרִי לְדֹר דֹּר (“this is my name forever, and this is my memorial-name to all generations”, Exod 3:15); his reign (Ps 146:10; Lam 5:19); his throne and fame (Ps 102:13 [102:12]; 135:13). Gen 9:12 even puts the two together the other way round. The Noachic sign holds לְדֹרֹת עוֹלָם (“for everlasting generations”, NASB95 “for all successive generations”), and the Septuagint has εἰς γενεὰς αἰωνίους (“for eternal generations”). [T]

Triage. The Septuagint renders ʿôlām with the αἰών family (“age, eternity”) in every Torah case. That is a category 1 fact. The Greek carries the Hebrew’s language of permanence, so a Greek-reading Jew met the same claim. Two places differ in substance:

  • Ezek 46:14 — category 2, uncertain. Hebrew עוֹלָם and תָּמִיד (“continually”) stand side by side, and the Greek has only διὰ παντός (“continually”). The BHS apparatus (in Leningrad) proposes that תָּמִיד may be an addition. It also records that the Cairo Genizah codex, several manuscripts and the versions read the singular חֻקַּת for Leningrad’s plural חֻקּוֹת. Neither variant touches the finding below, which rests on the quantities in 46:13–14, not on the formula. Moderate confidence that the Greek reflects a shorter Hebrew.
  • Ezek 37:25–26 — category 2, low stakes. The Greek lacks “they, and their sons and their sons’ sons, forever” (37:25) and “I will place them and multiply them” (37:26). The BHS apparatus marks both as absent from the Old Greek; Rahlfs confirms. So in the Greek the only εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα in 37:25 attaches to David, not to the people’s tenure of the land. High confidence on the data.

Claims Audited

Claim 1: ʿôlām “denotes indefinite duration within an order”

Source: §5.1, footnote. Presented as “the usual account”, without citation. Claim: when the Torah calls an institution עוֹלָם, it means “for as long as the order lasts”, not “without end”.

Text-first evidence. Each of these was read in the WLC before any lexicon was opened.

  1. The same word, measured by its referent.
    • The bond-slave serves לְעֹלָם (Exod 21:6, NASB95 “permanently”; Deut 15:17 עֶבֶד עוֹלָם, “a servant forever”). The horizon is his life. [T] for the wording; [I] for the horizon.
    • Samuel will stay at Shiloh עַד־עוֹלָם (1 Sam 1:22, “forever”), and the clause before it sets the start: “until the child is weaned”. [T]
    • Aaron’s garments are a statute “for him and his seed after him” (Exod 28:43). The duration is bounded by a line of descent. [T]
  2. The same word, unbounded. God is אֵל עוֹלָם (“the Everlasting God”, Gen 21:33); his arms are זְרֹעֹת עוֹלָם (“the everlasting arms”, Deut 33:27). Nothing in the word itself sets a limit. [T]
  3. An ʿôlām promise explicitly revoked. “I did indeed say that your house and the house of your father should walk before Me forever (עַד־עוֹלָם); but now the LORD declares, ‘Far be it from Me’” (1 Sam 2:30, NASB95). The object is the priestly house of Eli. God himself sets a stated “for ever” aside, on a stated ground. [T]
  4. An ʿôlām statute re-specified by a prophet. The daily offering is עֹלַת תָּמִיד (“a continual burnt offering”), “ordained in Mount Sinai” (Num 28:6): two lambs a day, one in the morning and one at evening, with a tenth of an ephah of flour and a quarter of a hin of oil (Num 28:3–5). Ezekiel’s temple law calls its daily offering חֻקּוֹת עוֹלָם תָּמִיד (“a perpetual statute, continually”, 46:14). But it prescribes one lamb, morning only, with a sixth of an ephah and a third of a hin (46:13–14). It closes with the same phrase as Numbers, עוֹלַת תָּמִיד (46:15). Inside the Hebrew Bible, a statute called perpetual keeps its name and changes its form. [T] for the comparison; [I] for the inference.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance.

Reasoning: the lexica (Jenni explicitly, checked on screen; NIDOTTE by implication) support “remote, indefinite time”. The text supports it more strongly than the footnote claims. It shows the horizon set by the referent (Exod 21:6; 28:43), a revoked ʿôlām (1 Sam 2:30) and a re-specified ḥuqqat ʿôlām (Ezek 46:13–15). But “within an order” is an abstraction the text does not use. What the text does is measure each ʿôlām by what it qualifies: a slave’s life, Aaron “and his seed after him”, “your generations”. So the word does not limit the duration; the thing it qualifies does. That is why the same word is unbounded of God.

Paper action required: cite the lexica, and add 1 Sam 2:30 and Ezek 46:13–15 as the decisive inner-biblical evidence (redraft below).

Claims 2 and 3: לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם, the “frequent companion” that “points that way”

Source: §5.1, footnote. Claim: “throughout your generations” often accompanies ʿôlām, and its presence shows that the duration is bounded by an order.

Claim 2 (frequency) — Confirmed. The figures are in the census table. 13 of the 27 occurrences of לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם share a verse with עוֹלָם; with לְדֹרֹתָם, 20 of 38. Read from the other side, 17 of the 32 Torah “perpetual statutes” carry a generations-phrase. “Frequent” is accurate. [T]

Claim 3 (inference) — Needs reframing. Two observations cut in opposite directions.

  • Against the footnote: the pairing as such proves nothing. דּוֹר and עוֹלָם are used together of God’s name (Exod 3:15) and God’s reign (Ps 146:10; Lam 5:19), where nobody supposes a limit. A reader who cites the pairing as evidence for limitation can be answered from Exodus 3. [T]
  • For the footnote, more strongly than it argues: the suffixed forms are another matter. “Your generations” and “their generations” index the duration to a people’s line of descent. They occur only between Gen 17:7 and Num 35:29, and their first use is the covenant of circumcision, sealed in the flesh of that line (Gen 17:7, 9, 12). Deuteronomy, which addresses the same people about the same laws, uses neither the formula nor the generations-phrase. When it speaks of perpetuity, it speaks of the good that follows obedience (Claim 5). So the idiom belongs to one block of legislation, and in that block the duration is reckoned in Israel’s generations. That is an argument for “measured by the people it constitutes”. It is a better argument than “within an order”, and it fits the paper’s own thesis in §7: the second kind of commandment constitutes Israel. [T] for the distribution; [I] for the inference.

Paper action required: replace “points that way” with the suffixed-form argument, and drop any reliance on the bare pairing (redraft below).

Claim 5: “None is attached to the Decalogue” — “the Torah’s own language of permanence attaches to the ceremonial, not the moral”

Source: §5.1, body text.

Text-first evidence. עוֹלָם occurs 12 times in Deuteronomy (WLC), never with חֻקָּה or חֹק. Two of the occurrences bear directly on the claim.

  • Deut 5:29, the verse after the Decalogue’s frame closes: “Oh that they had such a heart in them, that they would fear Me and keep all My commandments always, that it may be well with them and with their sons forever (לְעֹלָם)!” (NASB95). [T]
  • Deut 29:28 [29:29]: “the things revealed belong to us and to our sons forever (עַד־עוֹלָם), that we may observe all the words of this law” (NASB95). [T] (In Leningrad, the words for “to us and to our sons” carry the extraordinary dots of the Masorah. That is an apparatus feature, a reading tradition, not authorial. No finding here rests on it.)

Verdict: Confirmed for the statute formula. Needs reframing for “the Torah’s language of permanence”.

Reasoning: the statement that no ḥuqqat ʿôlām attaches to the Decalogue is exact. But Deuteronomy does attach עוֹלָם to the Decalogue’s world. It puts the perpetuity in a different place: not in the statute, but in the blessing on keeping “all my commandments” and in the people’s possession of “all the words of this torah”. So the Torah has two idioms of permanence. Exodus–Numbers speak of the statute as perpetual, and those statutes are cultic. Deuteronomy speaks of the people’s obedience and its good as perpetual, and that obedience covers the whole torah. The paper’s sentence is true of the first and silent on the second. As it stands, it lets the questioner reply with Deut 29:28. [I]

Paper action required: narrow “the Torah’s own language of permanence” to “the Torah’s perpetual-statute formula”, and add one sentence on Deuteronomy’s idiom (redraft below).

Claim 6: the test case — does Ezekiel 37:24–26 transfer or replace?

Source: tripartite-law-paper.md §7, item 3 (an open research question, not yet a claim).

The text (WLC; NASB95):

וְעַבְדִּי דָוִד מֶלֶךְ עֲלֵיהֶם וְרוֹעֶה אֶחָד יִהְיֶה לְכֻלָּם וּבְמִשְׁפָּטַי יֵלֵכוּ וְחֻקֹּתַי יִשְׁמְרוּ וְעָשׂוּ אוֹתָם׃ (37:24) — “My servant David will be king over them, and they will all have one shepherd; and they will walk in My ordinances and keep My statutes and observe them.”

… וְדָוִד עַבְדִּי נָשִׂיא לָהֶם לְעוֹלָם׃ (37:25b) — “and David My servant will be their prince forever.”

וְכָרַתִּי לָהֶם בְּרִית שָׁלוֹם בְּרִית עוֹלָם יִהְיֶה אוֹתָם … וְנָתַתִּי אֶת־מִקְדָּשִׁי בְּתוֹכָם לְעוֹלָם׃ (37:26) — “I will make a covenant of peace with them; it will be an everlasting covenant with them … and will set My sanctuary in their midst forever.”

Marks of continuity — the old covenant carried forward.

  1. The statutes are Leviticus’s statutes. The clause “walk in my statutes, keep [them] and do them” (חֻקָּה, “statute”; שָׁמַר, “keep”; עָשָׂה, “do”) stands in eight verses (WLC): Lev 18:4; 26:3; 1 Kgs 2:3; 6:12; 2 Kgs 17:19; Ezek 11:20; 20:19; 37:24. With מִשְׁפָּט (“ordinance”) as well, it stands in 15. All of those are in Leviticus 18–26, Kings or Ezekiel. The חֻקּוֹת Ezekiel carries into the everlasting covenant are the Holiness Code’s, the code that opens with the sexual and social ethics of Leviticus 18–20. This agrees with what §5.5 of the paper says of Ezek 36:27. [T]; high confidence.
  2. The blessings are Leviticus 26’s. Three of them recur:
    • “I will make my dwelling (מִשְׁכָּנִי) in your midst” (Lev 26:11) → “My dwelling place also will be with them” (Ezek 37:27). מִשְׁכָּן (“dwelling”) with תָּוֶךְ (“midst”) stands in only three verses, Lev 15:31, Lev 26:11 and Josh 22:19. So Ezekiel’s echo is conceptual rather than verbal; he writes מִקְדָּשִׁי בְּתוֹכָם (“my sanctuary in their midst”).
    • “I will be your God and you shall be my people” (Lev 26:12 → Ezek 37:23, 27).
    • “I will grant peace … and eliminate harmful beasts” (Lev 26:6) → Ezek 34:25. שָׁבַת (“cause to cease”) + חַיָּה (“beast”) + רָעָה (“harmful”) stands in Lev 26:6 and Ezek 34:25 only.
    [T]; high confidence that Ezekiel 34–37 enacts the blessing section of Leviticus 26.
  3. The covenant is remembered, then re-established.
    • Ezek 16:60 reads וְזָכַרְתִּי אֲנִי אֶת־בְּרִיתִי … וַהֲקִמוֹתִי לָךְ בְּרִית עוֹלָם (“I will remember My covenant with you in the days of your youth, and I will establish an everlasting covenant with you”). “Remember … covenant” is the language of Lev 26:42, 45: “I will remember for them the covenant with their ancestors”.
    • הֵקִים (“establish”) + בְּרִית + עוֹלָם in one verse occurs in exactly three verses: Gen 17:7, 17:19 and Ezek 16:60 (WLC). Ezekiel re-establishes the covenant in the words of the covenant with Abraham.
    [T]; high.
  4. The covenant of peace is Phinehas’s. “Covenant of peace” and “everlasting covenant” stand within one verse of each other in exactly two places: Num 25:12–13, “My covenant of peace … a covenant of a perpetual priesthood”, and Ezek 37:25–26. Ezekiel 37:26 is the only verse that has both in one sentence. A priestly prophet has taken the priestly-covenant pairing and applied it to a Davidic covenant. [T] for the distribution; [I] for the intention; moderate.

Marks of discontinuity — a new cutting.

  1. The verb is כָּרַת (“cut, make”), not “remember”. “I will cut (וְכָרַתִּי) for them a covenant of peace” (37:26; 34:25) is the verb Jeremiah uses: “I will make (וְכָרַתִּי) a new covenant … not like the covenant which I made with their fathers … My covenant which they broke (הֵפֵרוּ)” (Jer 31:31–32). Ezekiel has already used “break” (לְהָפֵר בְּרִית, 16:59) of the Sinai covenant, the same verb as Isa 24:5’s “they broke the everlasting covenant”. Against that background, the covenant he promises is “not by your covenant” (וְלֹא מִבְּרִיתֵךְ, 16:61). [T]
  2. The keeping is God’s own work. 37:24’s “they will walk … keep … do” is the future of 36:26–27: a new heart, and “I will put My Spirit within you and cause you to walk (וְעָשִׂיתִי אֵת אֲשֶׁר … תֵּלֵכוּ) in My statutes”. The condition of Lev 26:3 (“if you walk”) has become a promise. [T]
  3. The perpetuity moves to the person. The ʿôlām of 37:25–26 attaches to David as prince (לְעוֹלָם), to the covenant, and to the sanctuary. In the Greek, which lacks the land-tenure clause, it attaches to David and the sanctuary alone. No ʿôlām attaches to the Mosaic priesthood here. [T]
  4. The cult is reshaped — inside Ezekiel’s own book. Ezek 46:13–15 re-specifies a Sinai “perpetual” offering under the same name (Claim 1, point 4). And Ezek 44:24 still requires that the priests “keep My laws and My statutes in all My appointed feasts and sanctify My sabbaths”. Ezekiel keeps the institutions and changes their form. [T]; the inference that the form can change while the name stays is [I].

Verdict: Neither transfer nor replacement, simply. Ezekiel re-cuts the covenant: a new act, כָּרַת, after the old was broken. He carries forward its torah and its promised blessings, the Holiness Code’s statutes and Leviticus 26’s dwelling-presence, because God remembers the old covenant. And he reshapes its institutions: a Davidic shepherd-prince “for ever”, and a re-specified daily offering still called “perpetual”. On §7’s terms, ʿôlām in Ezekiel transfers the covenant relationship and its torah, and does not transfer the Mosaic form of the institutions. Moderate confidence. This is a synthetic finding: each constituent above is verified, but the synthesis across them is the audit’s own.

What this gives §5.1. It is the inner-biblical precedent the footnote lacks. Before the New Testament, a canonical prophet has already treated a ḥuqqat ʿôlām as keeping its name while changing its form. The Christian claim goes further than Ezekiel: Ezekiel keeps a sacrificial cult, and the New Testament does not. But it goes further along a line Ezekiel had already drawn.

What Hebrews does with ʿôlām — the answer the footnote is looking for.

  1. αἰώνιος in Hebrews. It occurs 6 times (SBLGNT): 5:9 salvation; 6:2 judgement; 9:12 redemption; 9:14 Spirit; 9:15 inheritance; 13:20 covenant. Never once of the first covenant, its priesthood or its law. [T]
  2. Two “for evers” in conflict, and how Hebrews settles them. The Torah gives Aaron’s sons לִכְהֻנַּת עוֹלָם לְדֹרֹתָם (“a perpetual priesthood throughout their generations”, Exod 40:15; Num 25:13 of Phinehas). Psalm 110:4 gives the Melchizedek priest נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה וְלֹא יִנָּחֵם אַתָּה־כֹהֵן לְעוֹלָם (“The LORD has sworn and will not change His mind, ‘You are a priest forever’”). Hebrews quotes the psalm three times (5:6; 7:17, 21). It argues from three things:
    • sequence: “the word of the oath, which came after the Law” (7:28);
    • oath: the psalm’s priest is appointed with an oath, the Levites without one (7:20–21);
    • consequence: “when the priesthood is changed, of necessity there takes place a change of law also” (7:12, NASB95).
    It concludes that the first covenant is “becoming obsolete and growing old” (8:13). [T] for each reference; [I] for the comparison.
  3. So Hebrews’ account is not “ʿôlām meant less than it said”. It is that a later “for ever”, sworn by God, outranks an earlier “for ever” given in law. That is the same structure as 1 Sam 2:30, where God’s later word sets aside his earlier עַד־עוֹלָם to Eli’s house. It is also the structure Ezekiel enacts (46:13–15). The footnote’s lexical account (“indefinite duration”) and Hebrews’ canonical account (“a later sworn word”) are compatible. But the second is the one the New Testament actually gives. It is also stronger against the Hebrew Roots objection, because it does not depend on proving that the word was weaker than it sounds. [I]; moderate–high.

What the Audit Adds

  1. The footnote’s conclusion is right, and three texts it does not cite make it much stronger. These are 1 Sam 2:30, an ʿôlām revoked by God; Ezek 46:13–15, a ḥuqqat ʿôlām re-specified; and Hebrews 7, a later sworn ʿôlām outranking an earlier one.
  2. “Your generations” is a better argument than the footnote makes of it, and the bare pairing is a worse one. The suffixed forms belong to Genesis 17–Numbers 35 alone, and they index perpetuity to Israel’s descent.
  3. The Torah has two idioms of permanence: the statute’s (Exodus–Numbers, cultic) and the people’s obedience (Deuteronomy, covering the whole torah). §5.1 currently describes only the first.
  4. The answer to §7’s open question: Ezekiel re-cuts the covenant, carries its torah and reshapes its institutions. This fits the paper’s twofold thesis closely. What Ezekiel carries forward is the Holiness Code’s ethic and the covenant relationship. What he reshapes is the cult that constitutes Israel as a priestly nation.

R1 — the ʿôlām footnote (replace).

The usual account is that ʿôlām denotes remote or indefinite time, whose horizon is set by what it qualifies (Jenni, TLOT, 857). Hence the bond-slave serves לְעֹלָם, “permanently”, for life (Exod 21:6), and Aaron’s garments are a statute “for him and his seed after him” (Exod 28:43). The same word is unbounded when it qualifies God (Gen 21:33). Three texts show the canon itself handling an ʿôlām that ends. In 1 Sam 2:30 God sets aside his own עַד־עוֹלָם to the house of Eli. In Ezek 46:13–15 a prophet re-specifies the daily offering, “ordained in Mount Sinai” as two lambs (Num 28:3–6), as one lamb, and still calls it חֻקּוֹת עוֹלָם תָּמִיד. In Heb 7:12, 20–28 a later sworn “for ever” (Ps 110:4) outranks the earlier “perpetual priesthood” (Exod 40:15). The phrase “throughout your/their generations” points the same way. It accompanies 17 of the 32 Torah occurrences, occurs only between Gen 17:7 and Num 35:29, and measures the duration in Israel’s descent. The unsuffixed pairing of דּוֹר with עוֹלָם proves nothing either way, since it is used of God’s own name (Exod 3:15). None of the three papers offers this account.

R2 — the sentence on Deuteronomy (add after “None is attached to the Decalogue or to the ethical commands of Leviticus 19”).

Deuteronomy uses the formula nowhere. It speaks of perpetuity differently, attaching עוֹלָם to the good that follows keeping “all my commandments” (Deut 5:29) and to Israel’s possession of “all the words of this law” (29:28 [29:29]).

R3 — “the Torah’s own language of permanence” (amend). Read: “the Torah’s perpetual-statute formula attaches to the ceremonial, not the moral”.

R4 — the count footnote (add). “A wider search adds Exod 12:24, where the Passover is a חֹק ‘for you and your children forever’, four words from עוֹלָם. Two of the 32 sit near the moral line: Lev 3:17 (blood, cf. Acts 15:20, 29) and Lev 17:7 (no sacrifice to goat-demons).”

R5 — §7 item 3 (now answered; move into the body or revise). “Ezekiel’s ‘everlasting covenant’ (16:60; 37:26) is newly cut (כָּרַת, cf. Jer 31:31) rather than merely renewed. But it carries the Holiness Code’s statutes (37:24, cf. Lev 18:4; 26:3) and the blessings of Leviticus 26, and it re-specifies the cult (46:13–15). It neither transfers the Mosaic institutions unchanged nor replaces the covenant’s torah.”
