## What this document is

This is the whole-book planning layer for Daniel. It maps the terrain so that the passage digs land well; it does not pre-decide what they will find. It is a Draft, built from the Hebrew and Aramaic text before any commentary was opened, and every structural and intertextual claim in it is meant to be tested by the digs and the claim audit.

Four features of the book shape everything below, and they are stated first so that no later section quietly forgets them.

1. **The book is written in two languages, and the language seams do not coincide with the genre seams.** Hebrew runs 1:1–2:4a, Aramaic 2:4b–7:28, Hebrew again 8:1–12:13. The switch happens mid-verse, immediately after the word אֲרָמִית ("in Aramaic", 2:4). But the *genre* divides at a different place: court narratives in chs 1–6, visions in chs 7–12. So the first narrative (ch. 1) is in Hebrew and the first vision (ch. 7) is in Aramaic. The two divisions overlap like two interlocking rings, and any outline that follows only one of them loses half the book's architecture. Of the 5,919 words of Daniel in the WLC, **3,599 (60.8 %) are Aramaic.** `[T]` (WLC, every word classified by its morphology code)

2. **The book's refrain is that the Most High gives kingdoms.** The sentence "the Most High is ruler over the realm of mankind, and bestows it on whom He wishes" (NASB95) stands, in the same three key words — שַׁלִּיט ("ruler"), מַלְכוּת ("kingdom"), and צְבָא ("to wish, to will") — in exactly four verses: 4:17, 4:25, 4:32 [Heb 4:14, 22, 29] and 5:21 (WLC, all four confirmed). The book opens on the same note in Hebrew: וַיִּתֵּן אֲדֹנָי ("and the Lord gave", 1:2), and the verb נָתַן ("to give") has God as subject three times in the first chapter (1:2, 9, 17). `[T]`

3. **The order of the book is not the order of events.** The visions of chs 7 and 8 are dated to the first and third years of Belshazzar (7:1; 8:1) — that is, *before* the night on which Belshazzar is killed (5:30). The prayer of ch. 9 and the opening of the last vision (11:1) are dated to the first year of Darius, the same reign as ch. 6. The book is arranged by kind — tales, then visions — and not by chronology. `[T]` This matters because the visions, read in their dated settings, were given to a Daniel who had not yet seen Babylon fall.

4. **The man who "understood all visions and dreams" (1:17) ends unable to understand.** בִּין ("to understand") is one of the book's two great Hebrew words — **22 occurrences in 19 verses** in the Hebrew sections (WLC, by lemma; the Aramaic chapters have their own cognate) — and its arc is a reversal: Daniel הֵבִין ("understood", 1:17) all visions; after the vision of ch. 8 there is אֵין מֵבִין ("none to explain it", 8:27); and at the end, וְלֹא אָבִין ("but I did not understand", 12:8). `[T]` A book that begins by giving its hero understanding ends by sealing the words "until the end time" (12:4, 9).

The English order (inherited from the Greek tradition) places Daniel among the Prophets, after Ezekiel. The reader may arrive by either route. `[T]` for the BHS order; `[S]` for the history of the two arrangements |
| **Reading implication** | In the Writings, Daniel is read **beside Esther and Ezra–Nehemiah** — as a book about Jews serving and surviving in foreign courts, and as the last word before the return under Cyrus — rather than as the fourth of the "major prophets". The book itself never calls Daniel a נָבִיא ("prophet"); it calls him one who *reads* a prophet (9:2) and who receives חָזוֹן ("vision") and its interpretation. The New Testament calls him "the prophet" (Matt 24:15, SBLGNT: διὰ Δανιὴλ τοῦ προφήτου, "through Daniel the prophet"). **Both placements are old; neither should be preached as the only one.** `[T]` for 9:2 and Matt 24:15; `[I]` for the reading implication |
| **Presupposes** | (a) **Jeremiah's seventy years** — named explicitly at 9:2: "the number of the years which was revealed as the word of the LORD to Jeremiah the prophet for the completion of the desolations of Jerusalem, namely, seventy years" (NASB95). The words שִׁבְעִים ("seventy") + שָׁנָה ("year") + יִרְמְיָה ("Jeremiah") stand together in **two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Dan 9:2 and 2 Chr 36:21** (WLC); the source texts are Jer 25:11–12 and 29:10. (b) **The curse of the law of Moses** — "the curse and the oath which are written in the law of Moses" (9:11; cf. 9:13): אָלָה ("curse") + כָּתַב ("to write") + מֹשֶׁה ("Moses") stand together only at Dan 9:11 (WLC); the nearest wording is Deut 29:20–21 [Heb 29:19–20], "every curse which is written in this book". (c) **The fall of Jehoiakim and the temple vessels** (1:1–2): 2 Kgs 24:1; 2 Chr 36:6–7. (d) **Joseph in Pharaoh's court** (Gen 40–41), the model the court tales follow — see the Intertextual Map. (e) **Ezekiel's throne-vision** (Ezek 1; 9–10), behind 7:9–10 and 10:5–6. *Every reference in this field has been checked against the WLC.* `[T]` for 9:2 and 9:11; `[I]` for (d) and (e) as presupposition |
| **Handoff** | Daniel ends its first chapter on a date — עַד־שְׁנַת אַחַת לְכוֹרֶשׁ ("until the first year of Cyrus", 1:21). The ordered phrase אַחַת לְכוֹרֶשׁ ("the first year of Cyrus") occurs in **three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Dan 1:21, Ezra 1:1 and 2 Chr 36:22** (WLC, plain and skeletal) — and in the BHS order those are this book, the book that follows it, and the book that closes the canon. The prayer of ch. 9 hands on its opening confession almost word for word to Neh 1:5 (see Intertextual Map). **The handoff is thematic and chronological; the lexical form of it is a design claim, held at moderate until the claim audit runs a baseline.** `[I]` |
| **Neighbours** | **Esther before; Ezra–Nehemiah after.** The adjacency is doing visible work. Esther and Daniel are both stories of Jews at a Persian or Babylonian court, threatened through the king's own legislation; the "law of the Medes and Persians, which may not be revoked" (6:8, 12, 15 [Heb 6:9, 13, 16]) is in Esther בְּדָתֵי פָרַס־וּמָדַי וְלֹא יַעֲבוֹר ("in the laws of Persia and Media, and it shall not pass away", Esth 1:19; cf. 8:8, a royal writing that "may not be revoked"). Ezra–Nehemiah opens on Cyrus' first year and is built, like Daniel, partly of Aramaic (Ezra 4:8–6:18; 7:12–26) and partly of Hebrew. **The density scan agrees:** of the ten chapters in the whole Hebrew Bible that share most rare vocabulary with Daniel, all ten are in Ezra–Nehemiah, Esther or 2 Chr 36 (see Intertextual Map for the caveat that much of this is shared language and period, not allusion). `[T]` for the wording; `[I]` for the adjacency's work |

**Where canonical and compositional order diverge.** Daniel's own internal order already diverges from chronology (chs 7–8 precede ch. 5 in time). Between books, the question of when Daniel was written — sixth century, as the visions' dates present it, or second century, as most critical scholarship holds — is a reconstruction from outside the text, and is reported under *Presenting Situation*, not resolved here. `[S]`

## Presenting Situation

**Narrator and voice.** The book never names its author. **Chapters 1–6 are told in the third person by an anonymous narrator**, with one long exception: 4:1–37 [Heb 3:31–4:34] is a royal proclamation in Nebuchadnezzar's own voice ("I, Nebuchadnezzar", 4:4 [Heb 4:1]), which shifts to the third person in the middle of the judgement (4:28–33 [Heb 4:25–30]) and back to the first for the restoration (4:34 [Heb 4:31]). **Chapters 7–12 are in Daniel's first person** ("I, Daniel", 7:15, 28; 8:1, 15, 27; 9:2; 10:2, 7; 12:5), each vision framed by a short third-person heading (7:1; 10:1). `[T]` That the first-person visions were written by Daniel is the book's presentation; whether they were is a question of authorship and is `[S]`.

**Setting (narrative world).** From the third year of Jehoiakim (1:1) to the third year of Cyrus (10:1): the Babylonian court under Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar, then the court of "Darius the Mede" (5:31 [Heb 6:1]; 9:1; 11:1) and of Cyrus. Six dated notices fix the frame: 1:1; 2:1; 7:1; 8:1; 9:1; 10:1 (with 11:1). `[T]` The identity of "Darius the Mede", and the "third year of Jehoiakim" against Jer 25:1 and 46:2, are well-known historical cruxes; they are `[S]` and belong to the passage digs, not to the overview.

**Compositional audience and date.** Contested, and contested in a way that affects how the visions are read. The visions themselves are dated to the sixth century and present themselves as foretelling what follows (8:19; 10:14; 11:2). Most critical scholarship dates the book's final form to the crisis under Antiochus IV (c. 167–164 BC) and reads 11:2–39 as history written as prophecy; conservative scholarship holds the sixth-century setting and reads 11 as predictive prophecy. **The text names Persia and Greece** — מָדַי וּפָרָס ("Media and Persia", 8:20), מֶלֶךְ יָוָן ("the king of Greece", 8:21; cf. 10:20; 11:2) — and names no king after that. The identifications of the "king of the South" and "king of the North" with the Ptolemies and Seleucids, and of the "despicable person" (11:21) with Antiochus IV, are `[S]`, though they are very widely held. **The Old Greek already read 11:30's כִּתִּים ("Kittim") as Ῥωμαῖοι ("Romans")** (Rahlfs, Logos export), where Theodotion keeps Κίτιοι — so the identification of Kittim with Rome is at least as old as that translation. `[T]` for the two Greek texts; `[S]` for the historical identifications.

**Canonical audience.** A reader of the Writings, who comes to Daniel after the Torah and the Prophets and already knows Jeremiah's seventy years, the law's curse, and the exile; who reads Esther just before and Ezra–Nehemiah just after. `[I]`

**Pressure.** Two pressures, one in each half, and the book's argument is that they are the same pressure.
- **In the tales (chs 1–6):** how is a faithful Jew to live inside a pagan empire whose king claims the power to define what is eaten (ch. 1), what is worshipped (ch. 3), and to whom one may pray (ch. 6) — when the empire also educates, promotes and depends on him (1:4–5, 19; 2:48–49; 6:2–3 [Heb 6:3–4])? `[T]`
- **In the visions (chs 7–12):** what happens when the empire turns from patronage to persecution — when a king "will speak out against the Most High and wear down the saints of the Highest One" (7:25), removes the תָּמִיד ("regular sacrifice", 8:11; 11:31; 12:11) and sets up the שִׁקּוּץ שֹׁמֵם ("abomination of desolation")? `[T]`

**Purpose.** To show that the God of heaven gives and removes every kingdom on earth, so that his people can serve inside the empire without worshipping it, and suffer under it without despairing of it — because the last kingdom is his, given to "one like a son of man" and to "the people of the saints of the Highest One" (7:13–14, 27), and because death is not the end of those who are wise (12:2–3). `[I]`

## The Two Languages and the Greek Text

Daniel is the only book of the Hebrew Bible written in two languages from end to end of a single narrative; Ezra is the only other with extended Aramaic. `[T]` for the WLC; the claim "only" refers to extended Aramaic passages (Gen 31:47 and Jer 10:11 are a phrase and a verse).

**Where the languages fall** (WLC morphology, every word):

| Section | Language | Verses (WLC) | Genre |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1–2:4a | Hebrew | 1:1–2:4 | Narrative |
| 2:4b–7:28 | Aramaic | 2:4 (from מַלְכָּא, "O king")–7:28 | Narrative (2–6) and vision (7) |
| 8:1–12:13 | Hebrew | 8:1–12:13 | Vision |

**The Aramaic section is the book's public face.** Its six chapters are full of words addressed to "all the peoples, nations and men of every language" — עַמְמַיָּא אֻמַיָּא וְלִשָּׁנַיָּא, in **seven verses, all in Daniel** (3:4, 7, 29; 4:1 [Heb 3:31]; 5:19; 6:25 [Heb 6:26]; 7:14; WLC). Three royal decrees go out to that audience (4:1 [Heb 3:31]; 6:25 [Heb 6:26]; cf. 3:29), and the last time the phrase is used they are not a king's subjects but the subjects of "one like a son of man" (7:14). `[T]` That the empire's own lingua franca carries the material addressed to the empire is an inference `[I]`; why the book is bilingual at all is a long scholarly debate `[S]`, and the Draft does not adjudicate it.

**Four Aramaic words run through all six Aramaic chapters** (WLC chapters 2–7, by lemma):
- מַלְכוּ ("kingdom") — 53 occurrences in 39 verses;
- יְהַב ("to give") — 20 occurrences, in every Aramaic chapter;
- שְׁנָה ("to change, to be different") — 19 occurrences, in every Aramaic chapter;
- עָלַם ("eternity, for ever") — 18 occurrences, in every Aramaic chapter. `[T]`

The pairing that matters most is the first and the third: kingdoms are *given* (2:37–38; 5:18; 7:14, 27), and the one who *changes* times and kings is God (2:21) — until a king presumes to do it himself (7:25). See the Echo Table.

**Two Hebrew words run through the Hebrew vision section** (WLC chapters 8–12, by lemma):
- עָמַד ("to stand, to arise") — **46 occurrences in 38 verses** of the whole book, 17 of those verses in ch. 11 alone; kings "arise", "stand" and "do not stand" (8:4, 7, 22, 25; 11:2–20), Michael "will arise" (12:1), and Daniel is promised that he "will rise again for [his] allotted portion" (12:13);
- בִּין ("to understand") — as above, 22 occurrences in 19 verses. `[T]`

**Unpointed (ketiv) forms.** Daniel has the densest ketiv share of any book in the Hebrew Bible by verse — **22.4 % of its verses carry an unpointed form** in the WLC (measurement recorded in the `_texts` README). The lemma index carries the right lemma for each, so every chain in this overview was checked by lemma, never by surface form. One ketiv/qere is load-bearing for preaching, at 9:24: see *Pulpit Notes*. `[T]`

**The Greek text.** There is no single Greek Daniel. The Logos exports hold the **Old Greek** (as Rahlfs prints it) and **Theodotion**, and they differ widely — most visibly in chs 4–6, and at points that bear on the New Testament:
- **7:13.** The Old Greek reads ὡς υἱὸς ἀνθρώπου ἤρχετο, καὶ ὡς παλαιὸς ἡμερῶν παρῆν ("one like a son of man was coming, and *as* the Ancient of Days he was present"); Theodotion, with the Aramaic, has the figure come ἕως τοῦ παλαιοῦ τῶν ἡμερῶν ("*to* the Ancient of Days"). `[T]` (Rahlfs and Theodotion exports) Whether the OG reading is a corruption or a deliberate identification is `[S]`; Rev 1:13–14, which gives "one like a son of man" the white hair of the Ancient of Days (7:9), stands in the same stream. `[I]`
- **9:24–26.** Theodotion joins "seven weeks and sixty-two weeks" in a single phrase before ἕως χριστοῦ ἡγουμένου ("until an anointed leader"), and reads τοῦ σφραγίσαι ἁμαρτίας ("to seal up sins") — the ketiv, not the qere. `[T]` (Theodotion export; BHS apparatus) See *Pulpit Notes*.
- **11:30.** As above: OG Ῥωμαῖοι ("Romans"), Theodotion Κίτιοι ("Kittim"). `[T]`
- **The additions.** Both Greek texts carry the Prayer of Azariah and the Song of the Three Young Men inside ch. 3 (3:24–90 in the Greek numbering); Rahlfs prints Susanna and Bel as separate books. `[T]` for the Rahlfs export; these are outside the Hebrew canon this overview maps.

**What the corpus could not see.** The Aramaic lemma index uses its own numbers, so a lemma search cannot find a Hebrew word answered by its Aramaic cognate, nor an Aramaic chapter alluding to a Hebrew one. **The density scan is therefore blind to cross-language allusion** — it ranks Daniel 7 only against the other Aramaic texts of the Bible (Ezra 4–7, Jer 10:11), and cannot see Ezek 1 behind 7:9–10 or Jer 27:6 behind 2:38. Every cross-language row in the Intertextual Map was checked by reading the two texts side by side, and is flagged *cross-language*. `[T]`

## Structural Arc Map

**The seams.** Stripped of chapter numbers, the book divides at four kinds of seam, all on the page: (1) **dated headings** — 1:1; 2:1; 7:1; 8:1; 9:1; 10:1 (with 11:1); (2) **the change of language** at 2:4 and 8:1; (3) **the change of voice**, from third-person narration to Daniel's "I" at 7:2; and (4) **the petuchah** (the open-paragraph marker פ). In the WLC a petuchah closes every chapter from 1 to 10 — and 4:1–3 [Heb 3:31–33] begins *after* the petuchah that closes the furnace story at 3:30, so the proclamation opens a new paragraph while carrying ch. 3's number. **There is no paragraph marker at 11:45**: the speech runs straight on into 12:1. `[T]` (WLC paragraph markers as the Leningrad codex has them — a reading tradition, not necessarily authorial)

**The last vision is one unit, not three chapters.** 10:1 dates a single "message" (דָּבָר, "word") to the third year of Cyrus; the speaker who begins at 10:11 is still speaking at 11:1 ("In the first year of Darius the Mede, I arose …") and at 12:4; the frame closes only at 12:13. The chapter breaks at 11:1 and 12:1 cut through a continuous speech, and the WLC's own paragraphing has no break at 11:45 to answer the chapter break at 12:1. `[T]` (WLC; paragraphing as the Leningrad codex has it)

| Verses | Movement | Language | Function |
|--------|----------|----------|----------|
| 1:1–21 | **Prologue: what God gave in Babylon** | Hebrew | Three gifts — the king and the vessels into Nebuchadnezzar's hand (1:2), favour before the chief official (1:9), understanding to the four youths (1:17). The renaming, the food test and the frame "until the first year of Cyrus" (1:21) set the book's terms |
| 2:1–6:28 [Heb 2:1–6:29] | **Five court tales: the kingdom is given and taken** | Aramaic from 2:4b | The dream of four kingdoms and the stone (2); the golden image and the furnace (3); the tree, the beast-king and the confession (4:1–37 [Heb 3:31–4:34]); the vessels profaned and the writing on the wall (5:1–31 [Heb 5:1–6:1]); the den of lions (6:1–28 [Heb 6:2–29]). Every tale ends in a king's acknowledgement of Daniel's God (2:47; 3:28–29; 4:34–37 [Heb 4:31–34]; 6:25–27 [Heb 6:26–28]) — except ch. 5, which ends in a king's death (5:30) |
| 7:1–28 | **The hinge: the four kingdoms from heaven's side** | Aramaic | The genre turns to vision while the language stays Aramaic. Four beasts from the sea, the Ancient of Days, "one like a son of man", and the kingdom given to the saints. It reworks ch. 2 (four kingdoms, a fourth of iron, a final everlasting kingdom) and opens the visions |
| 8:1–9:27 | **Two Hebrew visions of desolation** | Hebrew | The ram and the goat, the little horn and the removal of the תָּמִיד ("regular sacrifice") (8); Daniel reads Jeremiah, confesses Israel's sin, and receives the seventy weeks (9). Gabriel appears in both (8:16; 9:21) |
| 10:1–12:13 | **The last vision: kings of the North and South, the end, and the resurrection** | Hebrew | The man clothed in linen and the princes of Persia and Greece (10); the long succession of kings to "the time of the end" (11:2–45); Michael, the time of distress, the book, the awakening of the sleepers, the shining of the wise (12:1–3); the sealing of the words and Daniel's own end (12:4–13) |

**Device and shape.** The book is built on **two divisions that overlap**: by language (Hebrew–Aramaic–Hebrew) and by genre (tales 1–6, visions 7–12). Chapter 1 is the Hebrew door into the tales; chapter 7 is the Aramaic door into the visions. Each "half" is therefore bound to the other at its edge. `[T]` for the divisions; the purpose of the interlock is `[I]`.

**A concentric arrangement inside the Aramaic section — a design claim, baselined, held at *moderate*.** It is often proposed that the Aramaic chapters are arranged in pairs around a centre: **2 // 7** (four kingdoms ended by a God-given kingdom), **3 // 6** (Jews condemned to death for refusing a royal religious decree, delivered by an angel), **4 // 5** (a proud king judged; ch. 5 even retells ch. 4 at 5:18–21). `[S]` for the proposal. The draft tested it rather than adopting it:

- **The wording that pairs 3 and 6 is exclusive.** "My God sent His angel" — שְׁלַח מַלְאֲכֵהּ ("sent his angel") — stands at 3:28 and 6:22 [Heb 6:23] (WLC, both confirmed), and the verb שֵׁיזִב ("to deliver") occurs only in chs 3 and 6 (9 occurrences in 7 verses, WLC). `[T]`
- **4 and 5 share a refrain no other chapter has:** the four verses with שַׁלִּיט ("ruler") + מַלְכוּ ("kingdom") + צְבָא ("to will") are 4:17, 25, 32 [Heb 4:14, 22, 29] and 5:21. `[T]`
- **A chance baseline** compared the claimed pairing with all fifteen ways of dividing six chapters into three pairs, scoring each pair by the vocabulary it shares (with words common to every Aramaic chapter set aside). On three of four measures — overall overlap, and vocabulary shared by *only* the two chapters of a pair, each run on the Hebrew and on the English chapter divisions — **the concentric pairing ranked first of fifteen; on the fourth it ranked second.** But the margins were thin (on one measure, 0.654 against 0.645 for the runner-up), and the pairs differ in strength: **4 // 5 and 3 // 6 are well supported; 2 // 7 is the weakest lexically** — each of chs 2 and 7 shares more distinctive vocabulary with ch. 4 or ch. 3 than with each other. The 2 // 7 pairing rests on *structure and theme* (four kingdoms, a fourth of iron, an everlasting kingdom from God), not on shared words. `[I]`, design claim, **moderate** — a first target for the claim audit.

**Reading order against chronological order.** Set in the order of events, the book would run 1, 2, 3, 4, **7, 8**, 5, 6, **9, 10–12**. The arrangement puts the visions of Belshazzar's first and third years *after* the account of his death, so that the reader meets the beast-kingdoms of ch. 7 already knowing how Babylon ended. `[T]` for the dates; `[I]` for the effect.

## Microscript

*The Most High gives every kingdom and takes it back. So his people can serve in Babylon without bowing to it, and can suffer under its last and worst king without despair: the everlasting kingdom is given to one like a son of man and to the people of the saints, and those who sleep in the dust will awake.*

**Keynote:** the kingdom that is *given*. In Aramaic, מַלְכוּ ("kingdom") and יְהַב ("to give") — both in every Aramaic chapter; in Hebrew, נָתַן ("to give") in the prologue's three divine gifts (1:2, 9, 17). The Hebrew visions add a second pulse: בִּין ("to understand", 22×) and עָמַד ("to stand", 46×). `[T]` (WLC, by lemma)

**Tested against the movements.**
- *Prologue (1):* the Lord *gave* Jehoiakim into Nebuchadnezzar's hand — the first kingdom transfer of the book is God's act. ✓
- *Tales (2–6):* each tale is a kingdom given, threatened, confessed or removed (2:37–38, 44; 4:17, 25, 32; 5:18, 21, 28; 6:26). Serving without bowing: 1:8; 3:16–18; 6:10 [Heb 6:11]. ✓
- *Hinge (7):* the kingdom given to the son of man (7:14) and to the saints (7:18, 22, 27). ✓
- *Hebrew visions (8–9):* suffering under the king who removes the תָּמִיד ("regular sacrifice") — the "without despair" is carried by the confession of ch. 9, which grounds hope only in God's רַחֲמִים ("compassion", 9:18). ✓
- *Last vision (10–12):* the worst king (11:21–45), the wise who fall (11:33–35), Michael's deliverance and the awakening (12:1–3). ✓

**A caution about the microscript.** It is built on the book's own refrain, and the refrain is spoken *by and to pagan kings* (4:17, 25, 32; 5:21). The book's sovereignty theology is learned first in the throne-room of Babylon. That is part of the point, and a sermon that treats it as a statement made only to believers has missed where the book places it. `[I]`

## Intertextual Map

**Profile.** Daniel quotes almost nothing verbatim and names only one earlier book — Jeremiah (9:2) — and one body of law — "the law of Moses" (9:11, 13). But it is dense with **rare-word echoes**, concentrated in two places: the court tales lean on **Joseph** (Gen 40–41), and the last vision leans on **Isaiah**, **Numbers 24**, **Habakkuk 2:3** and **Ezekiel**. The Aramaic chapters can be compared with Hebrew sources only by reading, not by search (see *The Two Languages*), so their rows are flagged *cross-language*. `[T]`

### The density scan — every top-ten partner accounted for

The scan ranks every chapter of the Hebrew Bible by rare vocabulary shared with a block of Daniel (WLC). It was run for the whole book; for chs 1–6, 8–12, 1, 7, 9, and 11–12.

- **Whole book.** All ten are Ezra 5, 6, 4, 7, Neh 1, Ezra 9, Esth 1, Esth 7, Ezra 1, 2 Chr 36. **Ezra 4–7 rank first to fourth because they are the Bible's other Aramaic** — shared chancery language, not allusion; they do not enter the map. Neh 1 enters (9:4 // Neh 1:5; also שׁוּשַׁן הַבִּירָה, "Susa the citadel", 8:2 // Neh 1:1). Ezra 9 enters as the nearest penitential prayer (בֹּשֶׁת פָּנִים, "shame of face": 6 verses, including Dan 9:7, 8 and Ezra 9:7). Esth 1 and 7 are Persian-court diction (banquet, wine, the king's officials, "Susa the citadel" — 12 verses, ten in Esther, plus Neh 1:1 and Dan 8:2) and are carried in *Canonical Position* as neighbour, not as source. Ezra 1 and 2 Chr 36 enter through Cyrus' first year and the vessels.
- **Chs 1–6 (Hebrew-indexed ch. 1 only, plus the Aramaic).** Adds Jer 10 (its single Aramaic verse, 10:11 — shared language), Gen 40 and Gen 41 (enter, Joseph row), Jer 24 (Nebuchadnezzar's deportation of the young and skilled — thematic, common vocabulary; not entered), 2 Kgs 24 (enters, vessels row), Ezra 3 (common food-and-drink words; not entered), **Esth 2** (the eunuch in charge of the young, the special portions, the favour won — חֶסֶד, "favour", Esth 2:9, 17; Dan 1:9 — a court-tale parallel worth a line in the ch. 1 dig; not entered as a source).
- **Ch. 7.** Only the other Aramaic texts (Ezra 4–7, Jer 10). **This is the scan's blind spot, not an absence of partners**: ch. 7's Hebrew partners (Ezek 1, and possibly Ps 8) cannot be seen by it.
- **Ch. 9.** Neh 1 and Ezra 9 (entered); Mal 2 (covenant and priestly vocabulary — not entered); Jonah 4 (the formula of address at 9:4 against Jonah 4:2 — common prayer diction; not entered); Ps 20; **Zech 1** (the seventy years and "how long?" — carried as rival in the Jeremiah row); **2 Chr 6** (Solomon's prayer toward the city and house "called by your name" — cf. 6:10 [Heb 6:11]; 9:18–19; thematic, worth a line in the ch. 9 dig); Josh 23 (the law of Moses — common); 2 Chr 36 (entered); **Ps 141** (entered).
- **Chs 8–12 and 11–12.** Neh 1, Ezra 9, Esth 1, 3, 7, 8, 2 Chr 36, Ezra 1 (Persian-period diction and the neighbours, as above); Ezek 9 and Ezek 1 (entered); Mal 2 (not entered); Neh 8 (reading the book "with understanding", בִּין — thematically close to 9:2; not entered as source); Jonah 4 and Ps 20 (not entered); 2 Chr 14 and 32, Zech 14, Isa 63 (battle and boast vocabulary — common; 2 Chr 32's blaspheming king is a thematic rival to 11:36 and may repay the dig); **Amos 8** (entered, moderate–uncertain).

**What the scan shows as a whole.** In every run except ch. 7, the top of the list is the late books of the Writings — Esther, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles. Two explanations compete and the draft does not choose between them: **shared period and register** (Persian-era Hebrew and Aramaic, court and chancery vocabulary), or **canonical design** (the Writings' last books bound together by more than date). A run against a chance baseline would be needed to say more. `[I]`, **moderate**.

## Christological Trajectory

- **Prophecy and trajectory — "one like a son of man" (7:13–14).** The figure "with the clouds of heaven" is given שָׁלְטָן ("dominion"), יְקָר ("glory") and מַלְכוּ ("kingdom"), and "all the peoples, nations and men of every language" will פְלַח ("serve") him — the verb Daniel's friends refused to give Nebuchadnezzar's gods (3:12, 14, 18, 28) and the verb used of serving Daniel's God (6:16, 20 [Heb 6:17, 21]). `[T]` (WLC) Jesus takes the figure as his own at his trial: ὄψεσθε τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου … ἐρχόμενον μετὰ τῶν νεφελῶν τοῦ οὐρανοῦ ("you will see the Son of Man … coming with the clouds of heaven", Mark 14:62; cf. Matt 26:64, SBLGNT). `[T]` for the NT wording. *Confidence: high.*
  - **The figure and the people share one gift.** What is *given* to the son of man at 7:14 (יְהִיב, "was given") is *given* to "the people of the saints of the Highest One" at 7:27 (יְהִיבַת), in the same words: מַלְכוּ ("kingdom"), שָׁלְטָן ("dominion"), and an everlasting kingdom that all dominions serve. `[T]` Whether the figure is an individual, a symbol of the people, or a heavenly representative of them is the most debated question in the book `[S]`; the text's own answer is that the gift is first his and then theirs. The New Testament ground: the Son who reigns and the people who reign with him. `[I]`
- **The stone not cut by hands (2:34–35, 44–45).** The kingdom God sets up "will crush and put an end to all these kingdoms". Luke 20:17–18 joins Ps 118:22 to this image: of the stone, ἐφ' ὃν δ' ἂν πέσῃ, λικμήσει αὐτόν ("on whomever it falls, it will scatter him like chaff") — λικμήσει, the verb Theodotion uses of the kingdom at 2:44 (SBLGNT; Theodotion export). `[T]` for the Greek wording; `[I]` for the allusion. *Confidence: high.* The stone's "without hands" is answered in the Hebrew vision: the last king "will be broken without human agency" — בְּאֶפֶס יָד ("without hand", 8:25). `[T]`
- **Resurrection and everlasting life (12:2–3).** חַיֵּי עוֹלָם ("everlasting life") — the noun phrase stands **only at Dan 12:2 in the Hebrew Bible** (WLC; the two lemmas together occur in 13 verses, but elsewhere as "live for ever" or "life for evermore", Ps 133:3). Jesus' εἰς ἀνάστασιν ζωῆς … εἰς ἀνάστασιν κρίσεως ("to a resurrection of life … to a resurrection of judgement", John 5:29) and εἰς κόλασιν αἰώνιον … εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον ("to eternal punishment … to eternal life", Matt 25:46) keep Daniel's two destinies (SBLGNT). `[T]` for the wording; `[I]` for the allusion. *Confidence: high.*
- **The Servant and the wise (12:3).** The מַשְׂכִּילִים ("those who have insight") "lead the many to righteousness" in the words of Isa 53:11 (see map). They are the Servant's followers, not the Servant: they fall "by sword and by flame" (11:33) and are refined by it (11:35). The trajectory runs to the one Servant who makes many righteous by his own death, and to those who share his sufferings. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate–high.*
- **The anointed one cut off (9:25–26).** מָשִׁיחַ ("anointed one") stands twice, without the article. Whether it is messianic prophecy, and who is meant, is the book's most contested Christological text; the ESV and NASB95 diverge sharply on it (see *Pulpit Notes*). *Confidence: contested* — a ⭐ HIGH dig.
- **Type — the righteous sufferer vindicated.** Daniel is condemned under a law framed against his prayer, put in a pit, a stone laid on its mouth and sealed with the king's ring (6:17 [Heb 6:18]), and brought up alive at dawn. Matthew's guard "sealing the stone" (Matt 27:66, σφραγίσαντες τὸν λίθον) uses the vocabulary of Theodotion's 6:18 (ἐσφραγίσατο, λίθον). `[T]` for the Greek; `[I]` for the typology. *Confidence: moderate.* Heb 11:33 lists those who "shut the mouths of lions" (ἔφραξαν στόματα λεόντων; Theodotion 6:22 ἐνέφραξεν τὰ στόματα τῶν λεόντων) among the faithful — exemplar, not type.

**Two "son of man" cautions, from the text itself.**
1. **In Hebrew, Daniel himself is called בֶּן־אָדָם ("son of man", 8:17)**, as Ezekiel is throughout his book. A sermon that hears "son of man" in Daniel and thinks only of 7:13 has not read 8:17. `[T]`
2. **The fourth man in the furnace** is "like a son of the gods" (3:25) in the words of Nebuchadnezzar, who at 3:28 calls him God's מַלְאַךְ ("angel"). Identifying him as the pre-incarnate Christ is a theological inference; the text gives it as a pagan king's description. `[T]` for the wording; `[I]`/`[S]` for the identification.

**Moralism check.** The besetting sermon is "Dare to be a Daniel": resolve like Daniel (1:8), stand like the three (3:18), pray like Daniel (6:10 [Heb 6:11]). The book does commend them — their faithfulness is real and the narrative approves it — but **its subject is God's sovereignty, and its first verb for God is "gave"** (1:2, 9, 17). Daniel's own understanding is a gift (1:17; 2:23, 30), and the book ends with him not understanding (12:8). Its hope is not that the faithful will always be rescued — the three say "even if He does not" (3:18), and the wise "fall" (11:33–35) — but that God's kingdom will be given to the son of man and his saints, and the dead will rise. Preach the courage *from* the kingdom, not *instead of* it. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **"Dare to be a Daniel."** The hero-example sermon that makes the book a manual for moral courage. *Bites at:* 1:8–16; 3:16–18; 6:10. *Why it is wrong:* the narrator credits the outcome of 1:8–16 to God (1:9, 17), and every tale ends with a king confessing *God*, not Daniel. *Corrective:* see the moralism check above.
2. **The prophecy chart.** Treating chs 2, 7, 9 and 11 as a timetable to be decoded from the newspaper, or settling the identity of every horn and king from the pulpit. *Bites at:* 2:36–45; 7:7–8, 19–27; 9:24–27; 11:2–45. *Why it is wrong:* the book itself says its words are "concealed and sealed up until the end time" (12:9), and Daniel ends not understanding (12:8). The identifications are `[S]`, held by interpreters, not given by the text beyond Media–Persia and Greece (8:20–21). *Corrective:* preach what every reading agrees the vision *does* — puts beast-kingdoms under the Ancient of Days' court (7:9–10, 26) — and hold the identifications lightly.
3. **Splitting the book.** Stories for children, visions for specialists. *Bites at:* any series that stops at ch. 6. *Why it is wrong:* the language seams cross the genre seams (ch. 1 Hebrew narrative; ch. 7 Aramaic vision), and the Echo Table shows the tales and visions interpreting each other. *Corrective:* at least one sermon on ch. 7, and the ch. 2 // ch. 7 pairing made explicit.
4. **Rescue triumphalism.** "God always delivers his faithful from the fire." *Bites at:* chs 3 and 6. *Why it is wrong:* the tales end in rescue, but the visions end in martyrdom — "some of those who have insight will fall" (11:35) — and the book's last hope lies beyond death (12:2, 13). The three themselves leave room for God not to rescue (3:18). *Corrective:* preach chs 3 and 6 with 11:33–35 and 12:2 in view.
5. **Over-reading "son of man".** Hearing 7:13 in every "son of man" and every angelic figure. *Bites at:* 3:25; 8:15–17; 10:5–6, 16–18. *Why it is wrong:* Daniel is בֶּן־אָדָם at 8:17; 10:16 and 10:18 have figures כִּדְמוּת בְּנֵי אָדָם ("in the likeness of the sons of men") and כְּמַרְאֵה אָדָם ("with human appearance"). *Corrective:* keep 7:13–14 for 7:13–14, and let the others be what the text calls them.
6. **The dating debate as sermon content.** Spending the pulpit on sixth-century versus second-century authorship, or treating either position as a test of orthodoxy. *Bites at:* chs 8 and 11. *Why it is a landmine:* the debate is real and the preacher should know it `[S]`, but neither date changes what the text claims about God's rule; a congregation needs the claim, not the controversy. *Corrective:* one sentence of honest acknowledgement where it arises; the sermon stays on the text.
7. **Chronological flattening.** Preaching chs 7–8 as if they came after Babylon's fall. *Bites at:* 7:1; 8:1. *Why it matters:* Daniel saw the beasts and the horn while Belshazzar still reigned (see *Structural Arc Map*). *Corrective:* name the date in the sermon.

## Pulpit Notes — ESV against NASB95

Where the pulpit text (ESV) and the study text (NASB95) diverge in a way that bears on a finding above, and what the congregation will hear. **These are notes to the preacher, not verdicts on either translation.**

1. **9:25–26 — one period or two, and "Messiah" or "an anointed one".** NASB95: "until Messiah the Prince there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks … the Messiah will be cut off". ESV: "to the coming of an anointed one, a prince, there shall be seven weeks. And for sixty-two weeks it shall be built again … an anointed one shall be cut off". The ESV follows the Masoretic accentuation, which puts the main pause (athnach) on שִׁבְעָה ("seven") — as the WLC has it, a feature of the Leningrad accent tradition, not of the consonantal text. The NASB95 runs the two periods together, as Theodotion does (ἑβδομάδες ἑπτὰ καὶ ἑβδομάδες ἑξήκοντα δύο). **An ESV congregation will hear neither "Messiah" nor a single period of sixty-nine weeks.** This is the largest translation divergence in the book; a ⭐ HIGH dig must set out both readings before any sermon.
2. **9:24 — "to seal up" or "to make an end of" sins.** Both versions follow the qere (וּלְהָתֵם, "to finish") and the singular חַטָּאת ("sin"). The written text (ketiv) is וְלַחְתֹּם חַטָּאוֹת ("and to seal up sins"), supported by Theodotion (τοῦ σφραγίσαι ἁμαρτίας); the qere has many manuscripts and the Old Greek (BHS apparatus). The ketiv makes the verse say "seal" twice — sins, then vision and prophet. **Neither congregation will hear it.** `[T]` (BHS apparatus as the Logos export prints it)
3. **7:13–14 — capital letters.** NASB95: "One like a Son of Man … He came up … presented before Him. And to Him was given dominion". ESV: "one like a son of man, and he came … to him was given dominion". The capitals are the NASB95's interpretation; the Aramaic has none. **An ESV congregation hears no signal of identity in the wording**, so the link to Mark 14:62 must be made explicitly from the pulpit.
4. **2:21 and 7:25 — the echo of "change".** ESV: "He changes times and seasons" … "shall think to change the times and the law". NASB95: "changes the times and the epochs" … "will intend to make alterations in times and in law". **Here the pulpit text carries the echo and the study text hides it** — the ESV hearer can catch the horn usurping God's prerogative without help.
5. **1:4 and 12:13 — "stand".** ESV keeps עָמַד as "stand" at both ends: "competent to stand in the king's palace" (1:4) … "shall stand in your allotted place at the end of the days" (12:13). NASB95: "ability for serving in the king's court" … "rise again for your allotted portion at the end of the age". **The ESV preserves the book's Hebrew Leitwort; the NASB95 interprets 12:13 as resurrection** — a defensible reading, but one the ESV leaves to the preacher.
6. **1:4 and 11:33–12:10 — the מַשְׂכִּילִים.** ESV: "skillful in all wisdom" (1:4), "the wise" (11:33, 35; 12:3, 10). NASB95: "showing intelligence" (1:4), "those who have insight" (11:33, 35; 12:3, 10). **Neither version lets the hearer hear 1:4 in 12:3**; the preacher has to point it out.
7. **8:27 — who did not understand?** The Hebrew וְאֵין מֵבִין is "and there was none understanding". NASB95: "there was none to explain it" (no interpreter). ESV: "and did not understand it" (Daniel himself). The ESV sharpens the 1:17 → 12:8 reversal; the NASB95 locates the failure elsewhere.
