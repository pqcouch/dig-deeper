## Headline Findings

Five claims, each reached from the text before any secondary source was opened, each verified against the corpus, and each surfaced by more than one tool.

1. **The divine name comes back at Psalm 84 — and it comes back as יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת ("LORD of hosts").** In the Elohistic collection (Pss 42–83) יְהוָה stands in 40 verses and אֱלֹהִים in 209; across Pss 84–89 the ratio inverts to 29 and 15. No psalm from 78 to 83 carries the name in more than two verses. **Psalm 84 carries it in six of its thirteen** — and the hinge is in Psalm 83's own last two verses, the only two in that psalm that use the name: "that they may seek **your name**, O LORD" (83:16 [MT 17]), "that they may know that you alone, **whose name is the LORD**, are Most High" (83:18 [MT 19]). Psalm 83 ends asking that the name be known; Psalm 84 opens using it. `[T]` Surfaced by: Positional Necessity Check, Context, Repetition, Original Languages, Tool 1. *High confidence on the distribution; moderate-high on reading the junction as design.*

2. **The sons of Korah sing about the threshold — and the threshold was the family's job, and their father died for wanting past it.** Numbers 16 is a rebellion about *drawing near*: הִקְרִיב ("to bring near") five times in 16:5–17, Moses' charge "he has **brought you near** to himself… and you seek the priesthood also" (16:10), and Moses' warning to "depart from the **tents of these wicked men**" (16:26). Num 26:11 then says, in four Hebrew words, **"But the sons of Korah did not die."** 1 Chr 9:19 gives the surviving family's office: **"keepers of the thresholds" (שֹׁמְרֵי הַסִּפִּים)**. And Psalm 84:10 [MT 11] turns on a hapax legomenon built from that noun — **הִסְתּוֹפֵף**, "to be at the threshold", the only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible — preferred to **אָהֳלֵי־רֶשַׁע ("tents of wickedness"), a collocation that also stands in exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one.** `[T]` on every datum. Surfaced by: Vocabulary (proper-noun inventory and the outstanding-word check), Quotation/Allusion, Original Languages, Historical Background, Who Am I?. *High confidence on the lexical data; moderate-high on the allusion, for the reason given at Tool 11b.*

3. **Three beatitudes — and Psalm 84 is the only psalm in the Psalter that has three.** אַשְׁרֵי stands in 25 verses of the Psalter; four psalms have two (32; 119; 128; 137); **Psalm 84 has three** (vv. 4, 5, 12 [MT 5, 6, 13]). They fall at the three structural joints, and the third is the psalm's last line. More: they move *outward* — **those who dwell** in the house (MT 5, יוֹשְׁבֵי, a participle), **the man** on the road with highways in his heart (MT 6, אָדָם), **the man** who trusts (MT 13, אָדָם, with no mention of the house at all). A psalm by the temple's doorkeepers ends by locating the blessing somewhere other than the door. `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tool 1, So What?, Christological Reading. *High confidence.*

4. **Psalm 84 answers the question Psalm 42 asked.** Ps 42:2 [MT 3]: *מָתַי אָבוֹא וְאֵרָאֶה פְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים* — "when shall I come and **appear** before God?" Ps 84:7 [MT 8]: *יֵרָאֶה אֶל־אֱלֹהִים בְּצִיּוֹן* — "**he appears** before God in Zion." Same verb, same niphal. And **אֵל חַי ("the living God") stands in four verses of the whole Hebrew Bible — Josh 3:10; Hos 2:1; Ps 42:2 [MT 3]; Ps 84:2 [MT 3] — two of the four being these two psalms.** Psalm 42 opens the Korahite collection in Book II; Psalm 84 opens the Korahite block in Book III. `[T]` Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion (Move 4), Vocabulary, Positional Necessity Check, Tone and Feel. *High confidence on the lexical data; high on the link, given two independent rare carriers plus the shared authorship-ascription.*

5. **The psalm prays for the anointed whose fall Book III is about to mourn.** פְּנֵי מְשִׁיחֶךָ ("the face of your anointed") stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: **Ps 84:9 [MT 10]; Ps 132:10; 2 Chr 6:42** — the psalm, the Zion-and-David psalm, and Solomon's temple prayer. Within Book III, מָשִׁיחַ occurs **only** at 84:9 [MT 10], 89:38 and 89:51; and מָגֵן ("shield") **only** at 84:9, 84:11 [MT 10, 12] and 89:18. Psalm 84 asks God to *look on* the face of his anointed. Five psalms later Psalm 89 says he has *spurned* him (89:38) and the enemies mock *the footsteps of your anointed* (89:51). `[T]` Surfaced by: Positional Necessity Check, Quotation/Allusion (Move 4, planted-for-later), Vocabulary, Bible Timeline, Christological Reading. *High confidence on the distributions; high on the forward link, which is a distribution within one Book rather than a thematic impression.*

## Canonical position (Phase 0.55)

**Section.** Psalms heads the **Ketuvim**, the third division of the Tanak and in BHS order its first book. `[T]`

**Reading implication.** The Writings are the covenant people's answer: what Israel says back, with Torah and Prophets already behind it. A psalm may therefore quote either without introduction — and Psalm 84 does, reaching into Numbers for its own family history and into the temple tradition for its vocabulary. `[I]`

**Presupposes** — the testable claims, each checked:

|---|---|---|
| **Num 16; 26:11** | That "sons of Korah" is a name with a story — a rebellion about access to the sanctuary, and a line that survived it | קֹרַח stands in 37 verses: Gen 36 ×4 (an Edomite namesake); Exod 6:21, 24; Num 16–17, 26–27 ×13; the eleven Psalm superscriptions; 1 Chr 1:35; 2:43; 6:7, 22; 9:19 |
| **1 Chr 9:19, 22; 26:1** | That the Korahites were **gatekeepers**, keepers of the thresholds | סַף ("threshold") stands in 28 verses, 1 Chr 9:19 and 9:22 among them |
| **A functioning sanctuary with courts, altars and a house** | The psalm's whole furniture: מִשְׁכָּן, חָצֵר ×2, בַּיִת ×3, מִזְבֵּחַ | all present, listed at Tool 10 |
| **Zion as the place of appearing** | v. 7 [MT 8] *in Zion*, with no explanation offered | — |
| **An anointed king in being** | v. 9 [MT 10] asks God to look on his face | מָשִׁיחַ in the Psalter: 2:2; 18:50; 20:6; 28:8; 84:9; 89:38, 51; 105:15; 132:10, 17 |

**Handoff.** Within the Psalter, Psalm 84 hands forward to the rest of the Korahite block (85; 87; 88) and, through מָשִׁיחַ and מָגֵן, to the covenant crisis at Psalm 89. Beyond it, to the temple-and-presence theme that runs to John 1:14 and Rev 21:22. `[T]` for the Psalter handoff.

**Neighbours.** Psalm 83 (Asaph — the last of eleven consecutive Asaph psalms, and the last psalm of the Elohistic collection) before; Psalm 85 (Korah) after. Book III runs Asaph 73–83, **Korah 84–85**, David 86, **Korah 87–88**, Ethan 89. `[T]`

**Canonical and compositional order.** No live divergence for this psalm. The usual compositional question — whether Psalm 84 is pre-exilic temple poetry or a later composition looking back — bears on dating, not on canonical placement, and nothing in this report turns on it. Recorded at Open Questions.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**At passage level.** The psalm exists to say what the sanctuary is worth, and then to say where the blessing of it finally rests. Its verbs make the shape: the first movement is **longing** (נִכְסְפָה, כָּלְתָה — "has longed", "has even fainted"), the second is **travelling** (עֹבְרֵי, יֵלְכוּ — "passing through", "they go"), the third is **asking** (שִׁמְעָה, הַאֲזִינָה, רְאֵה, הַבֵּט — four imperatives to God in two verses), and the fourth is **preferring** (בָּחַרְתִּי — "I have chosen"). `[T]`

It is not an argument against an objection and it does not instruct anybody. Apart from the imperatives addressed to God, **the psalm issues no command to any human being at all** — a notable fact in a psalm so often preached as an exhortation to love church. What it does instead is pronounce three blessings and state one preference. `[T]`

**The purpose, stated:** to express — and so to teach — that nearness to God in his dwelling is the thing most worth wanting, and that the blessing of it belongs not only to those who live there but to those on the road and, finally, to anyone who trusts. `[I]` *High confidence; the three-beatitude movement is text-marked and does the work.*

**At book level.** Within the Psalter the psalm serves the arc by holding desire open at the point where Book III is losing everything the desire attaches to. Its purpose is not undermined by Psalm 89; it is what makes Psalm 89 bearable, because its last word is about trust rather than about the building. `[I]`

**Book-overview note.** The overview places Psalm 84 under "Zion / the temple" and lists its function as "the city of God and his dwelling as the ground of security and joy". That is right as far as it goes, and the psalm's last verse goes further: the ground named at the end is not the city but the LORD of hosts himself.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Question 1 — what has the preceding movement set up that this passage provides?**

Three things, and the first is the one most easily missed because it is a fact about ink rather than about theme.

**(a) The Elohistic Psalter ends at Psalm 83, and Psalm 84 is the first psalm out of it.** The distribution, counted by lemma against the WLC:

| Block | יְהוָה — verses | אֱלֹהִים — verses |
|---|---|---|
| Pss 1–41 (Book I) | 250 | 48 |
| **Pss 42–83 (the Elohistic collection)** | **40** | **209** |
| Pss 84–89 | 29 | 15 |
| Ps 78 / 79 / 80 / 81 / 82 / 83 | 2 / 1 / 2 / 2 / 0 / 2 | 8 / 3 / 5 / 3 / 3 / 3 |
| **Ps 84** | **6** | **6** |

`[T]` The name has been scarce for forty-two psalms; at Psalm 84 it returns, and it returns six times in thirteen verses — three times more often than in any of the six psalms before it.

**And the junction is marked in the text, not only in the statistics.** Psalm 83's two uses of the name are its final two verses, and both are *about the name*: "let them seek **your name**, O LORD" (83:16 [MT 17]); "let them know that you alone, **whose name is the LORD**, are Most High over all the earth" (83:18 [MT 19]). `[T]` Psalm 83 is a prayer that the nations should come to know the name. Psalm 84 opens by using it — and uses it in the form that belongs to the sanctuary: **יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת**. The Psalter answers Psalm 83's petition on the next page, in its own practice. `[I]` *Moderate-high confidence: the distribution is a hard fact, the two verses of Ps 83 are a hard fact, and the reading of the junction as deliberate is the inference.*

**(b) Psalm 83 is a prayer against those who want to seize God's dwelling.** Its enemies say "let us wipe them out as a nation, that the name of Israel be remembered no more" (83:4 [MT 5]) and "let us take possession for ourselves of **the pastures of God** (נְאוֹת אֱלֹהִים)" (83:12 [MT 13]). `[T]` Psalm 84 then opens: "How lovely are **your dwelling places** (מִשְׁכְּנוֹתֶיךָ)." Different words — נְאוֹת, plural of נָאָה ("pasture"), against מִשְׁכָּן ("dwelling") — so this is a conceptual sequence, not a verbal chain, and it is recorded as such. The enemy covets the dwelling; the psalmist loves it. `[I]` *Moderate confidence; the lemmas differ and I will not dress the link as verbal.*

**(c) Eleven consecutive Asaph psalms end, and the Korahites take the psalter back.** Book III's attributions: Asaph 73–83, **Korah 84–85**, David 86, Korah 87–88, Ethan 89. `[T]` The Asaph block is dominated by the nation's danger — the sanctuary burned (74:3–8), God's apparent forgetting (77), the nations in the inheritance (79:1), the enemies conspiring (83). The first thing said after eleven psalms of that is *how lovely are your dwelling places*.

**Question 2 — why does this psalm exist here and not elsewhere?**

Because this is the one place in the Psalter where *longing for the sanctuary* has to be said after the sanctuary has been described as burned and besieged, and before it is said that the covenant with its king has failed. Psalm 84 is held between Asaph's "they have set your sanctuary on fire" (74:7) and Ethan's "you have renounced the covenant with your servant" (89:39). It is the Book's moment of pure desire, and its position is what gives the desire its edge. `[I]`

**The positional reading sharpens two things in the psalm itself.**

First, **v. 9 [MT 10]** — "look on the face of your anointed" — is not a liturgical flourish. Within Book III, מָשִׁיחַ stands at 84:9 and then only at 89:38, 51, where God has *spurned* the anointed and the enemies mock his footsteps. `[T]` Psalm 84 asks for the look; Psalm 89 reports that it was withheld. The same is true of מָגֵן: in Book III, 84:9, 84:11 and 89:18 only, and at 89:18 "our **shield** belongs to the LORD, our king to the Holy One of Israel." `[T]`

Second, **the three beatitudes** read differently in a Book heading toward loss. "Blessed are those who dwell in your house" is a statement that will shortly be untrue of anyone; "blessed is the man who trusts in you" — the last line — is not. `[I]`

**Revisited after Tool 11.** Move 4 adds a backward link the Phase 0.6 pass did not have: Psalm 84 also answers Psalm 42, the opening psalm of the Korahite collection in Book II (Headline 4). So Psalm 84 looks in both directions along the Korahite thread — resolving the Korahites' opening question and planting the Korahite petition that Ethan's psalm will mourn.

**What it adds.** Psalm 84 ends on a statement about what God gives — grace, glory, no good withheld. Psalm 85 takes up the same verb, the same subject and the same object and turns them into petition for the land: *the LORD will give what is good, and our land will yield its increase* (85:12). The glory Psalm 84 longs to see in the house becomes, one psalm later, glory asked to *dwell in our land* — so the outward movement of Psalm 84's three beatitudes (house → road → anyone who trusts) carries on past the psalm's last line, to the land. `[I]` And Psalm 85 opens on forgiveness — "you forgave the iniquity of your people; you covered all their sin" (85:2 [MT 3]) — which is the answer to the question of standing that the Korah name raises in Psalm 84. `[I]` *Confidence: high on the lexical data — four shared words verified, and the three-word verse-pair unique in the Psalter; moderate-high on reading the pair as an editorial hinge; moderate on the two root-level links.*

**Why here and nowhere else — the position stated as a whole.** Four lines meet at Psalm 84:

1. **The end of the Elohistic collection** (Pss 42–83). The name returns here, in the temple form יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, one verse after Psalm 83 has prayed that the name be known. *(Question 1(a) above.)*
2. **The end of the Asaph block** (Pss 73–83). After eleven psalms in which God's dwelling is burned (74:7, the same noun מִשְׁכָּן) and coveted (83:12), the first thing said is *how lovely are your dwelling places*. *(Question 1(b)–(c).)*
3. **Book III's last hopeful word about the king.** מָשִׁיחַ and מָגֵן stand in Book III only here and in Psalm 89, where the anointed is spurned. *(Question 2; Tool 11d.)*
4. **The head of the Book III Korahite group** — answering Psalm 42's question across the Korahite thread, and handing forward into Psalm 85 by the links tabulated above. *(Tool 11d; this addition.)*

**What breaks if it moves.** Put it before Psalm 83 and the name returns before the prayer for the name, and the Elohistic collection loses its clean edge. Put it after Psalm 85 and *the LORD will give what is good* (85:12) loses the verse it answers. Put it after Psalm 89 and the prayer for the anointed is offered for a king the Book has just declared spurned. Take it from the head of the Korahite group and the answer to Psalm 42 no longer stands where the series reopens.

**What is not claimed.** Nothing rests on 84 as a *number*; the argument is positional, and any reading of the figure itself would be numerology. And "must" is an inference about editors (`[I]`), not a datum: what the text shows is that the psalm fits every neighbour and every neighbour fits it. **Ranked by strength:** the Elohistic boundary (a count, reproducible in one search) → the מָשִׁיחַ/מָגֵן distribution and the answer to Psalm 42 (rare words, each verified) → the forward hinge into Psalm 85 (four shared words and a verse-pair unique in the Psalter) → the 83→84 dwelling link and the root-level "near" link into Psalm 85, which corroborate and do not carry.

#### General context

**Immediately before.** Psalm 83 — the last Asaph psalm, a prayer against a named coalition of ten peoples, ending in a petition that the nations learn the name. **Immediately after.** Psalm 85 — Korahite, "LORD, you were favourable to your land; you restored the fortunes of Jacob" — a psalm of restoration-after-anger, and — as v1.1 shows — the sequel to Psalm 84 in its vocabulary as well as its theme: the Psalter's only two verses with *give*, *LORD* and *good* together (84:11; 85:12), *glory* (84:11 → 85:9), *walk* (84:11 → 85:13) and *Jacob* (84:8 → 85:1). See the table under the Positional Necessity Check. `[T]`

**The section.** Psalms 84–88 form the Korahite close of Book III, interrupted only by the Davidic Psalm 86. The block runs: longing for the house (84), restoration prayed (85), [David's prayer, 86], Zion's citizenship (87), and then **Psalm 88 — the darkest psalm in the Psalter, which ends with "darkness is my closest friend"**, also Korahite. `[T]` The Korahite block does not resolve upward. Psalm 84 is its brightest point and Psalm 88 its last word.

**Widely read out of context?** Yes, in one specific way. Verse 10 [MT 11] — "better is one day in your courts" — is among the most-quoted lines in the Psalter and is almost always read as a devotional comparison between church and the world. The verse is that; it is also the sentence in which a descendant of Korah says he would rather be back at the door than inside anybody's tent. See Tool 11b and the Pitfalls.

### 3. Structure

The three אַשְׁרֵי are the structural markers, and they are the psalm's own, not an imposition.

| Verses (Eng [MT]) | Content | Marker |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 [2–5] | The dwelling places; the longing of soul, heart and flesh; the bird at the altars | closes with **אַשְׁרֵי** — *those who dwell in your house* |
| 5–7 [6–8] | The man with highways in his heart; the valley of Baca; from strength to strength; appearing in Zion | opens with **אַשְׁרֵי** — *the man whose strength is in you* |
| 8–9 [9–10] | Prayer: hear, give ear, see, look | *Selah* at MT 9, as at MT 5 |
| 10–12 [11–13] | The comparison; the sun and shield; the LORD withholds no good thing | closes with **אַשְׁרֵי** — *the man who trusts in you* |

**The device is a widening.** The three beatitudes are not three ways of saying the same thing:

- **MT 5** — *אַשְׁרֵי יוֹשְׁבֵי בֵיתֶךָ*, "blessed are **those who dwell** in your house". A participle of יָשַׁב — the residents. In the psalm's own frame these are the priests, Levites and gatekeepers; the people whose address is the sanctuary.
- **MT 6** — *אַשְׁרֵי אָדָם עוֹז־לוֹ בָךְ*, "blessed is **the man** whose strength is in you". Not a resident: a man with roads in his heart, three verses from home at best.
- **MT 13** — *אַשְׁרֵי אָדָם בֹּטֵחַ בָּךְ*, "blessed is **the man** who trusts in you". The house is not mentioned. Neither is the road.

`[T]` — the lemma distribution is checked: אָדָם ("man") stands at MT 6 and MT 13; יָשַׁב ("dwell") at MT 5 only. The psalm moves from *resident* through *pilgrim* to *anyone*, and the final term has no spatial qualifier of any kind.

**Why this is the finding and not a tidy observation.** A psalm written for and by the sanctuary staff could have been expected to end where it began. It ends instead by relocating the blessing away from the building and onto the person trusting — which is both the psalm's climax and the reason it survives the loss of the building Psalms 74 and 89 are mourning. `[I]` *Moderate-high confidence on the reading; high on the distribution.*

**Two Selahs** (MT 5, MT 9) fall after the first beatitude and after the prayer, which is consistent with the four-part division above but does not by itself establish it. `[T]`

### 4. Linking Words

Sparse, as poetry usually is, and all the more load-bearing for it. Four connectives carry the psalm.

| Verse (Eng [MT]) | Hebrew | Force |
|---|---|---|
| 3 [4] | **גַּם** | "even / also" — the bird is an *a fortiori*: even she has found a house. What the psalmist is asking for is something a sparrow already has `[T]` |
| 6 [7] | **גַּם** | "also" — the second clause of the Baca verse, and the one BHS marks as corrupt (see Textual Variants) |
| 10 [11] | **כִּי** | "for" — grounds the three blessings and the prayer that precede it in a *comparison*: because a day here is better than a thousand elsewhere |
| 11 [12] | **כִּי** | "for" — grounds the comparison in a *statement about God*: because the LORD God is sun and shield |

**The two כִּי are the psalm's argument, and they stack.** Why is the man who trusts blessed? ← because one day in the courts beats a thousand (v. 10) ← because the LORD God himself is sun and shield, giver of grace and glory, and withholds no good thing (v. 11). `[T]` The chain runs *down* from the experience to its ground, and the ground is not the sanctuary but the one who lives in it. A reading that stops at v. 10 has the comparison without its reason.

**The גַּם at v. 3 [MT 4] is easy to flatten.** NASB95 "the bird **also**", ESV "**Even** the sparrow". The force is concessive-comparative: a creature with no covenant and no priesthood has nested at the altars, and the man who wrote this is outside wanting in. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

Hebrew poetry throughout. Three pairings do real work.

**v. 2 [MT 3] — the whole person, in three terms.**
> נִכְסְפָה וְגַם־כָּלְתָה **נַפְשִׁי** לְחַצְרוֹת יְהוָה / **לִבִּי וּבְשָׂרִי** יְרַנְּנוּ אֶל אֵל־חָי

נֶפֶשׁ in the first colon is answered by **לֵב וּבָשָׂר** ("heart and flesh") in the second — not a synonym but an expansion, and the expansion is *outward into the body*. `[T]` The longing is not interior piety: it has a body in it. And note the verbs invert — the first colon's are of *failing* (נִכְסְפָה, כָּלְתָה — "longed", "fainted"), the second's is of *singing* (יְרַנְּנוּ). The same person faints and sings in one verse.

**v. 10 [MT 11] — two comparisons, chiastically opposed.**
> a day **in your courts** … better than a thousand
> to be at the threshold **in the house of my God** … rather than dwelling **in tents of wickedness**

Two scales — *time* (one day against a thousand) and *place* (the doorstep against a tent) — and both resolve the same way. `[T]` The second is the sharper, because the comparison is not between the sanctuary and a worse building but between the sanctuary's *least position* and the alternative's *interior*. Outside at God's door beats inside anywhere else.

**v. 11 [MT 12] — a pairing that should be odd and is not.**
> כִּי **שֶׁמֶשׁ וּמָגֵן** יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים

Sun and shield: one gives, one guards. The second colon unpacks them in order — "**grace and glory** the LORD gives" (what a sun does: shines out), "**no good thing** does he withhold" (what a shield does: nothing gets through that should not). `[I]` *Moderate confidence on reading the second colon as an ordered unpacking of the first; the correspondence is clean but the text does not mark it.*

**A break worth noting.** v. 7 [MT 8] has no parallelism at all in its second colon — יֵרָאֶה אֶל־אֱלֹהִים בְּצִיּוֹן is a single flat clause after the chiming "from strength to strength". The line arrives rather than balances, which is the right shape for the end of a journey. `[T]`

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**N/A** — poetry, not narrative. There is no narratorial voice outside the action and no third-party frame.

The nearest thing is the **superscription** (MT 1), which speaks *about* the psalm in an editorial voice. Its presence is `[T]`; the historical claim that the sons of Korah composed or performed it is `[I]`/`[S]` — an attribution, which the `[T]` boundary excludes. What matters exegetically is that the ascription is *on the page* and that the psalm's own vocabulary turns out to be in conversation with the Korah narrative (Tool 11b) — which is a text-observable fact about the poem and does not depend on the attribution being historically right.

**Ancient versions check.** Run, and it returns the most substantial finding in this section.

**Translation-tradition split — v. 11 [MT 12].** Where the MT has *כִּי שֶׁמֶשׁ וּמָגֵן יְהוָה אֱלֹהִים* ("for a sun and shield is the LORD God"), **the LXX has no sun and no shield at all**: ὅτι ἔλεον καὶ ἀλήθειαν ἀγαπᾷ Κύριος ὁ θεός — "for the Lord God loves mercy and truth." The LXX tradition does not have the metaphor; the Hebrew does, once.

**Translation loss — not a variant (category 1).** The three hapax legomena cannot survive translation, and neither can the fact that the second and third beatitudes use אָדָם while the first does not. No English version carries them and none is at fault.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Homesickness, and then settledness.** The psalm's opening register is physical deprivation: נִכְסְפָה ("has longed") and כָּלְתָה ("has been consumed, fainted") are both verbs of *running out*. `[T]` The register is not devotional warmth; it is the feeling of being somewhere you do not want to be.

**The bird does the emotional work.** It is the psalm's only picture, and it is domestic, small and slightly comic — a bird has sorted out its housing at the altars, young and all, while the man who writes the psalm is outside. `[T]` C. S. Lewis's rule applies exactly: the psalm does not say "I feel displaced"; it shows a nesting sparrow and lets the reader feel it.

**The turn is at v. 5 [MT 6].** From there the verbs are of motion and increase — passing through, making a spring, going from strength to strength — and the grammar stops fainting. `[T]`

**The close is quiet.** The last movement is not a shout: it is a man stating a preference (*I have chosen*), a theological sentence about God, and a one-line beatitude. The psalm ends at conversational volume. `[T]`

**Soundtrack:** not brass. A single sustained line that opens out — and the last verse played very plainly.

**For the preacher:** do not preach this cheerfully from the first line. The psalm earns its brightness over twelve verses and it begins in deprivation. A sermon that starts where the psalm ends has thrown away the psalm.

**יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת — the Psalter's densest concentration.** The two lemmas co-occur in **14 verses of the Psalter**: 24:10; 33:6; 46:7, 11; 48:8; 59:5; 80:4, 19; **84:2, 4, 9, 13**; 89:8; 103:21. `[T]` Four of the fourteen are in this psalm — more than any other, with Pss 46 and 80 next at two each. The title also **frames** the psalm: it is the last phrase of v. 2 and the first phrase of v. 13.

**Why the title and not another.** צְבָאוֹת is the martial word — the LORD of armies. A psalm of longing for a quiet courtyard addresses God four times by his most military title, and three of the four fall in the frame. `[I]` The simplest reading is that the psalm is not sentimental about the sanctuary: the house it loves belongs to the God of the armies, and v. 9's petition — *look on the face of your anointed* — is a wartime request. *Moderate-high confidence.*

**The שֶׁמֶשׁ / מָגֵן pairing is answered by the two טוֹב.** v. 11 says "**good** is a day in your courts"; v. 12 says God "will not withhold **good**" — so the psalm's own comparison (the day in the courts) is folded into the general statement (God withholds no good thing) by a shared word. `[T]` The "good" the psalmist wants is named in the previous verse.

### 11. Quotation and Allusion

*Live sources for the Psalter, per the book-overview's intertextual map:* Torah, the Davidic covenant, creation, exodus/salvation history, **Zion/the temple**, wisdom, and the Psalter's own internal cross-links. Psalm 84 draws on three of these — Torah (through Numbers), Zion/temple, and the internal links — and the Torah source is the one the overview's map does not anticipate.

#### 11a. Independent candidate-finding

Derived from the psalm's own distinctive vocabulary before any source was consulted: *מִשְׁכָּן* plural (→ Ps 132:5; Ps 87:2); *אֵל חַי* (→ Josh 3:10; Hos 2:1; Ps 42:3); *יְדִידוֹת* (→ Ps 45:1; Deut 33:12); *פְּנֵי מְשִׁיחֶךָ* (→ Ps 132:10; 2 Chr 6:42); *סַף* behind the hapax (→ 1 Chr 9:19); *אֹהֶל* + ר־שׁ־ע (→ Num 16:26); *יֵרָאֶה* niphal of ראה with God as object (→ Ps 42:3; and the three pilgrimage laws). Each was then checked.

#### 11b. Numbers 16 and 26:11 — the sons of Korah at the door *(moderate-high confidence; the strongest finding in the report)*

**The claim.** The superscription's name is not a label but a hyperlink, and the psalm's climactic verse activates it.

**Move 1 — source context.** Numbers 16 is a rebellion about **access**. Korah the Levite, with Dathan and Abiram, challenges Moses and Aaron: "all the congregation are holy… why then do you exalt yourselves?" Moses' reply is built on one verb — **קָרַב** in the hiphil, "to bring near": "the LORD will make known who is his and who is holy, **and will bring him near** to himself; the one whom he chooses **he will bring near** to himself" (16:5); "is it too small a thing for you that the God of Israel has separated you… **to bring you near** to himself, to do the service of the tabernacle… and you seek the priesthood also?" (16:9–10). The lemma stands **five times in 16:5–17 and fourteen times across Num 16–18** `[T]`. Moses then warns the congregation: "depart from **the tents of these wicked men**" (16:26). The earth opens; Korah's household goes down.

Then, eleven chapters later, a four-word verse standing on its own with a *setumah* after it: **וּבְנֵי־קֹרַח לֹא־מֵתוּ** — "But the sons of Korah did not die" (Num 26:11). `[T]`

**Move 2 — book usage (the Psalter's use of Korah).** The name stands in **eleven Psalm superscriptions**: 42, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49 (Book II) and 84, 85, 87, 88 (Book III) `[T]`. The Korahite psalms are not a random scatter: they are disproportionately about God's dwelling and God's city — 46 ("there is a river whose streams make glad the city of God"), 48 ("beautiful in elevation… Mount Zion"), 87 ("the LORD loves the gates of Zion"), and this psalm. The family that was nearly destroyed over access to the sanctuary supplies the Psalter with its sanctuary poetry. `[I]` *Moderate-high confidence; the distribution is checked, the reading of it is inference.*

**Move 3 — OT-to-OT.** 1 Chr 9:19 and 9:22 give the surviving Korahites' office in the second temple's organisation: *שֹׁמְרֵי הַסִּפִּים* — **"keepers of the thresholds"** — with 1 Chr 26:1 listing the Korahites among the gatekeepers (שֹׁעֲרִים). סַף ("threshold") stands in 28 verses of the Hebrew Bible; these are two of them. `[T]` Chronicles stands in the Ketuvim alongside the Psalter, and its account of the Levitical guilds is the canonical reader's description of who the Korahites were.

**The activation — Psalm 84:10 [MT 11].** *בָּחַרְתִּי הִסְתּוֹפֵף בְּבֵית אֱלֹהַי מִדּוּר בְּאָהֳלֵי־רֶשַׁע*. Three things in one line:

- **הִסְתּוֹפֵף** is a hapax legomenon, a hithpoel formed from **סַף**, the noun in the Korahite job description. "I have chosen to **be-at-the-threshold** in the house of my God." `[T]`
- **אָהֳלֵי־רֶשַׁע ("tents of wickedness")** — אֹהֶל ("tent") with the abstract noun רֶשַׁע ("wickedness") stands in **exactly one verse of the Hebrew Bible: this one** `[T]`. Numbers 16:26 has אֹהֶל with the adjective **רָשָׁע** ("wicked") — "the tents of these **wicked men**". **Same root, different lemma.** The link is collocational and consonantal, not a verbal chain, and it is reported as such: I will not call two lemmas one.
- **דּוּר** ("to dwell"), the verb for what one does in those tents, is itself a hapax. `[T]`

**Weighing it honestly.** On the verbal evidence alone, אֹהֶל + ר־שׁ־ע would be a weak allusion — the roots are common and the lemmas differ. What raises it is that **four independent carriers point the same way**: the name in the superscription (a character-dossier carrier, Tool 11 §7); the hapax built from the family's own office-noun; the collocation shared with the one verse in Numbers 16 that names the alternative to the sanctuary; and the subject-matter of Numbers 16 being precisely *who may draw near*. No one of these would carry the claim; together they are more than coincidence looks like. **Moderate-high confidence — and the weakest link (the lemma difference) is named rather than smoothed.**

**What it adds, if it holds.** Psalm 84:10 stops being a devotional comparison and becomes a family's confession. *Our father wanted the inside and was swallowed for it; we were given the doorstep, and the doorstep is better than any tent.* `[I]` It also explains the psalm's structure: a family whose history is about the danger of grasping at nearness writes a psalm that ends by relocating the blessing from the house to the trusting.

**Load-the-story rule: satisfied.** Numbers 16, 26:11, 1 Chr 9:19, 9:22 and 26:1 were read, not recalled.

#### 11c. Psalm 132 and 2 Chronicles 6:42 — the face of the anointed *(high confidence on the data)*

**Move 1 — source context.** Psalm 132 is the Psalter's fullest treatment of the ark's installation and the Davidic oath. David swears not to rest "until I find a place for the LORD, **dwelling places (מִשְׁכָּנוֹת) for the Mighty One of Jacob**" (132:5); the psalm then pivots on **"for the sake of David your servant, do not turn away the face of your anointed" (אַל־תָּשֵׁב פְּנֵי מְשִׁיחֶךָ, 132:10)**; and it closes with the LORD's own choice: "the LORD has chosen Zion… 'This is my resting place for ever'" (132:13–14). `[T]`

**Move 2 — book usage.** Within the Psalter, מִשְׁכָּן stands in 11 verses: 26:8; 43:3; 46:4; 49:11; 74:7; 78:28, 60; **84:1**; 87:2; **132:5, 7** `[T]`. Two of these are the psalm's close neighbours and both are Korahite (46:4; 87:2); two are the Zion-and-David psalm (132); and 74:7 is "they have burned **your dwelling place**" — Asaph, eleven psalms before this one. **The same noun names what Asaph says was burned and what Psalm 84 says is lovely.** `[T]`

**Move 3 — OT-to-OT.** **פְּנֵי מְשִׁיחֶךָ ("the face of your anointed") stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Ps 84:9; Ps 132:10; 2 Chr 6:42** — and 2 Chr 6:42 is the closing line of Solomon's prayer at the temple's dedication, which quotes Ps 132:8–10 almost verbatim. `[T]` (A lemma co-occurrence search returns five verses; 1 Sam 2:35 has *before my anointed* and Isa 45:1 has *before him*, different constructions. The control is reported because it halved the result.) So the phrase already belongs to a canonical conversation about the temple and the king *before* Psalm 84 reaches for it: the psalm is using the dedication-prayer's own words.

**What it adds.** Verse 9's petition is not a general prayer for the monarch. It is the temple-dedication formula, prayed from outside the temple, in a Book that will shortly report that the prayer was not answered.

#### 11d. Move 4 — internal echoes within the Psalter

Three, and they run in both directions.

**Internal: *answers Ps 42* — high confidence.** `[T]`
Ps 42:2 [MT 3] asks *מָתַי אָבוֹא וְאֵרָאֶה פְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים*, "when shall I come and **appear** before God?" Ps 84:7 [MT 8] states *יֵרָאֶה אֶל־אֱלֹהִים בְּצִיּוֹן*, "**he appears** before God in Zion." Same root, same niphal, question answered by statement. Two further carriers: **אֵל חַי**, one of only four occurrences each in the Hebrew Bible and the Psalter's only two (42:3; 84:3); and **נֶפֶשׁ longing** — Ps 42:2 "as the deer pants… so my **soul** pants"; Ps 84:3 "my **soul** has longed and even fainted". Both psalms are Korahite; Ps 42 opens the Korahite collection in Book II and Ps 84 opens the Korahite block in Book III. *What it adds:* the Korahite thread has a shape. It opens with a man cut off from the house asking when he will get there, and resumes, forty-two psalms later, with the answer — and the answer is not "when you arrive" but "the one whose strength is in you is already blessed on the road."

**Internal: *planted for Ps 89* — high confidence.** `[T]`
Within Book III, **מָשִׁיחַ** stands only at 84:9; 89:38; 89:51, and **מָגֵן** only at 84:9; 84:11; 89:18. Psalm 84 prays *look on the face of your anointed* and calls God *our shield*; Psalm 89 says "you have **spurned** your anointed" (89:38), "your enemies have mocked the footsteps of **your anointed**" (89:51), and "our **shield** belongs to the LORD" (89:18). *What it adds:* Psalm 84's petition is Book III's last hopeful word about the king, and the Book records its refusal. A sermon on Psalm 84 that does not know this will preach the prayer as though it were answered.

**Internal: *sits with Ps 87* — moderate confidence.** `[T]` on the lexical data.
Ps 87:2, Korahite and three psalms later: *אֹהֵב יְהוָה שַׁעֲרֵי צִיּוֹן **מִכֹּל מִשְׁכְּנוֹת יַעֲקֹב*** — "the LORD **loves** the **gates** of Zion more than all the **dwellings** of Jacob." Psalm 84 has *how **lovely** are your **dwelling places***, the threshold at v. 11, and *the God of **Jacob*** at v. 9. The same three terms — loving, dwellings, Jacob — are redistributed, and where Ps 84 prefers the threshold to the tents, Ps 87 says God prefers the gates to the dwellings. *What it adds:* the preference the psalmist states in Ps 84:10 turns out, in Ps 87, to be God's own.

**Internal: *continued in Ps 85* — high confidence on the data (added in v1.1).** `[T]`
The Psalter's only two verses with נָתַן ("give") + יְהוָה + טוֹב ("good") are 84:11 and 85:12, in adjacent psalms; כָּבוֹד ("glory"), הָלַךְ ("walk") and יַעֲקֹב ("Jacob") also pass from Psalm 84 into Psalm 85. *What it adds:* the next Korahite psalm turns Psalm 84's closing statement — *the LORD gives… no good thing does he withhold* — into the people's petition, *the LORD will give what is good*, and moves the glory from the house to the land. Tabulated under the Positional Necessity Check.

**Opening material checked.** Psalms 1–2 were held open. Psalm 84's three אַשְׁרֵי are the Psalter's gateway word — Ps 1:1 *אַשְׁרֵי הָאִישׁ* and Ps 2:12 *אַשְׁרֵי כָּל־חוֹסֵי בוֹ* ("blessed are all who take **refuge** in him") `[T]`. Psalm 84's third and final beatitude is *אַשְׁרֵי אָדָם בֹּטֵחַ בָּךְ* — "blessed is the man who **trusts** in you" — which is Psalm 2:12's shape with a different verb (בָּטַח for חָסָה). The gateway's two beatitudes are Torah and refuge-in-the-King; Psalm 84 ends on the second of them. *Moderate confidence: the אַשְׁרֵי frame is a hard fact, the verbs differ, and אַשְׁרֵי is common enough in the Psalter that this is a resonance rather than a citation.*

#### 11e. New Testament use — an explicit nil, with the control stated

**Psalm 84 is not quoted in the New Testament.** The check: the psalm's distinctive LXX vocabulary was run against the SBLGNT — σκήνωμα (3 verses: Acts 7:46; 2 Pet 1:13, 14), ἐπιποθέω (9 verses, all Pauline or catholic and none citing a psalm), αὐλή (12 verses, all of courtyards in the passion narratives or John 10), στρουθίον (4 verses: Matt 10:29, 31; Luke 12:6, 7). No quotation formula, no catena, no sustained verbal run. `[T]`

**The one contact worth naming, and it is not this psalm's.** Acts 7:46 has Stephen say David asked "to find a **σκήνωμα** for the **God of Jacob**" — both terms stand in LXX Ps 83 (83:2, 11 and 83:9) — but the quotation is of **LXX Ps 131:5 (MT 132:5)**, which has the phrase as a unit. Psalm 84 shares the vocabulary because it shares the tradition (see 11c), not because Acts is citing it. *Reported so that nobody builds a link on the overlap.*

**And the one to resist.** στρουθίον in Matt 10:29 / Luke 12:6 is not an allusion to Ps 84:3. Jesus' sparrows are about providence and price; the psalm's bird is about access to an altar. See Preaching Pitfalls.

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew poetry** — the Psalter's genre, and where the Genre tool stops for a psalm. Within the book, Psalm 84 belongs to the **Korahite block that closes Book III** (84, 85, 87, 88) — a canonical sub-collection, not a form-critical class. `[T]`

Per the standing discipline this report assigns no Gunkel–Mowinckel *Gattung*. Psalm 84 is conventionally labelled a Zion song or a pilgrimage song, and the pilgrimage label in particular is doing interpretive work it has not earned: it is a reconstruction of a cultic use (`[S]`), and once accepted it turns v. 6's valley into a stage on a known road and v. 7's "appearing" into arrival at a festival. **What the text supports is weaker and more interesting**: people are travelling, they have roads in their hearts, and someone appears before God in Zion. The reading below keeps to that.

**Reading rules in force.** Parallelism is operative (Tool 5). The imagery is figurative — the sun and shield of v. 11 are metaphors, and so, probably, is the valley. The three אַשְׁרֵי are pronouncements of felicity, not conferrals of blessing, and not promises.

### 13. Copycat

**N/A for character-imitation** — there are no narrative characters. But the tool has one live question here, because the psalm contains a **stated choice**: *בָּחַרְתִּי*, "I have chosen" (v. 10 [MT 11]).

Is that prescriptive? It is not a command, and the psalm issues none to any human being. It is a **declared preference offered for agreement** — which is what the three beatitudes are too: *this is what blessedness looks like; see whether you agree.* The right category is **normative pattern by commendation**, not command. `[I]` The distinction matters for application: the psalm invites the hearer to want what the psalmist wants, and it does not tell him to do anything.

**The copycat error this psalm actually invites** is not about a character but about a *circumstance* — reading the psalmist's exclusion from the sanctuary as a template for a feeling ("do you long for church like this?") rather than noticing that his longing is caused by his being *unable to get there*. See Preaching Pitfalls.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** A functioning sanctuary with courts, altars and a house; an anointed king in being; a family of Levitical gatekeepers with a four-hundred-year-old disgrace in its name. On the Psalter's own arc: Book III, between the burning of the dwelling place (74:7) and the renouncing of the covenant (89:39). `[T]`

**Canonical position:** Ketuvim, as at Phase 0.55. The canonical reader of the Writings arrives **after** Malachi — knowing the exile, knowing that the second temple was built without the glory, knowing that no Davidic king reigns. For that reader, "look on the face of your anointed" is already a prayer with no present referent, and "blessed is the man who trusts in you" is the only one of the three beatitudes still available. `[I]` *This is the single most important timeline observation for preaching the psalm.*

**What has happened since, that changes the reading.**

- **The dwelling place became a person.** John 1:14 — ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο καὶ **ἐσκήνωσεν** ἐν ἡμῖν, "the Word became flesh and **tabernacled** among us" — uses the verb cognate to the LXX's σκήνωμα, the noun of Ps 83:2. `[T]` on the lexical relation; the connection is a trajectory, not a citation.
- **The threshold was opened.** Heb 10:19–22 — "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus… let us draw near (προσερχώμεθα)". The psalm's best hope is the doorstep; Hebrews' claim is the inside.
- **The anointed was looked upon.** The petition of v. 9 is answered in a way the psalm does not anticipate — not by the preservation of a Davidic king but by God looking on the face of one who was spurned (Ps 89:38) and raised.
- **The courts were abolished.** Rev 21:22 — "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty (ὁ κύριος ὁ θεὸς ὁ παντοκράτωρ) and the Lamb." **παντοκράτωρ is the Greek tradition's standard rendering of צְבָאוֹת** `[S]`, which makes Revelation's sentence the end of this psalm's own line: the LORD of hosts, whose courts the psalmist wanted one day in, *is* the temple. *Moderate-high confidence on the lexical point; the Swete Psalms renders צְבָאοτ as τῶν δυνάμεων rather than παντοκράτωρ, so the equivalence is a fact about the Greek tradition broadly rather than about this psalm's translator. Flagged accordingly.*

**Reading rule for the preacher.** Do not take the psalm's longing and apply it straight to a church building. The timeline runs: tabernacle → temple → burned → rebuilt without glory → Christ the dwelling → the church indwelt → no temple at all. Psalm 84 sits at the second stage and wants it badly, and every subsequent stage gives it more than it asked for.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Not the bird.** The bird is a foil: it has what the speaker wants. Reading oneself as the sparrow who "finds a home in God" inverts the verse.

**Not, straightforwardly, the resident.** The first beatitude is pronounced on people the psalmist is not currently among. If anything, the modern churchgoer who can walk into a service any Sunday is in the position of v. 4's residents — which should make v. 4 a sentence about *us* that we almost never hear as one. `[I]`

**The pilgrim, and then the truster.** The psalm's second and third beatitudes are pronounced on **אָדָם** — the generic term, and the term Psalm 1 does not use (Ps 1:1 has אִישׁ). `[T]` They are open. Whoever has strength in God and roads in his heart is in v. 5; whoever trusts is in v. 12. Neither requires a postcode.

**The anointed is not me.** Verse 9 asks God to look on the face of his anointed. That is not a prayer about the hearer, and a sermon that turns "your anointed" into "your anointed people" has removed the verse's one piece of royal particularity and with it the link to Psalm 89. `[T]` Christologically the verse is the psalm's richest line (see below) precisely because the anointed is *someone else*, on whose standing the congregation's own depends.

**Where the identification is genuinely available:** with the Korahite singer — not as a devotional posture but as a position. Someone whose family had every reason to be excluded is at the door, singing. `[I]`

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the psalm seeks.** Agreement. The psalm pronounces three blessings and states a preference, and what it wants is for the hearer to find them persuasive — to want God's presence more than anything the alternative offers, and to recognise that the wanting itself is already blessed.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

| Domain | What the psalm presses |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | That nearness to God is the measurable good — "a day… better than a thousand" is an exchange rate, and the psalm expects it to be believed. And that the good is *given*, not achieved: v. 11's three clauses all have God as subject (he is sun and shield, he gives, he does not withhold) |
| **Start** | Wanting. The psalm's imperative-equivalent is its beatitude: it tells you what blessedness looks like and lets the comparison work. Concretely: valuing access to God above comfort, position, and the interior of other people's tents |
| **Stop** | Measuring one's standing with God by proximity to a building or a role — which the psalm's own movement from *resident* to *truster* dismantles |
| **Motive** | Not duty. The psalm never commands. Its motive is *fitness and worth* — the thing is better, and the one who gives it withholds no good thing |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me.** The psalm's hardest question is not "do you long for God?" but "what do you prefer?" — because בָּחַרְתִּי is a choice, and it is made against a specific alternative that has a roof on it.
- **For a Christian friend.** For anyone cut off from the gathered church — ill, housebound, moved, exiled, ashamed — the psalm's second beatitude is pronounced on the person *on the road*, not the person who has arrived. That is a word the first beatitude cannot give them and the third confirms.
- **For the church.** The congregation is v. 4's residents and generally does not know it. And the psalm's three-step movement is a corrective to any ecclesiology that makes the building or the office the locus of blessing — written, remarkably, by the men who held the office.
- **For an unbeliever.** The last line is the widest in the psalm and names no qualification but one: *אַשְׁרֵי אָדָם בֹּטֵחַ בָּךְ*. Not the man who belongs, or serves, or lives nearby.

**Prayer in response.** That the longing would be real; that the preference would be made and not merely admired; that God would look on the face of his Anointed on our behalf.

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**Key terms** — the table at Tool 7 carries the lexical work; three grammatical and sound-level observations remain.

**Grammatical features**

- **v. 3 [MT 4] — the syntax of אֶת־מִזְבְּחוֹתֶיךָ.** The accusative particle with "your altars" has no governing verb. Options: (a) the altars are a loose accusative of place — the bird nests *at* them; (b) the phrase is an exclamation breaking the sentence — "*your altars*, O LORD of hosts!"; (c) it is secondary. **BHS 4ᵇ⁻ᵇ notes *frt add*** — "perhaps an addition". `[T]` for the syntactic difficulty. The NASB95's "even Your altars" and the ESV's "at your altars" each pick a different option without flagging it. *Moderate confidence that (a) is right; the difficulty is real and the versions hide it.*
- **v. 7 [MT 8] — יֵרָאֶה is third masculine singular with no expressed subject.** Both English versions supply one ("every one of them", "each one"). The Hebrew says "he will appear / he will be seen before God in Zion." `[T]` Who? Grammatically it can be the pilgrim of v. 6, or God himself (see Textual Variants). This is the psalm's sharpest grammatical crux and no English version lets the reader see it.
- **v. 10 [MT 11] — the comparative מִן three times in one verse.** מֵאָלֶף ("than a thousand"), מִדּוּר ("than dwelling"), and the implied comparison in בָּחַרְתִּי. `[T]` The verse is built as a scale.

**Wordplay and sound**

- **v. 8 [MT 8] — מֵחַיִל אֶל־חָיִל**, *mē-ḥayil ʾel-ḥāyil*, "from strength to strength". The one place in the psalm where the sound carries the sense: the same word twice with only the preposition changing, so the line *is* the repetition it describes. `[T]` A transliteration earns its place here and nowhere else in the psalm.
- **The hapax cluster.** הִסְתּוֹפֵף is a hithpoel — a stem that in a denominative formation typically means *to conduct oneself as, to take the position of*. "To station oneself at the threshold" is the sense, and the reflexive stem makes it a chosen posture rather than an assigned job. `[I]` *High confidence on the stem; moderate-high on the nuance — worth a lexicon line.*

**Recommended verification**

- הִסְתּוֹפֵף and its derivation from סַף — HALOT or BDB. The report's Headline 2 rests on it and it is a denominative claim.
- בָּכָא (v. 6 [MT 7]) — HALOT; and the relation, if any, to the בְּכָאִים of 2 Sam 5:23–24.
- מוֹרֶה (v. 6 [MT 7]) — "early rain" or "teacher"; the LXX took the second.

### Textual Variants

The MT of Psalm 84 is not quiet. BHS carries notes at eight of the thirteen verses, and one of them says the text is corrupt.

**(1) v. 6 [MT 7] — the BHS apparatus marks part of the verse `crrp`.** `[T]` Cited from BHS (Logos export). The verse reads *עֹבְרֵי בְּעֵמֶק הַבָּכָא מַעְיָן יְשִׁיתוּהוּ גַּם־בְּרָכוֹת יַעְטֶה מוֹרֶה*. BHS's notes on it run to eight sigla and include: a few manuscripts reading הבכה for הבכא; the LXX's εἰς τόπον for מַעְיָן; several manuscripts and the LXX reading a different form of יְשִׁיתוּהוּ; **`crrp`** on one clause; the proposal **בְּרֵכוֹת ("pools") for בְּרָכוֹת ("blessings")**; two manuscripts reading יַעֲשֶׂה for יַעְטֶה with the LXX's δώσει; and a conjecture **יַרְוֶה יהוה** for מוֹרֶה.

**Triage: mixed.** The בְּרָכוֹת / בְּרֵכוֹת question is **category 2** in form — it is a vocalisation, not a consonantal variant, so strictly it is a question about the pointing tradition rather than about the text. **The LXX supports the MT** (εὐλογίας, "blessings"); the ESV has adopted the emendation; the NASB95 has not. *Position: the MT reading is supported by the oldest version and should not be abandoned for a conjecture that merely makes better irrigation sense. Confidence: moderate* — "pools" is attractive in context and the consonants permit it, which is why the ESV took it.

The מוֹרֶה question is **category 1 shading into 2**: the consonants are agreed, and "early rain" versus "teacher" is a lexical choice the Greek made differently (ὁ νομοθετῶν, "the lawgiver"). *Confidence: moderate that "early rain" is right in a verse about a valley and water; the LXX's reading is not absurd and produces a quite different line.*

**For the preacher: do not build on verse 6.** It is the one verse in the psalm where the text itself is insecure, and it is also the verse most often made into a sermon's central image. Say what it probably means and move the weight elsewhere.

**(2) v. 7 [MT 8] — יֵרָאֶה, and what the pointing is doing.** `[T]` for the data; `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` for the reading-tradition claim.

MT points the verb as a **niphal**: *yērāʾeh*, "he will appear / be seen". **BHS proposes reading יִרְאוּ** (*yirʾû*, qal plural, "they will see"), and adds that perhaps אֵל should be read for אֱלֹהִים with the LXX, Aquila and the Syriac. **The LXX has ὀφθήσεται ὁ θεὸς τῶν θεῶν ἐν Σιών — "the God of gods will be seen in Zion"** — in which *God* is the one who appears, not the pilgrim. Swete and Rahlfs agree.

**Triage: category 2 — a substantive divergence, and a theologically weighted one.** The Hebrew pointing makes the pilgrim the subject of a passive-reflexive verb ("he is seen before God"); the Greek makes God the subject. This is the well-known alternation around the pilgrimage formula (cf. Exod 23:17; 34:23; Deut 16:16, where the same niphal is standardly rendered "shall appear before" rather than "shall see"). *Position:* the MT is the harder reading and I take it, but the divergence should not be flattened. *Confidence: moderate.*

**The spread question, stated honestly.** The claim one wants to make here — that the Masoretic pointing is a deliberate reverential adjustment away from "seeing God" — is a claim about a **reading tradition**, not about wording, and the corpus holds **one** manuscript tradition and prints the pointing without variants. From inside it a verified pointing and an unrepresentative witness look identical. **This finding is therefore tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`, capped at moderate confidence, and may not enter the book overview** until a library pass on the manuscript spread has been run. What *can* be said without that pass: BHS prints the niphal, BHS proposes the qal plural, and the Greek has God as subject. Those three are reportable as they stand, each with its witness named.

**(3) v. 11 [MT 12] — the LXX has no sun and no shield.** Treated in full at Tool 8 as a `**Translation-tradition split**` (category 2). *Position: the MT. Confidence: moderate-high.*

**(4) Swete against Rahlfs — two divergences, neither load-bearing.** `[T]`
- **LXX 83:5** — Swete μακάριοι **πάντες** οἱ κατοικοῦντες; Rahlfs μακάριοι οἱ κατοικοῦντες. The MT has no "all". Swete's Vaticanus supplies it.
- **LXX 83:12** — Swete οὐχ **ὑστερήσει**; Rahlfs οὐ **στερήσει**. Both render "will not withhold / will not deprive of".
Recorded because the standing rule is to name the edition: neither affects a finding, and everywhere else the two editions agree.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** A functioning sanctuary in Jerusalem with courts, a house, and altars in the plural; an anointed king; an organised Levitical establishment including gatekeepers. `[T]` for the furniture named in the psalm; `[S]` for any dating.

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

1. **Who the Korahites were, and what their name carried.** Not a neutral patronymic. Numbers 16 is one of the Torah's most memorable judgment scenes, and Num 26:11's "but the sons of Korah did not die" is the sort of note a reader keeps. An Israelite reading *לִבְנֵי־קֹרַח* above a psalm about standing at the door would hear the whole of it. `[I]` *Moderate-high confidence — the texts are explicit and the Psalter uses the ascription eleven times.*

2. **What a gatekeeper's day looked like.** 1 Chr 9:17–27 describes the office: lodging around the house of God, opening it every morning, responsible for the chambers and the treasuries. A "threshold-keeper" was not a nobody; he was a Levite with a stated post at the boundary. The hapax at v. 10 describes a real job. `[T]` on the Chronicles description.

3. **What "appearing before the LORD" meant.** The three pilgrim festivals required that every male "appear before the LORD" (Exod 23:17; 34:23; Deut 16:16), using the same niphal of ראה. `[T]` A reader meeting יֵרָאֶה in v. 7 hears a legal obligation being described as a blessing.

4. **That the sanctuary had been attacked.** Whatever the psalm's own date, the canonical reader meets it eleven psalms after Asaph's "they set your **sanctuary** on fire; they profaned the **dwelling place** of your name" (74:7) — the same noun, מִשְׁכָּן. `[T]`

5. **What "LORD of hosts" invoked.** The title is tied to the ark and to Israel's wars (1 Sam 1:3; 4:4; 17:45). It is not a gentle title, and it falls four times in this psalm. `[S]` on the ark-and-war association; `[T]` on the frequency here.

**What this changes about how we read.** Two things. The psalm is not a reverie about a pretty building: it is addressed four times to the God of armies and contains a wartime petition for the king. And the speaker's "threshold" is not false modesty — it is a real post, held by his family, that his ancestor's generation had thought beneath them.

**Uncertain — verify.** The meaning of *Gittith*; the identity of *Baca*; the precise duties of the second-temple threshold-keepers as against the monarchic ones (1 Chr 9 is a post-exilic list describing an earlier institution).

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Writings — so the reader arrives **after Malachi**, knowing the exile, the second temple, and the absence of a reigning Davidic king. `[I]` Canonical register: Job, Proverbs, Chronicles, Lamentations are in the same neighbourhood, and **Chronicles is where this reader learns who the Korahites were** (1 Chr 9:19; 26:1).

**The first hearers.** Israelites for whom the sanctuary was a real destination with a real distance to it, and for whom the Korah name was live.

**Where the passage fits their world.**

- The *a fortiori* of the bird lands harder in a world where getting to the sanctuary was a journey with a cost. The bird's advantage is not sentimental; it is logistical.
- "A day in your courts is better than a thousand" is an exchange rate quoted by someone who has had to count days.
- "Look on the face of your anointed" is a petition about national survival, not about the king's personal piety.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** the superscription. A psalm about proper nearness to God, ascribed to the family of the man the earth swallowed for improper nearness. `[I]`
- **Surprising:** the movement of the three beatitudes — that a psalm sung by the sanctuary's own staff should end by pronouncing blessing on *anyone who trusts*, with the house unmentioned.
- **Comforting:** v. 5's beatitude on the man still travelling. The psalm blesses the journey, not only the arrival.
- **Disturbing:** v. 10's alternative is not "the world" in the abstract but *tents* — other people's households, with the warmth that implies. The comparison costs something.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A church we can enter at will, which makes the psalm's longing hard to feel and v. 4's beatitude impossible to hear as about us. A tourist's relation to religious buildings. And an individualism that reads "my soul longs" as a private emotional state rather than as the complaint of a man separated from a people and a place.

**Implication for the sermon.** Recovering the Korah name turns the psalm from an exhortation to love church into a confession about who gets to come near — which is a gospel question, and the one the psalm's own ending answers.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge.

**(1) Temple / God's presence**

- **Where the passage sits:** at the settled-dwelling stage, and already under threat — the psalm's noun for the dwelling (מִשְׁכָּן) is the noun Asaph uses for what was burned (74:7).
- **Before:** Eden as proto-sanctuary; the tabernacle among the tribes; Solomon's temple and its dedication prayer, whose closing line Psalm 84:9 shares (2 Chr 6:42).
- **After:** the glory departs (Ezek 10); the second temple is built without it; **the Word tabernacles among us** (John 1:14, ἐσκήνωσεν, cognate to the LXX's σκήνωμα here); the church is the temple (1 Cor 3:16); and finally **no temple at all** (Rev 21:22), because the LORD God Almighty and the Lamb are it.
- **What it contributes:** the psalm's longing is not satisfied by a better building. Its trajectory runs to the abolition of the category, and the thing it actually wanted — nearness — is what each later stage gives more of.
- *Confidence: high.*

**(2) Access — who may draw near**

- **Where the passage sits:** on the far side of Numbers 16, in the mouths of the survivors.
- **Before:** Eden's expulsion and the cherubim at the gate (Gen 3:24); Sinai's boundaries (Exod 19:12); Nadab and Abihu (Lev 10); **Korah (Num 16)**; the veil.
- **After:** "we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus… **let us draw near**" (Heb 10:19–22); the curtain torn (Mark 15:38); "through him we both have **access** (προσαγωγή) in one Spirit to the Father" (Eph 2:18).
- **What it contributes:** the psalm's governing question is not *how much do I love church* but *may I come in* — and that is the question the New Testament answers in so many words.
- *Confidence: high.*

**Confluence — climactic convergence (c).** The two themes are not parallel here; they meet. The temple theme asks *where is God's dwelling* and the access theme asks *who may approach it*, and Psalm 84 is the place where a man who is doubtful of the second stands outside the first and says the doorstep will do. Both questions are answered in the same event: the one who is the dwelling is the one who opens the access. `[I]`

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** trajectory, with one typological strand and one contrast.

**Trajectory — the dwelling.** What the psalm wants is to be where God dwells. The canon's answer is not a better building but a person: John 1:14's ἐσκήνωσεν is cognate with the LXX's σκηνώματα here, and Rev 21:22 ends the line by removing the temple. **Tests:** theological category ✓ (temple); NT precedent ✓ (John 2:19–21; Rev 21:22); escalation ✓ (presence without a building); authorial pattern — the psalm raises an expectation it cannot satisfy, which is the weaker form of the test. *Three of four — high-moderate confidence; categorised as trajectory, not typology.*

**Typology — the anointed.** Verse 9 asks God to *look on the face of your anointed*, in a formula shared with Solomon's dedication prayer, in a Book that will report the petition refused (Ps 89:38). **Tests:** theological category ✓ (kingship); NT precedent ✓ (the Psalter's anointed read of Christ throughout — Acts 4:25–26 on Ps 2); escalation ✓ (the Anointed whose face God turns away at the cross and raises); authorial pattern ✓ (the psalm names the figure, the Book tracks him, and the thread is unresolved at 89). *Four of four — high confidence.* **This is the psalm's strongest Christological line, and it is the one most often skipped**, because v. 9 reads as a parenthesis.

**Contrast — the threshold.** The psalm's best hope is the doorstep, preferred to the inside of anywhere else. Hebrews 10:19–22 answers with the inside: *confidence to enter*, by blood, with the invitation to **draw near** — the Greek προσέρχομαι standing where Numbers 16's קָרַב hiphil stood. `[I]` The contrast is the sharper for the Korah reading: the family that lost its father for grasping at the inside is given, in Christ, exactly what he grasped at — and given it.

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** *long for God's house like the psalmist did.* The psalm's grammar will not support it — the psalm commands nobody, and turning its beatitudes into an exhortation converts a pronouncement of blessing into a test of devotional temperature.
- **The gospel grounding:** v. 11. Every clause has God as subject — he *is* sun and shield, he *gives* grace and glory, he *withholds* no good thing. The psalm grounds its own comparison in what God does, not in what the worshipper feels.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** the psalmist is not the hero of this psalm. The hero is the LORD of hosts whose house it is — and, in the trajectory, the Anointed whose face God was asked to look upon, who was spurned, and in whom the threshold became a door.

*Confidence:* **high** on the anointed strand; **high-moderate** on the dwelling trajectory; **moderate-high** on the access contrast, which depends on the Korah reading at Tool 11b.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**v. 6 [MT 7] — the valley of Baca.** The only genuinely obscure verse. A hapax place-or-thing, a clause BHS calls corrupt, a vocalisation the ESV and NASB95 split on, and a word (מוֹרֶה) the LXX read as "lawgiver". *Pastoral note: it is also the verse most likely to be made a sermon's governing image ("the valley of weeping becomes a place of springs"). That reading depends on a derivation the consonants do not establish and a translation the Hebrew does not require.* Preach it with the weight the text can bear.

**v. 9 [MT 10] — "look on the face of your anointed."** Contested in application rather than in text. Three readings circulate: the reigning king; the people as a corporate anointed; Christ. The first is what the psalm says, the second has nothing in the verse to support it, and the third is the canonical trajectory and should be reached *through* the first. See Preaching Pitfalls.

**v. 11 [MT 12] — "no good thing does he withhold from those who walk uprightly."** The pastoral landmine. Read as a promise it is falsifiable by any Tuesday, and read as a condition it makes the withholding the hearer's fault. It is neither: it is a statement about God's character, in a psalm whose speaker cannot get to the place he most wants to be, in a Book that is about to say God has renounced his covenant. **The verse is spoken by someone not currently getting what he wants.** That context is the whole pastoral key and it is text-observable. `[T]`

**No other difficulties.** No ethical problem, no imprecation, no apologetic flashpoint.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: "Even the sparrow finds a home" as a sermon about God's care for small things

- **What it looks like:** the bird of v. 3 joined to Matt 10:29 ("two sparrows for a penny… you are of more value"), and the psalm preached as reassurance that God notices you.
- **Why it's wrong:** two errors in one move. The Hebrew is צִפּוֹר, the generic small bird, not specifically a sparrow — "sparrow" is the English versions' specification. And the verse's logic is an *a fortiori about access*, not about providence: the bird's advantage is that she has nested **at your altars** while the psalmist is outside. Matthew's sparrows are about value and price and are not citing this psalm (the LXX/NT vocabulary check at Tool 11e found no quotation).
- **The corrective:** preach the גַּם ("even"). A creature with no covenant and no priesthood has what the psalmist wants, and that is the complaint, not the comfort.

### Pitfall: "Better one day in your courts" as a comparison between church and the world

- **What it looks like:** an exhortation to prefer Sunday to the alternatives; a sermon about commitment and attendance.
- **Why it's wrong:** it is not false, but it is a fraction of the verse. The line contains two of the Hebrew Bible's hapax legomena, prefers the *doorstep* to the interior of anywhere else, and is spoken under a superscription naming the family whose father died for wanting past that doorstep. And it is grounded by a כִּי in the *next* verse — "for the LORD God is a sun and shield" — which a sermon that stops at v. 10 never reaches.
- **The corrective:** preach vv. 10–11 as one unit, and let the superscription do its work. The verse is not an exhortation to attend; it is a confession about who gets to come near.

### Pitfall: making the valley of Baca the sermon's governing image

- **What it looks like:** "the valley of weeping becomes a place of springs" — the psalm as a template for turning suffering into blessing.
- **Why it's wrong:** every element of that reading is insecure. בָּכָא is a hapax and may be a place name; the "weeping" sense is the LXX's derivation, not a datum of the Hebrew; BHS marks part of the verse **corrupt**; the ESV and NASB95 part company over whether the rain brings "pools" or "blessings"; and the LXX read מוֹרֶה as "the lawgiver", not "the early rain". This is the one verse in the psalm whose text is in question.
- **The corrective:** say what it probably means — people on a hard road find water on it — and put the sermon's weight on verses that will carry it. There are eleven of them.

### Pitfall: turning "your anointed" (v. 9) into the congregation

- **What it looks like:** "we are God's anointed people — ask him to look on us."
- **Why it's wrong:** מָשִׁיחַ in the Psalter is a royal singular, and in Book III it stands at 84:9 and then only at 89:38 and 89:51, where it is unambiguously the Davidic king whom God has spurned. Corporatising the term severs the psalm's strongest link to Psalm 89 and deletes its strongest Christological line.
- **The corrective:** let the anointed be someone else. The congregation's standing in this verse is that it prays for him — which is exactly the posture the New Testament preserves, with the petition answered in a way the psalm could not have predicted.

### Pitfall: "No good thing does he withhold" preached as a promise

- **What it looks like:** v. 11b as an assurance that the upright will not lack — with the quiet corollary that lack means you were not upright.
- **Why it's wrong:** it is spoken by a man who is not getting the one thing he most wants, in the Book that ends by telling God he has renounced his covenant. Read as a transactional promise it is cruel and falsifiable; read as a statement about God's character held *in* deprivation it is the psalm's actual claim.
- **The corrective:** preach the speaker's position along with the sentence. The verse's force comes from who is saying it and where he is standing.

### Pitfall: preaching the psalm's brightness from the first line

- **What it looks like:** an upbeat sermon on loving God's house, pitched throughout at the energy of v. 10.
- **Why it's wrong:** the psalm opens with two verbs of running out — נִכְסְפָה and כָּלְתָה, "longed" and "fainted" — and it earns its settledness over twelve verses. It also sits in a Book that moves from a burned sanctuary to a renounced covenant, and the Korahite block it opens ends at Psalm 88, in the dark.
- **The corrective:** honour the psalm's own emotional architecture. Start where it starts.
