## The passage — NASB95 (English numbering)

> **A Contrite Sinner's Prayer for Pardon.**
> *For the choir director. A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba.*
>
> **1** Be gracious to me, O God, according to Your lovingkindness; / According to the greatness of Your compassion blot out my transgressions. **2** Wash me thoroughly from my iniquity / And cleanse me from my sin. **3** For I know my transgressions, / And my sin is ever before me. **4** Against You, You only, I have sinned / And done what is evil in Your sight, / So that You are justified when You speak / And blameless when You judge.
>
> **5** Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, / And in sin my mother conceived me. **6** Behold, You desire truth in the innermost being, / And in the hidden part You will make me know wisdom. **7** Purify me with hyssop, and I shall be clean; / Wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow. **8** Make me to hear joy and gladness, / Let the bones which You have broken rejoice. **9** Hide Your face from my sins / And blot out all my iniquities.
>
> **10** Create in me a clean heart, O God, / And renew a steadfast spirit within me. **11** Do not cast me away from Your presence / And do not take Your Holy Spirit from me. **12** Restore to me the joy of Your salvation / And sustain me with a willing spirit. **13** Then I will teach transgressors Your ways, / And sinners will be converted to You.
>
> **14** Deliver me from bloodguiltiness, O God, the God of my salvation; / Then my tongue will joyfully sing of Your righteousness. **15** O Lord, open my lips, / That my mouth may declare Your praise. **16** For You do not delight in sacrifice, otherwise I would give it; / You are not pleased with burnt offering. **17** The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; / A broken and a contrite heart, O God, You will not despise.
>
> **18** By Your favor do good to Zion; / Build the walls of Jerusalem. **19** Then You will delight in righteous sacrifices, / In burnt offering and whole burnt offering; / Then young bulls will be offered on Your altar.

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools. Each is corpus-verified; the verification commands are recorded in the Colophon.

1. **The psalm's central problem is that there is no sacrifice for what David did — and the psalm says so in the Torah's own vocabulary.** Numbers 15:31 sentences the one who "**despised** (*bāzâ*) the **word of YHWH**" (*dəḇar YHWH*) to be cut off, *with no offering provided*. Nathan's indictment at 2 Sam 12:9 uses **those two words in that order** — "why have you **despised** the **word of YHWH**?" And the psalm's last word on sacrifice is that the one thing God "will **not despise** (*lōʾ ṯiḇzeh*)" is a broken heart (v. 17 [MT 19]) — *the same verb*. `[T]` Surfaced by: Context, Repetition, Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion, Bible Timeline. This is why vv. 16–17 [MT 18–19] deny that God wants sacrifice: not because sacrifice is bad, but because for this sin the system has nothing to offer. Corpus: *bāzâ* (H959) verified present at Num 15:31, 2 Sam 12:9 and Ps 51:19 (WLC); *bāzâ* + *dāḇār* co-occur in eight OT verses, of which these are two.

2. **Psalm 51 is the answer the preceding psalm demands.** Psalm 50 is the sole Asaph psalm in Book II, standing immediately before the second Davidic collection opens at Psalm 51 `[T]`. Seven of its words are picked up in Psalm 51, several of them reversed: God will not take a **bull** from your house (50:9) → **bulls** on your altar (51:21); I do not drink the **blood** of goats (50:13) → deliver me from **bloods** (51:16); you cast (*šālaḵ*) my **words** behind you (50:17) → do not cast (*šālaḵ*) **me** away (51:13); what right have you to take my covenant on your **mouth** (*peh*, 50:16) → Lord, open my lips, and my **mouth** (*peh*) will declare your praise (51:17); to him who orders his **way** (*dereḵ*) I will show the **salvation** (*yēšaʿ*) of God (50:23) → I will teach transgressors your **ways** (*dereḵ*, 51:15), restore the joy of your **salvation** (*yēšaʿ*, 51:14). `[T]` Surfaced by: Positional Necessity Check, Context, Structure, Linking Words, Repetition. Psalm 50 shuts the wicked man's mouth; Psalm 51 is what it sounds like when God opens it again.

3. **The petition at the psalm's centre asks for an act of creation, and asks for it in the Psalter's creation vocabulary.** *bārāʾ* ("create") occurs six times in the Psalter — Pss 51:12; 89:13, 48; 102:19; 104:30; 148:5. **Five are cosmic; only Ps 51:12 [Eng 10] has a human heart as its object.** `[T]` Further: the triad *bārāʾ* + *ḥādaš* ("renew") + *rûaḥ* occurs in exactly **two verses in the entire Hebrew Bible** — Ps 51:12 and Ps 104:30 — and the two passages run the same sequence in the same order: God **hides his face** (104:29 / 51:11 [MT], both *sāṯar* + *pānîm*), then **sends/renews the spirit and creates** (104:30 / 51:12–13). `[T]` Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Quotation/Allusion (Move 3), Original Languages, Biblical Theology. The psalmist is not asking for improvement. He is asking for Genesis 1 to happen inside him.

4. **The whole psalm turns on one verb doing two jobs: God crushes, and the crushed thing is the offering.** *dāḵâ* occurs twice in Psalm 51 and nowhere else in the psalm's vicinity: "let the bones which **you have crushed** (*dikkîṯā*) rejoice" (v. 8 [MT 10]) and "a broken and **contrite** (*niḏkeh*) heart" (v. 17 [MT 19]). `[T]` Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Tone and Feel, Parallels. The sacrifice God accepts is one God himself has produced — which is also why the psalm can ask for it rather than bring it.

5. **The Deuteronomistic verdict-formula falls on a king exactly once in the Psalter, and he pronounces it on himself.** "Do the **evil** in the **eyes** of YHWH" (*ʿāśâ* + *raʿ* + *ʿayin*) is the formula by which Judges–Kings condemns Israel's kings; it occurs in 70 OT verses. **Psalm 51:6 [Eng 4] is its only occurrence in the Psalter**, and there it is first person with the pronoun changed: "the evil **in your eyes** I have done." 2 Samuel 11:27 and 12:9 are two of the other 69. `[T]` Surfaced by: Context, Quotation/Allusion, Historical Background, Who Am I?. This is the superscription's claim corroborated from inside the poem, on lexical evidence that does not depend on the superscription being true.

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical position

**Section.** Psalms stands at the head of the **Ketuvim** — the third division of the Tanak, and in the BHS order the first of its books (Psalms, Job, Proverbs, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles). `[T]`

**Reading implication.** The Writings are the canon's *response* literature: they are what the covenant people say back, having heard the Torah and the Prophets. A psalm therefore has the Torah's law and the Prophets' indictment already behind it, and may quote either without introduction. Psalm 51 does precisely this — it prays *inside* a legal situation the Torah has already defined (Num 15:30–31) and a prophetic confrontation the Former Prophets have already narrated (2 Sam 12). `[I]`

**Presupposes** — the testable claims, each checked against the corpus:

| Presupposed text | What Psalm 51 assumes | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| **Num 15:30–31** | That a "high-handed" (*bəyāḏ rāmâ*) sin has **no** prescribed offering; the sinner "despised (*bāzâ*) the word of YHWH" and is cut off, "his iniquity (*ʿăwōnâ*) upon him" | *bāzâ* + *dāḇār* both present, WLC |
| **Lev 14:4–7, 49–52; Num 19:6, 18** | That hyssop belongs to the rites for **uncleanness** — the cleansed leper, the leprous house, corpse-defilement — not to the sin-offering | *ʾēzôḇ* (H231) occurs 10× in the OT: Exod 12:22; Lev 14:4, 6, 49, 51, 52; Num 19:6, 18; 1 Kgs 5:13; **Ps 51:9**. Psalm 51 is its only occurrence outside Torah and Kings |
| **2 Sam 11–12** | That the speaker's offence involved another man's wife and that man's blood, and that a prophet named it | *raʿ* + *ʿayin* + *ʿāśâ* at 2 Sam 11:27, 12:9 and Ps 51:6; *bāzâ* + *dəḇar YHWH* at 2 Sam 12:9 and Num 15:31 |
| **Gen 1** (via the Psalter's own use) | That *bārāʾ* is God's word for making what was not there | *bārāʾ* (H1254) 6× in Psalms, 5 of them cosmic |
| **Ps 50** | That God has just denied needing sacrifice, and has just told a covenant-breaker to stop reciting the covenant | seven-lemma overlap, tabulated at Phase 0.6 |

**Handoff.** Psalm 51 hands forward, within the Psalter, to the second Davidic collection (51–65, 68–70) which it opens, and to Book II's close at Ps 72:20 ("the prayers of David… are ended"). Beyond the Psalter it hands to Ezekiel 36:25–27 — the new heart, the new spirit, the cleansing water — and, in the Greek, to Romans 3:4 and Hebrews 9–10. `[T]` for the Psalter handoff; `[I]` for the direction of the Ezekiel relation, which is left open below.

**Neighbours.** Ps 50 (Asaph; covenant lawsuit about sacrifice) before; Ps 52 (Doeg the Edomite) after. Pss 51 and 52 open their superscriptions with the identical formula *bəḇôʾ* — "when [X] came" — as does Ps 54. `[T]`

**Canonical order vs compositional order.** Psalm 51 has one live instance. The psalm's final petition — "build the **walls** of Jerusalem" (v. 18 [MT 20]) — is routinely read as marking a post-586 hand, which would put the psalm's *composition* later than its Davidic *canonical placement*. Per the Phase 0.55 discipline this report **reports both and resolves neither**. What is on the page is a petition for Zion by a man who has just said God does not want sacrifice; whether it is Davidic, exilic, or Davidic-with-an-exilic-coda is a compositional question, `[S]`, and named as an Open Question below. The reading offered at Tool 3 does not depend on the answer.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**(1) What has the preceding movement predicted, required, or set up that this passage now provides?**

Psalm 50 is a **covenant lawsuit**. God summons heaven and earth (50:1–6), addresses his people (50:7–15), then turns on "the wicked" (50:16–21). Its argument about sacrifice is exactly this: *your burnt offerings are continually before me* (50:8) — and that is the problem, not the solution; *I will not take a bull from your house* (50:9); *do I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?* (50:13); *offer to God thanksgiving* (50:14, 23). It ends by naming what God *will* honour, and by threatening the one who "forgets God" with being torn in pieces "and there is none to deliver" (50:22).

Psalm 50 therefore leaves a hole exactly the shape of Psalm 51. It has told a covenant-breaker that his sacrifices are worthless, that his mouth has no right to the covenant (50:16), and that he is in danger of being torn apart with no deliverer — **and it has not told him what to do about it.** Psalm 51 is the missing reply, and it is written in Psalm 50's vocabulary:

| Psalm 50 (MT = Eng) | Psalm 51 (MT) | Shared lemma | Movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50:8 "not for your **sacrifices**… your **burnt offerings**" | 51:18 "you do not delight in **sacrifice**… **burnt offering**" | *zeḇaḥ* (H2077), *ʿōlâ* (H5930) | Agreement — the psalmist concedes the charge |
| 50:9 "I will not take a **bull** from your house" | 51:21 "then **bulls** will go up on your altar" | *par* (H6499) | Reversal — but only after v. 20 |
| 50:13 "shall I drink the **blood** of goats?" | 51:16 "deliver me from **bloods**" | *dām* (H1818) | Transfer — the blood in question is now the psalmist's guilt, not the altar's |
| 50:16 "what right have you… to take my covenant on your **mouth**?" | 51:17 "open my lips, and my **mouth** will declare your praise" | *peh* (H6310) | Reversal — the closed mouth is opened by God |
| 50:17 "you **cast** my words behind you" | 51:13 "do not **cast** me away from your presence" | *šālaḵ* (H7993) | Reversal — the caster fears being cast |
| 50:23 "to him who orders his **way**… the **salvation** of God" | 51:15 "I will teach transgressors your **ways**"; 51:14 "the joy of your **salvation**" | *dereḵ* (H1870), *yēšaʿ* (H3468) | Fulfilment — the psalm supplies what Ps 50 promised to show |
| 50:7 "**Hear**, O my people" | 51:10 "make me **hear** joy and gladness" | *šāmaʿ* (H8085) | Reversal — the summoned hearer asks to be made to hear |

Every pair is corpus-verified by lemma in the WLC; *šālaḵ*, *dereḵ*, *yēšaʿ* and *šāmaʿ* occur in Psalms 50–51 **only** at the verses tabulated. `[T]` *Confidence: high for the lexical overlap; high-moderate for the claim that the overlap is editorial design rather than shared genre vocabulary — the strongest single indicator is that four of the seven are* reversals *rather than repetitions, which shared vocabulary alone does not produce.*

**(2) Why does this passage exist here, and not elsewhere?**

Three reasons, all text-observable.

**(a) It opens the second Davidic collection.** Book II runs Korah (42–49) → **Asaph (50, alone)** → **David (51–65, 68–70)** → Solomon (72). `[T]` The single Asaph psalm is not stranded: it is the door. Psalm 51 is what walks through it.

**(b) It is the only historical superscription in the Psalter in which David is the wrongdoer.** The Psalter's thirteen historical superscriptions are Pss 3, 7, 18, 34, 51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 59, 60, 63, 142. In twelve, David is pursued, slandered, cornered or delivered — Absalom, Cush, Saul, Abimelech, Doeg, the Ziphites, the Philistines, the cave, the wilderness. **In Psalm 51 alone the occasioning event is David's own sin, and the man who "comes" to him is a prophet rather than an enemy.** `[T]` Pss 51, 52 and 54 all open with *bəḇôʾ*, "when [X] came" — and only in 51 is the coming a mercy.

**(c) It is inside the Elohistic Psalter, and it is fully Elohistic.** In Pss 42–72 *ʾĕlōhîm* outnumbers *YHWH* 166 to 28 `[T]`. **Psalm 51 contains no occurrence of the divine name at all**: *ʾĕlōhîm* four times (MT 3, 12, 16, 19) and *ʾăḏōnāy* once (MT 17). `[T]` This is exegetically live rather than merely statistical, because the one non-*ʾĕlōhîm* address falls at the hinge: *ʾăḏōnāy, open my lips* — the moment the prayer turns from petition to praise. `[T]` for the distribution; `[I]` for the significance of its placement.

**What would be lost by moving it.** Read Psalm 51 on its own and it is a great prayer of penitence. Read it after Psalm 50 and it is something harder and better: the answer to a lawsuit the reader has just watched God win. Its most quoted lines — vv. 16–17 [MT 18–19] — are not a general truth about liturgy but a defendant conceding the prosecution's case.

## The sixteen tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**What the psalm is doing.** It is a petition, sustained for eleven verses, by a speaker who never once argues that he has been wronged. `[T]` This is worth stating plainly because it is nearly unique in the Psalter: the standard lament has an enemy. Psalm 51 has none. The nearest thing to an adversary is the speaker.

The purpose is visible in the verbs. Verses 1–12 [MT 3–14] are almost entirely **imperatives and jussives addressed to God** — *be gracious, blot out, wash, cleanse, purify, make me hear, hide, blot out, create, renew, do not cast, do not take, restore, sustain* — sixteen requests, and not one of them is anything the speaker can do for himself. `[T]` Then at v. 13 [MT 15] the grammar turns: the speaker takes the verbs — *I will teach* — but only as consequence (*then*). The last movement (vv. 14–19 [MT 16–21]) mixes the two: *deliver me… open my lips… do good to Zion*, with the speaker's *my tongue will sing, my mouth will declare* strung between them.

**The purpose, stated:** to obtain from God a cleansing and a re-creation that the speaker cannot obtain by the means the covenant provides, and — this is the part usually missed — **to get his mouth back**, so that the praise God said he had no right to utter (Ps 50:16) can be uttered. `[I]` *Confidence: high. The mouth/lips/tongue cluster is text-observable (vv. 13, 14, 15 [MT 15, 16, 17]) and the Ps 50:16 link is lemma-verified.*

**Not the purpose:** to model a technique of confession, or to reform Israel's worship. The psalm's one general statement about sacrifice (v. 16 [MT 18]) is immediately qualified by v. 19 [MT 21], which expects sacrifice to resume. See Tool 4 and Preaching Pitfalls.

### 2. Context

**Immediate literary context** — handled at Phase 0.6. Psalm 50 before, Psalm 52 after.

**The narrative context the superscription names.** Two distinct claims must be tagged apart, and the difference matters more here than in most psalms.

- That the superscription *reads* "For the choir director. A Psalm of David, when Nathan the prophet came to him, after he had gone in to Bathsheba" (MT 51:1–2) is **`[T]`** — it is on the page, and in the MT it is counted as two verses of the poem.
- That the psalm was *composed by David on that occasion* is **`[S]`** — a claim about authorship and occasion, which is exactly the category the `[T]` boundary excludes. It is a claim the text makes, but the text making a claim and the claim being demonstrable from the text are different things.

The reason to be careful here is that the psalm supplies **independent internal corroboration**, and that corroboration is worth far more than an assertion:

| 2 Samuel | Psalm 51 | Shared |
|---|---|---|
| 11:27 "the **thing** David had **done** was **evil** in the **eyes** of YHWH" | 51:6 [Eng 4] "and the **evil** in **your eyes** I have **done**" | *raʿ/rāʿaʿ* + *ʿayin* + *ʿāśâ* |
| 12:9 "why have you **despised** the **word** of YHWH, to **do the evil** in his eyes?" | 51:6; 51:19 [Eng 4; 17] | *raʿ* + *ʿayin* + *ʿāśâ*; *bāzâ* |
| 12:13 "I have **sinned** against YHWH" | 51:6 [Eng 4] "against you, you only, **I have sinned**" | *ḥāṭāʾ* (H2398) |
| 12:9 "Uriah… you struck with the **sword**… him you killed" | 51:16 [Eng 14] "deliver me from **bloods**" | *dām*, plural — the idiom for bloodguilt (cf. 2 Sam 16:7–8, David called *ʾîš haddāmîm*, "man of bloods") |

All four verified by lemma in the WLC. `[T]` **This is the strongest form the case can take**: not "the heading says so", but "the poem uses the narrative's own words, including a verdict-formula that occurs nowhere else in the Psalter." *Confidence: high for the verbal overlap; moderate-to-high for the inference that the psalm is deliberately pointing at 2 Sam 11–12 — the "evil in the eyes of YHWH" formula is common (70 verses), but its complete absence from the Psalter apart from this one first-person instance is not what coincidence looks like.*

**One tension the superscription creates, which the psalm does not resolve.** In 2 Sam 12:13 Nathan says God "has caused your sin to pass over (*heʿĕḇîr*); you shall not die" — *before* any prayer. Psalm 51 pleads for what Nathan has already announced. Two readings are available and the text does not choose: the psalm is the *response* to absolution (praying into a pardon already given), or the psalm is placed at a point in the narrative before Nathan's word lands. `[I]` Recorded as an Open Question. Homiletically the first reading is the richer and, given 51:13–15's forward-looking vows, the more natural.

**Historical context of the collection.** Book II's Elohistic character and the Ps 42–72 shape are given at Phase 0.6. Nothing further is needed here.

### 3. Structure

The psalm falls into four movements, and the seams are marked by the text rather than imposed.

**I. vv. 1–4 [MT 3–6] — the plea and the confession.**
Three imperatives (*be gracious, wash, cleanse*), three sin-words, three cleansing-words — see Tool 10. Then *kî* ("for", v. 3 [MT 5]) grounds the plea in the speaker's knowledge of his own sin, and *ləmaʿan* ("so that", v. 4 [MT 6]) turns the confession outward to God's vindication. The movement ends with God in the dock and God acquitted.

**II. vv. 5–9 [MT 7–11] — depth and cleansing.**
Two *hēn*'s ("Behold", vv. 5, 6 [MT 7, 8]) open it, and they are a pair: *behold*, the trouble goes as deep as my conception; *behold*, what you want goes as deep as the hidden places. `[T]` The imperatives resume at v. 7 [MT 9] with the ritual vocabulary — hyssop, washing, whiteness. The movement closes with *blot out* (*məḥēh*), which is where movement I began (v. 1 [MT 3]) — an **inclusio spanning vv. 1–9 [MT 3–11]**, verified: *māḥâ* (H4229) occurs in Psalm 51 at MT 3 and MT 11 and nowhere else. `[T]`

**III. vv. 10–13 [MT 12–15] — creation and commission.**
The centre. *bārāʾ* at its head; *rûaḥ* three times in three consecutive verses (MT 12, 13, 14); the movement's own turn at *then I will teach* (v. 13 [MT 15]). This is the only movement in which the speaker acquires a future.

**IV. vv. 14–19 [MT 16–21] — mouth, sacrifice, city.**
*Deliver me* returns to petition, then the mouth opens (v. 15 [MT 17]), then the sacrifice question is answered twice — negatively for the present (v. 16 [MT 18]), positively for a restored Zion (v. 19 [MT 21]) — with the broken heart standing between them as the offering that is available now (v. 17 [MT 19]).

**The shape as a whole.** Movements I–II ask for *removal*; movement III asks for *creation*; movement IV describes what the re-created person *does*. That is not a lament's shape (complaint → petition → vow of praise) but something closer to a re-making: **wash → create → speak → offer.** `[I]` *Confidence: moderate-high. The four movements are text-marked; the "re-making" description is my summary of them.*

**On the Zion ending (vv. 18–19 [MT 20–21]).** The commonest structural claim about this psalm is that these two verses are a later addition contradicting vv. 16–17. Two text-observable facts weigh against treating the join as a contradiction, whatever its compositional history:

1. **The verses share a lemma with what they supposedly contradict.** *ḥāpēṣ* ("delight in") appears at MT 18 (negative) and MT 21 (positive), and *zeḇaḥ* at MT 18, 19 and 21. `[T]` A glossator has either matched the vocabulary exactly, or the verses belong together.
2. **The sequence is conditional, not contradictory.** v. 19 [MT 21] opens with *ʾāz*, "then" — the same *ʾāz*-logic as v. 13 [MT 15] ("**then** I will teach"). Both times the pattern is: *God acts → then the right response becomes possible.* The psalm does not say sacrifice is abolished; it says sacrifice is not currently available as a remedy, and that when God does good to Zion it will be. `[T]`

The compositional question stays open (see Open Questions). The *structural* question does not need it resolved.

### 4. Linking Words

Psalm 51 is unusually explicit for Hebrew poetry — six load-bearing connectives, and the argument is carried by them.

| Verse (Eng [MT]) | Hebrew | Force | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 [3] | *kə-… kə-* | "according to… according to" | The **ground of the appeal**: not the speaker's case but God's *ḥeseḏ* and *raḥămîm*. The psalm's first move is to name what it is standing on `[T]` |
| 3 [5] | *kî* | "for" | Grounds the plea — and note **what** it grounds it in: not repentance but **knowledge**. "For I know my transgressions" `[T]` |
| 4 [6] | *ləmaʿan* | "so that / with the result that" | The hinge of the whole confession, and the verse Paul quotes. See Tool 11 |
| 5, 6 [7, 8] | *hēn… hēn* | "behold… behold" | Paired disclosure — the depth of the problem, the depth of the demand `[T]` |
| 13 [15] | *ʾălammədâ* (cohortative, no conjunction) | "let me teach" | Consequence carried by mood, not by a particle — the vow follows the petition `[T]` |
| 16 [18] | *kî* | "for" | Grounds vv. 14–15's petition: *why* the psalmist asks for lips rather than bringing an offering. **This is the most under-read word in the psalm** `[T]` |
| 19 [21] | *ʾāz* | "then" | Conditional restoration — see Tool 3 |

**The *kî* at v. 16 [MT 18] deserves its own note.** English translations render it "For" (NASB95, ESV) and readers hear a general theological aphorism beginning. It is not that. It is the *reason for the immediately preceding petition*: **open my lips** — *because* you do not delight in sacrifice. Praise is what he offers because it is what is left. `[T]` Surfaced independently by Structure and Author's Purpose.

### 5. Parallels

Hebrew poetry throughout. The psalm's parallelism does a particular kind of work here: it **stacks near-synonyms to make a set feel exhaustive**, and then breaks the pattern at the points that matter.

**Stacking (vv. 1–2 [MT 3–4]).** Three sins × three cleansings in two verses, in a strict interlock:

| | Sin term | Cleansing verb |
|---|---|---|
| v. 1 [3] | *pešaʿ* — transgressions | *māḥâ* — blot out |
| v. 2a [4a] | *ʿāwōn* — iniquity | *kābas* — wash (of clothes) |
| v. 2b [4b] | *ḥaṭṭāʾṯ* — sin | *ṭāhēr* — cleanse (cultic) |

The three sin-words are the standard OT triad (cf. Exod 34:7; Lev 16:21), and their pairing with three cleansing-verbs from three different domains — the erased document, the laundered garment, the cleansed worshipper — is the poem's way of saying *every kind of stain, by every kind of removal*. `[T]` *Confidence: high on the terms and the interlock; moderate on the "three domains" reading, which is mine.*

**The break that matters (v. 17 [MT 19]).** "The **sacrifices** of God" (plural, *ziḇḥê*) "are a **broken spirit**" (singular). The number does not agree, and the second line then names *two* things — "a broken and a contrite heart" — for the one thing the first line named. `[T]` The LXX regularises to the singular (θυσία, Swete and Rahlfs alike), which is a fair sign that ancient readers felt the roughness too. The effect in the Hebrew is that the plural of the whole sacrificial system is met by one singular offering.

**Chiastic touch at vv. 8, 17 [MT 10, 19].** *dāḵâ* opens as something God does to bones and closes as something God receives as a heart. The psalm's most important word-pair is not a parallelism inside a verse but across the poem. `[T]`

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**N/A — no narrator.** Psalm 51 is direct address from a first-person speaker to God, unbroken from MT v. 3 to MT v. 21; there is no narratorial voice and no third-party frame. `[T]`

The nearest analogue to a narrator's comment is the **superscription** (MT vv. 1–2), which speaks *about* the speaker in the third person. That is genuinely editorial voice, and it does the one thing a narrator's comment does: it tells you how to read what follows. Treat its *presence* as `[T]` and its *historical claim* as `[S]` — see Tool 2.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Relentless, then quiet.** Sixteen imperatives in eleven verses, most of them piled two or three to a verse, with no room for the speaker to say anything about himself except what is wrong with him. `[T]` There is no self-defence, no comparison with worse men, no appeal to past service — all three of which the Psalter elsewhere permits (Pss 7:3–5; 17:3–5; 26:1–6). Their absence is the tone.

**Where it turns.** The register changes twice. First at v. 8 [MT 10] — *make me hear joy and gladness* — the psalm's only sensory request, and its first mention of anything pleasant; the joy is asked for as something *heard*, from outside, not summoned from within. `[T]` Second at v. 15 [MT 17] — *O Lord, open my lips* — where the address shifts from *ʾĕlōhîm* to *ʾăḏōnāy* and the verbs turn from cleansing to speaking.

**The register at the end is not triumph.** vv. 18–19 [MT 20–21] widen from the speaker to the city, and the last image is bulls on an altar — the very thing v. 16 said God does not want, now restored. That is a strange note to end on, and the strangeness is the point: the psalm does not end with the speaker forgiven and content, but with worship working again. `[I]`

**A word on how it *sounds* in Hebrew.** vv. 3–4 [MT 5–6] are heavy with first-person suffixes — *pəšāʿay… ḥaṭṭāʾṯî… ʿāśîṯî* — and vv. 1–2 [MT 3–4] with second-person — *ḥasdeḵā… raḥămeḵā*. The poem's opening moves are literally a stacking of *yours* against *mine*. `[T]`

### 11. Quotation and Allusion

Psalm 51 contains no formal quotation. It contains a very great deal of allusion, and one of its verses is itself quoted in the New Testament. The Triad is run on the significant items.

---

#### 11a. Numbers 15:30–31 — the sin with no sacrifice (high confidence)

**The claim.** Psalm 51's denial that God wants sacrifice (v. 16 [MT 18]) is not a general theology of worship. It is a correct statement of Torah law about the speaker's *specific* offence, reached through a verbal chain that runs Num 15:31 → 2 Sam 12:9 → Ps 51:19.

**Move 1 — source context.** Num 15:22–29 provides offerings for sin committed *bišḡāḡâ*, "unintentionally". Then vv. 30–31: the person who acts *bəyāḏ rāmâ*, "with a high hand", blasphemes YHWH and shall be cut off — "**because he has despised (*bāzâ*) the word of YHWH** (*dəḇar YHWH*) and broken his commandment; that soul shall surely be cut off; **his iniquity (*ʿăwōnâ*) is upon him**." `[T]` **No offering is provided.** That is the structural point of the paragraph: it is the paragraph where the sacrificial system says *not this one*.

**Move 2 — how the source book uses it.** Numbers immediately illustrates the ruling with the Sabbath-wood case (15:32–36), a man executed with no offering offered. The Torah's own next move is to show the law working. `[T]`

**Move 3 — OT to OT.** 2 Sam 12:9: *maddûaʿ **bāzîṯā** ʾeṯ-**dəḇar YHWH***. Nathan indicts David in the exact two-word collocation of Num 15:31. `[T]` The pair *bāzâ* + *dāḇār* occurs in eight OT verses (2 Sam 12:9; Num 15:31; 2 Kgs 19:21; Isa 37:22; Neh 2:19; Esth 1:17; Eccl 9:16; 2 Chr 36:16); only Num 15:31, 2 Sam 12:9 and 2 Chr 36:16 have *the word of YHWH* as the despised object. `[T]` Nathan is not being generally severe. He is reading David a statute — the one that ends *there is no offering for this*.

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Ps 51:19 [Eng 17]: *lēḇ-nišbār wəniḏkeh ʾĕlōhîm **lōʾ ṯiḇzeh*** — "a broken and contrite heart, O God, **you will not despise**." Same verb, *bāzâ*, now with God as subject and a broken heart as object. `[T]` The psalm answers its own death sentence with the only thing the sentence did not cover.

**Why this matters more than any other finding in the report.** It converts the psalm's most-quoted lines from a general aphorism about heart-religion into an argument: *You do not want sacrifice* — no, and the Torah agrees, because for a high-handed sin there is none; *the sacrifices of God are a broken spirit* — this is what is left, and here is the word for it: the verb of my condemnation, negated. *Confidence: high on every lexical link; high-moderate on the synthesis.*

---

#### 11b. Isaiah 1:10–20 — the same argument, prophetic register (high confidence, direction of dependence open)

**The claim.** Psalm 51 and Isaiah 1:10–20 share an unusually dense cluster of vocabulary, running through the same sequence — sacrifice refused, hands full of blood, wash, be clean, white as snow.

**Move 1 — source context.** Isa 1:10–20 addresses "rulers of Sodom": God is sated with burnt offerings, does not **delight** (*ḥāpēṣ*) in the blood of bulls (1:11); he **hides his eyes** because their hands are full of **bloods** (*dāmîm*, 1:15); *wash* (*rāḥaṣ*), *make yourselves clean* (*hizzakkû*, 1:16); though your sins be scarlet they shall be **white as snow** (1:18). `[T]`

**Move 2 — book usage.** Isaiah returns to the blotting-out of transgression in his own vocabulary at 43:25 and 44:22, both using *māḥâ* — the psalm's opening verb, and one of only 32 OT verses that carry it. `[T]`

**Move 3 — OT to OT, the overlap tabulated:**

| Element | Isaiah 1 | Psalm 51 (MT) | Lemma | Verified |

| Hiding the face/eyes | 1:15 *ʾaʿlîm ʿênay mikkem* | 51:11 *hastēr pāneḵā mēḥăṭāʾāy* | different verbs, same gesture — **inverted** | ✓ |

Seven elements, two of which are **two-verse pairs in the whole Hebrew Bible**. `[T]` This is far past what shared genre produces.

**The inversion is the interesting part.** In Isaiah, God *hides his eyes from the people* because their hands are full of blood — a judgment. In Psalm 51 the speaker asks God to *hide his face from my sins* (v. 9 [MT 11]) and then, in the very next verse, *do not cast me away from your presence* (*pānîm*, v. 11 [MT 13]). `[T]` He asks for half the judgment idiom and against the other half, in consecutive verses. Isaiah's *wash yourselves, make yourselves clean* is, in Psalm 51, addressed to God: *wash me, cleanse me*. `[I]` *Confidence: high for the lexical data; moderate-high for the reading of the inversion.*

**Direction of dependence: open.** Nothing in either text settles which is prior, and per Phase 0.55 this report does not resolve it. Named as an Open Question.

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Within the Psalter, the *ḥāpēṣ* + *zeḇaḥ* collocation appears at **Ps 40:7** ("sacrifice and offering you have not desired… my ears you have opened") — which pairs with Ps 51 not only in vocabulary but in the organ mentioned: Ps 40 has God *dig out ears*, Ps 51 has God *open lips*. `[T]` Both psalms are Davidic, both deny that God wants sacrifice, and both replace the offering with a bodily faculty God has to act on first. See 11d.

#### 11c. Hyssop, Leviticus 14 and Numbers 19 — the wrong rite, deliberately (high confidence)

**The claim.** When the psalmist asks to be purged with **hyssop** (v. 7 [MT 9]), he reaches past the sin-offering to the rites for *uncleanness* — the two rites in the Torah that deal with conditions no sacrifice can touch.

**Move 1 — source context.** *ʾēzôḇ* occurs 10 times in the OT: **Exod 12:22** (Passover — blood applied to the doorframe); **Lev 14:4, 6, 49, 51, 52** (the cleansed leper, and the leprous house); **Num 19:6, 18** (the red heifer, and the water of impurity for corpse-defilement); **1 Kgs 5:13** (Solomon's botany); **Ps 51:9**. `[T]` **Psalm 51 is the only figurative use in the Hebrew Bible.**

**Move 2 — how those books use it.** Leviticus 14 and Numbers 19 handle the two conditions that put a person *outside the camp* and that ordinary sacrifice does not remedy: *ṣāraʿaṯ* and contact with a corpse. In both, hyssop applies a cleansing agent from outside to a person who cannot cleanse himself. `[T]`

**Move 3 — OT to OT.** The combination is the argument: a man guilty of adultery and of a man's death asks for the ritual that treats *corpse-contact* and *exclusion* — because it is the one available to him. The Passover instance (Exod 12:22) adds a further note that a preacher may want: there, hyssop applies **blood**, and the result is that death passes over. `[I]` *Confidence: high on the inventory; moderate-high on the "deliberately the wrong rite" reading — the alternative is that hyssop is simply the era's stock cleansing image; against that stands its total absence from figurative use everywhere else.*

**Move 4 — internal echo.** None within the Psalter: *ʾēzôḇ* occurs once. `[T]`

---

#### 11d. Ps 51:4 quoted at Romans 3:4 — the New Testament's own text (full triad; category 3 divergence)

**The quotation.** Rom 3:4 (SBLGNT): καθὼς γέγραπται· **Ὅπως ἂν δικαιωθῇς ἐν τοῖς λόγοις σου καὶ νικήσεις ἐν τῷ κρίνεσθαί σε.** LXX Ps 50:6 (Swete and Rahlfs): **ὅπως ἂν δικαιωθῇς ἐν τοῖς λόγοις σου καὶ νικήσῃς ἐν τῷ κρίνεσθαί σε.** Word-for-word apart from the last verb's form. `[T]`

**The Hebrew.** MT 51:6b: *ləmaʿan **tiṣdaq** bəḏoḇreḵā, **tizkeh** ḇəšopṭeḵā* — "so that you may be **righteous/justified** in your speaking, **clear** in your judging."

**The divergence, triaged.** Three shifts, and they are not the same kind:

| Element | MT | LXX | Triage |
|---|---|---|---|
| *bəḏoḇreḵā* — "in your speaking" (infinitive) | verbal | ἐν τοῖς λόγοις σου — "in your words" (plural noun) | **Category 1 — translation.** Hebrew infinitives construct routinely become Greek nouns |
| *tizkeh* — "be clear/pure" | *zāḵâ* | νικήσῃς — "prevail, win" | **Category 1 — translation, contextually driven.** In a lawsuit, "be found clean" and "win the case" are the same outcome. Not a different Vorlage |
| *bəšopṭeḵā* — "**in your judging**" (active) | *šāpaṭ* qal inf. | ἐν τῷ **κρίνεσθαί** σε — "**when you are judged**" (middle/passive inf. with accusative subject) | **Category 1, but consequential.** *šāpaṭ* in the niphal means *to enter into litigation*, and the Greek renders the infinitive as though pointed that way; the result is that **God moves from bench to dock** |

**No category-2 substantive variant is claimed.** Swete and Rahlfs agree; the MT is unproblematic; the difference is a translator's reading of an unpointed verb, not a different text. `[T]`

**Category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text.** Paul is not quoting the Hebrew loosely; he is quoting the Greek exactly, and **the Greek is what his argument needs.** Rom 3:3–5 asks whether Israel's unfaithfulness nullifies God's faithfulness; the answer is "let God be true and every man a liar", followed by this quotation, followed immediately by "if our unrighteousness demonstrates God's righteousness…" (3:5). The whole passage is about **God being vindicated when he is put on trial** — which is what the LXX's passive infinitive says and the MT's active does not. `[T]`

**Move 1 — source context.** In Psalm 51 the clause is the *purpose* of the confession: the speaker confesses so that God is shown right. He is not excusing himself; he is refusing to leave God with any charge to answer. `[T]`

**Move 2 — Psalter-level usage.** The Psalter's normal move under accusation is to assert the speaker's innocence and ask God to judge (Pss 7:8; 26:1; 35:24; 43:1). **Psalm 51 reverses the courtroom**: the speaker pleads guilty and the verdict sought is on *God*. `[T]` *Confidence: high — the pattern is easy to check across the lament psalms.*

**Move 3 — OT to OT.** The concession "against you, you only, have I sinned" (v. 4 [MT 6]) is the same theological move as 2 Sam 12:13 (*ḥāṭāʾṯî laYHWH*), and the same move Achan is asked to make at Josh 7:19 — *give glory to YHWH and confess*, where confession is framed as an act that vindicates God. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate for the Joshua link; high for the 2 Samuel one, which is lemma-verified.*

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Paul quotes **two** Davidic penitential psalms within eleven verses, and they bracket his justification argument: **Rom 3:4 = LXX Ps 50:6** (this psalm) and **Rom 4:7–8 = LXX Ps 31:1–2 = MT Ps 32:1–2** ("blessed are those whose lawless deeds are forgiven"). Rom 4:7 is verbatim; Rom 4:8 is verbatim minus the LXX's final clause. `[T]` And the verb the LXX supplies at Ps 50:6 — **δικαιόω** — is the verb of Romans. *Confidence: high. This is Move 4 doing real work: the two psalms are the same man on the same subject, and Paul uses them as the two ends of one argument.*

**A note for anyone checking the Greek.** SBLGNT prints **νικήσεις** (future indicative) at Rom 3:4 and marks it as a point of divergence among its base editions; the LXX has **νικήσῃς** (aorist subjunctive). Which form NA28 prints, and what its apparatus reports, is **not** answerable from this corpus — the SBLGNT carries no apparatus. Referred to Logos. Recorded as an Open Question. Nothing in this report's argument depends on the answer.

#### 11e. Ezekiel 36:25–27 — the same three things, and a different verb (high confidence on the overlap; direction open)

**Move 1 — source context.** Ezek 36:22–32: God acts for his own name's sake, not Israel's merit. He will sprinkle **clean water** and they shall be **clean** (*ṭāhēr*, twice in 36:25); he will **give** (*nāṯan*) a **new heart** and a **new spirit**; he will put **my spirit** within them (36:27).

**Move 2 — book usage.** Ezekiel repeats the promise at 11:19 and — decisively for our purposes — issues it as a **command** at 18:31: "**cast away** (*hašlîḵû*) from you all your **transgressions** (*pišʿêḵem*)… and **make yourselves** a new heart and a new spirit." `[T]`

**Move 3 — OT to OT.** Psalm 51 asks for the same three things in the same order — **cleansing, a heart, a spirit** — and differs in the verb:

| | Ezekiel | Psalm 51 (MT) |
|---|---|---|
| Cleansing | *ṭāhēr* (36:25, ×2) | *ṭāhēr* (51:4, 9), *lēḇ ṭāhôr* (51:12) |
| Heart | *nāṯan* — "**give** a new heart" (36:26) | ***bārāʾ*** — "**create** a clean heart" (51:12) |
| Spirit | *nāṯan* — "put **my spirit** within" (36:27) | *ḥādaš* — "**renew** a steadfast spirit"; *rûaḥ qoḏšəḵā* (51:12–13) |
| Transgression | *hašlîḵû… pišʿêḵem* (18:31) | *māḥâ pəšāʿāy* (51:3); *ʾal-tašlîḵēnî* (51:13) |

`[T]` on every lemma. **Ezekiel 18:31 commands the people to *cast away* their transgressions and make themselves a new heart; Psalm 51 asks God not to *cast away* the speaker, and to create the heart himself** — the same verb *šālaḵ*, and the psalm hands the making back to God. `[I]` *Confidence: high for the lexical overlap; moderate-high for the reading of the reversal.*

**Direction of dependence: open**, and named as an Open Question. Note that the direction is not settled by dating either text, because the strongest datum is not a date: **Psalm 51 uses the stronger verb.** *nāṯan* gives what exists; *bārāʾ* makes what does not.

**Move 4 — internal echo.** Ps 104:29–30, the closest thing in the Psalter to Ps 51:11–13, and the only other verse in the Hebrew Bible carrying *bārāʾ* + *ḥādaš* + *rûaḥ*:

> **Ps 104:29–30:** you **hide your face** (*tastîr pāneḵā*), they are dismayed… you send your **spirit** (*rûḥăḵā*), they are **created** (*yibbārēʾûn*); you **renew** (*təḥaddēš*) the face of the ground.
> **Ps 51:11–13 [Eng 9–11]:** **hide your face** (*hastēr pāneḵā*) from my sins… a clean heart **create** (*bərāʾ*) for me… a steadfast **spirit** (*rûaḥ*) **renew** (*ḥaddēš*) within me… your holy **spirit** do not take from me.

Same four elements, same order. `[T]` *Confidence: high — this is the tightest verified intertext in the report, and it is inside the Psalter, which makes it Move 4 rather than Move 3.* What Ps 104 says God does to the created order every spring, Ps 51 asks him to do to one man.

#### 11f. The *rûaḥ qōḏeš* phrase — three occurrences in the Hebrew Bible

The exact construct phrase **"holy spirit"** (*rûaḥ* + *qōḏeš*) occurs in **three verses in the whole Hebrew Bible**: **Ps 51:13** (*rûaḥ qoḏšəḵā*, "your holy spirit"), **Isa 63:10** and **Isa 63:11** (*rûaḥ qoḏšô*, "his holy spirit"). `[T]`

The verification matters, because the co-occurrence of the two lemmas in a verse is *not* the phrase: seven verses carry both lemmas (add 1 Chr 28:12; Exod 35:21; Ezek 42:20; Isa 57:13), and in those four they are unconnected. Checked individually; only the three above have the construct. `[T]`

**Isaiah 63:10–11 is a remarkable partner.** There the people "rebelled and **grieved his holy spirit**, so he turned to be their enemy" (63:10), and then ask "where is he who **put his holy spirit** in their midst?" (63:11). Psalm 51's petition — *do not take your holy spirit from me* — sits exactly between Isaiah's two clauses: after grieving, before removal. `[I]` *Confidence: high for the three-occurrence datum; moderate for the reading.*

**Note on capitalisation.** NASB95, ESV and KJV all print "Holy Spirit" at v. 11. That is a translational decision about a phrase the Hebrew Bible uses three times, twice of God's presence with Israel in the exodus. It is not wrong, and the OT-to-NT trajectory is real (see Christological Reading), but a sermon should not build a doctrine of the third person of the Trinity on the capital letters. See Preaching Pitfalls.

#### 11g. Lesser links, Move 1 only (moderate confidence)

- **Exod 32:32–33** — Moses asks to be **blotted out** (*māḥâ*) of God's book; God replies that he blots out the sinner. Ps 51:3, 11 asks for the sins to be blotted out instead. *māḥâ* is the flood-verb (Gen 6:7; 7:4, 23) — God blotting out *people*. `[T]` on the inventory (32 OT verses); `[I]` on the reading. The psalm asks for a verb usually applied to sinners to be applied to sins.
- **Num 6:24–26** — the Aaronic blessing's *yəḥunnekkā*, from *ḥānan*, the psalm's first verb (*ḥonnēnî*, MT 3). Within Book II, *ḥonnēnî ʾĕlōhîm* also opens Pss 56:2 and 57:2. `[T]` The psalm begins with the ordinary word for a priestly blessing, from a man who cannot go to a priest.
- **Gen 1:1** — *bārāʾ*. Treated at Headline 3 and Biblical Theology.
- **1 Sam 15:22; Hos 6:6** — the other members of the *ḥāpēṣ*/*zeḇaḥ* family. Hos 6:6 is in the five-verse collocation set; 1 Sam 15:22 uses a different construction (*ḥēpeṣ* noun + *ʿōlôṯ ûzəḇāḥîm*) and is the closer conceptual parallel but not a lemma match in the same shape. Flagged so no one cites it as one.

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew poetry**, per the Psalter's book-level classification. `[T]`

Per the standing discipline this report does not assign a Gunkel–Mowinckel *Gattung*. Psalm 51 is conventionally labelled an individual lament or penitential psalm and grouped with Pss 6, 32, 38, 102, 130, 143 — a grouping that the Psalms book-overview also carries. That grouping is a **`[S]` scholarly and ecclesial category** (the seven penitential psalms are a fifth-century Christian selection, not a marking in the Hebrew text), and nothing in this report rests on it.

What *is* text-observable about the psalm's form:

- **No enemy.** Unlike the great majority of first-person psalms, there is no adversary, no *ʾōyēḇ*, no request for the wicked to be dealt with. `[T]`
- **No protestation of innocence** — see Tone and Feel.
- **No complaint about God.** The one thing said about God's conduct is that he is right (v. 4 [MT 6]). `[T]`
- **A vow of praise**, in the ordinary lament position (vv. 13–15 [MT 15–17]) — but the vow is *contingent on a faculty being restored*, which is not ordinary. `[T]`
- **A superscription assigning it to the choir director** (*lamnaṣṣēaḥ*, MT 3) — i.e. the psalm that says God does not want sacrifice is filed for public liturgical use. `[T]` That tension is on the page and is exegetically interesting; see Original Audience.

### 13. Copycat

**What is *not* transferable.** The specifics of the occasion. Nothing in the psalm invites the hearer to reproduce David's sin as a route to David's prayer, and the superscription is a *label*, not a template.

**What *is* transferable, and why.** The psalm is a prayer, in the first person, with no marks of office. The speaker asks for nothing that requires him to be a king, a prophet, or a priest — indeed he asks precisely because the priestly route is closed to him. `[T]` And the psalm itself makes the transfer explicit: *then I will teach transgressors your ways* (v. 13 [MT 15]) — the speaker's stated intention is that others use what he has learned. `[T]`

**The one real copycat trap** is the sacrifice statement (v. 16 [MT 18]). Lifted out, it becomes "God doesn't want religious observance, he wants your heart" — a sentiment the verse can be made to carry and the psalm does not. The psalm's own next-but-one verse restores the offerings. See Preaching Pitfalls.

**The verdict:** the *prayer* is copycat material of the highest order; the *situation* is not; the *sacrifice statement* is copycat material only with its context attached.

### 14. Bible Timeline

Psalm 51 stands at a specific place on the line, and the place is what makes it preachable.

**Behind it:** a sacrificial system that works (Leviticus), and a clause in that system saying it does not work for this (Num 15:30–31). A covenant with David (2 Sam 7) that has just been dishonoured by David. A prophet who applies the statute (2 Sam 12:9). `[T]`

**In it:** a man asking for two things the covenant does not provide — the cleansing of a deliberate sin, and the creation of a new interior. He asks for both on the ground of *ḥeseḏ* alone. `[T]`

**Ahead of it:** the promise that God will do exactly what the psalm asks, and do it corporately — Ezek 36:25–27, Jer 31:31–34, Joel 2:28–29. And, in the New Testament, the two ends of the psalm's problem answered separately:

- **The sacrifice problem** — Heb 10:4, "it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins", introducing a quotation of **LXX Ps 39:7–9 (MT Ps 40:7–9)** in Heb 10:5–8, using **the same Greek pair** the LXX uses at Ps 50:18 (θέλω + εὐδοκέω + ὁλοκαυτώματα). `[T]` The one psalm-family that says God does not want sacrifice is the family Hebrews reaches for to say why Christ came.
- **The cleansing problem** — Heb 9:19 has Moses sprinkling with **hyssop** (ὕσσωπος, one of only two NT occurrences, with John 19:29); Heb 9:13–14 and 10:22 have hearts **sprinkled** (ῥαντίζω) clean, the LXX's own verb at Ps 50:9 (ῥαντιεῖς με ὑσσώπῳ). Heb 10:22: *ῥεραντισμένοι τὰς **καρδίας*** — sprinkled as to the **heart**. `[T]` That is Ps 51:7 and 51:10 in one clause.

**The timeline point for a preacher.** Psalm 51 is a prayer whose answer had not yet arrived. It asks for a purging the Torah cannot supply and a creation only God can perform, and it asks in the dark. To preach it as though the answer were already in the psalmist's hand is to flatten it. To preach it without saying that the answer came is to leave the congregation where he was. `[I]`

### 15. Who Am I in This Passage?

The psalm makes this unusually simple and unusually uncomfortable.

**Not God.** The psalm's God gives *ḥeseḏ*, blots out, creates, opens lips, does not despise. None of that is ours to do. `[T]`

**Not the exceptional sinner.** The temptation with this psalm is to identify with David's *magnitude* — a great sin, a great repentance, a great restoration — which flatters as much as it humbles. The psalm's own vocabulary blocks it: he calls himself a *pōšēaʿ* and promises to teach *pōšəʿîm*, the same word (v. 13 [MT 15]). `[T]` He is not a special case; he is the case.

**The speaker.** Everyone praying this psalm is in the position of a person who wants something the machinery cannot give. The universalising line is v. 5 [MT 7] — *in iniquity I was brought forth* — which locates the problem before any of the speaker's choices. `[T]` It is not there to excuse; it is there in the middle of a confession, and its function is to say *the trouble is deeper than the incident*.

**One place the reader is invited to stand that is not the speaker.** *Then I will teach transgressors your ways, and sinners will return to you* (v. 13 [MT 15]). Every hearer of this psalm is downstream of that promise. `[I]`

### 16. So What?

The four-audience test.

**(1) Those who do not believe.** The psalm's opening ground is not the speaker's sincerity but God's *ḥeseḏ*, and its central request is for something to be *created* rather than repaired. `[T]` That is an unusually direct address to the person who assumes religion is self-improvement. What the psalm asks for is not available by trying.

**(2) New believers.** vv. 10–12 [MT 12–14] are among the clearest OT statements that the Christian interior life is God's work and is asked for, not achieved. The *rûaḥ* count (Tool 7) makes the point sharper: the spirit he wants God to make in him and the spirit he offers God are the same word — one crushed, one steadfast.

**(3) Mature believers.** The psalm is severe about what mature believers are worst at: the sin that has no procedure. It addresses the person who knows perfectly well how to be religious and has done something for which being religious offers no route back. `[I]`

**(4) The struggling / the guilty.** v. 17 [MT 19] — the one thing God will not despise — read against Num 15:31 and 2 Sam 12:9 is the strongest pastoral sentence in the psalm, and its force depends entirely on knowing what *bāzâ* has already been used for. A broken heart is not a lesser offering God accepts reluctantly. It is the offering *for the sin that has no offering*.

**And the "so what" the psalm itself states:** *then I will teach transgressors your ways*. The psalm's own account of why it exists is that a forgiven sinner talks. `[T]`

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **The imperative stack.** Sixteen 2ms imperatives/jussives addressed to God in MT 3–14, against **one** cohortative for the speaker (*ʾălammədâ*, MT 15). `[T]` The grammar is the theology.
- **Word order at MT 51:12.** *lēḇ ṭāhôr bərāʾ-lî ʾĕlōhîm* — **object first**. Hebrew default is verb-initial; fronting "a clean heart" is emphatic. `[T]` The verse's stress falls on *what* is to be created before *who* is to create it.
- **MT 51:6, *ləmaʿan* + imperfect.** Purpose or result — Hebrew does not distinguish, and the theological difference is large (*did he sin so that God would be justified*, or *is God's vindication the outcome*?). The LXX's ὅπως ἄν keeps the same ambiguity; Paul's use requires only the result sense. Flag rather than resolve. *Confidence: moderate.*
- **MT 51:9–11, imperfects.** *təḥaṭṭəʾēnî*, *təḵabbəsēnî*, *tašmîʿēnî* are 2ms imperfects, readable as petition ("purge me") or as confident future ("you will purge me"). English versions take them as petition; **the LXX takes them as futures** (ῥαντιεῖς, πλυνεῖς, ἀκουτιεῖς, and at 50:17 ἀνοίξεις). `[T]` A real difference of mood across the whole cleansing section — see Textual Variants.

#### Wordplay and sound-features

- ***dikkîṯā* / *niḏkeh*** (MT 10, 19) — the psalm's one deliberate root-play, and it carries the argument. `[T]`
- ***pəšāʿay* / *pōšəʿîm*** (MT 3, 5 / MT 15) — noun to participle, sin to sinners. `[T]`
- ***ḥonnēnî*** opening (MT 3) against ***ḥāpēṣ*** closing the sacrifice section (MT 18, 21) — assonance only, not a root link. Noted and **not** claimed as a device.
- **No acrostic, no refrain, no *Selah*.** Checked. `[T]`

#### Greek observations (the LXX as a text in its own right)

- **πνεύματι ἡγεμονικῷ** (LXX 50:14) for *rûaḥ nəḏîḇâ*. ἡγεμονικόν is the **Stoic technical term for the ruling faculty of the soul**. The translator has rendered a word about *willingness* with a word about *governance*. `[T]` on the rendering; `[S]` on the philosophical provenance of the term, which is standard but worth verifying in a lexicon of the LXX. *Confidence: high on the datum, moderate-high on the Stoic identification — recommend LEH or Muraoka.*
- **ὀστᾶ τεταπεινωμένα** (LXX 50:10) for *ʿăṣāmôṯ dikkîṯā*. The Hebrew says **you** crushed them; the Greek has a passive participle, "humbled bones", with **no agent**. `[T]` This softens the psalm's hardest claim. But the Greek then rebuilds the link its own way: 50:19 has καρδίαν συντετριμμένην καὶ **τεταπεινωμένην** — the same participle. So both languages connect vv. 10 and 19; they use different words to do it. `[T]` *A good example of a divergence that changes the route without changing the destination.*
- **ἐνκαίνισον / ἐγκαίνισον** (LXX 50:12) for *ḥaddēš*. The verb appears twice in the NT, both in Hebrews (9:18; 10:20), where it means **inaugurate** a covenant and a way. `[T]` Semantic overlap only — Hebrews is not quoting the psalm. *Confidence: moderate; do not present as an allusion.*
- **ἐξάλειψον** (LXX 50:3, 11) for *māḥâ*. In the NT: Acts 3:19 ("that your sins may be **blotted out**"), Col 2:14, Rev 3:5; 7:17; 21:4. `[T]` Acts 3:19 is the closest verbal contact and is worth a preacher's attention: Peter uses the psalm's Greek verb for the psalm's request.
- **κτίσον** (LXX 50:12) for *bārāʾ*. κτίζω in the NT: 13 verses, of which **Eph 2:10, 2:15, 4:24 and Col 3:10** are the anthropological ones — Eph 4:24 (*τὸν καινὸν ἄνθρωπον τὸν κατὰ θεὸν κτισθέντα*) being the nearest to "create in me a clean heart". `[T]` on the inventory; the trajectory is treated under Christological Reading.
- **συντετριμμένον** (LXX 50:19). συντρίβω in the NT includes **Matt 12:20**, κάλαμον **συντετριμμένον** οὐ κατεάξει — "a bruised reed he will not break", from Isa 42:3. `[T]` Same participle, same object-class. *Confidence: moderate as an intertext — the shared word is common enough that this is suggestive rather than demonstrative. But the thought is identical: the already-broken is the thing this God does not finish off.*

### Textual Variants

**One MT variant, one Greek reading-decision cluster, and one NT apparatus question.**

**(1) MT 51:4 [Eng 2] — *hrbh* / *hereḇ*. Ketiv–Qere.** The WLC prints the consonantal **הרבה unpointed**, the standard marker of a *Ketiv* whose reading is supplied elsewhere. **BHS** (Logos export, `_texts/logos-exports/BHS Psalms.txt` line 835) prints **הֶ֭רֶב** with an apparatus siglum. `[T]` The two options are the adverbial infinitive absolute *harbēh* ("abundantly, thoroughly") and the hiphil imperative *hereḇ* ("multiply!" — i.e. "wash me much"). **The LXX supports the adverbial sense**: ἐπὶ πλεῖον, "still more" (Swete and Rahlfs). `[T]`

**Preaching impact: none.** Every English version renders it "thoroughly" or equivalent, and both readings mean the same thing. Recorded because a reader with BHS open will see the siglum and wonder. *Confidence: high that the sense is secure; the BHS apparatus's own note has not been read and is referred to Logos.*

**(2) The Greek cleansing-section moods (LXX 50:9–11, 17).** Not a variant in the manuscript sense — Swete and Rahlfs agree — but a **systematic reading-decision**: the translator took MT's imperfects as **futures**, turning petitions into predictions. "You **will** sprinkle me with hyssop and I shall be clean… you **will** wash me… you **will** open my lips." `[T]` **Triage: category 1** (translation, not a different Vorlage). Worth naming because it changes the psalm's emotional register in Greek — the Greek psalm is more confident than the Hebrew one, and a congregation hearing the psalm through the New Testament's Greek would have heard the confident version.

**(3) LXX 50:8 for MT 51:8.** MT's two difficult body-words become τὰ ἄδηλα καὶ τὰ κρύφια **τῆς σοφίας σου** — "the **unclear** and **hidden things of your wisdom** you have shown me". The Greek has no anatomy in it at all. **Triage: category 1 or 2 — genuinely undecided.** Either the translator could not construe the Hebrew and paraphrased from *ḥoḵmâ* at the verse's end, or he read a different text. `[I]` *Confidence: low. Evidence needed: the BHS apparatus at 51:8 and a Göttingen-level Greek apparatus for Psalms. Referred to Logos. Recorded as an Open Question.*

**(4) SBLGNT Rom 3:4 νικήσεις vs LXX νικήσῃς.** Referred to NA28. See Tool 11d. `[T]` that the two corpus texts differ; `[unverified]` as to what NA28 prints and what its apparatus reports.

**Nothing in this report's findings turns on any unresolved item above.** The one place a variant could have mattered — the Romans quotation — is unaffected, because Paul's argument rests on ἐν τῷ κρίνεσθαί σε, on which every edition consulted agrees.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** The superscription places the psalm in Jerusalem in the united monarchy, after the events of 2 Sam 11. `[T]` for the placement as a claim of the text; `[S]` for its historicity.

**What the original audience knew without being told:**

1. **That there was no sacrifice for this.** Num 15:30–31 is not obscure law; it is the paragraph that defines the *limit* of the sacrificial system. An Israelite hearing "you do not delight in sacrifice" from a man guilty of adultery and murder would not hear a critique of ritual. He would hear a man stating the obvious. `[I]` *Confidence: high — the law is explicit and the vocabulary link is verified.*

2. **What hyssop was for.** A small aromatic plant used as an applicator, and used in exactly two everyday contexts: the cleansing of a healed leper and the removal of corpse-defilement. Both restore someone to the camp. `[T]` on the Torah usage. An Israelite hearing "purge me with hyssop" would picture a priest with a bunch of herbs and a bowl, and a person coming back in.

3. **Which sacrifices are named, and what they cost.** *ʿōlâ* (burnt offering) is the offering wholly consumed; *kālîl* (v. 19 [MT 21]) is the intensive of the same idea; *pārîm* (bulls) are the most expensive class of animal. `[T]` The psalm's closing verse is not a modest restoration of worship but the most costly form of it.

4. **What the Zion/walls petition would mean.** For a pre-exilic hearer, a prayer for God's *rāṣôn* on Zion and the building up of its walls; for a hearer after 586, an unmistakable prayer for the ruined city. The verse does the second without ceasing to be able to do the first. `[I]`

5. **What "bloods" meant.** *dāmîm* in the plural is the standard idiom for **bloodguilt** — the legal liability incurred by causing a death, which pollutes the land and requires atonement the ordinary system cannot supply (Num 35:33). `[T]` The plural is not intensification.

6. **What *lamnaṣṣēaḥ* meant.** A direction to a musical functionary. `[T]` on the word's presence; the precise nature of the role is `[S]` and disputed. The point that matters is that this psalm was filed for **use**, not archived as a private document.

**What this changes about how we read.**

- v. 16 [MT 18] stops being a theological generalisation and becomes a statement of fact about the speaker's legal position.
- v. 7 [MT 9] stops being a poetic flourish about cleanness and becomes a specific and slightly shocking request: *treat me as the leper and the corpse-toucher are treated, because that is the only category the law has that fits.* `[I]`

**Uncertain — verify:** the precise identification of *ʾēzôḇ* (probably *Origanum syriacum*, not the modern hyssop) and the exact function of *lamnaṣṣēaḥ*. Neither bears weight here.

### Original Audience

**Who first heard this, and how.** Two audiences, and the psalm belongs to both.

**(1) A hearer inside the narrative.** For a hearer who takes the superscription's setting, the psalm is a king's prayer, and the shocking thing is *what a king is not able to do*. David commands the treasury and the priesthood and could have burnt a thousand bulls. `[T]` The psalm is the king discovering that the machinery he owns cannot help him. Note that this hearer would also register something the modern reader does not: the psalm's petition for Zion (v. 18 [MT 20]) is a king praying about his own city, and the last line puts sacrifice back onto an altar he built.

**(2) A hearer of the Psalter as a book.** For the reader who arrives at Psalm 51 having just read Psalm 50, the effect is different and, I think, primary. Psalm 50 has just told a covenant-breaker that God does not need his bulls, that he has no right to recite the covenant, and that no one will deliver him. `[T]` Turning the page to Psalm 51 is discovering what that man says next.

**What the first hearers would have expected, and did not get.**

- They would have expected a lament to name an enemy. It does not. `[T]`
- They would have expected an offering to be brought. It is refused (v. 16 [MT 18]) — and then, disorientingly, expected again (v. 19 [MT 21]).
- They would have expected the divine **name** in a prayer of this intensity. It never appears. `[T]` Whether this is the Elohistic editing or something the psalm itself is doing is not decidable from the psalm alone; the Book II statistics (166:28) make editing the likelier account. `[I]`

**The one feature a modern audience is guaranteed to miss.** That the psalm's final word about the broken heart uses the verb of the sentence of death. Num 15:31 → 2 Sam 12:9 → Ps 51:19. Without that chain, v. 17 is a beautiful sentiment. With it, it is an acquittal. `[T]`

### Biblical Theology

Three themes run through the psalm, and each is traceable.

**(1) Creation.** *bārāʾ* is Genesis 1's verb, never used in the OT with a human subject. Its Psalter usage is cosmic five times out of six. Its one exception is here, and its object is a heart. `[T]` The theme runs: Gen 1:1 → Ps 104:30 (creation renewed by the *rûaḥ*) → **Ps 51:12** (one man asks for the same act) → Isa 65:17 (new heavens and new earth, *bārāʾ*) → Eph 2:10; 4:24; Col 3:10 (κτίζω of the new humanity). The psalm's claim is that the renewal of a sinner is an act of the same order as the making of the world — not a metaphor for it. `[I]` *Confidence: high on the lexical spine; moderate-high on the theological claim, which is the standard reading and here independently reached.*

**(2) Spirit.** The construct *rûaḥ qōḏeš* appears three times in the OT — Ps 51:13 and Isa 63:10, 11 — and in Isaiah it is the exodus Spirit, present in Israel's midst, grieved by rebellion. `[T]` The theme runs: the Spirit on Israel's leaders (Num 11:17, 25–29; 1 Sam 16:13–14, where the Spirit **departs from Saul** — the fear behind Ps 51:13) → **Ps 51:13** (a king asks not to be Saul) → Ezek 36:27; Joel 2:28–29 (the Spirit given to all) → Acts 2. *Confidence: high for the *rûaḥ qōḏeš* datum; moderate-high for the 1 Sam 16:14 background, which is `[I]` — the psalm does not cite it, but it is the only OT narrative of the Spirit being taken from a king, and it is the immediately preceding king.*

**(3) Sacrifice, and its limit.** The chain: Leviticus provides; Num 15:30–31 excepts; Ps 40:7, Ps 51:18, Isa 1:11, Hos 6:6 declare God's non-delight (the five verses carrying *ḥāpēṣ* + *zeḇaḥ*); 1 Sam 15:22 says obedience is better; Ps 51:19 supplies the offering that remains; Heb 10:4–10 quotes Ps 40 to say why Christ came. `[T]` on every link.

**The synthesis the three make together.** Psalm 51 asks for a **new creation**, by the **Spirit**, because the **sacrificial system has nothing for him**. Those are the three things the New Testament says the gospel supplies, and the psalm asks for all three in eleven verses, without knowing how they will be given. `[I]`

**A note on covenant.** The psalm never uses *bərîṯ*. `[T]` Psalm 50 does, twice (50:5, 16). The absence is worth registering: the man who has broken the covenant does not appeal to it. He appeals to *ḥeseḏ* — which is covenant loyalty, but named as a disposition of God rather than as a claim he can make. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate-high. The word-absence is a fact; the reading is mine.*

### Difficult Verses

**v. 4 [MT 6] — "Against You, You only, I have sinned."** The difficulty is obvious: Uriah is dead and Bathsheba is wronged. Three considerations, none of which is evasion:

- The verse is not a statement about *whom the act harmed*; it is a statement about *before whom the case is tried*. Its second half is entirely about God's verdict being upheld. `[T]`
- The idiom is 2 Sam 12:13's — *ḥāṭāʾṯî laYHWH* — spoken by David in the presence of a prophet who has just described Uriah's death in detail. Neither text imagines the human victims are unreal. `[T]`
- Hebrew *ḥāṭāʾ lə-* regularly means "sin against" a person (Gen 20:9; 1 Sam 19:4). The psalm's *ləḵā ləḇaddəḵā* ("to you, to you alone") is therefore a deliberate narrowing, and what it narrows is the *court*, not the *damage*. `[I]`

**Do not resolve it by saying human sin doesn't matter.** Resolve it by saying that the reason David has no defence is that his judge is the one he offended. *Confidence: high.*

**v. 5 [MT 7] — "in sin my mother conceived me."** The verse has carried an enormous doctrinal load (original sin) and an ugly misreading (that conception or the mother is sinful). What the Hebrew says is that the speaker's *ʿāwōn* and *ḥēṭəʾ* were the medium in which he was brought forth and conceived. `[T]` The subject throughout is **the speaker**, not the mother: *ḥôlālətî* ("I was brought forth") is 1cs. `[T]` The verse is an intensifier inside a confession — *the trouble is older than the incident* — not a treatise on transmission. It is legitimate raw material for the doctrine; it is not by itself the doctrine. *Confidence: high on the grammar; the doctrinal question is beyond this tool's scope.*

**v. 11 [MT 13] — "do not take Your Holy Spirit from me."** The pastoral landmine: hearers who fear losing salvation. Three text-observable checks:

- The phrase is one of three OT instances, and the other two (Isa 63:10–11) are about **God's presence with Israel**, corporately, in the exodus. `[T]`
- The nearest narrative background is 1 Sam 16:14, the Spirit departing from **Saul** — a matter of office and anointing, not of a New Testament believer's security. `[I]`
- The New Covenant texts to which this psalm looks forward promise exactly the permanence the psalmist cannot assume (Ezek 36:27; John 14:16). `[I]`

The honest pastoral line: **the psalmist's fear is real, and the reason we do not share it is not that he was wrong but that something has changed.** *Confidence: moderate-high.*

**vv. 16–17 vs 18–19 [MT 18–19 vs 20–21] — the sacrifice crux.** Handled at Tool 3 and Tool 11a. The two text-observable facts that matter: the shared vocabulary across the supposed seam (*ḥāpēṣ*, *zeḇaḥ*) and the *ʾāz*-logic. `[T]`

**A weaponised use to name.** "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit" has been used to press the already-crushed further down — to tell a suffering person that their brokenness is what God wanted. The psalm does not say that. It says that the heart God has broken is a heart God will not despise, which is a promise about God's reception, not a recommendation of the breaking. `[I]` *This distinction is worth making explicitly from the pulpit.*

### Christological Reading

Raw material for `/point-purpose`; not a Teaching Point.

**The trajectory the psalm itself opens.** Psalm 51 is a set of requests with no stated means. It asks for cleansing that the sacrificial system cannot give, a heart that only a creator can make, and a Spirit it fears losing. Each has a New Testament answer, and in each case **the New Testament reaches for this psalm's own Greek vocabulary**:

| The psalm asks (LXX Ps 50) | The New Testament answers | Verbal contact |
|---|---|---|
| ἐξάλειψον τὸ ἀνόμημά μου (50:3, 11) | Acts 3:19 πρὸς τὸ **ἐξαλειφθῆναι** ὑμῶν τὰς ἁμαρτίας; Col 2:14 | ἐξαλείφω `[T]` |
| **ῥαντιεῖς** με **ὑσσώπῳ** (50:9) | Heb 9:19 (**ὑσσώπου** + ἐράντισεν); Heb 10:22 **ῥεραντισμένοι τὰς καρδίας** | ῥαντίζω, ὕσσωπος `[T]` |
| καρδίαν καθαρὰν **κτίσον** ἐν ἐμοί (50:12) | Eph 4:24 τὸν καινὸν ἄνθρωπον τὸν κατὰ θεὸν **κτισθέντα**; Col 3:10 | κτίζω `[T]` |
| οὐκ ἠθέλησας **θυσίαν**… **ὁλοκαυτώματα** οὐκ **εὐδοκήσεις** (50:18) | Heb 10:5–8, quoting LXX Ps 39:7–9 with the same pair, to say why Christ came | θέλω, εὐδοκέω, ὁλοκαύτωμα `[T]` |
| **πνεῦμα συντετριμμένον** (50:19) | Matt 12:20 κάλαμον **συντετριμμένον** οὐ κατεάξει | συντρίβω `[T]`, moderate as an allusion |

`[T]` on every lexical contact; `[I]` on reading them as a trajectory rather than five coincidences of common vocabulary. The strongest are Heb 9–10, where **hyssop, sprinkling, the heart, and the insufficiency of bulls' blood all appear within twenty verses** — the four elements of Psalm 51's problem, in one argument. *Confidence: high.*

**The typological point, stated carefully.** David is not a type of Christ in this psalm. He is the opposite: the anointed king who despised the word of YHWH and had nothing to bring. `[T]` The psalm's Christological force runs the other way — it is a statement of the problem to which Christ is the answer, and the New Testament treats it as such (Rom 3:4 uses it to establish that God is vindicated and every man a liar; Rom 4:7–8 pairs it with Ps 32 to define justification apart from works).

**Two trajectories worth carrying to `/point-purpose`:**

1. **The offering God does not despise.** Num 15:31 → 2 Sam 12:9 → Ps 51:19 → Heb 10:4–14. The sin with no sacrifice, met by the sacrifice that ends sacrifice. This is the psalm's own line of argument, extended.
2. **Create in me.** Gen 1 → Ps 104:30 → Ps 51:12 → Ezek 36:26 → 2 Cor 5:17 / Eph 4:24. The request that the psalm cannot explain how God would grant.

**One thing to resist.** Preaching Psalm 51 as "David's repentance, and how to repent like him". The psalm's own centre is not the quality of the speaker's contrition but the character of God's *ḥeseḏ* and the magnitude of what he is asked to do. `[I]`

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: "God doesn't want religion, he wants your heart"

- **What it looks like:** v. 17 preached as a general contrast between ritual and sincerity — church attendance and liturgy on one side, authentic feeling on the other.
- **Why it's wrong:** The psalm restores sacrifice two verses later (v. 19 [MT 21]), using the same vocabulary (*ḥāpēṣ*, *zeḇaḥ*, *ʿōlâ*) and the most expensive offerings in the system. `[T]` And the *kî* at v. 16 grounds the statement in the **speaker's specific situation**, not in a doctrine of worship. What is unavailable is unavailable *for this sin* (Num 15:30–31).
- **The corrective:** Preach the whole run vv. 16–19. God has not stopped wanting offerings; he has told this man there is no offering for him — and then named the one thing left, using the verb of his condemnation.

### Pitfall: "Against you, you only, have I sinned" as a licence to ignore human victims

- **What it looks like:** A restoration sermon in which a wrongdoer's relationship with God is treated as the whole of the matter, and the people harmed are a footnote.
- **Why it's wrong:** The verse's second half is entirely about **God's verdict being upheld** — the courtroom, not the casualty list. `[T]` The narrative the psalm points at spends a chapter and a half on Uriah, and 2 Sam 12:10–14 is all consequence.
- **The corrective:** Say what the verse says: the reason he has no defence is that his judge is the one he offended. Then say what the psalm's own vocabulary says — *dāmîm*, bloodguilt (v. 14 [MT 16]) — a word that exists precisely because a death cannot be settled privately with God.

### Pitfall: "Take not your Holy Spirit from me" as a proof of losing salvation

- **What it looks like:** Either "you can lose the Spirit, so be careful", or a hasty reassurance that flattens the verse into "he didn't really mean it".
- **Why it's wrong:** The phrase occurs three times in the OT, twice of God's presence with **Israel** in the exodus (Isa 63:10–11) `[T]`, and its nearest narrative background is the Spirit's departure from **Saul** — an anointing for office. `[I]` Reading a New Testament doctrine of indwelling straight off the verse imports what is not there, in either direction.
- **The corrective:** Let the psalmist's fear stand. It was well-founded; he had watched it happen to his predecessor. Then say why we do not share it — because of what Ezekiel promised and what Christ secured — rather than by telling him he was worrying about nothing.

### Pitfall: "In sin my mother conceived me" preached at, or about, mothers

- **What it looks like:** Either a doctrinal aside about conception being tainted, or (worse) a sermon illustration in which the mother is the problem.
- **Why it's wrong:** Every verb in the verse has the **speaker** as its subject. `[T]` The verse is an intensifier inside a confession — *this goes back further than the thing I did* — and it is in the mouth of a man refusing to date his guilt to a single evening.
- **The corrective:** Preach it as the confession's deepest point, not as an anthropological thesis. It legitimately feeds the doctrine of inherited sin; it is not that doctrine's exposition.

### Pitfall: "Be like David — repent well"

- **What it looks like:** A sermon on the quality of David's contrition as a model to imitate: he was thorough, he was honest, he didn't make excuses. Be like that.
- **Why it's wrong:** Moralism, and moralism that the psalm's grammar refuses. Sixteen imperatives addressed to God; one cohortative for the speaker, and that one is a *consequence*. `[T]` The psalm's argument is not that David repented well enough but that God's *ḥeseḏ* is what it stands on (v. 1 [MT 3]) and that what is needed is a **creation** (v. 10 [MT 12]).
- **The corrective:** Preach what the man asks for and who he asks. The psalm's own emphasis is on the size of the request, not the quality of the requester.

### Pitfall: preaching the brokenness as the goal

- **What it looks like:** "God wants you broken" — pressed on people already in pieces.
- **Why it's wrong:** *dāḵâ* at v. 8 [MT 10] has **God** as the subject of the crushing. `[T]` The psalm describes what God has done, and then says God will not despise its result. It does not recommend that anyone pursue it, and it does not tell the crushed that the crushing was the point.
- **The corrective:** The promise is about God's **reception** — "you will not despise" — not about the value of brokenness in itself. Preach the promise to the broken, not the brokenness to the whole.

### Pitfall: reading the psalm without its neighbour

- **What it looks like:** Psalm 51 as a free-standing devotional classic.
- **Why it's wrong:** Seven lemma-verified links to Psalm 50, four of them reversals, and the Asaph psalm's deliberate placement at the head of the Davidic collection. `[T]`
- **The corrective:** Read Psalm 50 in the service, or at least summarise it. The sermon's opening move is available for free: *God has just said he doesn't need your bulls, and that you have no right to say his covenant out loud. Turn the page.*

**Two corrections made in the course of the run**, recorded because the discipline that caught them is the point of it:

1. **The run brief stated that *rûaḥ* occurs three times in MT 51:12–14. It occurs four times in the psalm** — MT 12, 13, 14 and **19**. The fourth is the one that matters, and assuming the brief would have lost the report's fourth Headline Finding. Caught by lemma count, not by reading.
2. **The "Holy Spirit" phrase was to be verified rather than asserted, and the verification changed the claim.** Seven verses carry both *rûaḥ* and *qōḏeš*; only three carry the construct phrase. Reporting the seven would have been wrong; asserting the three without checking would have been right by luck.
