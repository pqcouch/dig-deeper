Point & Purpose: Psalm 51

Passage: Psalm 51 (English numbering throughout; MT in brackets where it matters — MT = English + 2 from v. 1 onwards) Exegesis: dig-deeper-psalm-51.md, 10 September 2026 — a full sixteen-tool run, text-first from the WLC and Swete, with BHS, Rahlfs-Hanhart and NASB95 cited where a reading is load-bearing. Study text: NASB95 Pulpit text: not yet declared. This plan is built on the NASB95 and flags every place the choice bites — see Translation decisions still to make. Date: 12 September 2026 Scope: Single sermon (rationale below).

Phase 0 — Scope decision

One sermon.

Nineteen verses; one unbroken prayer from one speaker to God, with no narratorial break, no change of addressee, and no seam a preacher could honestly stop at. The dig-deeper report identifies four text-marked movements, but they are movements of a single argument — remove (vv. 1–9), create (vv. 10–13), offer (vv. 14–19) — not four preachable units. Split it and the argument dies: a sermon on vv. 1–9 alone has a man asking for cleansing with no account of why the usual route is closed, and a sermon on vv. 16–19 alone is the sermon everyone preaches and the one the report’s Preaching Pitfalls warn against hardest.

One decision to make about the boundary, though. The exegesis is emphatic that Psalm 51 is the answer Psalm 50 demands — seven lemma-verified links, four of them reversals. Read Psalm 50 in the service, or summarise it in the introduction. It is not a second sermon; it is the question this sermon answers. Treating it as optional background is the surest way to lose the psalm.

Fallen Condition Focus

We all know how to be religious. None of us knows what to do with the sin our religion has no remedy for — so we bury it, or we try to pay for it with offerings God has already refused.

This is not an imported felt-need. It is the psalm’s own situation, and it is anchorable at three points in the text:

  • The speaker asks for hyssop (v. 7) — the rite for the leper and the corpse-toucher, the two conditions that put a person outside the camp and that ordinary sacrifice does not touch. He reaches past the sin-offering because the sin-offering is not for him.
  • He says God does not delight in sacrifice (v. 16) — and the kî (“for”) that opens the verse grounds it in his own case, not in a doctrine of worship.
  • Nathan’s indictment (2 Sam 12:9) quotes the one paragraph in the Torah that provides no offering at all (Num 15:30–31), in its own two words: he has despised the word of the LORD.

The shared human ground is not “we all sin”. It is more uncomfortable and more specific: we are competent at the sins our routines can absorb, and helpless before the ones they cannot. Everyone in the room who has been religious for any length of time knows the difference between the sin they confess on Sunday and the one they have never said out loud.

Teaching Point

For the sin the law provides no sacrifice for, God provides the one offering he will not despise — a broken heart which he himself creates — and in Christ he has given once for all what David could only ask for.

Subject: What can a person bring to God for a sin the sacrificial system does not cover? Complement: Nothing he produces — only what God himself makes in him: a created, broken heart, which God promises not to despise, and which Christ’s single offering has now secured.

Traceability. Built from four convergent Headline Findings, not one tool:

Element of the TP Where it comes from
“the sin the law provides no sacrifice for” Num 15:31 → 2 Sam 12:9 → Ps 51:19 [Eng 17], the bāzâ chain — three occurrences of one verb, corpus-verified
“the one offering he will not despise” The third link in that chain: lōʾ tiḇzeh, the verb of the death sentence, negated
“which he himself creates” bārāʾ at MT 51:12 — the Psalter’s only use with a human heart as object (six occurrences; the other five cosmic)
“a broken heart” The fourth rûaḥ (MT 19) answering the three petitioned rûaḥs (MT 12–14); and dāḵâ twice — God crushes the bones (v. 8), God receives the crushed heart (v. 17)
“Christ has given once for all” Heb 10:4–14, which quotes Ps 40 using the same Greek pair the LXX uses at Ps 51:16 (θέλω + εὐδοκέω + ὁλοκαυτώματα)

Anti-moralism check. God is the actor in every clause: God provides, God creates, God will not despise, God has given. David is the man with nothing to bring. The sermon’s payoff is not repent as thoroughly as David did but ask God for what only God can make. Passed.

Purpose Statement

To move hearers from managing their worst guilt — hiding it, or covering it with religious effort God has already refused — to asking God for what only he can give: a cleansing the system cannot supply and a heart he creates.

Differentiated:

  • To the crushed — assurance that the offering they fear is worthless is precisely the one God has promised not to despise, and that the brokenness they are ashamed of is something God himself produced (v. 8: “the bones which You have broken”).
  • To the respectably religious — warning that competence in devotion is no remedy for a despised word, and that the machinery they are good at running has a paragraph in it that says not this one.
  • To the unconvinced — the psalm’s opening move is that the ground of the appeal is God’s ḥesed, not the speaker’s sincerity, and its central request is for something to be created rather than repaired. What this psalm asks for is not available by trying.
  • To all — the psalm’s own stated outcome (v. 13): a forgiven sinner talks. The sermon should send people out with their mouths back.

Candidate Big Question

What do you bring to God for the sin there is no offering for?

Candidate. Alternatives worth weighing at the one-sentence stage:

  • “What do you do with the sin you have never said out loud?” — warmer, more pastoral, slightly less tied to the text’s own argument.
  • “When religion has nothing left to offer you, what has God?” — sharper against the respectably religious; risks sounding like a cheap swipe at liturgy, which this psalm does not make (it puts bulls back on the altar at v. 19).

The first is recommended because it keeps the sacrificial question — which is the psalm’s own question — in the room from the start.

Governing image: a mouth shut, and a mouth opened

Worth carrying as the sermon’s spine, because it is the psalm’s own turn and it is concrete.

Psalm 50 ends by shutting a man’s mouth: “What right have you to tell of My statutes, and to take My covenant in your mouth?” (50:16). Psalm 51 is that man speaking — and at its hinge he asks for the one thing he cannot take back for himself: “O Lord, open my lips, that my mouth may declare Your praise” (v. 15). Same Hebrew word, peh, in both verses. Then the psalm’s stated outcome is teaching and singing (vv. 13–15).

The arc of the sermon is a man moving from a mouth God shut to a mouth God opens — and what stands between the two is the discovery that there is nothing he can bring, and the promise that God will not despise what God himself has broken.

Use it in the introduction (Psalm 50), at the hinge (v. 15), and in the conclusion. Don’t over-work it in the middle — movement 2 is about creation, not speech, and forcing the image there will strain it.

Proposed structure

Introduction — the page before. Psalm 50: God does not need your bulls; you have no right to say his covenant out loud; and there is none to deliver. Turn the page. (2–3 minutes. Read it or summarise it, but do not skip it — see Keep honest #4.)

1. Nothing to bring (vv. 1–6)

Function: surface the FCF and establish why this is not an ordinary confession.

    1. 1 — the ground of the whole prayer is named in the first line, and it is not the speaker: according to Your lovingkindness… Your compassion. He offers no reason of his own, here or anywhere.
  • vv. 1–2 — three sins met by three cleansings from three different worlds: the erased document, the laundered garment, the cleansed worshipper. Everything, by every means.
    1. 4 — “Against You, You only”. The courtroom, not the casualty list: he is not saying Uriah does not matter (see Keep honest #2) but that his judge is the one he offended, which is why he has no defence.
  • The background to lay here, not later: Nathan’s charge (2 Sam 12:9) quotes Num 15:31 word for word — he has despised the word of the LORD — the paragraph that provides no offering. This is the sermon’s foundation and it must go in early, or v. 16 will be heard as an aphorism.

2. Wash me, make me (vv. 7–13)

Function: establish the Teaching Point’s centre — that what he needs, only God can do.

    1. 7 — hyssop. Ten occurrences in the Old Testament, and this is the only figurative one. Nine of them are the leper, the leprous house, the corpse, and the Passover doorframe. He is asking to be treated as the man outside the camp, because that is the only category the law has that fits him.
    1. 8 — “the bones which You have broken.” God is the subject. Hold this; it returns in movement 3.
  • vv. 9–10 — the psalm’s centre. Create (bārāʾ) — the Psalter’s only use of the verb with a human heart as its object; every other use in the book is the making of the world. He is not asking to be improved. He is asking for Genesis 1 to happen inside him.
  • vv. 10–12 — three requests for a spirit: steadfast, holy, willing. Note the fear behind v. 11 (the Spirit departing from Saul, 1 Sam 16:14) without over-pressing it (see Keep honest #5).
    1. 13 — the first thing he promises to do with what God gives: teach transgressors. He does not graduate out of the category; he offers to teach it from the inside.

3. The offering he will not despise (vv. 14–19)

Function: land the Teaching Point and press the Purpose Statement.

    1. 15 — the hinge. The mouth Psalm 50 shut, opened by God. Note that the psalm’s one departure from calling God “God” falls exactly here: O Lord.
    1. 16 — the “For” is load-bearing. It grounds the previous petition: open my lips, because there is no offering for me. This is not a general theology of worship; it is a man stating his legal position accurately.
    1. 17 — the sermon’s destination. “You will not despise” — the verb of Numbers 15:31, the verb of Nathan’s indictment, now with God as subject and a broken heart as object. The one thing God will not despise is the one thing this man has, and God made it (v. 8).
  • Where Christ enters, and he should enter here rather than as an appendix: Hebrews 10:4 says what Psalm 51 discovered — it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins — and then quotes a psalm of the same family to say why Christ came. Hebrews 9:19 and 10:22 put the psalm’s own two images together: hyssop, sprinkling, and hearts sprinkled clean. What David asked for without knowing how it could be given, Christ has given.
  • vv. 18–19 — do not skip them, and do not apologise for them. The offerings come back. The psalm does not abolish sacrifice; it says there is none available for this, and that when God does good to Zion, worship works again. The last picture is not a forgiven man at peace but an altar back in use.

Close on the two things the psalm ends with: a mouth that has been given back its words, and a city God is asked to build.

Keep honest

1. Do not preach v. 17 as “God wants sincerity, not religion.” This is the sermon everyone preaches and it is not in the text. Two verses later the psalm restores burnt offerings, whole burnt offerings and bulls — the most expensive worship in the system — using the same vocabulary (ḥāpēṣ, zeḇaḥ, ʿōlâ). What is unavailable is unavailable for this sin. Preach vv. 16–19 as one run, or not at all.

2. Do not let “against You, You only” erase Uriah. The verse is about whose verdict stands, not about whether the harm was real. The psalm’s own vocabulary blocks the easy reading: bloodguiltiness at v. 14 is dāmîm, the legal term for liability for a death — a word that exists precisely because a killing cannot be settled privately between a man and his God. Say both.

3. Do not preach the brokenness as the goal. “God wants you broken” pressed on people already in pieces is a cruelty the psalm does not license. In v. 8 God is the one who broke the bones; in v. 17 God is the one who will not despise the result. The promise is about God’s reception, not about the value of being crushed. Preach the promise to the broken; do not preach the brokenness to the whole.

4. Do not preach it without Psalm 50. Seven verified verbal links, four of them reversals. Read on its own, Psalm 51 is a beautiful prayer of penitence. Read after Psalm 50 it is a defendant conceding a case the congregation has just watched God win — which is both truer and better.

5. Do not make v. 11 a sermon on losing your salvation — in either direction. “Take not Your Holy Spirit from me” is one of only three occurrences of that phrase in the Old Testament; the other two (Isa 63:10–11) are about God’s presence with Israel in the exodus, and the nearest narrative background is the Spirit departing from Saul — a matter of anointing for office. The honest pastoral line is neither “you could lose him” nor “he didn’t really mean it”, but: his fear was well-founded, he had watched it happen to his predecessor, and the reason we do not share it is that something has changed (Ezek 36:27; John 14:16).

6. Do not preach “repent like David.” The commonest moralism this passage invites, and the grammar refuses it: sixteen imperatives addressed to God across vv. 1–12, and exactly one first-person cohortative — let me teach — which is a consequence, not a contribution. The psalm’s weight is on the size of the request and the character of the one asked, not on the quality of the asker.

Translation decisions still to make

The pulpit text is undeclared. Three places where the choice changes what the congregation hears:

Verse What is at stake
v. 4 ESV reads “justified in your words / blameless in your judgment” — which is Romans 3:4 word for word, so the Paul link is audible from the pew without explanation. NASB95 reads “justified when You speak / blameless when You judge” — closer to the Hebrew infinitives, but the congregation will not hear the match unless you make it. Neither is wrong; the Greek the LXX supplies is what Paul’s argument needs. If you preach the ESV, the link is free. If you preach the NASB95 and want it, you must build it.
v. 16 NASB95 “You do not delight in sacrifice” reads as a standing truth about worship. ESV “you will not delight in sacrifice” reads as a statement about this case. The Hebrew imperfect allows either; the kî and the surrounding argument favour the narrower reading — which means the ESV here helps the sermon and the NASB95 makes Keep honest #1 harder work.
v. 18 rāṣôn (“favour”, “good pleasure”) is the same root as tirṣeh at v. 16 (“You are not pleased with burnt offering”). Both translations lose the link. The psalmist asks God to act in the very good pleasure he has just said is absent from burnt offerings. Worth restoring out loud, whichever text is in the pew.

Versification. The pulpit Bible will use English numbering (1–19). Where the exegesis is quoted in the sermon, use English numbers only — the MT’s 1–21 will confuse a congregation and gains them nothing.
