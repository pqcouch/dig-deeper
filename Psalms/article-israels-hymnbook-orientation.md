# Israel's Hymnbook?

*A Reassessment of a Modern Assumption about the Book of Psalms*

It is commonly asserted, often as though it were self-evident, that the
book of Psalms was "the hymnbook of Israel." The designation appears in
popular preaching, in the introductions to study Bibles, and in
textbooks of Old Testament theology. Yet the claim rests on assumptions
that the biblical text does not obviously bear, and it carries a history
that is more recent, and more critical in provenance, than its
conservative-sounding pedigree would suggest.

This article examines the hypothesis on two fronts. It asks, first, what
the biblical-textual evidence actually attests; and, second, where the
designation itself came from. It argues that the label "hymnbook of
Israel," when applied to the pre-exilic or monarchic period, is
anachronistic; that the biblical evidence attests the liturgical use of
individual psalms rather than the existence of a standardised songbook;
and that, by a considerable irony, the reduction of the Psalter to a
collection of discrete liturgical pieces was entrenched by form
criticism rather than inherited from the older reading it is often
supposed to preserve.

## A distinction that must be kept

Three claims are regularly run together in this discussion, and the
argument below affirms one of them while denying the others. They are:

\(a\) that the psalms were used in Israel's worship;

\(b\) that a bounded collection --- the Psalter as we now have it ---
existed and functioned as the nation's songbook during the monarchy;

\(c\) that the book of Psalms is, in its purpose and design, a hymnbook.

The first is not in dispute. The biblical evidence for it is good, and
nothing that follows is intended to deny it. What is disputed is (b) and
(c). To show that Israel sang psalms is not to show that Israel
possessed the Psalter, still less that the Psalter was compiled in order
to be sung. Much of the confusion here arises from treating the three as
a single claim, so that a denial of (c) is heard as a denial of (a).

## 1. The biblical evidence examined

The strongest textual support for organised, psalm-centred worship comes
from the Chronicler. David is said to have appointed musicians --- the
sons of Asaph, Heman, and Jeduthun --- set apart "to prophesy with
lyres, harps, and cymbals," those under Jeduthun's direction prophesying
"in giving thanks and praising the LORD" (1 Chron. 25:1--7). Four
thousand Levites were appointed to praise the LORD with instruments that
David had made for the purpose (1 Chron. 23:5). David appointed singers
to minister "with song before the tabernacle of the tent of meeting"
until Solomon built the temple (1 Chron. 6:31--32). Later, Hezekiah
commanded the Levites to sing praises to the LORD "with the words of
David and Asaph the seer" (2 Chron. 29:25--30), which shows that psalmic
compositions were already being employed as liturgical material during
the monarchy.

This is genuine evidence --- but of what? It attests an organised guild
of temple singers and the liturgical performance of Davidic and Asaphite
compositions. It does not attest a single, bounded, standardised
collection --- a "Psalter" --- functioning as the authorised songbook of
the nation. The historical books do indeed show various psalms employed
at key junctures in pre-exilic Israelite history; but individual psalms
embedded in narrative is precisely what we find, and precisely what the
"hymnbook" thesis must transcend. David's song in 2 Samuel 22 parallels
Psalm 18, and portions of Psalms 105, 96, and 106 appear in the
Chronicler's account of the ark's arrival (1 Chron. 16). These show that
some psalms were in circulation and were used liturgically --- not that
they comprised a fixed corpus already known and used as "the Psalter."

A second line of supposed evidence is weaker, though the point must be
put carefully. The Psalms themselves contain explicit calls to communal
singing: Psalm 95 invites, "O come, let us sing for joy to the LORD";
Psalm 149 summons the congregation to "sing to the LORD a new song";
Psalm 150 directs praise "in his sanctuary" with trumpets, harps, lyres,
timbrels, strings, pipes, and cymbals. Such texts are real evidence
about how those particular psalms present themselves, and it would be
perverse to deny that they envisage corporate praise. What they cannot
establish is the further claim --- that the collection which contains
them was the book from which that praise was sung. To cite the Psalter's
own calls to worship as evidence that the Psalter was Israel's hymnbook
is to reason in a circle. Nowhere does the Old Testament state, or
plainly imply, that the 150 psalms as a collection functioned as the
nation's standard book of praise.

Two further pieces of evidence deserve more attention than they usually
receive, since they bear directly on the question of the collection
rather than of the pieces.

The first is the colophon at Psalm 72:20: "The prayers of David, the son
of Jesse, are ended." This is not the language of a hymnal. It is the
language of an editor closing a corpus --- and it survives, awkwardly,
in a book that goes on to attribute a further twenty psalms to David.
Its presence tells us that bounded sub-collections existed early and
were preserved even when a later arrangement overtook them. That is
editorial evidence, and it tells for the reading defended below rather
than against it.

The second cuts the other way and must be faced squarely. In 1
Chronicles 16 the Chronicler's ark psalm concludes with the doxology
"Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to
everlasting!" followed by "Then all the people said, 'Amen!' and praised
the LORD" (16:36). That doxology is the closing formula of Book Four of
the Psalter (Ps. 106:48), and the "Amen" belongs with it. If the
Chronicler is quoting a psalm together with its book-ending doxology,
then some form of the five-book division was known to him --- which
would place the shaping of the collection earlier than the argument of
the next section allows.

The objection is a real one, and no honest treatment should suppress it.
Three responses are available, and they are not mutually exclusive. The
doxologies may have been standing liturgical formulae before they were
pressed into service as editorial seams. The Chronicler may be quoting a
source that already ended in this way, without implying anything about
the arrangement of the Psalter. Or the Chronicler may indeed attest an
early form of Book Four, in which case the dating below requires
adjustment while the substance of the argument stands unaffected. What
may not be done is to cite 1 Chronicles 16 as evidence for a hymnbook.
On even the strongest reading it is evidence for a *book*.

## 2. "The hymnbook of the Second Temple": the anachronism

Where the "hymnbook" description does find purchase, it points not to
the monarchy but to the post-exilic period. The Psalter has long been
described as "the hymnal of the second temple". Motyer notes that it is
"sometimes called 'The Hymnbook of the Second Temple,'" and that it was
"probably given its present division into five 'books'" during that
later period.[^1] The point is not merely lexical. Some psalms "can
hardly have originated before the exile" --- Psalm 137 is the plainest
case --- and investigations into early post-exilic history make a
collection completed before Ezra unlikely. The retrospective label
"hymnbook of Israel," in other words, describes a book that did not yet
exist in its canonical form during the very period --- the monarchy ---
to which the designation is most often, and least defensibly, applied.

The manuscript evidence sharpens the point. The Great Psalms Scroll from
Qumran Cave 11 (11QPsa), copied in the first century AD, contains the
material of Psalms 101--150 in an order markedly different from the
received text, interspersed with compositions absent from the canonical
book.[^2] Whether this represents a genuine variant edition of the
Psalter, as its editor argued, or a secondary liturgical selection drawn
from an already-fixed collection, as others have maintained, remains
contested.[^3] Either way it shows that the arrangement of the later
books was not universally settled at a date long after the Persian
period. Any argument about when the Psalter "existed" must reckon with
it.

The scroll is worth citing for a second reason. It contains a prose
insert, "David's Compositions," crediting David with 3,600 psalms and a
further 450 songs for the daily, sabbath, festival and other offerings.
This is among the earliest surviving statements of how the psalms were
regarded --- and it is striking that it presents David as a prolific
composer of liturgical song without presenting the book of Psalms as the
collection in which that song was gathered.

## 3. The irony: "hymnbook" as a modern construct

The most striking feature of the debate is easily missed. The hymnbook
approach to the Psalter, though frequently regarded as conservative, was
constructed and entrenched by critical scholarship of the late
nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

A distinction is needed here, and it strengthens rather than weakens the
point. The description of the Psalter as the hymnbook of the second
temple congregation belongs to the Wellhausen era and predates the form
critics; it arose from a reconstruction in which the psalms were the
devotional deposit of post-exilic Judaism rather than the poetry of the
monarchy.[^4] What Hermann Gunkel and Sigmund Mowinckel contributed was
not the label but the method that made it irresistible.

Nor did the two men mean the same thing by it, and they are too often
treated as one voice. Gunkel, whose commentary *Die Psalmen* appeared in
1926 and whose *Einleitung* was completed after his death by Joachim
Begrich, classified the psalms by literary type, each type correlated
with a setting in life.[^5] But he held that many of the psalms as we
have them are literary and spiritualised imitations of cultic forms,
detached from actual temple worship --- private poetry in a public
idiom. Mowinckel, in the *Psalmenstudien* of 1921--24 and later in *The
Psalms in Israel's Worship*, pressed in the opposite direction, treating
the psalms as the working liturgical texts of the cult and
reconstructing from them an annual enthronement festival for which there
is no independent evidence.[^6] The popular "hymnbook of Israel" is thus
a fusion that neither scholar would recognise: Gunkel's atomising
instinct wedded to Mowinckel's cultic maximalism, and both transposed
backwards onto the age of David.

The consequences for method were far-reaching. These scholars discarded
the canonical order of the 150 psalms in favour of attention to
individual pieces, and relegated the superscriptions to the status of
late, non-canonical labels --- even though the superscriptions stand in
all the ancient manuscripts and translations and had been treated as
Scripture by Christian interpreters before Gunkel's day. The abandonment
of canonical order and of the titles created a vacuum that required a
new interpretative framework, and form criticism supplied it: Gunkel
identified patterns among the psalms, then classified, arranged, and
interpreted them by their forms, each form tied to a setting in Israel's
worship. The "hymnbook" conception --- the Psalter as a repertoire of
discrete liturgical pieces, extracted from any editorial context --- was
thus not the deposit of ancient tradition but the very framework that
displaced the traditional reading.

## 4. What preceded the critics

If the hymnbook thesis owes this much to modern criticism, what was read
before it? The pre-critical landscape operated on different assumptions,
though not identical to the canonical reading later recovered. Older
commentary tended to treat the psalms as expressions of individual
religious life. One influential nineteenth-century commentator regarded
them as "composed or compiled for the expression of individual religious
life ... never for liturgical use, however naturally appropriated to
such use in later times".[^7]

More significant for the present argument are the older observations
about arrangement. Delitzsch drew sustained attention to the
chain-linking of adjacent psalms by shared catchwords and motifs, a
phenomenon he treated as evidence of deliberate ordering rather than
accident.[^8] Hengstenberg noticed that certain psalms form trilogies or
series, each "complete in itself, and yet designed to be connected with
the others and interpreted by them".[^9] Such observations reached to
thematic resonance between neighbouring poems, and not yet to a
sustained account of the Psalter's architecture as a whole. But they
show that the instinct to read the collection as an arranged whole is
considerably older than the criticism which suppressed it. The older
tradition did not read the collection as a national hymnbook; neither
had it fully developed the editorial-unity reading. Its energy flowed
toward individual psalms and their spiritual lessons.

## 5. The recovery: the canonical shape and editorial unity

A second revolution in twentieth-century Psalms study reversed the
fragmenting priority. The canonical approach, initiated by Gerald
Wilson's *The Editing of the Hebrew Psalter* (1985), reoriented the
discipline.[^10] Where form criticism had encouraged the comparison of
psalms of the same genre rather than the exploration of relationships
between neighbours, the canonical approach inverted the question. Erich
Zenger set out its principles: attend to the connections between a psalm
and those beside it; examine its position within its redactional unit;
treat the titles as an interpretative horizon; and weigh the connections
and repetitions across the collection.[^11] Wilson himself, drawing on
ancient Near Eastern hymnic collections, identified the marks of
editorial activity: the five-book division, the doxologies that close
each book, the framing of the whole by Psalms 1--2 and 146--150, the
grouping of psalms by attributed author, and the placing of royal psalms
at the structural seams.

The resulting reading restores a theological architecture. Psalm 1
functions as an introduction to the whole collection, while Psalm 2
introduces the first book. Royal psalms concerning the Davidic covenant
stand at the seams of the first three books --- affirmed in Books 1 and
2, but lamented in Psalm 89, where God appears not to be keeping his
promises --- and Book 4 answers the crisis by declaring that "the LORD
reigns," reassuring Israel that God remains their refuge. So arranged,
the canonical shape drives the reader toward a messianic interpretation,
the Davidic psalms placed later in the post-exilic collection suggesting
that the psalms embody Israel's future hope rather than merely
reflecting its past experience.

Brevard Childs had already discerned the deeper transformation: the
Psalter's movement from a cultic role to a wisdom book for guiding the
godly into blessing, with Psalm 1 orienting the reader to receive the
prayers that follow as Scripture.[^12] On this reading, each psalm asks
to be interpreted both within its immediate literary context and within
the context of the whole book. What emerges is not a miscellany of
liturgical pieces but a deliberately shaped book, moving from the
law-meditating righteous of Psalm 1, through complaint and lament, to
the cosmic crescendo of Psalms 146--150, in which everything that has
breath praises the LORD for what he has purposed and accomplished in and
through his anointed King.

## 6. What is at stake

The question is not merely one of labels. What the hymnbook conception
costs is the argument of the book.

If the Psalter is a repertoire, Psalm 89 is simply a lament --- one
liturgical piece among others, to be reached for when a lament is
wanted. If the Psalter is a book, Psalm 89 is a crisis placed
deliberately at the close of Book 3, in which the covenant of 2 Samuel
7, celebrated in Psalm 2 and rehearsed in Psalm 72, is confronted with
the evidence of history: "Lord, where is your steadfast love of old,
which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" (89:49). The answer
given is not a retraction of the promise but a relocation of the throne:
"The LORD reigns" (93:1; 96:10; 97:1; 99:1). Book 5 then returns to
David --- but to a David who has become the subject of hope rather than
the object of memory, and the movement runs on to a priest-king after
the order of Melchizedek (110:4) and a rejected stone become the
cornerstone (118:22).

Read as a hymnbook, that is a sequence of moods. Read as a book, it is
an argument: the promise appeared to fail; God reigns nonetheless; the
promise therefore lies ahead of Israel and not behind. It is an act of
hope made under post-exilic conditions, and it is why the apostles could
treat the Psalter as prophecy rather than as anthology (Acts 2:25--36;
4:25--26; Heb. 1:5--13).

The label matters, in short, because it determines whether the book is
permitted to say anything at all.

## Conclusion

The claim that the Psalter was "Israel's hymnbook" cannot be sustained
as a description of the monarchic period. The biblical evidence attests
an organised guild of temple singers and the liturgical use of
individual psalms; it does not attest a bounded, standardised collection
functioning as a national songbook, and the psalms' own summons to
praise cannot, without circularity, be pressed into service as proof
that the Psalter was that book. In so far as the "hymnbook" description
fits at all, it fits the Second Temple and not the age of David --- and
even there the manuscript evidence shows an arrangement still unsettled
long afterwards.

More striking still, the reduction of the Psalter to a repertoire of
discrete liturgical genres is not the inheritance of ancient tradition
but the achievement of modern criticism. The reading that honours the
book's deliberate shape --- its canonical order, its superscriptions,
its five-book structure, its royal seams, and its doxological crescendo
--- is at once older than the "hymnbook" and, in its recent canonical
recovery, better grounded in the arrangement of the book itself.
