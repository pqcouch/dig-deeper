## What this document is

This is the whole-book planning layer for Lamentations. In v1.0 it is no longer only a plan: the passage digs have tested it, the audits have tested the digs, and what follows is what survived. It is still meant to be revised.

Three features of the book shape everything below, and they are stated here so that no later section quietly forgets them.

1. **The form is the first datum.** Four of the five poems are alphabetic acrostics, and the fifth has exactly twenty-two verses but no acrostic. Nothing in the book is more certain than its form, and an overview that treats the form as decoration has missed the first thing the text says. `[T]`
2. **God never speaks — with one exception — and is said to hear only twice.** The whole book is addressed *about* God or *to* God. The only words of God quoted in it are two words remembered in the past tense: אַל־תִּירָא ("Do not fear!", 3:57). The verb שׁמע ("hear") has God as subject only at 3:56 and 3:61; elsewhere it is the peoples and the enemies who hear (1:18, 21). `[T]` (WLC, every occurrence read)
3. **The book does not resolve.** It ends on כִּי אִם־מָאֹס מְאַסְתָּנוּ ("unless You have utterly rejected us", 5:22). Any reading that makes 3:22–24, or 4:22, the book's last word has to explain why the book itself does not end there. `[T]`

**What the sweep, the digs and the audits taught.** Seven audits agree on one pattern, and this version is built around it.

- **Single exact contacts are where the evidence is.** Short exclusive phrases, and pairs of rare words that the Greek also keeps, held in audit after audit.
- **Designs and fields mostly fell.** Every claimed programme — the concentric frame, the "planted answers", the four-psalm dialogue, the seven-root Isa 54 match, Lam 5 praying Jer 31 "in order", the David–Saul field, the prophetic "end" field — came down to *low* or *low–moderate* once a chance baseline was run.
- **Pairs of ordinary words are exclusive by chance about one time in five** (one in six in poem 4) `[S: audit 5; audit 6]`. An "only these two verses" claim for common words is reported here with its partner's rank, its sense or the Greek, or it is not reported.
- **Partners turned out to be particular to poems.** Ps 89 belongs to poem 2, Ps 31 and Ps 143 to poem 1, the Song of Moses, Isa 30 and Zeph 3 to poem 4, Isa 64 and Jer 31 to poem 5. Jer 14 alone runs through the whole book. That observation is itself untested as a design (see the Colophon).

Every change from v0.2.1 is listed in *What Changed in v1.0*, near the end.

The Talmud's order (*B. Bat.* 14b) is different again `[S]`. |

**One further canonical fact, worth holding in view throughout.** In the BHS order **Job stands five books earlier** in the same section.

- **Exclusive images.** Job shares with Lamentations a run of images found nowhere else: the target and the kidneys (Job 16:12–13; Lam 3:12–13); the taunt-song (Job 30:9; Lam 3:14); cheek and reproach (Job 16:10; Lam 3:30); the crown of the head (Job 19:9; Lam 5:16); the inner parts poured out to the ground — Job's gall, Zion's liver (Job 16:13; Lam 2:11) `[S: audit; audit 4]`.
- **Wider contacts.** Job 19:6–8 is the joint-densest window in the Hebrew Bible against Lam 3:1–9, *moderate–high* `[S: audit 2]`; Job 28:16, 19 alone share the construction "weighed against fine gold" with Lam 4:2 `[S: audit 6]`; Job 30:30 has skin, bones and blackening with Lam 4:8 `[S: dig]`; and "does God pervert justice?" (Job 8:3; 19:6; 34:12) is the question Lam 3:35–36 asks — a shared question, *moderate*, not a debate demonstrably entered `[S: audit 6]`.
- **Not the frame.** Lam 3 has equally exclusive contacts with Jer 20:7, Isa 50:6 and Pss 77, 88 and 143, and by density Job is a mid-band partner. **Lam 3 is a mosaic.** `[S: audit]`
- **Uz.** Lam 4:21 names "the land of Uz" (עוּץ, "Uz"), otherwise Job's homeland (Job 1:1) and an Edomite clan (Gen 36:28). It is better read with Jer 25:20, the only other verse where cup, Uz and Edom meet `[S: audit 6]`. **Neither Greek edition has Uz at 4:21** (Swete; the Rahlfs export; BHS "> 𝔊*") `[T]`.

## Presenting Situation

**Author.** The Hebrew book is **anonymous**. It has no superscription and never names Jeremiah or any other author. `[T]` — verified: the name יִרְמְיָהוּ ("Jeremiah") does not occur in Lamentations, with a positive control on the same search returning 117 verses of Jeremiah. The attribution to Jeremiah comes from the **Greek prologue** to 1:1 in Swete (Vaticanus): "after Israel was taken captive and Jerusalem laid waste, Jeremiah sat weeping and lamented this lament over Jerusalem". `[T]` *for Swete*; `[S]` *as authorship*. The Talmud (*B. Bat.* 15a) and the older defenders of Jeremianic authorship (Keil and Delitzsch; M'Clintock and Strong) follow it `[S; Logos 2]`; 2 Chr 35:25 says that Jeremiah lamented Josiah and that the laments "are written in the Laments" (הַקִּינוֹת, "the laments") — a statement about laments for Josiah, not about this book. `[T]` *for what Chronicles says*; `[S]` *for the identification*. The NASB95 export is titled "The Lamentations of Jeremiah" and heads chapter 3 "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction"; both are editorial. `[T]` *for the export*.

**Lamentations and Jeremiah's language.** Jer 14 is the book's densest partner in the canon (see the map). Whether the poet knew Jeremiah's words, or both drew on one language of grief, has **no consensus** in the commentaries Patrick owns; the majority view is that **Lamentations knows Jeremiah's language** (Lalleman; Salters; most clearly Schnittjer, *Old Testament Use of Old Testament* 610, on Jer 14:19 and Lam 5:22: "the poet of Lamentations had an eye on the context of Jeremiah 14"). Driver notes the same temperament but a different standpoint — the hope of Egyptian help at 4:17, which Jeremiah opposed. `[S: Logos 2; Logos 3]` A preacher may say "the poet seems to have known Jeremiah's words"; not "Lamentations quotes Jer 14", and not "Jeremiah wrote it".

**Voices within the book.** The book speaks in several voices, and identifying them is half of reading it: `[T]`

- a **narrator** who describes Zion in the third person (1:1–11b; 1:17; 2:1–12; 4:1–16);
- **Zion herself**, personified as a woman, speaking in the first person (1:9c, 11c–16, 18–22; 2:20–22);
- the narrator **turning to address Zion** in the second person (2:13–19), in the poem's only run of imperatives (2:18–19);
- **the man**, הַגֶּבֶר ("the man", 3:1), speaking as "I" (3:1–39, 48–66);
- a **community "we"** (3:40–47; 4:17–20; the whole of ch. 5). On the Hebrew text the first "we" of ch. 3 is earlier, at 3:22, כִּי לֹא־תָמְנוּ ("for we are not consumed"); both English versions follow an emendation and lose it `[S: dig]`;
- an **address in oracle form** to Edom and to Zion (4:21–22) — not the narrator's description but a pronouncement in the second person `[S: dig]`.

**Poem 1 has two halves of eleven verses**: the narrator speaks of "her" (1:1–11), Zion of "me" (1:12–22), and the comforter refrain moves with them — three times "for her" (1:2, 9, 17), once "for me" (1:21) `[S: dig]`.

**Occasion.** The fall of Jerusalem: siege and famine (2:11–12, 19–20; 4:3–10; 5:9–10), the breach and burning (2:3–9; 4:11–12), the temple violated and the feasts ended (1:4, 10; 2:6–7), the king captured in flight (4:19–20), exile (1:3, 5, 18; 4:22), and Edom's gloating (4:21–22). `[T]` That this is the Babylonian destruction of 587/586 BC is a **near-certain inference, not a statement**: **the book never names Babylon, the Chaldeans or Nebuchadnezzar** (all three verified absent, each with a positive control in Jeremiah). The only nations it names are Egypt and Assyria (5:6) and Edom (4:21–22). `[T]` for the absence; `[I]` for the date, *confidence high*. The hope in "a nation that could not save" (4:17) fits Pharaoh's army of Jer 37:5–11, but Lam 4:17 names neither Pharaoh nor Egypt: the tie identifies a likely event, not a borrowed text `[S: audit 6]`. **The silence about Babylon is theological before it is historical: the agent the book names is the LORD.** `[I]` Poem 2 makes the LORD the subject of thirty finite verbs in eight verses (2:1–8), and the enemies boast "We have swallowed her up!" (2:16) of what the poem has already said the LORD swallowed (2:2, 5, 8) `[S: dig]`.

**Date of composition.** Unknown. The vividness of chs 2 and 4 and the land-bound "we" of ch. 5 (5:2–18, the people under foreign servants in their own land) fit a date soon after the fall; nothing in the book requires the return from exile, and 5:20–22 assumes none. `[I]`, *confidence moderate*. A date between 586 and c. 520 is the usual range `[S]`.

**Audiences.**

- *Narrative world:* the survivors — in the land (ch. 5) and in exile (1:3; 4:15, 20). `[T]`
- *Compositional:* a worshipping community that needed words for the catastrophe. That the poems were used in public mourning is likely (cf. Zech 7:3–5; 8:19, fasts in the fifth month) `[I]`, *moderate*; the later reading at the Ninth of Av is `[S]`.
- *Canonical:* the reader of the Writings, who arrives with the Torah's curses, the Prophets' warnings and the Psalter's laments in the ear. **This is the audience this overview reads for.** `[I]`

**Pressure.** How can the covenant people speak to the God who has become their enemy? `[I]` The book holds three things together and will not let any of them go:

1. **The LORD has done this.** "The Lord has become like an enemy" (2:5); "The LORD has done what He purposed" (2:17). `[T]`
2. **The LORD is right to have done it.** "The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against His command" (1:18); "We have transgressed and rebelled" (3:42); "Woe to us, for we have sinned!" (5:16). `[T]`
3. **What has been done is beyond bearing, and he must be told so.** "See, O LORD, and look! With whom have You dealt thus? Should women eat their offspring?" (2:20). `[T]`

Poem 1 shows the three held in one mouth: Zion, who confesses "The LORD is righteous" (1:18), also asks "is there any pain like my pain?" (1:12) and prays "let all their wickedness come before You" (1:22). It is the book's poem of reasons — fourteen of the book's twenty-nine uses of כִּי ("for, because") `[S: dig]`.

**Purpose.** To give a ruined people ordered words — confession, protest, memory and petition — with which to keep addressing the LORD when he is silent, because his חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") is not exhausted and his throne endures (3:22; 5:19). `[I]`

## The Greek Text of Lamentations

The Greek differs from the Hebrew at points that matter for preaching, and they are gathered here once. **Swete** is the observation layer (Vaticanus, diplomatic); the **Rahlfs** export is cited where they differ.

1. **The prologue naming Jeremiah** stands at 1:1 in Swete. **It is not in the Rahlfs export as exported**, which begins directly at Πῶς ἐκάθισεν ("How she sat…"). Whether Rahlfs prints it as a heading the export did not carry should be **checked on screen in Logos** before anything is made to rest on it. `[T]` for both files. *Still owed.*
2. **Lam 3:22–24 is absent from the Old Greek — and so is 3:29.** Swete has no text at 3:22, 23, 24 or 29, and the Rahlfs export runs 3:21 → 3:25 and 3:28 → 3:30. BHS's apparatus agrees: "v 22–24 > 𝔊*" and "v 29 > 𝔊ᴹˢˢ". `[T]` **The most-quoted verses of the book — "Great is Your faithfulness" — are not in the earliest Greek.** This is not evidence against the Hebrew: the ח stanza is required by the acrostic, so its loss is a loss *from* the Greek, not an addition to the Hebrew. `[I]`, *confidence high*. Hexaplaric manuscripts supply the verses `[S]`. **The preacher should know it; the congregation does not need to.**
3. **Letter names as headings.** Swete prints the Hebrew letter names before the verses (Ἄλεφ, Βήθ, Γίμελ…). Its labels run in standard alphabetical order, so in chs 2 and 4 the label Ἄιν (ayin) stands on the Hebrew **פ** verse (2:16; 4:16) and Φή (pe) on the **ע** verse (2:17; 4:17). The Greek labels "correct" an order the Hebrew deliberately keeps (see *Structural Arc Map*). Swete also re-divides 4:17–19 `[S: dig]`. `[T]`
4. **Lam 4:20.** The Hebrew מְשִׁיחַ יְהוָה ("the LORD's anointed") becomes χριστὸς κυρίου ("the Lord's anointed") in the Rahlfs export, and χριστὸς Κύριος in Swete — which can be read as "Christ the Lord". The patristic Christological reading of this verse rests on the Greek `[S]`. In the Hebrew the anointed is the Davidic king — in context, Zedekiah, captured in flight (2 Kgs 25:5–6). `[I]`, *confidence high*. See *Preaching Traps*.
5. **The last verse.** Where the Hebrew has the ambiguous כִּי אִם ("unless" / "but rather" / "even if"), the Greek reads ὅτι ἀπωθούμενος ἀπώσω ἡμᾶς ("for You have utterly rejected us"): a plain statement, with no "unless". `[T]` BHS notes that אִם ("if") is absent in 𝔊 and the Syriac at 5:22, leaving a plain כִּי ("for") — which is what the Greek translates `[T]`.
6. **The comforter.** At all six places where the Hebrew has the root נחם ("comfort") — 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21; 2:13 — Swete has the verb παρακαλέω ("comfort"). Isa 40:1 opens with the same verb twice (Παρακαλεῖτε παρακαλεῖτε, "Comfort, comfort"). `[T]` This word-family runs through to Matt 5:4, Luke 2:25 and John 14:16; see *Christological Trajectory*.
7. **Lam 2:13 reads a cup.** Where the Hebrew has כִּי־גָדוֹל כַּיָּם שִׁבְרֵךְ ("for your ruin is as vast as the sea"), the Greek has ὅτι ἐμεγαλύνθη ποτήριον συντριβῆς σου ("for the **cup** of your ruin has been made great"), and earlier in the verse τίς σώσει καὶ παρακαλέσει σε ("who will save and comfort you?"). BHS identifies the translator's reading as כּוֹס ("cup") and מִי יוֹשִׁיעַ לָךְ וְנִחַמְךָ ("who will save you and comfort you"). In Greek, then, 2:13 holds all three elements of Isa 51:17–19: the cup, the ruin, and "who will comfort you?". A substantive Greek reading: weigh it; do not default. The Hebrew stands as the text. `[T]` for Swete and the BHS note; `[S: audit]`; `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.
8. **Lam 4:16, "elders" or "prophets".** The Rahlfs export reads πρεσβύτας ("elders"), as the Hebrew does. Swete (Vaticanus) reads προφήτας ("prophets"), which loses the book's sharpest contact with Deut 28:50. `[T]`
9. **Poem 4 in the two editions** `[S: audit 6]`. Both make 4:1's fine gold "silver" (ἀργύριον), so the gold pair of 4:1–2 is lost. At 4:7 Rahlfs reads ἐπυρρώθησαν ("were reddened"), keeping the Song's colour word, where Swete reads ἐπυρώθησαν ("were refined"). Both read "watchmen" (ἐγρήγοροι) at 4:14 where the Hebrew has "blind", so every "blind" contact of 4:14 exists only in the Hebrew. `[T]`
10. **3:54 and Jonah.** The Greek of 3:54, εἶπα Ἀπῶσμαι ("I said, I am thrust away"), is word for word Jonah 2:5. The verb renders גרשׁ ("drive away") in Jonah and גזר ("cut off") in Lamentations, so the identity is **a translation choice, not a sign of shared Hebrew** `[S: audit 7]`. The Hebrew formula itself is in the map (Jonah 2:5; Ps 31:23). `[T]`
11. **3:55–61 in the past.** Both editions render the perfects of 3:55–61 as aorists, and 3:59 as "You judged". Rahlfs prints 3:56b as a quotation; Swete does not `[S: audit 6]`. See *Pulpit Notes* on 3:56. `[T]`
12. **4:17 and 4:22 share a Greek verb.** ἐξέλιπον ("our eyes failed", 4:17) and ἐξέλιπεν ("your iniquity has failed", i.e. ended, 4:22) bracket the passage in Greek only; the Hebrew has two different verbs (כלה, תמם) `[S: dig]`. And at 4:21 the Greek adds "of the Lord" to the cup (τὸ ποτήριον Κυρίου), a plus BHS records, which makes 4:21 resemble Hab 2:16. `[T]`

## Structural Arc Map

### The form, counted

| Poem | Verses | Acrostic | Words (WLC) | Words per verse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 22 | one verse per letter; **ע before פ** | 376 | 17.1 |
| 2 | 22 | one verse per letter; **פ before ע** | 381 | 17.3 |
| 3 | 66 | **each letter heads three verses**; פ before ע | 381 | 5.8 |
| 4 | 22 | one verse per letter; פ before ע | 259 | 11.8 |
| 5 | 22 | **none** — 22 verses only | 145 | 6.6 |

Counts are of words as tokenised in the WLC lemma index (counted as space-separated words the figures are 330 / 333 / 347 / 231 / 136, and the shape is the same); the opening letters were read off every verse of the WLC reading text. `[T]`

Three things follow from the table.

- **The first three poems are the same length** (376, 381, 381 words), **and the last two shrink** (259, then 145). The book's volume falls away after its centre. `[T]` That the diminution enacts exhaustion is a reading `[I]`, *moderate*.
- **Chapter 3 intensifies the form** — every letter three times — at the point where the book's theology is densest. `[T]`/`[I]`
- **Chapter 5 keeps the number and abandons the alphabet.** The prayer is still "complete" in count, but the order has gone. `[T]` for the fact; the meaning is contested — collapse of order, or a deliberate turn from poem to plain prayer `[I]`, *open*.

**The last letter.** Every acrostic ends with a ת verse, and only poem 4's begins with a word meaning "complete": תַּם ("it is finished, complete", 4:22). Poem 1's begins תָּבֹא ("let it come"), poem 2's תִּקְרָא ("You called"), poem 3's three ת verses תָּשִׁיב, תִּתֵּן, תִּרְדֹּף ("You will repay … give … pursue"). `[T]`; that the alphabet's last letter is made to say "complete" is a reading `[I]`, *moderate* `[S: dig]`.

**The פ/ע order.** In ch. 1 the alphabet runs in its standard order (ע at 1:16, פ at 1:17). In chs 2, 3 and 4 the order is פ then ע (2:16–17; 3:46–51; 4:16–17). `[T]` BHS records a few manuscripts, the Lucianic Greek and the Syriac that transpose to the standard order in all three chapters `[T for BHS]`, so **the reversal is the Leningrad and majority reading, not a universal one**. Another alphabetic order with פ before ע is attested in early Hebrew abecedaries, and the Qumran manuscript 4QLam reportedly has פ before ע in ch. 1 as well `[S]`. *No sermon should rest on the reversal.*

**Two four-line verses.** BHS marks a line as "probably added" at 1:7 ("all her precious things that were from days of old") and as "added" at 2:19 ("who faint for hunger at the head of every street"), because each verse otherwise has three lines. **No manuscript or version omits either line**; both stand in the Greek. These are the BHS editor's metrical conjectures, not variants — preach both lines `[S: Logos 1]`.

**The centre of the book.** Lamentations has 154 verses (WLC), so the arithmetic midpoint falls **between verse 77 (3:33) and verse 78 (3:34)**. It is also the midpoint of ch. 3. The stanza that ends the first half is the כ stanza (3:31–33), and every line of it begins with כִּי ("for"): `[T]`

> "For the Lord will not reject forever, / For if He causes grief, Then He will have compassion According to His abundant lovingkindness. / For He does not afflict willingly Or grieve the sons of men." (NASB95)

**The centre is a verse-count fact.** Counted by words in the WLC index, the midpoint falls at 3:3, so state the unit when citing it. `[S: audit]`

**The usual claim that 3:22–23 is the book's centre is close but not exact; the centre is 3:31–33.** The two stanzas belong together — 3:22 and 3:32 share חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") and the root רחם ("compassion"). They are the **only two places** in the book where חֶסֶד occurs. `[T]` The order is reversed (חֶסֶד … רַחֲמִים at 3:22; רחם … חֶסֶד at 3:32), but the pair occurs in either order about equally often elsewhere, so it is **a frame, not a designed chiasm** `[S: audit 3]`. And the centre answers poem 1: "though He causes grief (הוֹגָה), He will have compassion according to the multitude (כְּרֹב) of His lovingkindness" (3:32) takes up "the LORD has caused her grief (הוֹגָהּ) for the multitude (רֹב) of her transgressions" (1:5) — the only two verses in the Hebrew Bible with both words `[S: audit 2]`. Poem 1's vocabulary clusters in 3:19–33, the turn and the centre, above chance (p ≈ 0.02–0.10) `[S: audit 2]`.

## Microscript

*Jerusalem has fallen, and the LORD did it. Lamentations gives the ruined city ordered words — alphabetical, but ending unresolved — with which to admit that he was right, to protest that the suffering is past bearing, and to keep asking him to see, remember and restore. Its ground for doing so is that his חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") has not been used up and his throne has not fallen.*

**Keynote:** אֵין מְנַחֵם ("there is none to comfort") — **four times** in ch. 1 (1:2, 9, 17, 21), with "far from me is a comforter" (1:16) as a fifth use of מְנַחֵם ("comforter"), and the narrator's helpless "that I may comfort you" (2:13) as the sixth and last use of the root נחם ("comfort") in the book. v0.2.1 counted the phrase five times; the phrase is four, the root in poem 1 five, in the book six `[S: dig; audit 5]`. **Counter-note:** חַסְדֵי יְהוָה ("the LORD's lovingkindnesses", 3:22) and "You, O LORD, rule forever" (5:19; לְעוֹלָם תֵּשֵׁב, "forever You sit enthroned"). **The God who could comfort never speaks, except for "Do not fear!" — remembered (3:57) — and is said to hear only at 3:56 and 3:61.** `[T]`

**The movement of the voices.** The book moves from "she" to "I" to "we": the narrator's Zion (poem 1), Zion's own voice (1:12–22; 2:20–22), the man (poem 3), and the community, whose "we" begins — on the Hebrew text — at 3:22 and takes the whole of poem 5 `[S: sweep; dig]`. `[T]`/`[I]`

**Test against the movements.**

- *Ch. 1:* no comforter; the LORD's day of anger; confession and protest in one mouth. ✓
- *Ch. 2:* the LORD himself as the destroyer — the hardest form of "he did it" — and a word kept, not a city abandoned to chance (2:17). ✓
- *Ch. 3:* the ground of hope — חֶסֶד — found inside affliction, not after it; a hearing remembered (3:55–57); pardon not yet given (3:42). ✓
- *Ch. 4:* the term of punishment declared complete (4:22), the enemy's turn to come — **declared, not seen: poem 5 still asks**. ✓
- *Ch. 5:* asking to be restored, with rejection still possible. ✓

## Christological Trajectory

Lamentations is not predictive prophecy, and nothing in it is offered as a promise of a Messiah. Its connections to Christ run through **trajectory** and **typology**, and through the questions it leaves open. `[I]` The routes below are in order of security.

1. **The comforter who does not come — trajectory.** The book's refrain is that there is no מְנַחֵם ("comforter"; in Swete, παρακαλῶν). The canon answers in stages: "Comfort, comfort My people" (Isa 40:1); "to comfort all who mourn … those who mourn in Zion" (Isa 61:2–3, answering the mourning roads of Zion, Lam 1:4 `[S: audit 5]`); "Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Matt 5:4, in Isa 61:2's Greek words); Simeon "looking for the consolation (παράκλησις) of Israel" (Luke 2:25); and "another Helper (παράκλητος)" (John 14:16). Hos 11:8 sharpens it by contrast: there it is God's heart that turns over and God's compassions that are kindled; at Lam 1:20–21 Zion's heart turns over and no comforter comes `[S: Logos 2]`, *untested*. Every Greek step is verified. *Confidence: moderate–high.* `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the line.
2. **The new covenant — trajectory.** Poem 5 prays Jer 31 in reverse: the dance turned to mourning (5:15; Jer 31:13), and Ephraim's prayer, "restore us … that we may be restored" (5:21; Jer 31:18) `[S: audit 3]`. The chapter whose consolation poem 5 lacks goes on to promise the new covenant (Jer 31:31–34), which Heb 8:8–12 quotes. And the community's "You have not forgiven" (3:42) stands between Solomon's request (1 Kgs 8:50) and the promise "I will forgive their iniquity" (Jer 31:34; 33:8) `[S: audit 6]`. **The most secure route for poem 5.** *Confidence: moderate–high.* `[I]`
3. **The cup — typology through an established category.** Zion drank the cup of the LORD's wrath; at the end of ch. 4 it passes from her (4:21–22), as in Isa 51:22 — "you will never drink it again". Lam 4:21 is the Bible's only cup that "passes" (cup + עבר in one verse). In the Gospels the Son asks that the cup pass and then drinks it (Matt 26:39; John 18:11). The category — the cup of wrath — is the prophets' own (Jer 25:15; Isa 51:17), so this is not allegory; the Greek verbs differ (διελεύσεται in Lam 4:21; παρελθάτω in Matt 26:39), so the "passing" is a conceptual contrast, not a verbal echo. *Confidence: high (category); moderate (Lamentations' own contribution).* `[I]`
4. **The throne that remains — trajectory.** "You, O LORD, rule forever; Your throne is from generation to generation" (5:19) is said in the words of Ps 102:13 (exclusive) and Ps 89:5, after "Mount Zion, which lies desolate" (5:18) `[S: audit 3]`. Ps 102:26–28 [Eng 102:25–27] is quoted of the Son at Heb 1:10–12. *Confidence: moderate.* `[I]`
5. **The anointed taken — trajectory and contrast.** "The breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed, Was captured in their pits" (4:20). "The LORD's anointed" is a Samuel phrase — nine of its ten verses are in 1 Sam 24; 26; 2 Sam 1; 19, where the king is the one no hand may touch — and here the anointed is the one taken `[S: audit 6]`. In the Hebrew it is the Davidic king, and his capture is the failure of the hope invested in him; in the Greek, χριστὸς κυρίου (Rahlfs) or χριστὸς Κύριος (Swete) invited a Christological reading `[S]`. The honest route runs through the Davidic covenant: Lam 2 speaks the language of Ps 89's lament over the rejected anointed — נאר ("spurned") only at Lam 2:7 and Ps 89:40 [Eng 89:39], and 2:17 in the grammar of Ps 89:43 [Eng 89:42] — and commentators have seen it (Estes; the Reformation Study Bible) `[S: Logos 2]`. Lam 4:20 puts the king "caught in their pits" in the figure of Ezek 19:4, 8 `[S: audit 2]`. **Ezek 21:32 ("until he comes whose right it is") falls to *low*** — no shared word but "come". **The Dan 9 route holds at *low–moderate***: Anderson's link of Lam 4:22 with Dan 9:24 concerns the completed penalty, not the anointed of 9:25–26 `[S: audit 6; Logos 3]`. The captured anointed opens a question about David's line that only a greater anointed can close. *Confidence: moderate.*
6. **The man under the rod — type and contrast.** The גֶּבֶר ("man") of ch. 3 is afflicted by God's own hand, mocked "all the day" (3:14), gives his cheek to the smiter (3:30), waits in silence (3:26–28), is hunted "without cause" (3:52), says "I am cut off" and is heard (3:54–56), and is redeemed (גאל, "redeem", 3:58). The pattern runs through Isa 50:6 — with Job 16:10 one of only two exclusive partners of 3:30 — to the Passion `[S: audit 2]`. **"They hated Me without a cause" (John 15:25)** fits several psalms, and Lam 3:52 is among the candidates `[S: audit 6; Logos 3]`, *moderate*. The passers-by of Matt 27:39 may touch Lam 2:15 in the Greek, but Jer 18:16 shares the image and Ps 22 governs the scene, *low–moderate*. **But the contrast must be kept:** the man says "we have transgressed" (3:42); Christ bears the rod for sins not his own. And where the man prays "You will repay them" (3:64, in Ps 28:4's words), Christ prays "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). Isa 53:11 — the only other verse where the verb סבל ("bear a load") takes "iniquities" — stands beside "we have borne their iniquities" (5:7) as **canonical reflection, not an echo** `[S: audit 3]`. *Confidence: moderate (type); high (contrast).* `[I]`
7. **The day of the LORD's anger — trajectory.** Lamentations treats the fall of Jerusalem as the day of the LORD come early, on God's own people (1:12; 2:1, 21–22; 3:2). The New Testament places the decisive day of wrath at the cross and its vindication (Rom 3:25–26; 5:9). The Greek "did not spare" of Lam 2:17 (οὐκ ἐφείσατο) is Paul's exact form at Rom 8:32, "He who did not spare His own Son" — whose source is Gen 22, not Lamentations: a canonical reflection, *moderate* `[S: dig]`. `[S]` for the NT theology, *moderate*.
8. **Outside the camp — contrast.** The bloodstained priests and prophets are driven out as unclean (4:14–15), and the Greek of 4:13 has αἷμα δίκαιον ("righteous blood"), the words of Matt 23:35 — a Greek route, *moderate* `[S: audit 6]`. Christ suffered "outside the gate" for the unclean (Heb 13:12–13), not as one of them. *Low–moderate.* `[I]`

**Moralism check.** The besetting temptation is **"Be resilient like the man of chapter 3: remember God's faithfulness and you will find hope."** It turns 3:21–24 into a technique of positive recall and makes the sufferer the hero. The text resists it three times. First, the man's hope is grounded *outside* himself, in the character the LORD has revealed (Exod 34:6–7) — his חֶסֶד is not used up, his compassion does not fail. Second, the confession of 3:40–42 is not a technique that obliges God: it is prayed as Solomon prescribed, and it records "You have not forgiven" `[S: audit 6]`. Third, the book does not end in his recovery: it ends in a community still asking (5:22). **The gospel ground is the LORD who keeps covenant and the Son who drank the cup, not the sufferer's ability to remember.** The other temptation is the reverse moralism: **"They sinned, so they suffered; do not sin."** The book does confess sin (1:8, 18; 3:42; 4:6, 13; 5:16), but it also protests (2:20; 5:7) and never lets confession silence lament. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **Extracting "Great is Thy faithfulness".** Preaching 3:22–24 as the book's message, with the surrounding poems as background. It sounds like a sermon on hope that never sits in the ruins. The ESV heads all of ch. 3 "Great Is Your Faithfulness" (editorial) and supplies "But" at 3:21. *Bites at:* 3:19–24, and any series that preaches ch. 3 first or alone. *Corrective:* the verses are the centre of a lament, not its escape. The arithmetic centre is 3:31–33, and those verses also have to reckon with 2:1–8 and 5:22. **And the Greek lacks 3:22–24 altogether** — not a reason to doubt them, but a reminder that the book did not circulate as a hymn of hope with some lament attached.
2. **Resolving what the book leaves open.** Ending the series on the doxology (5:19), or translating 5:22 as though the question has been answered. *Bites at:* 5:19–22; 4:22. *Corrective:* the doxology is followed by "Why?" (5:20) and "unless" (5:22). The Greek removes even the "unless". **Let the congregation leave with the question asked well, not answered prematurely.** A pastoral landmine: hearers in fresh grief may need to hear that lament is itself faithful speech before they hear anything else.
3. **"It is finished" at 4:22.** Reading תַּם ("complete") as though the book's story ends there, or hearing John 19:30 in it. *Bites at:* 4:21–22. *Corrective:* 4:22 declares an end; poem 5 then prays as though it had not yet seen one (5:20–22). **The book declares the end of the punishment and still asks.** The hope of 4:22 is spoken in the vocabulary of guilt — uncovered, punished, completed — not of comfort `[S: dig]`.
4. **"God didn't do this — Babylon did."** Softening ch. 2 to protect God's reputation. *Bites at:* 2:1–9, 17, 21–22; 3:1–18, 37–38. *Corrective:* the book names the LORD as the agent again and again — thirty verbs in 2:1–8 — and never names Babylon. Its theology holds three claims together (see *Presenting Situation*); the sermon must hold all three.
5. **"They deserved it" — confession without lament.** The reverse error: making the book a lesson in just deserts. *Bites at:* 1:8, 18; 3:39–42; 4:6, 13. *Corrective:* the same poems ask "With whom have You dealt thus?" (2:20) and say "it is we who have borne their iniquities" (5:7). The book is a prayer, not a verdict.
6. **"At least we're not Sodom" — or "blame the clergy".** Using 4:6 to measure a congregation's guilt against others', or 4:13–16 to put the catastrophe on the leaders alone. *Bites at:* 4:6, 13–16. *Corrective:* 4:6 may rank punishments, not conduct (see *Pulpit Notes*), and every comparison in 4:3–10 finds Zion lower, not someone else. The leaders' guilt (4:13) answers poem 2's question about the slaughtered priest and prophet (2:20), but the community that names it goes on to confess "we have sinned" (5:16) `[S: dig]`.
7. **Reading it as Jeremiah's diary.** Hanging the poems on Jeremiah's biography — the pit of Jer 38 at Lam 3:52–55, his tears at 1:16. *Bites at:* 3:1–21, 48–55; the NASB95 heading "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction". *Corrective:* the Hebrew book is anonymous; the man of ch. 3 is a representative "I"; and **Jeremiah's cistern had no water (Jer 38:6) while the man of 3:54 has water over his head.** Use the Jeremian vocabulary as intertext, not as authorship. **Jeremiah 14 is Lamentations' densest partner in the canon** `[S: audit]`; the majority view is that the poet knew Jeremiah's language (Lalleman; Salters; Schnittjer), and those who infer Jeremianic authorship do so from tone, not from the text `[S: Logos 2; Logos 3]`.
8. **Christologising too fast.** Hearing Christ speak at 1:12 ("Is it nothing to you?", a Good Friday text in Christian liturgy, `[S]`) or reading 4:20 as about Jesus because the Greek says χριστός. *Bites at:* 1:12; 3:1–30; 4:20. *Corrective:* in its own voice 1:12 is Zion, and 4:20 is Zedekiah; Swete's "Christ the Lord" at 4:20 is one edition's reading (Rahlfs: "the Lord's anointed"). Christ is reached through the categories — the cup, the anointed, the new covenant, the day of wrath — not by changing the speaker.
9. **Counselling the abused to submit.** Using 3:27–30 ("let him give his cheek to the smiter") to tell a victim to bear abuse in silence. *Bites at:* 3:25–33. *Corrective:* the next stanza condemns exactly that abuse — crushing prisoners, perverting a man's justice, subverting him in his lawsuit — in the words of the Torah's law against corrupt judges (3:34–36; Exod 23:6) `[S: audit 6]`, and the poem ends in a plea for justice: "Judge my case" (3:59). The man's silence is before God, not before his oppressor `[S: sweep; dig]`.
10. **Skipping the imprecations.** Omitting 1:21–22; 3:59–66; 4:21–22 as sub-Christian. *Bites at:* those verses. *Corrective:* they hand vengeance to God (3:64, "You will repay them") rather than taking it, and 4:22 sets the end of Zion's punishment beside the start of Edom's. "Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom" (4:21) is a taunt, not an invitation (Dobbs-Allsopp `[S, not owned — unverified]`). Preach them as prayer, not as permission.

## Pulpit Notes — ESV against NASB95

Where the declared pulpit text and the study text diverge in ways that bear on the findings above. These are notes to the preacher about what the congregation will hear, not verdicts on the translations. Wording checked against the ESV and NASB95 exports.

**Links the congregation cannot hear**

1. **3:22.** Both versions follow the emended reading תַּמּוּ ("they [the mercies] cease"), as BHS's apparatus proposes with one manuscript, the Syriac and the Targum: ESV "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases"; NASB95 "The LORD's lovingkindnesses indeed never cease". The Hebrew text has תָמְנוּ ("we are [not] consumed"; KJV "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed"). **The congregation will hear neither the first "we" of ch. 3 nor the link to 4:22 (תַּם, "completed").**
2. **"See, O LORD".** The ESV renders רְאֵה יְהוָה three ways — "O LORD, behold" (1:9), "Look, O LORD, and see" (1:11; 2:20), "Look, O LORD" (1:20) — so the refrain of poem 1 cannot be heard as one cry. Both versions render the verb of 3:36 "approve", so the "see" chain that 3:59–60 resolves ("You have seen") is hidden at its turning point.
3. **The chains of "grief" and "wandering".** ESV "the LORD has afflicted her" (1:5) hides the link to 3:32; NASB95 "has caused her grief … causes grief" keeps it. ESV "wandering" (1:7) keeps the link to 3:19 ("my wanderings"); NASB95 "homelessness" loses it.
4. **"Reject".** ESV "bereft" (3:17) and "cast off" (3:31) hide the chain 2:7 → 3:17 → 3:31; NASB95 "rejected … reject" keeps it.
5. **"Uncover".** ESV "they have not exposed your iniquity" (2:14) and "he will uncover your sins" (4:22) break the book's exclusive link between those verses; NASB95 "exposed … expose" keeps it.
6. **"Poured out".** ESV "The holy stones lie scattered" (4:1) hides the first of poem 4's three "poured out" (4:1, 11, 13).
7. **"Afflict" and "faint".** ESV "raped", NASB95 "ravished" (5:11) hide the link to 3:33 ("He does not afflict"). ESV "our heart has become sick" (5:17) breaks the echo of "my heart is faint" (1:22; 1:13), which NASB95 keeps.
8. **"Sit".** ESV "reign forever", NASB95 "rule forever" (5:19) both hide תֵּשֵׁב ("sit enthroned"), the verb of 1:1 ("How lonely sits the city") and of Ps 102:13.
9. **מוֹעֵד ("appointed feast").** ESV: "festival" (1:4), "assembly" (1:15), "meeting place" and "festival" (2:6), "day of festival" (2:7), "festival day" (2:22). NASB95 keeps "appointed" audible in most places. **In the ESV the chain cannot be heard.**

**Readings the versions decide for the hearer**

10. **1:8.** ESV "she became filthy", NASB95 "she has become an unclean thing": both close the question in favour of impurity. HALOT and the Greek read "an object of head-shaking" `[S: Logos 1]`. Say so if the sermon dwells on 1:8.
11. **1:21.** ESV: "You **have brought** the day you announced"; NASB95: "Oh, that You **would bring** the day". The Hebrew perfect הֵבֵאתָ can be read either way. The ESV hears a statement; the NASB95 a prayer — which makes the echo to 4:21–22 an *answered* prayer.
12. **3:56–57.** Both versions print 3:56b as a quotation — the prayer that was heard — so both commit the hearer to **a remembered hearing** (MacKay). The forms equally allow **perfects of certainty**, a deliverance so sure it is told as done — by Keil and Delitzsch's report, the view of "nearly all expositors" of their day (on 1:21, pp. 491–492). Both readings put the answer in the past tense of faith; the difference is whether the man is remembering or anticipating `[S: audit 6; Logos 3]`. *Check K&D's own comment on 3:56 (p. 523) before quoting them for either reading.*
13. **3:36.** ESV "the Lord does not approve", NASB95 "Of these things the Lord does not approve": both choose the statement. The unmarked question ("Has not the Lord seen?" — Kaiser) is equally possible; the slogan reading is the weakest `[S: audit 6; Logos 3]`.
14. **4:3.** Both read "jackals", following the qere; the written text allows "serpents", the reading on which Deut 32:33 is heard `[S: audit 7]`.
15. **4:6.** ESV: "the **chastisement**… greater than the **punishment** of Sodom"; NASB95: "the **iniquity**… greater than the **sin** of Sodom". עָוֹן and חַטָּאת can mean either guilt or its penalty. **The ESV congregation hears that Jerusalem suffered more; the NASB95 reader hears that Jerusalem sinned more.** This bears on traps 5 and 6.
16. **4:15.** ESV "So they became **fugitives and wanderers**" makes Cain's sentence (Gen 4:12, 14 ESV) audible in English; the Hebrew link is low–moderate.
17. **4:21.** ESV "to you also the cup shall pass"; NASB95 "the cup will come around to you as well". The ESV keeps the Bible's only "passing cup".

**Small things worth knowing**

18. **3:33.** ESV "he does not afflict **from his heart**" (literal: מִלִּבּוֹ); NASB95 "**willingly**". The ESV keeps the anthropology of the verse, and the frame with "I bring back to my heart" (3:21).
19. **2:18a.** "Their heart cried out to the Lord" (both versions keep the MT); BHS and many critics emend to the feminine imperative "Cry out!" `[S: Logos 2]`. Headline points stand on either reading.
20. **Headings.** ESV heads ch. 3 "Great Is Your Faithfulness" and supplies "But" at 3:21 (the Hebrew has none; its first word is זֹאת, "this"); NASB95 heads it "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction" and titles the book "The Lamentations of Jeremiah". **Neither heading is in the Hebrew.** See traps 1 and 7.

**Every unit now has a solo dig, and each has been cleared for point-purpose on its audited verdicts** (1:1–22 and 2:1–22 after audit 5; 3:1–33 and 5:1–22 after audit 3; 3:34–66, 4:1–16 and 4:17–22 after audit 6). `[S: dig]`

| 3:1–24 | From the rod to "the LORD is my portion" | ⭐ HIGH |

*Unit boundaries:* the stanza seams in ch. 3 are marked by a setumah after every third verse in Leningrad (BHS agrees), so any split falls on a stanza boundary. **Do not divide 3:19–24 from 3:25–33**: they are one argument across the ח and ט stanzas, and the ending of a sermon at 3:24 is exactly trap 1. **Nor 3:25–33 from 3:34–36**: the court of 3:34–36 is the corrective to the misuse of 3:27–30 (trap 9). `[T]` for the setumot; `[I]` for the advice. *The dig boundaries (3:1–33 | 3:34–66) follow the arithmetic midpoint; the sermon boundaries above follow the argument. Both are stanza seams.*

**Option C — poem 4 in two.** Where poem 4 is preached on its own, the digs' division serves: 4:1–16 (*Gold grown dim*: beauty undone, the hinge at 4:11, the leaders' guilt) and 4:17–22 (*The cup passed on*: the false hopes confessed, the anointed taken, the end declared). Keep 4:21–22 with 4:17–20, or the declaration of 4:22 loses the confession it answers.
