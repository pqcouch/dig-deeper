## What this document is

This is the whole-book planning layer for Lamentations. It exists to make the passage digs sharper, not to settle them in advance.

Three features of the book shape everything below, and they are stated here so that no later section quietly forgets them.

1. **The form is the first datum.** Four of the five poems are alphabetic acrostics, and the fifth has exactly twenty-two verses but no acrostic. Nothing in the book is more certain than its form, and an overview that treats the form as decoration has missed the first thing the text says. `[T]`
2. **God never speaks — with one exception.** The whole book is addressed *about* God or *to* God. The only words of God quoted in it are two words remembered in the past tense: אַל־תִּירָא ("Do not fear!", 3:57). `[T]`
3. **The book does not resolve.** It ends on כִּי אִם־מָאֹס מְאַסְתָּנוּ ("unless You have utterly rejected us", 5:22). Any reading that makes 3:22–24 the book's last word has to explain why the book itself does not end there. `[T]`

## Canonical Position

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Section** | **Ketuvim**, among the five **Megilloth**. In the BHS order it stands seventh in the Writings: after Ecclesiastes, before Esther. In the English Bible it follows Jeremiah — the order of the Greek, where Swete prints it after Jeremiah and Baruch and before the Epistle of Jeremiah. `[T]` for both orders as printed. The Talmud's order (*B. Bat.* 14b) is different again `[S]`. |
| **Reading implication** | Read in the Writings, Lamentations is an **anonymous** voice of the worshipping community, set among the books of prayer and wisdom (Psalms, Job) and beside the other Megilloth. Read after Jeremiah, it becomes the prophet's own postscript to his prophecy. **The Hebrew canon does not make that connection; the Greek does** (see *The Greek Text of Lamentations*). Both placements are real and the difference is worth naming in the pulpit, because the English reader hears "Jeremiah" before a word of the book is read. `[I]` |
| **Presupposes** | **The event**, which the book never narrates: the siege, famine, breach and flight of 2 Kgs 25:1–7 (the pursuit, 25:5, = Jer 39:5; 52:8 — with רדף, "pursue", at Lam 4:19); told again at 2 Chr 36:17–20, which shares more rare vocabulary with Lam 1–2 than all but five chapters of the canon. **The covenant curses**, two of them echoed exclusively: Deut 28:50 (no respect for the old, no favour to the young; Lam 4:16) and 28:65, 45 (no rest among the nations; pursued and overtaken; Lam 1:3); with 28:44 (the foe as head; Lam 1:5) and the siege-cannibalism curse of Lev 26:29; Deut 28:53; Jer 19:9 (Lam 2:20) `[S: audit]`. **The prophets of the fall**, Jeremiah above all: Jer 14:17, 19 (Lam 2:13, 18; 3:48; 5:22) and Jer 8:21 (Lam 2:11). **The assembly law** of Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] (Lam 1:10). **The leper's cry** of Lev 13:45 (Lam 4:15, where it is turned on the defiled priests and prophets). **Respect for the old**, Lev 19:32 (Lam 5:12). **The divine character** of Exod 34:6–7 (Lam 3:22, 32; 5:7). **Sodom**, Gen 19:24–25 (Lam 4:6). **The temple warning**, 1 Kgs 9:8 (Lam 2:15). **Zion's beauty**, Ps 48:2 [Heb 48:3] (Lam 2:15). **Every reference in this field has been checked against the WLC lemma index** (see the Colophon). `[T]` for the wording; `[I]` that these are presupposed rather than coincident |
| **Handoff** | Lamentations ends asking whether God has rejected his people (5:20–22). In canonical order, the confessional prayers that follow in the Writings — **Dan 9** above all, then Ezra 9 and Neh 9 — take up the question **thematically**: confession over a desolate Jerusalem, and an appeal to God's "great compassion" (Dan 9:18). **The link is not lexical.** On the claim audit's measure, Dan 9 ranks near the bottom of the confession family for shared wording, and its "open Your eyes and see" is Hezekiah's formula (2 Kgs 19:16 = Isa 37:17). Lamentations' closest lexical partners in confession are Isa 63–64, Ps 74, Ps 89 and Jer 14 `[S: audit]`. Esther, next door, shows the people living בַּגּוֹיִם ("among the nations", Lam 4:20) under a foreign king, with God unnamed. `[I]` *Confidence: moderate (Daniel, thematic); low (Daniel, lexical); moderate (Esther).* |
| **Neighbours** | **Ecclesiastes before, Esther after.** One small lexical touch with the book before: Ecclesiastes' keyword הֶבֶל ("vapour, futility") appears once in Lamentations, at 4:17 ("our help was useless"). Whether the adjacency is doing work is *uncertain*. The touch with Esther is thematic (exile, a hidden God, Edom — Haman is an Agagite, and Amalek descends from Esau, Gen 36:12), *uncertain*. In Leningrad itself the Ketuvim open with Chronicles (`_texts/README.md`); that the sequence Ecclesiastes–Lamentations–Esther is the same there is recalled, not checked `[S]`. The neighbour readings are `[I]`. |

**One further canonical fact, worth holding in view throughout.** In the BHS order **Job stands five books earlier** in the same section.

- **Four exclusive images.** Job 16, 19 and 30 share them with Lamentations. Target and kidneys at 3:12–13; the taunt-song at 3:14; cheek and reproach at 3:30; the crown of the head at 5:16. Each pair occurs in only two verses of the Hebrew Bible: the Job verse and the Lamentations verse.
- **Not the frame.** Lam 3 has equally exclusive contacts with Jer 20:7, Isa 50:6 and Pss 77, 88 and 143, and by density Job is a mid-band partner. **Lam 3 is a mosaic.** `[S: audit]`
- **Uz.** Lam 4:21 names "the land of Uz" (עוּץ, "Uz"), otherwise Job's homeland (Job 1:1) and an Edomite clan (Gen 36:28). It is better read with Jer 25:20, where Uz stands in the cup oracle beside Edom. `[T]`

## Presenting Situation

**Author.** The Hebrew book is **anonymous**. It has no superscription and never names Jeremiah or any other author. `[T]` — verified: the name יִרְמְיָהוּ ("Jeremiah") does not occur in Lamentations, with a positive control on the same search returning 117 verses of Jeremiah. The attribution to Jeremiah comes from the **Greek prologue** to 1:1 in Swete (Vaticanus): "after Israel was taken captive and Jerusalem laid waste, Jeremiah sat weeping and lamented this lament over Jerusalem". `[T]` *for Swete*; `[S]` *as authorship*. The Talmud (*B. Bat.* 15a) and many commentators follow it; 2 Chr 35:25 says that Jeremiah lamented Josiah and that the laments "are written in the Laments" (הַקִּינוֹת, "the laments") — a statement about laments for Josiah, not about this book. `[T]` *for what Chronicles says*; `[S]` *for the identification*. The NASB95 export is titled "The Lamentations of Jeremiah" and heads chapter 3 "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction"; both are editorial. `[T]` *for the export*.

**Voices within the book.** The book speaks in several voices, and identifying them is half of reading it: `[T]`

- a **narrator** who describes Zion in the third person (1:1–11b; 1:17; 2:1–12; 4:1–16);
- **Zion herself**, personified as a woman, speaking in the first person (1:9c, 11c–16, 18–22; 2:20–22);
- the narrator **turning to address Zion** in the second person (2:13–19);
- **the man**, הַגֶּבֶר ("the man", 3:1), speaking as "I" (3:1–39, 48–66);
- a **community "we"** (3:40–47; 4:17–20; the whole of ch. 5).

**Occasion.** The fall of Jerusalem: siege and famine (2:11–12, 19–20; 4:3–10; 5:9–10), the breach and burning (2:3–9; 4:11–12), the temple violated and the feasts ended (1:4, 10; 2:6–7), the king captured in flight (4:19–20), exile (1:3, 5, 18; 4:22), and Edom's gloating (4:21–22). `[T]` That this is the Babylonian destruction of 587/586 BC is a **near-certain inference, not a statement**: **the book never names Babylon, the Chaldeans or Nebuchadnezzar** (all three verified absent, each with a positive control in Jeremiah). The only nations it names are Egypt and Assyria (5:6) and Edom (4:21–22). `[T]` for the absence; `[I]` for the date, *confidence high*. **The silence is theological before it is historical: the agent the book names is the LORD.** `[I]`

**Date of composition.** Unknown. The vividness of chs 2 and 4 and the land-bound "we" of ch. 5 (5:2–18, the people under foreign servants in their own land) fit a date soon after the fall; nothing in the book requires the return from exile, and 5:20–22 assumes none. `[I]`, *confidence moderate*. A date between 586 and c. 520 is the usual range `[S]`.

**Audiences.**

- *Narrative world:* the survivors — in the land (ch. 5) and in exile (1:3; 4:15, 20). `[T]`
- *Compositional:* a worshipping community that needed words for the catastrophe. That the poems were used in public mourning is likely (cf. Zech 7:3–5; 8:19, fasts in the fifth month) `[I]`, *moderate*; the later reading at the Ninth of Av is `[S]`.
- *Canonical:* the reader of the Writings, who arrives with the Torah's curses, the Prophets' warnings and the Psalter's laments in the ear. **This is the audience this overview reads for.** `[I]`

**Pressure.** How can the covenant people speak to the God who has become their enemy? `[I]` The book holds three things together and will not let any of them go:

1. **The LORD has done this.** "The Lord has become like an enemy" (2:5); "The LORD has done what He purposed" (2:17). `[T]`
2. **The LORD is right to have done it.** "The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against His command" (1:18); "We have transgressed and rebelled" (3:42); "Woe to us, for we have sinned!" (5:16). `[T]`
3. **What has been done is beyond bearing, and he must be told so.** "See, O LORD, and look! With whom have You dealt thus? Should women eat their offspring?" (2:20). `[T]`

**Purpose.** To give a ruined people ordered words — confession, protest, memory and petition — with which to keep addressing the LORD when he is silent, because his חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") is not exhausted and his throne endures (3:22; 5:19). `[I]`

## The Greek Text of Lamentations

The Greek differs from the Hebrew at points that matter for preaching, and they are gathered here once. **Swete** is the observation layer (Vaticanus, diplomatic); the **Rahlfs** export is cited where they differ.

1. **The prologue naming Jeremiah** stands at 1:1 in Swete. **It is not in the Rahlfs export as exported**, which begins directly at Πῶς ἐκάθισεν ("How she sat…"). Whether Rahlfs prints it as a heading the export did not carry should be **checked on screen in Logos** before anything is made to rest on it. `[T]` for both files.
2. **Lam 3:22–24 is absent from the Old Greek — and so is 3:29.** Swete has no text at 3:22, 23, 24 or 29, and the Rahlfs export runs 3:21 → 3:25 and 3:28 → 3:30. BHS's apparatus agrees: "v 22–24 > 𝔊*" and "v 29 > 𝔊ᴹˢˢ". `[T]` **The most-quoted verses of the book — "Great is Your faithfulness" — are not in the earliest Greek.** This is not evidence against the Hebrew: the ח stanza is required by the acrostic, so its loss is a loss *from* the Greek, not an addition to the Hebrew. `[I]`, *confidence high*. Hexaplaric manuscripts supply the verses `[S]`. **The preacher should know it; the congregation does not need to.**
3. **Letter names as headings.** Swete prints the Hebrew letter names before the verses (Ἄλεφ, Βήθ, Γίμελ…). Its labels run in standard alphabetical order, so in chs 2 and 4 the label Ἄιν (ayin) stands on the Hebrew **פ** verse (2:16; 4:16) and Φή (pe) on the **ע** verse (2:17; 4:17). The Greek labels "correct" an order the Hebrew deliberately keeps (see *Structural Arc Map*). `[T]`
4. **Lam 4:20.** The Hebrew מְשִׁיחַ יְהוָה ("the LORD's anointed") becomes χριστὸς κυρίου in the Rahlfs export, and χριστὸς Κύριος in Swete — which can be read as "Christ the Lord". The patristic Christological reading of this verse rests on the Greek `[S]`. In the Hebrew the anointed is the Davidic king — in context, Zedekiah, captured in flight (2 Kgs 25:5–6). `[I]`, *confidence high*. See *Preaching Traps*.
5. **The last verse.** Where the Hebrew has the ambiguous כִּי אִם ("unless" / "but rather" / "even if"), the Greek reads ὅτι ἀπωθούμενος ἀπώσω ἡμᾶς ("for You have utterly rejected us"): a plain statement, with no "unless". `[T]` BHS notes that אִם ("if") is absent in 𝔊 and the Syriac at 5:22, leaving a plain כִּי ("for") — which is what the Greek translates `[T]`.
6. **The comforter.** At all six places where the Hebrew has the root נחם ("comfort") — 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21; 2:13 — Swete has the verb παρακαλέω ("comfort"). Isa 40:1 opens with the same verb twice (Παρακαλεῖτε παρακαλεῖτε, "Comfort, comfort"). `[T]` This word-family runs through to Luke 2:25 (παράκλησις, "consolation") and John 14:16 (παράκλητος, "Helper"); see *Christological Trajectory*.
7. **Lam 2:13 reads a cup.** Where the Hebrew has כִּי־גָדוֹל כַּיָּם שִׁבְרֵךְ ("for your ruin is as vast as the sea"), the Greek has ὅτι ἐμεγαλύνθη ποτήριον συντριβῆς σου ("for the **cup** of your ruin has been made great"). Earlier in the verse it has τίς σώσει καὶ παρακαλέσει σε ("who will save and comfort you?"). BHS identifies the translator's reading as כּוֹס ("cup") and מִי יוֹשִׁיעַ לָךְ וְנִחַמְךָ ("who will save you and comfort you"). In Greek, then, 2:13 holds all three elements of Isa 51:17–19: the cup, the ruin, and "who will comfort you?". This is triage category 2, a substantive Greek reading: weigh it; do not default. The Hebrew stands as the text; whether the translator read Lamentations through Isaiah is an open question. `[T]` for Swete and the BHS note; `[S: audit]`; `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.
8. **Lam 4:16, "elders" or "prophets".** The Rahlfs export reads πρεσβύτας ("elders"), as the Hebrew does. Swete (Vaticanus) reads προφήτας ("prophets"), which loses the book's sharpest contact with Deut 28:50. `[T]`

## Structural Arc Map

### The form, counted

| Poem | Verses | Acrostic | Words (WLC) | Words per verse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 22 | one verse per letter; **ע before פ** | 376 | 17.1 |
| 2 | 22 | one verse per letter; **פ before ע** | 381 | 17.3 |
| 3 | 66 | **each letter heads three verses**; פ before ע | 381 | 5.8 |
| 4 | 22 | one verse per letter; פ before ע | 259 | 11.8 |
| 5 | 22 | **none** — 22 verses only | 145 | 6.6 |

Counts are of words as tokenised in the WLC lemma index (counted as space-separated words the figures are 330 / 333 / 347 / 231 / 136, and the shape is the same); the opening letters were read off every verse of the WLC reading text. `[T]`

Three things follow from the table.

- **The first three poems are the same length** (376, 381, 381 words), **and the last two shrink** (259, then 145). The book's volume falls away after its centre. `[T]` That the diminution enacts exhaustion is a reading `[I]`, *moderate*.
- **Chapter 3 intensifies the form** — every letter three times — at the point where the book's theology is densest. `[T]`/`[I]`
- **Chapter 5 keeps the number and abandons the alphabet.** The prayer is still "complete" in count, but the order has gone. `[T]` for the fact; the meaning is contested — collapse of order, or a deliberate turn from poem to plain prayer `[I]`, *open*.

**The פ/ע order.** In ch. 1 the alphabet runs in its standard order (ע at 1:16, פ at 1:17). In chs 2, 3 and 4 the order is פ then ע (2:16–17; 3:46–51; 4:16–17). `[T]` BHS records a few manuscripts, the Lucianic Greek and the Syriac that transpose to the standard order in all three chapters `[T for BHS]`, so **the reversal is the Leningrad and majority reading, not a universal one**. Another alphabetic order with פ before ע is attested in early Hebrew abecedaries, and the Qumran manuscript 4QLam reportedly has פ before ע in ch. 1 as well `[S]`. *No sermon should rest on the reversal.*

**The binding device — a formally centred book.** `[S: audit]` for the revision.

1. **The form is the binding device, and it is certain.** `[T]`
   - Four alphabetic acrostics.
   - Poem 3 a **triple** acrostic at the centre, its voice changed from a city to a man.
   - Poem 5 with twenty-two verses and no alphabet.
   - The volume falling away in poems 4–5.
   - The verse midpoint at 3:33 | 34.
   - The acrostic holds the grief inside a boundary: each poem runs from א to ת and then stops (`[I]` for that function).
2. **Lexically, poem 2 is a hub, tied both to poem 1 and to poem 4.** Three items are exclusive within Lamentations to poems 2 and 4, all verified by lemma:
   - prophets (נָבִיא: 2:9, 14, 20; 4:13);
   - the LORD pouring out his wrath (שׁפך + חֵמָה: 2:4; 4:11);
   - "at the head of every street" (2:19; 4:1).

   But at phrase level, poems 1 and 2 are linked as closely (rare verse-links per 10⁴ word-pairs: 1–2, 0.98; 2–4, 0.91). And on whole vocabulary, the 2–4 pairing is exactly at chance (2,000 random reshuffles; observed ÷ expected = 1.00). `[S: audit]` *Confidence: moderate (2 ↔ 4 phrases).*
3. **A full concentric frame (1 ↔ 5, 2 ↔ 4) is not demonstrated.**
   - 1 ↔ 5 rests on one exclusive word, אַלְמָנָה ("widow", 1:1; 5:3), and the soft cognate pair "rest" (מָנוֹחַ, 1:3; הוּנַח, 5:5).
   - "See, O LORD" cannot mark it, since 2:20 repeats 1:11 word for word.
   - אֵיכָה ("How!") cannot pair 2 with 4, since it also opens poem 1.
   - Held as a proposal for the digs to test. *Confidence: low–moderate.* `[I]`

**The centre of the book.** Lamentations has 154 verses (WLC), so the arithmetic midpoint falls **between verse 77 (3:33) and verse 78 (3:34)**. It is also the midpoint of ch. 3. The stanza that ends the first half is the כ stanza (3:31–33), and every line of it begins with כִּי ("for"): `[T]`

> "For the Lord will not reject forever, / For if He causes grief, Then He will have compassion According to His abundant lovingkindness. / For He does not afflict willingly Or grieve the sons of men." (NASB95)

**The centre is a verse-count fact.** Counted by words in the WLC index, the midpoint falls at 3:3, so state the unit when citing it. `[S: audit]`

**The usual claim that 3:22–23 is the book's centre is close but not exact; the centre is 3:31–33.** The two stanzas belong together — 3:22 and 3:32 share חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") and the root רחם ("compassion"). They are the **only two places** in the book where חֶסֶד occurs. `[T]`

## Microscript

*Jerusalem has fallen, and the LORD did it. Lamentations gives the ruined city ordered words — alphabetical, but ending unresolved — with which to admit that he was right, to protest that the suffering is past bearing, and to keep asking him to see, remember and restore. Its ground for doing so is that his חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") has not been used up and his throne has not fallen.*

**Keynote:** אֵין מְנַחֵם ("there is none to comfort her") — five times in ch. 1 (1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21), with the narrator's helpless "that I may comfort you" (2:13) as the sixth and last use of the root נחם ("comfort") in the book. **Counter-note:** חַסְדֵי יְהוָה ("the LORD's lovingkindnesses", 3:22) and "You, O LORD, rule forever" (5:19). **The God who could comfort never speaks, except for "Do not fear!" — remembered (3:57).** `[T]`

**Test against the movements.**

- *Ch. 1:* no comforter; the LORD's day of anger; confession and protest. ✓
- *Ch. 2:* the LORD himself as the destroyer — the hardest form of "he did it". ✓
- *Ch. 3:* the ground of hope — חֶסֶד — found inside affliction, not after it. ✓
- *Ch. 4:* the term of punishment ended (4:22), the enemy's turn to come. ✓
- *Ch. 5:* asking to be restored, with rejection still possible. ✓

## Intertextual Map

Lamentations quotes no earlier text with a formula, and almost nothing word for word at length. Its intertextuality is **dense, short and phrasal**. It shares two-, three- and four-word idioms with the prophets of the fall (above all Jeremiah), the city-humiliation poems (Isa 47; Nah 3), the covenant curses, the Psalter's laments and Job. **So both Move 2 and Move 4 will be heavily loaded in the sweep.** `[T]`/`[I]`

**What v0.2.0 changed.** Every row below was audited, or re-checked, against the claim audit of 3 October 2026 `[S: audit]`. The changes:

- Each row now carries a **rarity count**: how many verses of the Hebrew Bible (WLC) contain the shared lemma set. Where the audit ran a rival search, the row also names the **closest rival**.
- Two new tests apply throughout:
  - **Wording found in more than about ten verses is idiom**, and is not rated above *moderate* on wording alone.
  - **A use counts towards "live" only if it survives the rival search.**
- **Confidence rates the allusion, not the words.** The words are verified in every row. **The direction of dependence is open throughout**: "source" means "the other text", not "the earlier text".

## Christological Trajectory

Lamentations is not predictive prophecy, and nothing in it is offered as a promise of a Messiah. Its connections to Christ run through **trajectory** and **typology**, and through the questions it leaves open. `[I]`

1. **The comforter who does not come — trajectory.** The book's refrain is that there is no מְנַחֵם ("comforter"; in Swete, παρακαλῶν). The canon answers in stages: "Comfort, comfort My people" (Isa 40:1); Simeon "looking for the consolation (παράκλησις) of Israel" (Luke 2:25); and "another Helper (παράκλητος)" (John 14:16). Every Greek step is verified. *Confidence: moderate–high.* `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the line.
2. **The cup — typology through an established category.** Zion drank the cup of the LORD's wrath; at the end of ch. 4 it passes from her (4:21–22), as in Isa 51:22. In the Gospels the Son drinks the cup (Matt 26:39; John 18:11). The category — the cup of wrath — is the prophets' own (Jer 25:15; Isa 51:17), so this is not allegory. *Confidence: high (category); moderate (Lamentations' own contribution).* `[I]`
3. **The anointed taken — trajectory and contrast.** "The breath of our nostrils, the LORD's anointed, Was captured in their pits" (4:20). In the Hebrew it is the Davidic king, and his capture is the failure of the hope invested in him. In the Greek, χριστὸς κυρίου invited a Christological reading `[S]`. The honest route runs through the Davidic covenant. Lam 2 speaks the language of Ps 89's lament over the rejected anointed: נאר ("spurned") occurs only at Lam 2:7 and Ps 89:40 [Eng 89:39], and the enemy's horn raised at 2:17 inverts Ps 89:18, 25, 43 [Eng 89:17, 24, 42]. Lam 4:20 puts the king "caught in their pits" in the figure of Ezek 19:4, 8 `[S: audit]`. The captured anointed opens a question about David's line that only a greater anointed can close. The Gospels' verb for Jesus' arrest, συλλαμβάνω ("seize", John 18:12; Luke 22:54), matches Swete's συνελήμφθη ("was seized") at 4:20 — a verbal touch, *uncertain* as design. *Confidence: moderate.*
4. **The man under the rod — type and contrast.** The גֶּבֶר ("man") of ch. 3 is afflicted by God's own hand, mocked "all the day" (3:14), gives his cheek to the smiter (3:30), waits in silence (3:26–28), and is redeemed (גאל, "redeem", 3:58). The pattern runs through Isa 50:6 — "I gave … my cheeks to those who pluck out the beard", which with Job 16:10 is one of only two exclusive partners of 3:30 — to the Passion `[S: audit]`. The passers-by of Matt 27:39 may touch Lam 2:15 in the Greek, but Jer 18:16 shares the image and Ps 22 governs the scene. *Low–moderate.* **But the contrast must be kept:** the man says "we have transgressed" (3:42); Christ bears the rod for sins not his own. *Confidence: moderate (type); high (contrast).* `[I]`
5. **The day of the LORD's anger — trajectory.** Lamentations treats the fall of Jerusalem as the day of the LORD come early, on God's own people (1:12; 2:1, 21–22; 3:2). The New Testament places the decisive day of wrath at the cross and its vindication (Rom 3:25–26; 5:9). `[S]` for the NT theology, *moderate*.

**Moralism check.** The besetting temptation is **"Be resilient like the man of chapter 3: remember God's faithfulness and you will find hope."** It turns 3:21–24 into a technique of positive recall and makes the sufferer the hero. The text resists it twice. First, the man's hope is grounded *outside* himself, in the character the LORD has revealed (Exod 34:6–7) — his חֶסֶד is not used up, his compassion does not fail. Second, the book does not end in his recovery: it ends in a community still asking (5:22). **The gospel ground is the LORD who keeps covenant and the Son who drank the cup, not the sufferer's ability to remember.** The other temptation is the reverse moralism: **"They sinned, so they suffered; do not sin."** The book does confess sin (1:8, 18; 3:42; 4:6, 13; 5:16), but it also protests (2:20; 5:7) and never lets confession silence lament. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **Extracting "Great is Thy faithfulness".** Preaching 3:22–24 as the book's message, with the surrounding poems as background. It sounds like a sermon on hope that never sits in the ruins. The ESV heads all of ch. 3 "Great Is Your Faithfulness" (editorial). *Bites at:* 3:19–24, and any series that preaches ch. 3 first or alone. *Corrective:* the verses are the centre of a lament, not its escape. The arithmetic centre is 3:31–33, and those verses also have to reckon with 2:1–8 and 5:22. **And the Greek lacks 3:22–24 altogether** — not a reason to doubt them, but a reminder that the book did not circulate as a hymn of hope with some lament attached.
2. **Resolving what the book leaves open.** Ending the series on the doxology (5:19), or translating 5:22 as though the question has been answered. *Bites at:* 5:19–22; 4:22. *Corrective:* the doxology is followed by "Why?" (5:20) and "unless" (5:22). The Greek removes even the "unless". **Let the congregation leave with the question asked well, not answered prematurely.** A pastoral landmine: hearers in fresh grief may need to hear that lament is itself faithful speech before they hear anything else.
3. **"God didn't do this — Babylon did."** Softening ch. 2 to protect God's reputation. *Bites at:* 2:1–9, 17, 21–22; 3:1–18, 37–38. *Corrective:* the book names the LORD as the agent again and again, and never names Babylon. Its theology holds three claims together (see *Presenting Situation*); the sermon must hold all three.
4. **"They deserved it" — confession without lament.** The reverse error: making the book a lesson in just deserts. *Bites at:* 1:8, 18; 3:39–42; 4:6, 13. *Corrective:* the same poems ask "With whom have You dealt thus?" (2:20) and say "it is we who have borne their iniquities" (5:7). The book is a prayer, not a verdict.
5. **Reading it as Jeremiah's diary.** Hanging the poems on Jeremiah's biography — the pit of Jer 38 at Lam 3:52–55, his tears at 1:16. *Bites at:* 3:1–21, 48–55; the NASB95 heading "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction". *Corrective:* the Hebrew book is anonymous; the man of ch. 3 is a representative "I"; and **Jeremiah's cistern had no water (Jer 38:6) while the man of 3:54 has water over his head.** Use the Jeremian vocabulary as intertext, not as authorship. **Jeremiah 14 is Lamentations' densest partner in the canon** — a shared language of grief to be traced, not a signature `[S: audit]`.
6. **Christologising too fast.** Hearing Christ speak at 1:12 ("Is it nothing to you?", a Good Friday text in Christian liturgy, `[S]`) or reading 4:20 as about Jesus because the Greek says χριστός. *Bites at:* 1:12; 3:1–30; 4:20. *Corrective:* in its own voice 1:12 is Zion, and 4:20 is Zedekiah. Christ is reached through the categories — the cup, the anointed, the day of wrath — not by changing the speaker.
7. **Skipping the imprecations.** Omitting 1:21–22; 3:59–66; 4:21–22 as sub-Christian. *Bites at:* those verses. *Corrective:* they hand vengeance to God (3:64, "You will recompense them") rather than taking it, and 4:22 sets the end of Zion's punishment beside the start of Edom's. Preach them as prayer, not as permission.

## Pulpit Notes — ESV against NASB95

Where the declared pulpit text and the study text diverge in ways that bear on the findings above. These are notes to the preacher about what the congregation will hear, not verdicts on the translations.

1. **3:22.** Both versions follow the emended reading תַּמּוּ ("they [the mercies] cease"), as BHS's apparatus proposes with one manuscript, the Syriac and the Targum: ESV "The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases"; NASB95 "The LORD's lovingkindnesses indeed never cease". The Hebrew text has תָמְנוּ ("we are [not] consumed"; KJV "It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed"). **The congregation will not hear the link to 4:22 (תַּם, "completed").**
2. **4:6.** ESV: "the **chastisement**… greater than the **punishment** of Sodom"; NASB95: "the **iniquity**… greater than the **sin** of Sodom". עָוֹן and חַטָּאת can mean either guilt or its penalty. **The ESV congregation hears that Jerusalem suffered more; the NASB95 reader hears that Jerusalem sinned more.** This bears on traps 3 and 4.
3. **1:21.** ESV: "You **have brought** the day you announced"; NASB95: "Oh, that You **would bring** the day". The Hebrew perfect הֵבֵאתָ can be read either way. The ESV hears a statement; the NASB95 a prayer — which makes the echo to 4:21–22 an *answered* prayer.
4. **מוֹעֵד ("appointed feast").** ESV: "festival" (1:4), "assembly" (1:15), "meeting place" and "festival" (2:6), "day of festival" (2:7), "festival day" (2:22). NASB95 keeps "appointed" audible in most places. **In the ESV the chain cannot be heard.**
5. **3:33.** ESV "he does not afflict **from his heart**" (literal: מִלִּבּוֹ); NASB95 "**willingly**". The ESV keeps the anthropology of the verse.
6. **Headings.** ESV heads ch. 3 "Great Is Your Faithfulness"; NASB95 heads it "Jeremiah Shares Israel's Affliction" and titles the book "The Lamentations of Jeremiah". **Neither heading is in the Hebrew.** See traps 1 and 5.
