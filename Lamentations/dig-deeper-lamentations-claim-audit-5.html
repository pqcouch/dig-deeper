What this audit tested, and how

Three blind auditors, run one after another. None of them saw the 1:1–22 dig, the earlier audits or each other’s reports. Each received a neutral brief, the claims restated, the staged corpus and the same lemma-index searches, and was told to try to break the claims.

Method for each claim:

  1. Check the wording on both sides in Hebrew and Greek.
  2. Count how rare the shared lemma set is, searching every entry wherever the lemma index splits a word-family.
  3. Run a fair chance test for any claim that rests on an “exclusive pair or triple of common words” (below).
  4. Search for rival sources, and rank the proposed partner among all partners of the Lamentations verse.
  5. Run a chance baseline for any field or design claim, and report every rank under at least two settings.
  6. Score the seven Hays criteria. “History of interpretation” is “not checked” throughout, because no commentary was consulted.
  7. Give a verdict and a recommended confidence.

The fair chance test — the audit’s main methodological result. The 1:1–22 dig’s new contacts were mostly two or three ordinary words found together in only two verses of the Hebrew Bible. Two auditors measured independently how often that happens by chance:

  • Auditor A. Of all pairs of moderately common words (each in 10–200 verses) that stand in one verse of Lam 1, about one in five (16–27 %, by setting) is shared with exactly one other passage. Twenty of the twenty-two verses have at least one such pair. Lam 2, Lam 4, Jer 14, Ps 102 and Isa 1 give the same rate (17–22 %).
  • Auditor B. Every verse of Lam 1 shares a pair or triple of words exclusively with a median of 19.5 different verses elsewhere. A set of words fixed in advance is rarely exclusive (about 15 % of pairs, 8 % of triples, 1.5 % of sets of four), but a set picked out of the overlap between two verses will almost always be exclusive somewhere.

So “only these two verses” carries no weight by itself. An exclusive common-word pair counts only when the partner also ranks high on the rarity-weighted measure, when the shared words agree in form, sense and syntax, when the contact recurs, or when the Greek keeps it. Every verdict below applies that rule.

Positive controls were run before every nil or “only” result. Every exclusivity search returned the Lamentations verse itself. The ketiv at Lam 2:19 (בליל, “in the night”) was found by lemma, not by spelling. Index splits searched: עֳנִי / ענה (“affliction” / “afflict”); the groan verb and noun; לֵב / לֵבָב (“heart”); the noun and verb of the cry root (רִנָּה / רנן); שַׂר / שָׂרָה (“prince” / “princess”); אַיִן / מֵאַיִן (“there is none” / “from where?”); the hope and wait families; and the two senses of נחם (“comfort” / “relent”) and of בלע (“swallow” / “confound”), which the index does not separate.

Claims Audited

The 1:1–22 headline claims (Auditor A)

L64 — “Lam 1:3–11 tells the exile in the vocabulary of the exodus”

Claim. עֳנִי (“affliction”) + עֲבֹדָה (“servitude”) within a verse either side only at Deut 26:6–7 and Lam 1:3; the groan root אנח + servitude only at Exod 2:23 and Lam 1:3–4; see + affliction + the divine name in eight verses, including Exod 3:7 and Lam 1:9; and in Greek, ταπείνωσις (“humiliation”) six times in poem 1, as Deut 26:7, with καταστενάζω (“groan”) shared with Exod 2:23.

What held. [T]

  • Affliction + servitude: Deut 26:7 and Lam 1:3 only at one and two verses either side; counting the verb ענה (“afflict”) as well, Deut 26:6 and Lam 1:3 only in a single verse.
  • Groan + servitude: Exod 2:23 only in one verse; Exod 2:23 and Lam 1:3–4 only at one to three verses either side.
  • The groan root occurs in Lamentations only in poem 1 (the verb at 1:4, 8, 11, 21; the noun at 1:22).
  • See + affliction + the divine name: the eight verses as listed.

What did not.

  • The preposition. In Lam 1:3 the “from” is attached to “much” (וּמֵרֹב עֲבֹדָה), not to “servitude” as in Exod 2:23 (מִן־הָעֲבֹדָה). Five other verses put “from” before servitude, among them Exod 6:9 and Isa 14:3. It is not a marker.
  • 1:9’s form. The imperative “See my affliction” (רְאֵה עָנְיִי) stands elsewhere only in the Psalter’s petitions — Ps 9:14 [Eng 9:13]; 25:18; 119:153 — and in Greek Lam 1:9 is word for word those psalms: ἴδε, κύριε, τὴν ταπείνωσίν μου. The exodus texts state that God saw. 1:9 is a psalm petition with the exodus behind it, not an exodus quotation.
  • The Greek. ταπειν- in poem 1 renders three Hebrew words, not two: affliction (1:3, 7, 9), “cause grief” (1:5, 12) and “despise” (1:8). And καταστενάζω also translates Ezek 9:4’s “those who groan” in Jerusalem — the same niphal participle as Lam 1:4, 11 — which matches Lamentations’ form more closely than Exod 2:23’s narrative verb.
  • The span. Of all the exclusive pairs in 1:3–11, Auditor A found only two with an exodus partner (Exod 1–15 or Deut 26), against 1.3 expected by chance.

What the claim missed.

  • Exod 3:7. See + מַכְאוֹב (“pain”) in one verse: Exod 3:7, Lam 1:12 and Lam 1:18 only. Exod 3:7 is also the only verse with both “affliction” and “pain”. At the burning bush God sees the affliction and knows the pain; Zion asks the LORD to see her affliction (1:9) and asks the passers-by and the peoples to see her pain (1:12, 18). [T] (Auditor A). The Greek loses it (ὀδύνη / ἄλγος).
  • Exod 1:11. Forced labour (מַס) + servitude within three verses: Exod 1:11 and Lam 1:1 only. Exod 1:11 has “taskmasters” — literally “princes of forced labour” — “to afflict them”; Lam 1:1 sets “princess” beside “forced labour”. Exod 1:14 adds “they made their lives bitter”, beside Lam 1:4 “she is bitter” (Auditor B, under L72).

The main session’s test. The field claim was re-tested with the contacts both auditors had found. The block Exod 1:1–3:10 with Deut 26:5–9 (62 verses) was set against random contiguous blocks of 62 verses elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, counting the exclusive same-verse pairs of Lam 1 (one verse either side in the partner text) that fall in the block:

  • words in 5–400 verses: 3 of 91 exclusive pairs fall in the exodus block (Lam 1:3 ~ Deut 26:7; Lam 1:9 and 1:12 ~ Exod 3:7); random blocks average 0.23; 0.7 % of them reach 3;
  • words in 10–200 verses: 2 of 56 (random mean 0.14; 1.7 % reach 2);
  • the exodus block’s three is equal to the best single chapters for poem 1 at this setting (Isa 61 and Jer 30, three each).

The block was chosen after the contacts were known, so the figure overstates; Exod 1:11 and Exod 2:23 do not enter it, because their contacts are across verses. The fair reading: an exodus colouring is present at two places in the poem, above chance but not a field. [I]

Rivals. Deut 28:65 (“no rest among the nations”, exclusive with 1:3; the Greek keeps it) and Deut 28:45 for the curse; Isa 14:3 for rest from hard servitude (closer in Greek than any Exodus verse); the Psalter for 1:9.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: moderate for an exodus colouring at 1:1–4 (Exod 1:11–14; 2:23; Deut 26:6–7) and 1:12, 18 (Exod 3:7); low–moderate for “1:3–11 in exodus vocabulary” as worded. Present 1:9 as the Psalter’s petition, with the exodus formula behind it. Correct the Greek.

L65 — “2 Kgs 14:26 is a precedent behind Lam 1:7–9”

  • Stronger than claimed. Lam 1:7 by itself holds affliction + helper + see + “there is none” — a four-word set found in one verse only at 2 Kgs 14:26 and Lam 1:7 (though in 1:7 the seers are the foes). 2 Kgs 14:26 is Lam 1:7’s best partner at three weightings (score 15.7, close to the chapter median). [T]
  • The Greek keeps it. ταπείνωσις + ὁ βοηθῶν (“the one who helps”) stand together in Swete only in these two verses.
  • Rival. Ps 107:10–12 shares more with Lam 1 overall (affliction; “rebel” at 107:11 ~ 1:18, 20; “stumble” at 107:12 ~ 1:14; “no helper”) but lacks “see”, and its Greek differs. “No helper” alone is idiom (eight verses).
  • Index caution. At 2 Kgs 14:26 the index files מֹרֶה (“bitter”) under מרה “rebel”, the same entry as Lam 1:18, 20. That apparent extra contact belongs to the index, not to the text.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: moderate. State the single-verse finding; name Ps 107:10–12; say “closest parallel”, not “precedent”, since the direction is open.

L66 — “Lam 1:2 reverses Jer 3:20”

  • Held. בגד (“deal treacherously”) + רֵעַ (“companion”) in one verse: Jer 3:20 and Lam 1:2 only; the cognate entries all return nothing. The sixteen verses with the intensive participle מְאַהֲבִים (“lovers”) are confirmed, all in Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Hosea, Zech 13:6 and Lam 1:19. [T]
  • Weak. At one verse either side the pair occurs in four passages (adding Job 6:14–15; Prov 22:12; 25:19). Jer 3:20 ranks only 11th–14th among Lam 1:2’s partners, and Jer 3 249th of 858 chapters. The Greek loses it: the friend words differ, and Jer 3:1 LXX reads “shepherds”.
  • Missed and stronger. Jer 30:12–17: “all your lovers have forgotten you” (30:14), “for the multitude of your iniquity” (עַל רֹב עֲוֺנֵךְ, 30:14–15; cf. Lam 1:5 עַל רֹב־פְּשָׁעֶיהָ), “your pain” (מַכְאֹבֵךְ, 30:15; cf. 1:12, 18), “I have done these things to you” (30:15; cf. 1:21 “You have done it”), the adversaries into captivity (30:16). The “lovers” participle with “pain” within a verse: Jer 30:14–15 and Lam 1:19 only (main session). Jer 30 ranks 25th–28th of 858 chapters for Lam 1.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: low–moderate for Jer 3:20; moderate for Jeremiah’s betrayed-wife language as the poem’s register. Add Jer 30:12–17 as the stronger Jeremian partner (moderate, new).

L67 — “Lam 1:3 reverses Hos 2:9 [Eng 2:7]; the Greek plus at 1:19 recalls Hos 2:7 LXX”

  • Held, Hebrew. Pursue + overtake is battlefield idiom (14 verses); with “find”, Hos 2:9 and Lam 1:3 only, in the Hebrew and in the Greek. Pursue + overtake + “love” within two verses: Hos 2:9 and Lam 1:2–3 only. Hos 2:9 is Lam 1:3’s best partner at all three weightings, and Hos 2 is 14th–15th of 858 chapters for Lam 1 — the only chapter named in L64–L67 in the top 25. What lifts it above chance is recurrence: the lovers participle (Hos 2 five times; Lam 1:19), the nakedness and the ended feasts. [T]
  • Not held, Greek. Seek + “did not find” closes a sentence in 28 verses of Swete. Lam 1:3 (οὐχ εὗρεν ἀνάπαυσιν) and 1:6 (οὐχ εὑρίσκοντες νομήν) already supply it inside the poem. The plus at 1:19 is explained as well by idiom or by harmonisation within the poem as by Hosea.
  • Rival. Deut 28:45, 65 for the curse colour of 1:3 [S: audit]; Jer 29:14 (exile + nations + find).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: moderate–high for Hos 2:9 at 1:3; low (Uncertain) for the Greek plus at 1:19 — demote it to a note.

L78 — Design: “Poem 1 plants answers later in the book”

  • Every count is right (Auditor A): the adjective רַב (“great, many”) at 1:1 (twice), 1:22 and 3:23; the noun רֹב (“multitude”) at 1:3, 1:5 and 3:32; the causative of שׁוב (“turn back”) with a first-person object and the LORD as subject only at 1:13 and 5:21. [T]
  • No sign of design. Of the 114 words found in 3–8 verses of Lamentations, 14 have a “poem 1, then poem 3 or 5” spread; random placement gives 11.9 (P ≈ 0.29). Picking two of fourteen chance-level chains is selection, not design. The inclusio 1:1 / 1:22 is the only first-and-last-verse word in all five poems — against about 0.5 expected; not distinguishable from chance.
  • Rival explanations. The Psalter already pairs “multitude” with transgression and with lovingkindness: Ps 51:3 [Eng 51:1] has all three words in one verse (the only verse that does), and Ps 5 has “the multitude of Your lovingkindness” (5:8 [Eng 5:7]) and “the multitude of their transgressions” (5:11 [Eng 5:10]). The hiphil-then-qal shape of 5:21 is the idiom of Jer 31:18 — its only exact twin [S: audit 3] — so the qal (1:8) → hiphil (1:13) order of poem 1 is not needed to explain it.
  • Missed. The verb רבה (“multiply”) at 2:5, 22; “turn back” with the LORD as subject at 2:3; “let us return” (וְנָשׁוּבָה) at 3:40; and the fact that 5:21’s “that we may return” is an unpointed ketiv whose qere is 3:40’s form.
  • Greek. Lam 3:22–24 are absent from the Old Greek, so “great is Your faithfulness” has no Greek counterpart; the Greek instead links 1:1 πεπληθυμμένη with πλῆθος at 1:3, 5 and 3:32.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate as “planted answers”; the chains stand (high) as echoes a reader hears, not as a design the poem lays down. Keep them out of any overview as design.

The dig’s counts (fact-check)

  • The refrain. מְנַחֵם (“comforter”) five times in poem 1 (1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21); four with אֵין (“there is none”; at 1:2 with לָהּ between); 1:16 “far from me is a comforter”; the root six times in the book. Confirmed — the overview’s and sweep’s “five times” for the phrase needs correcting. [T]
  • כִּי (“for, that”). 29 in the book; 14 in poem 1, in 11 verses; 1 in poem 2 (2:13); 8 in poem 3; 4 in poem 4; 2 in poem 5. Confirmed.
  • The LORD as agent. The narrator names the LORD as subject only at 1:5 and 1:17, as claimed. But at 1:10 the narrator addresses God: “whom You commanded” (צִוִּיתָה) — the narrator’s only apostrophe to God in the poem. The dig’s statement that the narrator “never addresses God” (Tool 6) is wrong. And the comparison was unlike-for-like: like for like, named subject — narrator 2, Zion 4; any subject — narrator 3, Zion 13–15. The contrast stands; the figures should be stated on one basis.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: high on the counts.

Psalms, Prophets and the Writings at Lam 1 (Auditor B)

L68 — Ps 31:8–11 [Eng 31:7–10] as the Psalter’s closest partner for Zion’s speech

  • Held. “For I am in distress” (כִּי צַר־לִי): three verses (Ps 31:10; 69:18; Lam 1:20). Stumble + strength: four verses. The identical form כֹּחִי (“my strength”) at Ps 31:11 and Lam 1:14, each with sin as the cause (“because of my iniquity”; “the yoke of my transgressions”). Ps 31 is 4th and 14th of 1,136 twelve-verse windows; 2nd at eight-verse windows. The contact is particular to poem 1: the same four psalm verses rank 149th against Lam 2, 110th against Lam 3 and 308th against Lam 5. [T]
  • The Greek tightens it: ὅτι θλίβομαι (“for I am in distress”) = Lam 1:20; ἠσθένησεν … ἡ ἰσχύς μου (“my strength has failed”) = Lam 1:14 word for word; τὴν ταπείνωσίν μου = 1:9.
  • Co-equal rival. Ps 143:3–6 — the enemy pursuing (1:3, 6), remembering the days of old (1:7), spreading out the hands (1:17) — ties or beats Ps 31:8–11 at the strict setting; Ps 143:3 is also Lam 3:6 verbatim [S: audit 3].
  • Errors. יָגוֹן (“sorrow”) is a separate lemma from the verb יגה; the link is by root only, and Lam 1 has no יָגוֹן. At Ps 69:21 [Eng 69:20] “but there was none” belongs to “sympathy”; the comforters clause is “and for comforters, but I found none”.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: moderate–high — one of the two closest Psalter partners of poem 1 (with Ps 143:3–6), and particular to it.

L69 — Jer 8:23 [Eng 9:1] at Lam 1:2; Jer 13:17 at Lam 1:16

  • Held. Tears + weep + night: Jer 8:23 and Lam 1:2 only. Weep + run down + eye: Jer 13:17 and Lam 1:16 only. Both are their Lamentations verses’ best partners (17.1; 16.5). Jer 8 ranks 6th–8th of 858 chapters for Lam 1. The Greek keeps both.
  • Weak. “The eye runs down with tears or water” is a stock phrase (seven verses, three of them in Lamentations). And Jer 8:23 shares an equally exclusive triple (weep + eye + water) with Lam 1:16 — so 1:16 has two “exclusive” Jeremiah partners.
  • Rivals. Jer 14:17 ~ Lam 2:18 is the stronger Jeremian weeping contact [S: audit]; Jer 31:15–16 (weep + comfort + tears within a verse, exclusive) at 1:2.

Verdicts. L69a Jer 8:23: Confirmed with nuance — moderate, framed as the weeping-prophet passage Jer 8:18–23 beside Lam 1 (1:2, 16, 22) [S: audit] for 8:18. L69b Jer 13:17: Needs reframing — low–moderate, one member of the weeping-eye idiom.

L70 — Zeph 3:18 “answers” Lam 1:4

  • Held, and rarer than claimed. The index files Zeph 3:18 נוּגֵי and Lam 1:4 נוּגוֹת under יגה, and they are the only two niphal participles of the verb in the Hebrew Bible. Grieve + appointed feast: these two verses only. [T]
  • But the text is contested. BHS at Lam 1:4 recommends reading נְהוּגוֹת (“led away”), with the Greek ἀγόμεναι and Aquila and Symmachus. The single rare word that carries the claim is the one BHS proposes to emend. And Swete’s Zeph 3:18 has neither “grieving” nor “feast”. The Greek loses the link at both ends.
  • Zeph 3:19. “Those who afflict you” (מְעַנַּיִךְ) is the verb ענה, not the noun of 1:3, 7, 9 — a root link only.
  • Rival, missed. Isa 61:2–3: “to comfort all who mourn … the mourners of Zion” (אֲבֵלֵי צִיּוֹן). Mourning (אָבֵל) + Zion: Isa 61:3 and Lam 1:4 only (main session). Isa 61:3 is Lam 1:4’s best partner (13.0, just ahead of Zeph 3:18 at 12.7), and Isa 61 is 5th of 858 chapters for poem 1.

Verdict: Uncertain — flag for research. Confidence: low–moderate (moderate only if MT נוּגוֹת is defended against BHS). Drop “answers”; state the textual problem; name Isa 61:2–3 as the stronger answer to 1:4 and to the comfort refrain (new candidate).

L71 — Lam 1:15 “inverts” Lev 23

  • Verse classification correct: of the ten verses with קרא (“proclaim”) + מוֹעֵד, the festival sense is Lev 23:2, 4, 37 and Lam 2:22; three are the tent of meeting, two a set time.
  • Weak. In Lev 23 Israel proclaims; at 1:15 the Lord does. The Lev 23 verses rank 52nd–54th of 157 partners for 1:15; Lev 23 ranks 430th–530th of 858 chapters. The Greek reads 1:15 as καιρόν (“a set time”), not a feast; only 2:22 has ἑορτή.
  • Idiom. Proclaiming a sacred occasion is common with other nouns (fast, ten verses; solemn assembly, five; convocation, six; release, five).
  • Missed. Jer 34:17: “I am proclaiming a release to you — to the sword, to pestilence and to famine” — the LORD proclaims a sacred institution and turns it into judgement on his people.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate. The feast reversal is Lamentations’ own (1:4 → 1:15 → 2:6–7 → 2:22); Lev 23 is background vocabulary. Name Jer 34:17; note the Greek καιρός.

L72 — Lam 1:1 “has become forced labour” recalls Deut 20:11

  • The count is wrong as worded. Of the 13 verses with הָיָה + מַס, four do not say “became forced labour” (Josh 17:13; Judg 1:28; 1 Kgs 5:27, 28 [Eng 5:13, 14]); the true set is nine.
  • Not special. Deut 20:11 is not a scoring partner of 1:1 at the default weighting (55th of 546 at a looser one). The Greek ἐγενήθη εἰς φόρον (“became tribute”) matches Judg 1:30, 33, 35 word for word, not Deut 20:11.
  • Missed and stronger. Exod 1:11–14 (see L64): forced labour + servitude within three verses only there and Lam 1:1; “princes of forced labour” beside “princess … forced labour”; “to afflict them”; “they made their lives bitter”. Isa 31:8 (“his young men shall become forced labour”) has its own hook to 1:15 “my young men”.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low for Deut 20:11. Recast as a forced-labour reversal with Exod 1:11–14 leading (low–moderate to moderate, folded into L64), and Judg 1 as the Greek’s own echo.

L73 — Eccl 4:1 ~ Lam 1:2

  • Held. Tears + “no comforter” (and tears + comfort alone): Eccl 4:1 and Lam 1:2 only. Eccl 4:1 is Lam 1:2’s second partner (15.9, above the chapter median), at both settings; Eccl 4 ranks 17th–25th of 858 chapters. The Greek keeps it (οὐκ ἔστιν αὐτοῖς παρακαλῶν, twice).
  • The dig’s list of “no comforter” verses has three false hits: Jer 8:6 is the “relent” sense; at Jer 31:15 the “not” belongs to the children; at Zech 10:2 it belongs to “no shepherd”. The true verses are Eccl 4:1 (twice) and Lam 1:2, 9, 17, 21; with Ps 69:21 and Lam 1:16 near.
  • Missed (a lemma split). Nah 3:7, “where shall I seek comforters for you?” — filed under “from where?”, not “there is none”. (Audit 2 discarded Nah 3:7 at 2:13 because 2:13 lacks “grieve”; this is a different contact, with the refrain.)
  • The neighbouring-book argument is a fact about codex order, not evidence of a relationship.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: moderate. Correct the list; add Nah 3:7 and Jer 31:15–16 as rivals; drop the neighbour argument.

L74 — Job 2:11 ~ Lam 1:21

  • Manufactured. The four-word set was chosen from the overlap; three of its words (hear + calamity + come) occur together in fifteen verses. Job 2:11 ranks only 14th for 1:21 (12.1, below the median). The senses of בוא differ (“came” against “You brought”), and BHS queries Lam 1:21’s form.
  • Better fits. Job 42:11 (“they consoled and comforted him for all the adversity that the LORD had brought on him”) and Ezek 14:22–23 have the causative “brought” with comfort and calamity. In Greek, Job 42:11 matches Lam 1:21 on all four counts, including ἐπάγω (“bring upon”) with the Lord as agent.
  • What is real. Job 1–2 is a neighbour of poem 1 as a whole (Job 2 ranks 10th, and 2nd at a looser setting, of 858): friend + comfort only Gen 38:12, Job 2:11 and Lam 1:2; bones at 1:13; Job 2:13’s “pain” (כְּאֵב) beside 1:12, 18 (the same root).

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate. Restate as a contrast with the Job prologue (Job 2:11–13; 42:11) and Ezek 14:22; withdraw the “only”.

L75 — Ruth 4:15 ~ Lam 1:16; Ruth 1:21 ~ Lam 1:13

  • “Restorer of life” is an idiom Lam 1 itself uses three times (1:11, 16, 19); Ps 19:8 [Eng 19:7] has the same participle. Ruth 4:15 ranks 496th of 779 partners for 1:16. In Greek, Ps 18:8 [MT 19:8] is as close as Ruth.
  • Ruth 1:21’s הֱשִׁיבַנִי means “brought me back (home)”; Lam 1:13’s means “turned me back” in defeat. The Greek of 1:13 (ἀπέστρεψέν με εἰς τὰ ὀπίσω) is Ps 43:11 [MT 44:11] word for word.
  • Ruth 1:20’s “bitter” is a different lemma; “bitter to her” is 2 Kgs 4:27.
  • אַלְמָנָה (“widow”) does not occur in Ruth.

Verdict: Discard as a lexical claim. Confidence: low. A thematic juxtaposition of the Megilloth may be kept only if labelled as such. At 1:13, Ps 44:11 is the partner (new candidate).

L76 — Neh 4:4 [Eng 4:10] ~ Lam 1:14

  • Exclusivity depends on adding the common verb “be able” (182 verses); without it the set is four verses (see L68). Neh 4:4 is 2nd for 1:14, behind Ezek 33:12 (stumble + transgression + “not able”). The Greek of Nehemiah loses “fail”; the Greek of Ps 31:11 matches Lam 1:14 word for word.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate as a verbal contact; low as an allusion. Present it as one of four “strength fails” verses; Ps 31:11 leads.

L77 — Isa 51:3 and Zech 1:17 as the canon’s lexical answers to “no comforter”

  • Held. Comfort + Zion in one verse: four verses (Isa 51:3; Zech 1:17; Lam 1:17; 2:13). Comfort + Zion + Jerusalem: Zech 1:17, Lam 1:17 and 2:13 only; Zech 1:17 is Lam 1:17’s best partner. [T]
  • Less exclusive than it looks. Comfort + Jerusalem occurs in eight verses (with “relent” senses removed, Isa 52:9; 66:13 and Ezek 14:22 join). The comfort-plus-Zion-or-Jerusalem field is overwhelmingly Isaianic: Isa 40:1; 49:13; 51:3, 12, 19; 52:9; 61:2; 66:13. The Greek of Zech 1:17 reads ἐλεήσει (“will have mercy”), losing “comfort” (1:13 keeps it).
  • Missed. Isa 51:19 “how shall I comfort you?” — the form אֲנַחֲמֵךְ only there and at Lam 2:13 (already the audited partner of 2:13 [S: audit]); Isa 54:11 “afflicted, storm-tossed, not comforted”; Isa 61:2–3 (L70).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance as a field. Confidence: moderate that Isa 40–66 (with Zech 1:12–17) use the vocabulary that answers Lam 1–2; low for “the canon’s answers” and for singling out Isa 51:3 and Zech 1:17. Say “answering vocabulary”.

L55 — Ps 88:2–3 [Eng 88:1–2] behind the night cry of 2:18–19

  • Held, with a condition. With noun and verb of the cry root merged, cry + “cry out” + “night” within one to three verses: Ps 88:3 and Lam 2:19 only. Without the merge, neither text matches. “Cry out” + “night” in one verse: Ps 88:2 and Lam 2:18 only — the most striking single fact. The ketiv בליל at 2:19 was found by lemma. [T]

Ps 77:2–3 and Ps 63:6–8 are equally exclusive rivals. The Greek loses the link (Lam 2:19 renders רֹנִּי as ἀγαλλίασαι, “exult”).

Lam 2:19 turns the summons to joy into a cry of lament in the night. And Ps 88 is Lam 3’s top psalm partner at every setting [S: audit 3], so the relation is real at book level.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate. “Shares the cry-out-at-night collocation with Ps 88:2–3”, not “lies behind”; Jer 14:17 is 2:18’s primary partner; add the joy-call inversion.

L56 — Ps 62:9 [Eng 62:8] behind “pour out your heart … before the Lord” (2:19)

  • Held. “Pour out the heart” — counting both spellings of “heart” — only at Ps 62:9 and Lam 2:19, both imperatives (a match the claim missed); pour + heart + “before” likewise. The Greek keeps it (ἐκχέω + καρδία in Swete only at Ps 61:9 [MT 62:9], Jer 22:17 and Lam 2:19).
  • Co-equal rivals. Lam 2:19 sits at the meeting of three exclusive triples: Ps 62:9 (heart, before God), Ps 22:15 [Eng 22:14] (pour + heart + “like water” — the simile is shared only there; audit 4 was right that Ps 22:15 does not pour out the heart, but it holds the other three words) and Ps 102:1 (faint + pour + “before the LORD”; faint + pour in one verse only Ps 102:1 and Lam 2:11, 12, 19).
  • Missed, in the claim’s favour. Silence + salvation in one verse: Ps 62:2 [Eng 62:1] and Lam 3:26 only — a second exclusive Ps 62–Lamentations contact (new candidate).

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate. A node in a pool of prayer idiom, with Ps 62 the closest wording.

L57 — Isa 14:12 at 2:1

“How!” + “heaven” in one verse: Isa 14:12 and Lam 2:1 only, and the Greek keeps it. But a fifth of pairs of this class are exclusive by chance, and אֵיכָה (“How!”) is the book’s fixed opener. Isa 66:1 (heaven, earth, footstool) is Lam 2:1’s second-best partner (16.9); Isa 14:12 is 25th (9.8); Isa 14 ranks 224th–399th of 858 chapters, Isa 66 40th–59th.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low — a shared motif of falling from heaven to earth. This partly reverses audit 4, which preferred Isa 14:12 to Isa 66:1: by rank Isa 66:1 is the stronger verbal partner, by audit 4’s own reasoning its footstool is Zion-cult idiom (Ps 99:5; 132:7). Rate both low–moderate; neither is a source.

L58 — Jer 21:5 at 2:3–5

“Fight” (לחם) is absent from Lamentations; what 2:3–4 shares with Jer 21:5 is anger + wrath (39 verses). Jer 21:5 ranks 548th as a partner of 2:4. But Jer 21 is a strong partner of Lam 2 (8th–24th of 858 chapters), through other verses: “like fire my wrath” (21:12) — only Jer 4:4, 21:12 and Lam 2:4; fire devouring all around (21:14; cf. 2:3); “he will not spare” with the sword (21:7) — three verses, Jer 21:7, Lam 2:21 and 2 Chr 36:17.

Verdict: Discard as worded (low). Reframe to Jer 21:4–14 — moderate (new candidate). God fighting against the city is a shared concept; the verb is not shared.

L59 — Zeph 3:3–8 with Lam 2 and Lam 4

  • Error carried from audit 4. At Lam 2:6 the LORD does the violence (“He has done violence to His booth”); the priest is the object. Lam 2:6 does not describe “priests’ violence”. Violence + priest occurs only at Ezek 22:26, Zeph 3:4 and Lam 2:6, but the roles are reversed.
  • Lam 4: strong. Pour + burning anger + fire + devour: Zeph 3:8 and Lam 4:11 only; Zeph 3:8 is Lam 4:11’s best partner (23.9). Prophets + priests + “in her midst” + righteous within two verses: Zeph 3:3–5 and Lam 4:13 only (with the roles reversed again: in Zephaniah the righteous one in her midst is the LORD). Zeph 3 is first of 858 chapters for Lam 4 at both settings; Zeph 3:1–8 ranks 3rd–6th of 3,364 eight-verse prophetic windows.
  • Lam 2: weak. Zeph 3:1–8 ranks 168th–196th of the same windows; Ezek 22:24–31 ranks 7th. Falsehood + whitewash: Ezek 22:28 and Lam 2:14 only [S: audit]. The strongest Zephaniah contact with Lam 2 is Zeph 2:15 at 2:15, already confirmed [S: audit 4].

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: moderate–high for Zeph 3:1–8 as Lam 4’s partner (4:11, 13); low for Lam 2, where Ezek 22:24–31 is the partner (moderate–high at 2:6, 14). Correct the reading of 2:6.

L60 — Ps 37:12–14 as a co-equal rival to Ps 35 at Lam 2

“Gnash” occurs in five verses only, always with teeth. Gnash + teeth + “plot” within a verse: Ps 37:12 and Lam 2:16–17 only — a genuinely low-chance result (0.3 % of such triples are exclusive). Ps 37 is 1st–2nd of 150 psalms for Lam 2 at all four settings (Ps 35 1st–11th). But for 2:16 itself Ps 35 is closer: “we have swallowed” (Ps 35:25), the mouths opened wide, “our eye has seen”; and κατεπίομεν stands in Swete only at Ps 34:25 [MT 35:25] and Lam 2:16 [S: audit 4]. Bend + bow is idiom (sixteen verses); Ps 7:13 [Eng 7:12] has God as the archer, as Lam 2:4 and 3:12. The agents are reversed (the wicked in Ps 37; the LORD in Lam 2).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Confidence: moderate — co-equal at chapter scale; Ps 35 closer at 2:16; Ps 7:13 for the divine archer.

L61 — Hab 2:16 at 4:21

Cup + “also”: Hab 2:16 and Lam 4:21 only — but 29 % of pairs of this class are exclusive by chance, and “also” is in 661 verses. The verbs differ (Habakkuk “come round”, Lamentations “pass over”). A better Habakkuk set: cup + drunk + nakedness within a verse, Hab 2:15–16 and Lam 4:21 only. Isa 51:17–23 is at least as strong: cup + drunk + “pass over” within a verse only Isa 51:22 and Lam 4:21; cup + “no more” only Isa 51:22 and Lam 4:21–22; and Isa 52 is the 2nd chapter of 858 for Lam 4. In Greek the nakedness vanishes at both ends; Lam 4:21’s “cup of the Lord” (κυρίου added) matches Hab 2:16 and Isa 51:17.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate for Hab 2:16; moderate for Isa 51:17–23 as the stronger partner — which reverses audit 4’s preference at L36. Correct the verb pairing.

L62 — Ps 130:5–7 behind 3:21–26

The four-word set (lovingkindness, morning, hope, wait) is exclusive only at three verses either side or wider; it fails at one and two. The Lam 3:22 window holds four equally exclusive four-word sets, pointing to Ps 130, Ps 98, Hos 2 and Ps 25. Ps 130 ranks 52nd–116th of 150 psalms for Lam 3. Ps 92:3 [Eng 92:2] is tighter: morning + faithfulness only there and at Lam 3:23; Lam 3:23’s best partner [S: audit 4]. Lam 3:22–24 are absent from the Old Greek; BHS records תַּמּוּ (“they are not ended”) for MT תָמְנוּ (“we are not consumed”).

Verdict: Needs reframing. Confidence: low–moderate. Ps 92:3 leads at 3:22–23; Ps 62:2 at 3:26.

L63 — Ps 55:10 [Eng 55:9]: בַּלַּע אֲדֹנָי at 2:2

The two words stand adjacent only at Ps 55:10 and Lam 2:2. But the forms differ (imperative / perfect), Ps 55:10’s verb is probably “confound” (paired with “divide their tongue”; cf. Isa 19:3), “swallow” is a key word of Lam 2 (five times) and אֲדֹנָי its preferred divine title; and 15 % of Lamentations’ adjacent pairs of this class are shared with exactly one other verse. The Greek reads both as “swallow up” (καταπόντισον / κατεπόντισεν), a closer echo than the Hebrew. Ps 55:10–12’s siege vocabulary (walls, day and night, the city’s squares) deserves its own test.

Verdict: Discard. Confidence: low.

Critical assessment — the gaps in this audit

  1. History of interpretation was not checked. Every verdict rests on the corpus. Hos 2 and Jer 30 at Lam 1, Exod 3:7 at 1:12, and the BHS recommendation at 1:4 are the likeliest to have a commentary history; all are on the Logos list.
  2. The auditors were blind to the dig, but not to its phrasing. The claims were restated from the dig. Their corrections to the restatement (the preposition at 1:3; the three Hebrew words behind ταπειν-; Ruth has no “widow”; Lam 2:6’s agent) show the wording was tested, not trusted.
  3. The main session’s block test (L64) was post hoc. The exodus block was drawn after the contacts were known. It is reported as support for a colouring, not as proof of a field, and its P-values overstate.
  4. The chance tests measure exclusivity, not meaning. A pair that beats chance can still be idiom; a pair at chance can still be an echo a reader hears. The rule in What the audit adds is a floor, not a ceiling.
  5. The poem-distribution baseline (L78) treats every word alike. A designed echo might use a rare word in a marked place; the test cannot see that. Its verdict (“no evidence of design”) is a statement about frequency, and leaves room for a reading.
  6. One textual question was found, not settled: BHS at 1:4. Until it is read in Logos, the יגה link at 1:4 (and Zeph 3:18) carries [unchecked — apparatus spread].
