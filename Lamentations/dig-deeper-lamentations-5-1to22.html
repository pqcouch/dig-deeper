Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

  1. Poem 5’s closing address to God (5:20–22) is spoken in the words of two prophetic prayers — Isa 64 and Jer 14 — while Isa 54 stands as the promise that reverses it. (Rewritten in v1.1.)

    The verbal partners [S: audit 3]:

    • 5:22b ~ Isa 64:8 [Eng 64:9] — קצף (“be angry”) + עַד (“to”) + מְאֹד (“very”): “do not be exceedingly angry, O LORD” / “you are exceedingly angry with us”. Exclusive. Isa 64 is the first of 858 chapters against Lam 5 in both scans.
    • 5:22a ~ Jer 14:19 — the doubled מאס (“utterly reject”), with God as subject. Exclusive. Jer 14:20–21 adds the fathers’ iniquity, “we have sinned”, the throne and “remember” (5:1, 7, 16, 19).

    Isa 54 — what v1.0 claimed, and what stands. Poem 5 speaks to the LORD with seven roots:

    • זכר (“remember”, 5:1);
    • חֶרְפָּה (“reproach”, 5:1);
    • אַלְמָנָה (“widow”, 5:3);
    • שׁכח (“forget”, 5:20);
    • עזב (“forsake”, 5:20);
    • מאס (“reject”, 5:22);
    • קצף (“be angry”, 5:22).

    All seven occur in Isa 54:4–9, addressed to Zion, each one reversed:

    • “the reproach of your widowhood you will remember no more” (54:4), and the shame of your youth you will “forget”;
    • “like a wife forsaken … when she is rejected” (54:6);
    • “for a brief moment I forsook you” (54:7);
    • “in overflowing anger” (54:8);
    • “I have sworn that I will not be angry with you” (54:9).

    v1.0 reported that no other six-verse window in the Hebrew Bible holds five or more of the seven. Claim audit 3 showed that uniqueness to be a selection effect [S: audit 3]: the seven were chosen from Lam 5 after the two texts had been read together, and Lam 5’s other roots of similar frequency find seven-or-more matches in 14 windows of 7 chapters (Jer 14:16–21, Hos 4:5–10 and Lam 2:16–21 hold eight). Control poems behave the same way. Isa 54 also reverses the subject: in 54:4 it is Zion who forgets and does not remember.

    What stands: Isa 54 is a thematic counterpart to Lam 5 — the forsaken wife and widow, the brief abandonment, the oath against anger — with one exclusive tie elsewhere in the book (Isa 54:8 ~ Lam 3:32).

    • Surfaced by: Tool 11 (independent search; claim audit 3), Repetition, Vocabulary.
    • Warrant: [T] for the contacts; [I] for the counterpart reading. The direction of dependence is open.
    • Confidence: high (Isa 64:8; Jer 14:19 wording); moderate (Isa 54 as counterpart); low (the seven-root design).

  3. The two confessions (5:7, 5:16) hold the fathers’ guilt and our own together, in the words of Jer 14:20.

    • 5:7: “Our fathers sinned … we have borne (סָבָלְנוּ) their iniquities (עֲוֺנֹתֵיהֶם)”.
    • 5:16: “Woe to us, for we have sinned” (כִּי חָטָאנוּ).
    • סבל (“bear a load”) + עָוֹן (“iniquity”) occurs in only two verses: Lam 5:7 and Isa 53:11, “and he will bear their iniquities” (וַעֲוֺנֹתָם הוּא יִסְבֹּל).
    • The lemma set עָוֹן + אָב (“father”) + חטא (“sin”) occurs only at Jer 14:20 (“the iniquity of our fathers, for we have sinned against you”), Ezek 18:20, and Lam 5:7. Jer 14:20 holds both of Lam 5’s confessions in one verse.

    The canon answers 5:7 twice. Ezek 18 and Jer 31:29–30 say each bears his own iniquity. Isa 53 says the Servant bears theirs.

    • v1.1 — Isa 53:11 lowered [S: audit 3]. סבל (“bear”) is a rare synonym within a common idiom: “bear iniquity” with the usual verb (נשׂא + עָוֹן) occurs in 37 verses. The Greek loses the link, and the theology runs the other way (complaint / vicarious bearing). Use Isa 53:11 as canonical reflection — “the only other place this verb takes ‘iniquities’” — not as an echo.
    • Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tool 11, Christological Reading, Difficult Verses.
    • Warrant: [T] for the contacts.
    • Confidence: moderate–high (Jer 14:20–21); low (Isa 53:11 as allusion). The Greek loses the Isaiah link: Lam 5:7 has ὑπέσχομεν (“we underwent”), Isa 53:11 ἀνοίσει (“he will bear”).

  4. God is addressed only in the frame. 5:19 confesses, in the words of Ps 102:13, that the LORD abides when the mountain does not.

    • The address. The only second-person forms in the poem are in 5:1 and 5:19–22 (WLC morphology). The catalogue of 5:2–18 is spoken in God’s hearing, but never to him.
    • The sequence. 5:18 ends on “Mount Zion, which lies desolate”; 5:19 begins “You, O LORD, abide for ever (לְעוֹלָם תֵּשֵׁב)”. What the community confesses: the mountain could not abide for ever; the LORD does. [I] — a reading of the sequence 5:18 → 5:19.
    • The wording of 5:19 [S: audit 3]:
      • Ps 102:13 [Eng 102:12] — יְהוָה + עוֹלָם + ישׁב + דּוֹר (“LORD … for ever … abide … generation”): only Ps 102:13 and Lam 5:19. High.
      • Ps 89:5 [Eng 89:4] — כִּסֵּא (“throne”) + דּוֹר וָדוֹר + עוֹלָם: exclusive. In the psalm the throne is David’s; here it is the LORD’s. Moderate–high.
      • Ps 93:2 — “you” + “for ever” + “throne”: exclusive. Moderate–high.
      • Joel 4:20 [Eng 3:20] — “Judah shall abide for ever, and Jerusalem to generation and generation”: the pair said of a place. Moderate.
      • v1.0’s Ps 125:1 (“Mount Zion … abides for ever”) is lowered to low as an allusion; it remains a thematic parallel.
    • Surfaced by: Structure, Tool 11, Move 4 (1:1 “she sits alone” → 5:19 “you sit for ever”), Original Audience (4:12).
    • Warrant: [T] for the wording; [I] for the transfer.
    • Confidence: high (the frame; Ps 102:13); moderate (the mountain-to-LORD reading).

  5. Poem 5 asks God to undo what poem 2 said he did. The pulpit text hides two of the links.

    The links back to poem 2:

    • Poem 2: “he did not remember his footstool” (2:1). Poem 5: “Remember, O LORD” (5:1).
    • Poem 2: “the LORD has made Zion forget appointed feast and sabbath” (2:6). Poem 5: elders and young men have “ceased” (שָׁבָתוּ) … “the joy of our heart has ceased” (שָׁבַת, 5:14–15), and “why do you forget us?” (5:20). שׁכח (“forget”) occurs in the book only at 2:6 and 5:20.

    The link to 3:33. The piel of ענה (“afflict, violate”) occurs in the book only at:

    • 3:33, “he does not afflict from his heart”;
    • 5:11, “they afflicted (ravished) the women in Zion”.

    What the Lord does not do from his heart, the enemy has done to Zion’s women.

    What the pulpit text hides. The ESV renders 5:11 “raped” (and NASB95 “ravished”), so the 3:33 link cannot be heard. The ESV’s “our heart has become sick” (5:17) breaks the echo of “my heart is faint” (1:22), which the NASB95 keeps.

    • Surfaced by: Move 4, Repetition, Vocabulary, Translations.
    • Warrant: [T] for the chains.
    • Confidence: high.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level (overview v0.2.1; sweep v1.1): to give a ruined people ordered words — confession, protest, memory and petition — so that they can keep addressing the LORD when he is silent. The ground is that his חֶסֶד (“lovingkindness”) is not exhausted and his throne endures (3:22; 5:19). [I]

Passage level. Poem 5 teaches the community what the man of ch. 3 learned, now in the plural. It does four things:

  • Remember. It asks God to remember (5:1).
  • Lay it before him. It sets the whole present misery before him, item by item (5:2–18).
  • Confess. It makes both confessions, the fathers’ sin and ours (5:7, 16).
  • Hold God to his throne. It confesses that his throne abides (5:19), asks “why?” (5:20), asks to be turned back and renewed (5:21), and states honestly the one condition under which the prayer would fail (5:22).

The book ends by handing the reader the words to go on praying, not by announcing the answer. [I]

Words per verse fall to about 6.6 in this poem (145 words in 22 verses, WLC). The alphabet is gone; only the count of 22 remains. The poem is the shortest and the most corporate in the book. About a quarter of its words carry a first-person plural marker (36 of 145 by WLC morphology). [T] for the counts; [I] for the reading.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

What the preceding movement has set up.

  1. Poem 4 ended on the book’s first word of a future [T]:
    • “the punishment of your iniquity is completed (תַּם), daughter of Zion; he will no longer exile you” (4:22);
    • its last stanza before that reported the anointed king “captured in their pits” (4:20).
  2. The book has asked God to “see” four times, and once he has “seen”.
    • “See, O LORD” — 1:9, 11, 20; 2:20.
    • “You have seen” — the man, 3:59–60.
    • The full collocation ראה (“see”) + נבט (“look”) + יְהוָה (“the LORD”) occurs in the book at 1:11, 1:12 (addressed to passers-by), 2:20 and 5:1. [T]
  3. Poem 2 said what God had done to memory.
    • “He did not remember (לֹא־זָכַר) his footstool” (2:1).
    • “The LORD has made Zion forget (שִׁכַּח) appointed feast and sabbath” (2:6). [T]
  4. Poem 3 moved one man from “I” to “we”.
    • The Masoretic text has the poem’s first “we” at 3:22 (“we are not consumed”).
    • The cohortatives begin at 3:40 (“let us search … let us return”).
    • The man asked God to “remember” (3:19). [T]

Why poem 5 exists here, after 4:22 and not elsewhere.

  • The promise of 4:22 has been spoken, and nothing has yet changed. The inheritance is still with strangers (5:2), slaves still rule (5:8), and Mount Zion is still desolate (5:18).
  • So the book cannot end on 4:22. It must end in prayer. A declared end to punishment is not yet a restored people. The gap between the word (4:22) and the world (5:2–18) is filled by a community asking God to act (5:21). [I]
  • The “See!” of chs 1–2 is taken up for the last time, and now by “us”. Zion, the narrator and the man prayed it in the singular; the community prays it in the plural (5:1). [T]
  • Poem 2’s “he did not remember … he made Zion forget” is turned into petition: “Remember” (5:1); “why do you forget us?” (5:20). [T]
  • The man’s “we” (3:22, 40–47) becomes the whole poem’s voice. [T]

Implication. Poem 5 is not an appendix of complaint. It is where the book puts the reader: after the promise (4:22), before its fulfilment, praying. Its open ending (5:22) belongs there because the book’s own position is an open one. [I]

Revisited after Tool 11 (v1.1). The prophetic partners of poem 5 are prayers (Isa 64; Jer 14:19–22) and promises of reversal (Jer 31:13, 18; Isa 54 as a counterpart). Poem 5 prays the prayers and does not yet see the promises. [I] Moderate; synthetic. (v1.0’s “Amos 8 the threat fulfilled” is withdrawn: 5:20 inverts Amos 8:7 rather than fulfilling it [S: audit 3].)

Immediate context. Before, 4:21–22: Edom’s cup and Zion’s punishment ended. After: nothing — the book ends.

Canonical neighbour after. In BHS order (and Leningrad’s), Esther follows. Esther 9:22 is the only other verse that joins הפך (“turn”) and אֵבֶל (“mourning”) as a reversal enacted: “the month that was turned (נֶהְפַּךְ) for them from sorrow (יָגוֹן) to gladness and from mourning to a good day”. It uses the same niphal form as Lam 5:15 (נֶהְפַּךְ), and it notes that “they rested (נָחוּ) from their enemies” (cf. 5:5, “no rest is given us”). See Tool 14. [T] for the wording; [I] for the canonical reading. Moderate.

Devices.

  • The address frame. God is addressed in the second person only in 5:1 and 5:19–22 (WLC morphology: 2ms forms at 5:1, 19, 20, 21, 22, and nowhere in 5:2–18). The catalogue is set inside two addresses. [T] High.
  • The two confessions divide the catalogue.
    • Confession 1 (5:7) follows the five verses of dispossession.
    • Confession 2 (5:16) follows the eight verses of collapse.
    • Both turn on חטא (“sin”): “our fathers sinned” (חָטְאוּ) and “we have sinned” (חָטָאנוּ).
    [T] for the verb; [I] for the design. Moderate–high.
  • Bracket on the reversal verb. הפך (“turn”) opens the catalogue — “our inheritance turned over to strangers” (5:2) — and closes the list of losses: “our dancing turned to mourning” (5:15). [T] Moderate–high.
  • The 22 lines without the alphabet. The poem keeps the alphabet’s number and abandons its order. [T] for the fact; [I] that prayer has loosened the form. Lam 4:22 had ended poem 4 on ת (Tav), the last letter.

How the sections relate.

  • Address (1) → evidence (2–18), broken by two confessions → theology (19) → question (20) → petition (21) → condition (22).
  • The evidence is laid before God before the theology is confessed. The throne (5:19) is confessed after the desolate mountain (5:18), not instead of it. [I]

9. Tone and Feel

Two registers.

  1. 5:1–18: the inventory of loss, flatly told.
    • Short couplets, nearly every one a plain statement in the first person plural.
    • No metaphor until 5:10 (“hot as an oven”); no exclamation until 5:16 (“woe to us”).
    • The emotion is in the accumulation: water, wood, bread, skin, women, princes, elders, young men, boys, gate, music, joy, dancing, crown, heart, eyes, mountain.
    • The soundtrack is a list read aloud slowly in a ruined place.
  2. 5:19–22: the turn to God, then the question, then the petition, then the edge.
    • 5:19 is the book’s only doxology. Its calm is set against the foxes of 5:18.
    • 5:20 is the book’s only “why?” (לָמָּה) addressed to God.
    • 5:21 has two imperatives of hope.
    • 5:22 drops the voice: “unless … very angry with us”.

The poem ends quieter than it began. Do not read it, or preach it, as though it ended on 5:19 or 5:21. [I]

What the tone forbids: turning 5:2–18 into illustration. The list is the prayer. To lay it out before God, item by item, is the act of faith the poem models. [I]

12. Genre

Hebrew poetry: a communal lament-prayer of 22 lines. The number is alphabetic but the order is not. The reading rules of lament apply in full: it gives permission to grieve and does not resolve too fast.

Two features:

  1. Lament, not curse. The poem lists misery to God; it asks for no vengeance (contrast 1:22; 3:64–66; 4:21–22). Unlike poems 1, 3 and 4, it ends without asking for vengeance on the enemy. [T] [I]
  2. The ending is a genre-feature, not a defect. Laments can end without resolution (Ps 88). Lamentations ends with a petition and a condition. Read it as it stands.

No Gunkel–Mowinckel Gattung label is used.

13. Copycat

N/A as narrative, but the poem is offered to be prayed. As a normative pattern (prayers in Scripture given as words for God’s people), it commends four things:

  • bringing the specifics of loss before God;
  • confessing both inherited and personal sin;
  • confessing God’s enthronement while asking “why?”;
  • asking to be turned and renewed.

[I] Hos 14:2 [Heb 14:3] commends “take with you words” — compare Lam 5:21.

14. Bible Timeline

  • Where the passage sits: after the fall of Jerusalem, before any return. The land is held by strangers and the throne of David is empty (4:20; 5:16). [I] for the date.
  • Where the reader sits: this side of the cross and resurrection.
    • The Servant has borne the iniquities (Isa 53:11 → 1 Pet 2:24).
    • The new covenant of Jer 31 has been inaugurated (Luke 22:20; Heb 8:8–13).
    • The throne of 5:19 is the throne of God and of the Lamb (Rev 22:1).
    • But the church still prays “renew” and “restore” (Acts 3:19–21; Rom 8:23).
  • How that reframes it: 5:22’s “unless” has been answered in Christ — God has not utterly rejected his people (Rom 11:1–2) — but the book itself does not say so, and the preacher should say where the answer comes from. The poem remains the church’s language for seasons when the restoration is not yet seen.
  • Canonical position: Ketuvim, the Megilloth. In BHS order Lamentations stands between Ecclesiastes and Esther, and Leningrad has the same sequence for these two books. The book after this prayer is Esther, in which a month is “turned from sorrow to gladness and from mourning to a good day” and the Jews “rest from their enemies” (Esth 9:22) — the vocabulary of Lam 5:5, 15 reversed.
    • [T] for the wording.
    • [I] for the significance of the sequence. The reversal there is from danger, not exile, and the divine name is absent.
    • Report only; do not preach it as design.
  • Compositional and canonical order: whether Lam 5 prays Isa 54 and Jer 31, or they answer it, is open.

15. Who Am I?

We are the “we”. This is the one poem in the book whose voice the congregation may adopt without translation. It is the community at prayer. But note the following.

  • We are the children of 5:7 and the sinners of 5:16 together. Not the fathers’ innocent victims only, nor only guilty ourselves.
  • We are not the fathers who are “no more”. The poem speaks for the survivors.
  • Christ is not the speaker of this poem. He is present by answer rather than type:
    • the Servant who bears iniquities (Isa 53:11);
    • the one who also cried “why have you forsaken?” (Ps 22:2; Mark 15:34);
    • the king whose crown fell (5:16) and is restored (Heb 2:9).

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. To pray: to bring the specific losses of God’s people before him, to confess the sins of the fathers and our own, to confess that his throne abides even while his mountain lies desolate, to ask why, and to ask him to turn us back and renew us. And to pray this honestly, without pretending that the answer has already come. [I]

Stage 2.

Domain Application
Worldview The ruin of the place does not unseat the God of the place. The throne abides (5:19) when the mountain does not (5:18)
Stop Praying in generalities. Blaming only the fathers (or only ourselves). Ending prayers for the church’s ruin with an answer we have supplied
Start Telling God the particulars (“water … wood … bread … skin”). Praying “restore us … that we may be restored” — asking God to do the turning
Motivation His throne (5:19); his past dealings, “as of old” (5:21); and, now, the Servant who bore the iniquities (Isa 53:11) and the covenant renewed in his blood

Four audiences.

  • For me: have I learned to pray “we”?
  • For a Christian friend: the one in a long season with no sign of restoration has a prayer that ends where they are.
  • For the church: a congregation in decline, or a denomination in ruin, has here its liturgy — specific, confessional, enthroning God, asking.
  • For an unbeliever: the God of the Bible lets his people ask “why?” and “unless …?”. He answered it once and for all at the cross.

Prayer. “Remember, O LORD, what has befallen us … Restore us to yourself, O LORD, that we may be restored; renew our days as of old.”

Grammatical and syntactical features.

  • 5:21–22: two doubled-verb figures in adjacent lines.
    • 5:21: הֲשִׁיבֵנוּ … וְנָשׁוּבָה, hiphil then qal of שׁוב (“restore … return”).
    • 5:22: מָאֹס מְאַסְתָּנוּ, infinitive absolute + finite of מאס (“utterly reject”).
    • Of the six doubled-verb figures in Lamentations, only these two have an exclusive partner, and both partners are in Jeremiah (31:18; 14:19) [S: audit].
    • The book’s last two lines are Jeremiah’s two prayers — one answered in Jer 31, one refused in Jer 14. [I] Moderate–high.
  • 5:19: אַתָּה (“you”). The independent pronoun, fronted for emphasis, follows “Mount Zion … foxes”. The contrast is in the word order, not in a conjunction. [T]
  • 5:20: לָמָּה לָנֶצַח (“why … for ever”). The double adverbial frames the two verbs in chiasm: forget us / forsake us, “for ever” / “for length of days”. [T]

Wordplay and sound.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting.

  • Time: after 586 BC, under occupation. [I] for the date.
  • Place: Judah, with Zion desolate (5:18) and the people in the land (5:2–14) under “slaves”, i.e. officials of the empire.
  • Political context: Babylonian rule. Gedaliah’s governorship and assassination (2 Kgs 25:22–26; Jer 40–41) may lie behind “the sword of the wilderness” (5:9). [S], uncertain.

What the original audience knew:

  • Land as inheritance (נַחֲלָה): the allotment under the covenant (Num 26; Josh 13–21). Losing it to strangers was covenant curse (Deut 28:30–33, 49–52). [I]
  • Water and wood were common goods; having to buy them is the economy of the dispossessed (cf. Deut 29:10 [Eng 29:11], “hewers of wood and drawers of water”). [I]
  • “Giving the hand” is the gesture of treaty and submission (2 Kgs 10:15; Ezek 17:18; 1 Chr 29:24). [I]
  • The mill was women’s or slaves’ work (Exod 11:5; Judg 16:21 — Samson grinding in prison). Young men at the mill is humiliation. [I]
  • The gate was the place of the elders’ justice (Ruth 4:1–11; Prov 31:23). Elders absent from the gate means no justice. [I]
  • The fasts. Zech 7:3–5 and 8:19 record fasts in the fifth and seventh months for the fall and for Gedaliah; this prayer could have been prayed in them. [S] / [I] — uncertain as to use.

What this changes. The poem is a social catalogue of a conquered people: land, economy, food, sexual violence, execution, forced labour, the end of justice and of joy. Its prayer is political and economic, not merely spiritual. To preach it as private feelings is to miss what is listed. [I]

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience.

  • Section: Ketuvim. The reader arrives post-exilic.
  • What they know: they know the promises of Isa 54 and Jer 31, and know that the return did not restore everything (Hag 2:3; Ezra 3:12; Neh 9:36–37, “we are slaves this day in the land you gave our fathers”).
  • Neh 9:36–37 is the post-exilic echo of Lam 5:2, 8: slaves in the land, its produce going to kings. [T] for Neh; [I] for the echo.

The first hearers. Survivors under occupation, without king, temple or festival. They brought the memory of the Zion hymns (Ps 48; 125), the prophets’ promises (Isa 54; Jer 31), and the fathers’ sins they had inherited.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.

  • Shocking: 5:11–12 — the violation of women and the hanging of princes, stated flatly.
  • Surprising: the confession “we have sinned” (5:16), after “our fathers sinned … we have borne their iniquities” (5:7).
  • Comforting: “You, O LORD, abide for ever” (5:19); “restore us … renew our days” (5:21).
  • Disturbing: “unless you have utterly rejected us” (5:22).

What we bring that they didn’t.

  • An individualism that hears “I” where the poem says “we”.
  • A discomfort with confessing ancestral sin.
  • An expectation that every prayer must end resolved.

Biblical-Theological Themes

(1) Exile and return: “Restore us, that we may be restored”

  • Where the passage sits: exile, before return.
  • What comes before: Deut 30:1–10 (return to the LORD, and he will restore your fortunes); Jer 31:18 (Ephraim’s prayer); Ps 80 (the refrain).
  • What comes after:
    • the return under Cyrus, and its incompleteness (Neh 9:36–37);
    • the call of John and Jesus to “return” (Mark 1:4, 15);
    • Acts 3:19–21 (“repent and turn … that times of refreshing may come … the time for restoring all things”);
    • the new creation: “I make all things new” (Rev 21:5; cf. 5:21 חַדֵּשׁ, Greek ἀνακαίνισον, “renew”).
  • What it contributes: the petition asks God to do the turning (“restore us that we may be restored”). Restoration is grace before it is response.
  • Confidence: high.

(2) Iniquity borne: the fathers, the children and the Servant

  • Where the passage sits: the complaint of inherited guilt (5:7) and the confession of personal guilt (5:16).
  • What comes before:
    • Exod 20:5; 34:7 (iniquity visited to the third and fourth generation);
    • the sour-grapes proverb (Jer 31:29; Ezek 18:2).
  • What comes after:
    • Ezek 18 and Jer 31:29–30 (each bears his own);
    • Isa 53:4–12 (the Servant bears theirs);
    • 1 Pet 2:24 (“he himself bore our sins in his body on the tree”).
  • What it contributes: the canon’s two answers to 5:7 meet in the gospel. Each is responsible for his own sin (5:16), and one has borne it for all who are his (Isa 53:11).
  • Confidence: high.

Confluence: mutual illumination. Restoration (1) is possible because iniquity has been borne (2). In Isaiah the order is 53 → 54, Servant → Zion restored. In Jeremiah, new covenant → “I will forgive their iniquity” (31:34). Lam 5 prays for (1) while confessing (2). The gospel supplies the ground the prayer lacks. [I]

Christological Reading

Type of connection: trajectory (primary), with contrast and the NT’s own use of the partner texts.

How the passage points to Christ.

  • The bearer of iniquities (5:7 → Isa 53:11) — canonical reflection (v1.1). The only other place סבל (“bear”) takes “iniquities” is the Servant’s, and the NT applies Isa 53 to Christ (Acts 8:32–35; 1 Pet 2:22–25). Claim audit 3 rates it low as an allusion [S: audit 3], so present it as the canon’s answer to 5:7, not as an echo the poet intended.
    • Category: sacrifice/atonement.
    • NT precedent: strong for Isa 53; none for Lam 5:7.
    • Escalation: yes (the Servant bears others’ iniquity, and finishes it).
    • Authorial pattern: Lam 5:7 itself is a complaint, not a prophecy.
    • Trajectory, moderate, as canonical reflection; not typology of Lam 5:7 itself.
  • The new covenant (5:21 → Jer 31:18 → 31:31–34). The chapter whose words the community prays culminates in the promise the Lord’s Supper declares fulfilled (Luke 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25; Heb 8:8–13). Trajectory; high (NT precedent for Jer 31). v1.1: this is now the most secure Christological route from poem 5.
  • The enthroned LORD (5:19 → Ps 102:13 → Heb 1:10–12). The psalm that supplies 5:19’s words is the psalm Hebrews applies to the Son (“you, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth … you remain”, Ps 102:26–28 at Heb 1:10–12). Trajectory; moderate–high (NT precedent for Ps 102).
  • The cry “why have you forsaken?” (5:20 → Ps 22:2 → Mark 15:34). The Son took the forsaken people’s question onto his own lips. Trajectory, moderate.
  • The fallen crown (5:16) and the enthroned LORD (5:19).
    • The Greek of 5:16 has στέφανος (“crown”), the word of Matt 27:29 (the crown of thorns), whose verb stands at Heb 2:9 (“crowned with glory and honour”).
    • The verbal link is common Greek and should not be pressed (low).
    • The theological link runs through the Davidic king (4:20) and the throne confessed in 5:19 (Heb 1:8, Ps 45:7, “your throne, O God, is for ever”). Moderate.

Trajectory.

  • What the passage anticipates: restoration, renewal, the throne’s permanence, iniquity borne.
  • What Christ fulfils: the iniquity borne (Isa 53:11); the covenant renewed (Jer 31:31); the forsakenness taken (Mark 15:34); the throne shared (Rev 3:21).
  • What remains: “renew our days as of old” awaits the new creation (Rev 21:5). The church still prays 5:21.

Contrast. The community’s “we have sinned” (5:16) is not Christ’s. He bears what they confess. [I]

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptation: “Be honest in prayer like Israel.” True as far as it goes, but the poem’s hope is not its honesty.
  • Gospel grounding: the throne (5:19), the God who restores (5:21), and in Christ the bearing of the iniquity (Isa 53:11).
  • Christ as hero: the one who answered “unless you have utterly rejected us” by being forsaken himself.

Confidence: high (trajectory via Isa 53 and Jer 31); moderate (Ps 22; the crown).

Difficult / Contested Verses

5:22 — כִּי אִם: how does the book end?

  • Category: interpretive crux, pastoral.

  • The difficulty: the last line can be read as a condition, a statement, a concession or a question.

  • Rhetorical function: the final line after the petition (5:21). Whatever it means, it is the book’s honest acknowledgement of the one thing that would make 5:21 vain.

  • The main options:

    Reading Rendering Witnesses
    (a) Exceptive “unless you have utterly rejected us” NASB95, ESV — the petition stands, with a fear
    (b) Adversative “but you have utterly rejected us” KJV — a lament that ends in despair
    (c) Concessive “even though you had utterly rejected us” (and been very angry) — the petition is grounded despite the rejection
    (d) Interrogative “or have you utterly rejected us?” —
    Greek / Syriac “for you have utterly rejected us” Greek, Syriac

  • Honest exegesis:

    • כִּי אִם after a negative or a petition usually means “but rather” or “unless”. Here it follows the imperative of 5:21, so (a) is the most natural reading. (c) is possible.
    • Jewish public reading repeats 5:21 after 5:22, so that the reading does not end on rejection [S] (a liturgical practice, attested in the Masoretic tradition and the synagogue; spread unchecked).
    • The Hebrew text ends where it ends.

  • Suggested handling: name the options briefly; preach (a). Do not end the sermon on 5:19, and do not supply an answer the book does not give without saying where it comes from.

5:7 against 5:16 — inherited guilt

  • Category: doctrinal, pastoral.
  • The difficulty: do children bear their fathers’ guilt (5:7; Exod 20:5), or each his own (Ezek 18:20)?
  • Rhetorical function: 5:7 is complaint; 5:16 is confession. The poem does not let 5:7 stand alone.
  • Honest exegesis:
    • The consequences of the fathers’ sin have fallen on the children.
    • The children confess their own sin too.
    • Ezek 18 and Jer 31:29–30 do not deny 5:7’s experience. They deny that it is the last word.
  • Handling: address briefly; let 5:16 answer 5:7 in the text before the canon answers it.

5:11 — sexual violence

  • Category: pastoral landmine.
  • The difficulty: the rape of women and virgins, stated without comment.
  • Handling:
    • Name it plainly and briefly.
    • Note that the verb is the one 3:33 denies of God’s heart. The poem lays the atrocity before God as an injustice, not as his will.
    • There may be survivors in the congregation. Do not use the verse as illustration.

5:6 — “Egypt … Assyria”

  • Category: apologetic/historical (anachronism).
  • Handling: a brief note only, if asked. The confession is most naturally of the fathers’ alliances (5:7).

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Ending on 5:19 or 5:21

  • What it looks like: the sermon closes on “But you, O LORD, reign forever”, or on “renew our days”, as the book’s last word.
  • Why it’s wrong: the book ends on 5:22. The ESV’s “But” makes 5:19 sound like the turn.
  • The corrective: read to 5:22. Preach 5:21 as the prayer and 5:22 as the honest condition. Say where the answer to the condition is found (Isa 53–54; the cross), and that the book itself does not say it.

Pitfall 2: Skipping the list

  • What it looks like: “verses 2–18 describe their suffering” — then on to 5:19.
  • Why it’s wrong: the list is the prayer, and it is the longest part of the poem. It contains the confessions (5:7, 16).
  • The corrective: read it, or at least read it aloud in full in the service. Let the congregation hear that God may be told particulars.

Pitfall 3: Blaming the fathers (or only ourselves)

  • What it looks like: a sermon on generational curses from 5:7, or on personal repentance only from 5:16.
  • Why it’s wrong: the poem holds both, and 5:16 answers 5:7.
  • The corrective: both confessions, and then Isa 53:11 — the one who bore “their iniquities”.

Pitfall 4: Spiritualising the catalogue

  • What it looks like: “Water, bread and wood stand for spiritual thirst and hunger.”
  • Why it’s wrong: these are the economics of occupation (Historical Background).
  • The corrective: let it be literal. Its application is to the church’s real losses and to the real suffering of believers under oppression today.

Pitfall 5: Using 5:11 as illustration

  • What it looks like: extended description to raise emotion.
  • Why it’s wrong: this is a pastoral landmine, and the text itself is terse.
  • The corrective: one plain sentence, and the link to 3:33: not God’s heart.

Pitfall 6: Resolving 5:22 by translation

  • What it looks like: “It really means ‘even though you were angry …’”, offered as settled.
  • Why it’s wrong: it is a crux, and “unless” is the most natural reading.
  • The corrective: show the options; preach the natural reading; bring the answer from elsewhere in Scripture, openly.
