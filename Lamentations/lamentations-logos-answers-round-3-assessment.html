Q1.1 — The ketiv at 4:3

Result. HALOT, following Rudolph (KAT), reads the ketiv תנין as the plural of תַּן (“jackal”) with an Aramaic ending, comparing Lam 1:4. BDB agrees (“so most”) and lists the form under “jackal”, citing GKC §87e. Lange’s commentary has two voices. Nägelsbach keeps the singular “sea-monster” and lists serpent texts, among them “Deut. 32:23” — evidently a slip for 32:33, the serpent verse. His American editor (W. H. H.) argues for jackals, chiefly because the verbs and the suffix are plural. [S]

Corpus check. Lam 1:4 has the Aramaic-type ending in שׁוֹמֵמִין (“desolate”). The verbs of 4:3 are plural (חָלְצוּ, הֵינִיקוּ) and the suffix is feminine plural (גּוּרֵיהֶן), which fits a plural subject. Deut 32:23 has no serpent; 32:33 has תַּנִּינִם with אַכְזָר (WLC). [T]

Verdict. Low stands for Deut 32:33 at 4:3. History: one older commentator (Nägelsbach) took the “serpent” sense, but without arguing for an echo of Deut 32:33. The Song of Moses case rests on 4:9 and 4:11, as audit 6 said.

Q1.2 — 3:56b and the perfects of 3:55–61

Result.

  • BHS at 3:56: the Greek has εἰς τὴν δέησίν μου εἰς τὴν βοήθειάν μου (“for my supplication, for my help”), which may represent לִישׁוּעָתִי לִתְחִנָּתִי; BHS proposes deleting either לרוחתי or לשׁועתי. [S]
  • MacKay (Mentor, 168): the plea “do not hide Your ear” is the content of the prayer already heard; the perfects report a past deliverance. [S]
  • Keil–Delitzsch (8:523): the perfect “You have heard” is followed directly by the optative, so the plea is the prayer that was heard. The answer then attributes to K&D the remark that “nearly all expositors” take the perfects of 3:56–61 as perfects of certainty — what is sure to happen, told as done. The Assistant’s summary runs the two together. [S], to be checked.
  • Faithlife Study Bible: the perfects express confidence, as in the thanksgiving psalms. [S]

Corpus check. Swete reads φωνήν μου ἤκουσας· μὴ κρύψῃς τὰ ὦτά σου εἰς τὴν δέησίν μου (“You heard my voice; do not hide Your ears at my supplication”), which confirms the shorter Greek text BHS describes. [T]

Verdict. The dig’s reading — 3:56b is the prayer that was heard, so 3:55–57 remembers a hearing — now has history of interpretation (MacKay; K&D). The ESV’s quotation marks follow it. The other reading is now named more precisely: perfects of certainty, a confidence that God will act, told as already done, which some expositors (as K&D report them) hold for 3:56–61. Present the dig’s reading as the main one (moderate–high), and the certainty-perfect reading as the alternative. Check K&D 8:523 in Logos before quoting the “nearly all expositors” remark.

Q1.3 — The crux of 3:36

Result. Kaiser (Hard Sayings, 312) takes 3:34–36 as one sentence ending in a question: “Has not the Lord seen?” — yes, He has. MacKay (150–151) takes it as a statement of divine disapproval: the Lord does not approve such things. Lint links 3:36 to 3:59 (“God has seen”). No source connects 3:36 with Ps 10, Ps 94 or Ezek 8–9. [S]

Verdict. Audit 6 confirmed by the history: the question (Kaiser) and the statement (MacKay) are both held; the slogan reading was found in no commentary. The 3:34–66 dig’s Headline 1 can cite both, and keep the slogan as the weakest reading (uncertain, low). The internal chain 3:36 → 3:59 has a commentator (Lint).

Q1.4 — Dan 9:24 and Lam 4:22

Result. Sprinkle (EBTC, 255–256): the ketiv “to seal up sins” took its verb from later in the verse; the qere (“to complete sin”, the H-stem of תמם) is most likely original; most versions follow the qere; Theodotion follows the ketiv. Anderson (Sin: A History, 86, 93) links Lam 4:22 “your iniquity … has been completed (tam)” with Dan 9:24 “to bring to completion (le-hātēm)” the sin, as the idiom of paying off a debt of sin through a term of suffering, and compares Isa 40:2. He renders 4:22 “the debt accrued by your iniquity, Fair Zion, has been paid in full”. [S]

Corpus check. Swete’s Theodotion text has τοῦ συντελεσθῆναι ἁμαρτίαν, καὶ τοῦ σφραγίσαι ἁμαρτίας (“to complete sin, and to seal sins”) — “seal”, so it follows the ketiv, as Sprinkle says. The Old Greek has no “seal” in this clause (συντελεσθῆναι τὴν ἁμαρτίαν καὶ τὰς ἀδικίας σπανίσαι, “to complete sin and to make iniquities scarce”). [T]

Verdict. Dan 9:24 at 4:22 now has history of interpretation (Anderson), and a Daniel commentator prefers the qere (Sprinkle). Raise the contact from “moderate (low–moderate as a source)” to moderate, stated on the qere, with Isa 40:2 beside it (already audited for 4:22a [S: audit 4]). The Greek: Theodotion loses it; the Old Greek has “complete” without “seal”. The Christological route through Dan 9 stands at low–moderate: Anderson’s link concerns the completed penalty, not the anointed of 9:25–26.

Q2.1 — Isa 30 at Lam 4

Result. Lessing (Concordia, 387): the hope in Egypt at Lam 4:17 is Jer 37:5–11, and “Isaiah captures this idea” in Isa 30:7; Isaiah’s two woes on going down to Egypt (30:1–2; 31:1). Childs comments on Isa 30:16 but not on Lamentations. Unger’s dictionary and Lockyer place Lam 4:2 beside Isa 30:14 (with Ps 2:9; Jer 19:11). [S]

Verdict. History present for Isa 30 at 4:17 (Lessing). For 4:2, a dictionary pairing only (Unger), weak. For the swift pursuers (Isa 30:16 / Lam 4:19), none. Isa 30 stays moderate–high; Lessing fills the history criterion for the 4:17–20 half.

Q2.2 — Jonah 2 and Ps 88 at 3:54–56

Result. Lessing: “water flowed over my head” (3:54, צוּף) parallels Jonah 2:6 [Eng 2:5]; the niphal of גזר (“I am cut off” = “I am dead”) occurs at Isa 53:8; Ezek 37:11; Ps 88:6. The HCSB cross-references pair 3:54 with Jonah 2:3, 5 and 3:55 with Ps 88:6 and Jonah 2:6. NOAB links the water and Sheol imagery to Jonah 2:3–6 and Pss 69; 88. The Greek identity (εἶπα Ἀπῶσμαι, Jonah 2:5 and Lam 3:54) was not addressed. (The answer calls the Greek “Theodotion’s” and glosses ἀπῶσμαι “I am cut off”; both are mistakes — it is the Old Greek, and the verb means “I am thrust away”.) [S]

Verdict. The “cut off — heard” family (Jonah 2; Ps 88; Ps 31; Ezek 37:11) now has history of interpretation (Lessing), on the Hebrew. Moderate stands; Lessing’s list of niphal גזר texts is the same as audit 6’s. The Greek identity with Jonah 2:5 remains an unremarked corpus finding.

Q2.3 — Ps 35 at 3:52–59

Result. TSK and HCSB cross-reference Lam 3:52 with Ps 35:7, 35:19 and John 15:25. The NIV Study Bible lists Ps 35:19; 69:4; 109:3; 119:161 and Lam 3:52 as possible sources of John 15:25 and says it is not known which John meant. Nothing was found on 3:59 ~ Ps 35:22. [S]

Verdict. Cross-reference level history for 3:52 ~ Ps 35, and a study-Bible note that puts Lam 3:52 itself among the candidates for John 15:25. That strengthens the dig’s Christological route slightly: John’s wording fits several texts, and Lamentations is one of them. The stronger contact, “You have seen, O LORD” (Ps 35:22; Lam 3:59), has no history found. Ps 35 stays moderate.

Q2.4 — The Song of Moses in Lam 4

Result. Hamilton links Lamentations with Deut 4:25–31; Kaiser (KJV Study Bible) explains Judah’s guilt above Sodom by greater privilege. The sentence “directly echoing the Sodom comparison in Deut 32:32–33” is the Assistant’s own inference; Kaiser’s note, as quoted, does not mention Deut 32. Nothing was found for 32:13 or 32:22. Lange’s slip “Deut 32:23” (see Q1.1) is the only reference to the Song. [S]

Verdict. History not found. The Song of Moses stays at moderate–high on the corpus alone (4:9 the verbatim “produce of the field”; 4:11 foundation + fire). Do not cite Kaiser for Deut 32.

Q3.1 — Zeph 3 and Ezek 22 at 4:11–14

Result. No direct comparison found. Boase (The Fulfilment of Doom?, 191) says 4:13–16 evokes the prophetic literature in its account of the prophets’ and priests’ failings; Lessing notes how unusually specific the sin of 4:13 is; Malone says Ezek 22’s priestly language expands Zeph 3:4. [S]

Verdict. Not found. Zeph 3 stays moderate–high and Ezek 22:25–28 moderate on the corpus. Malone’s point (Ezek 22:26 expands Zeph 3:4) explains why the two cluster together at 4:13. Boase’s monograph is the obvious next question: it is a study of exactly this relationship.

Q3.2 — Jer 14 at 4:9–15

Result. Driver (Introduction, 462) lists, among the Jeremiah–Lamentations parallels, the guilt of the prophets and priests: Lam 2:14; 4:13–15 with Jer 2:7–8; 5:31; 14:13; 23:11–40. Lalleman pairs Lam 2:11–13 with Jer 14:17. Nothing for Jer 14:16–18 at 4:9. [S]

Verdict. History present for Jer 14 at 4:13–15 (Driver, on Jer 14:13). Jer 14:18 at 4:9 was audited earlier [S: audit 4]. Jer 14 stays moderate at poem 4. Driver lists the parallel among the evidence for a common outlook, so keep the authorship question out of it (overview trap 5).

Q4.1 — The Greek “portion” at 4:16

Result. Not discussed. The Lexham lexicon and House read the Hebrew as “disperse / scatter”. [S]

Verdict. Keep as a note in the 4:1–16 dig; do not carry it into the overview.

Q4.2 — Edom at 4:21

Result. Dobbs-Allsopp (Interpretation, 137): the command to rejoice is sarcastic, a taunt, made plain by the cup and the stripping. Ash (on Ps 137) and K&D (10:229) link 4:21–22 with Ezek 25:12–14 and Ezek 35, which use nearly the same language of Edom’s hostility; Dyer adds Jer 49:7–22. Dobbs-Allsopp lists the cup texts: Isa 51:17–22; Jer 25:15–29; Ezek 23:30–34; Obad 16. Nothing on Jer 25:20 (Uz) or Obad 12 specifically. [S]

Verdict. The irony now has history (Dobbs-Allsopp), and Ezek 35 has history (Ash; K&D). The joy-call reading stays moderate; Ezek 35:14–15 moderate, now with history; Jer 25:20 stays a corpus finding.

Result. Boase was not reached. In its place the Assistant offered an HCSB cross-reference (Ezek 22 at Lam 4:13), MacArthur’s Quick Reference Guide (Zeph 3:4 with Lam 4:6, 13, 22; a popular work), and Schnittjer, Old Testament Use of Old Testament (610): Jer 14:19 (“Have you utterly rejected Judah? … Why have you struck us down so that there is no healing for us?”) and Lam 5:22 (“unless You have utterly rejected us”), “suggesting the poet of Lamentations had an eye on the context of Jeremiah 14”. [S]

Corpus check. The infinitive absolute with the finite verb of מאס (“utterly reject”) stands in only these two verses (Jer 14:19; Lam 5:22) (WLC). [T]

Verdict.

  • Zeph 3 and Ezek 22 at 4:11–14: still not found in any commentary. They stay on the corpus alone (Zeph 3 moderate–high; Ezek 22:25–28 moderate).
  • Jer 14:19 at Lam 5:22 now has history of interpretation in a work Patrick owns (Schnittjer), and Schnittjer takes a view on the direction: Lamentations uses Jeremiah 14. This joins Lalleman and Salters in the majority view recorded for L1 in round 2. L1 stays closed as “no consensus, majority view: Lamentations knows Jeremiah’s language”; Schnittjer is now the clearest statement of it. Jer 14:19 at 5:22 was audited earlier [S: audit 1].

F2 — Lessing and Dobbs-Allsopp on 4:3, 4:9 and 4:11

Result. Not answered; neither work is owned.

Verdict. The Song of Moses in Lam 4 stays moderate–high on the corpus alone, history not found. The 4:3 ketiv stays as HALOT and BDB read it.

F3 — Keil–Delitzsch on the perfects

Result. The full passage (pp. 491–492) is K&D’s comment on Lam 1:21, not on chapter 3. K&D reject a Hebrew “optative perfect” (the perfect as a wish), allow that the perfect may mark “what is viewed as certain to follow”, and then say: “The perfects in 3:56–61 are taken in this sense by nearly all expositors.” They use that to read 1:21 as “Thou bringest on the day which Thou hast proclaimed”. [S]

What this changes. It reverses part of round 3’s reading of the answer. K&D report that, in their day, nearly all expositors read 3:56–61 as perfects of certainty — the deliverance so sure that it is spoken of as done — and not as a report of a past hearing. Whether K&D themselves read 3:56 as the prayer already heard (the claim attributed to them at 8:523) is still unchecked; the round 3 summary may have run their two comments together. MacKay remains a clear modern witness for the retrospective reading.

Verdict. Two readings, both with history:

  • a remembered hearing (MacKay; the ESV’s quotation marks at 3:56b; the dig’s reading);
  • perfects of certainty (by K&D’s report, the majority of nineteenth-century expositors).

The forms allow both (audit 6). Lower the dig’s reading from “the main one, moderate–high” (round 3, above) to moderate, and present the certainty reading as a strong alternative, not a minority one. Preaching note: both readings put the answer in the past tense of faith; the difference is whether the man is remembering or anticipating. The ESV commits the congregation to the first.

Aside: K&D note that the Vulgate renders 1:21 adduxisti diem consolationis (“You have brought the day of consolation”), probably with Isa 40:2 in view. Isa 40:2 already stands beside 4:22 [S: audit 4]; this is a note, not a contact.

F4 — The Greek of 3:54 and Jonah 2:5

Result. Sasson (Anchor Bible, Jonah, 176–178; not owned) cites Lam 3:54 as a parallel to Jonah 2:5, and notes that Jonah 2:5 and Ps 31:23 each divide the line into “I said” and a passive of being driven or cut off. The Greek question itself was not answered; the closing sentence about “the Greek tradition” is the Assistant’s padding. [S, not owned]

Corpus check. The three lines share one formula (WLC). Corrected by claim audit 7: Jonah’s גרשׁ and Ps 31’s גרז differ by one letter; Ps 31’s גרז and Lam’s גזר by the order of two (metathesis).

  • Jonah 2:5 — אָמַרְתִּי נִגְרַשְׁתִּי מִנֶּגֶד עֵינֶיךָ (“I said, I am driven away from before Your eyes”);
  • Ps 31:23 — אָמַרְתִּי … נִגְרַזְתִּי מִנֶּגֶד עֵינֶיךָ (“I said … I am cut off from before Your eyes”);
  • Lam 3:54 — אָמַרְתִּי נִגְזָרְתִּי (“I said, I am cut off”).

Jonah 2:5 and Ps 31:23 are almost word for word; Lam 3:54 has the short form, with the root of Ps 88:6. In Greek, Jonah 2:5 and Lam 3:54 share εἶπα Ἀπῶσμαι, although the Hebrew roots differ (גרשׁ against גזר): the Greek translator of Lamentations used the Jonah wording, or both used a stock rendering. [T]; [I].

Verdict. The “I said, I am cut off — You heard” family (Jonah 2; Ps 31; Ps 88; Lam 3:54–56) is firmer than audit 6 said: it is one formula with three spellings, and a commentator on Jonah (Sasson) has the Lamentations parallel. Raise it from moderate to moderate–high as a family. Ps 31:23 is no longer “low alone”: it is the formula’s closest match to Jonah, and Lamentations has the formula’s short form. Claim audit 7: the formula is confirmed (moderate–high), but metathesis does apply to Ps 31 / Lam 3:54, Jonah has no “waters over the head”, and Ps 88 is a separate word-level partner, not a member of the formula.
