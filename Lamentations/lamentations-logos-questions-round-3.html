Lamentations: Questions for the Logos Research Assistant, Round 3

Purpose: questions for the Research Assistant, drawn from claim audit 6 and the three digs now at v1.1 (3:34–66; 4:1–16; 4:17–22). They follow logos-research-assistant-guidelines in the Dig Deeper folder. They have two aims, both ahead of Finalise:

  • to settle four textual and grammatical questions the corpus cannot decide;
  • to fill the “history of interpretation” criterion, which audit 6 recorded as not checked, for the links Finalise would put at moderate or above.

Use:

  • Ask one question per turn.
  • Set the scope to a Lamentations commentaries collection unless the question says otherwise.
  • Paste each answer back with its footnotes, under the question as asked.

Date: 21 October 2026

How the List Is Arranged

The questions are grouped by kind, in order of how much they matter to Finalise.

Each question has three parts:

  • Ask: the wording to type.
  • Why: the open point it settles, and where the answer would be used.
  • Watch for: the check from the “Reading the Answer” section of the guidelines most likely to matter.

Owned, from rounds 1–2:

  • Commentaries and introductions: Keil–Delitzsch; Lalleman (TOTC); Salters; Ellison; the Cornerstone volume; MacKay; Driver; the Lexham materials.
  • Reference works: HALOT; BDB; the NET notes; the Reformation Study Bible; Estes (NAC, Psalms).

House, Berlin, Provan, Hillers, Parry and Dobbs-Allsopp were not confirmed in round 1. If the Assistant cites any of them, check that it is quoting the work and not general knowledge.

Left out on purpose:

  • counts, word chains and “only here” claims, which the corpus has already settled;
  • L1 (the direction between Jer 14 and Lamentations), closed in round 2 with no consensus. Question 3.2 asks only about Jer 14 at poem 4;
  • the 78 queued single contacts that carry no headline.

1. Text and Grammar

1.1 The ketiv at 4:3: “serpents” or “jackals”?

  • Ask: In Lamentations 4:3 the written text (ketiv) is תנין and the reading (qere) is תַּנִּים. How do commentators and lexicons (HALOT, BDB, Keil–Delitzsch, Salters) explain the ketiv? Is it read as “jackals” (an Aramaic-type plural of תַּן), as “sea monsters/serpents” (תַּנִּין), or as a simple spelling variant? Does anyone connect 4:3 with Deuteronomy 32:33?
  • Why: Audit 6 rated the Deut 32:33 link at 4:3 low, because the qere and the jackal–ostrich idiom (Isa 13:22; 34:13; Mic 1:8; Job 30:29) explain the verse better. If a commentator defends “serpent” and the Deut 32:33 echo, that verdict needs a note. The rest of the Song of Moses case (4:9; 4:11) does not depend on it.
  • Watch for: whether the answer distinguishes the ketiv from the qere at all. Many English discussions simply say “jackals” without noticing the spelling.

1.2 3:56b: a quotation, or a present plea?

  • Ask: In Lamentations 3:56, “You have heard my voice: do not hide Your ear from my relief, from my cry for help”, is “do not hide Your ear” the content of the past prayer that was heard, or a present plea breaking into the report? How do commentators read the perfects of 3:55–61 (“You heard … You drew near … You said … You pleaded … You redeemed”) — as a past deliverance, as precatives, or as prophetic perfects? Please also say what BHS notes at 3:56 (a Greek doublet; delete לרוחתי or לשׁועתי?).
  • Why: The 3:34–66 dig v1.1 reads 3:55–57 as a remembered hearing, and the perfects from 3:58 as grounds for the present plea. Audit 6 judged that reading permissible but not required. The ESV’s quotation marks at 3:56b take one side, so the answer is also a pulpit note.
  • Watch for: the “precative perfect” (perfects as prayers). If a commentator takes it, he removes the past hearing altogether. Note which verses he applies it to.

1.3 The crux of 3:36

  • Ask: In Lamentations 3:36, אֲדֹנָי לֹא רָאָה, do commentators read “the Lord does not approve”, “does the Lord not see?” (an unmarked question), or “the Lord does not see” (a slogan of the wicked, as in Ezekiel 8:12; 9:9; Psalm 94:7)? Does anyone connect 3:36 with Psalm 10:11, 14 or with 3:59–60 (“You have seen”)?
  • Why: Audit 6 found three possible readings, with the question and the statement roughly level and the slogan weakest (it lacks any verb of saying). The 3:34–66 dig’s Headline 1 now presents all three; a commentator’s preference would be recorded as history of interpretation.
  • Watch for: whether a commentator argues from 3:37–38 (two further unmarked questions), which is the text’s own evidence for the question reading.

1.4 Daniel 9:24’s qere and Lamentations 4:22

  • Ask: In Daniel 9:24 the ketiv reads ולחתם חטאות (“to seal sins”) and the qere לְהָתֵם חַטָּאת (“to complete/finish sin”). Which do commentators prefer, and what does Theodotion’s Greek read? Does any commentator on Daniel 9 or on Lamentations 4:22 (“your iniquity is complete”, תַּם עֲוֹנֵךְ) link the two?
  • Why: Audit 6 made the qere the strongest contact between 4:18–22 and Daniel 9, and Dan 9 is now the only surviving Christological route at 4:20–22 (Ezek 21:32 fell to low). The link depends on reading the qere.
  • Watch for: set the scope to Daniel commentaries for the first half. Check that “Theodotion” is the Greek text and not a commentator’s paraphrase.

2. Did a Commentator See It? (History of Interpretation)

2.1 Isaiah 30 at Lamentations 4:2 and 4:17–20

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 4 connect 4:17 (“we watched for a nation that could not save”) or 4:19 (“our pursuers were swifter …”) with Isaiah 30:1–16 (Egypt’s help is vapour; “your pursuers shall be swift”)? Does anyone connect 4:2 (“earthen jars, the work of a potter’s hands”) with Isaiah 30:14?
  • Why: Isa 30 is the only partner audit 6 confirmed across both halves of poem 4 (moderate–high). Finalise would put it in the overview’s Isaiah row; a commentator would fill the one unchecked criterion.
  • Watch for: answers that cite only Jer 37:5–11 (the Egyptian relief) for 4:17. That is the event, not Isaiah’s wording.

2.2 Jonah 2 and Psalm 88 at Lamentations 3:54–56

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 3:53–58 (“waters closed over my head; I said, ‘I am cut off’ … I called on Your name from the lowest pit … You heard my voice”) compare Jonah 2:3–6 or Psalm 88:6–7? Does anyone note that the Greek of 3:54 (εἶπα Ἀπῶσμαι) is the same as Jonah 2:5?
  • Why: Audit 6 found Jonah 2 and Ps 88 equal to or stronger than Ps 31:23, the partner the dig had named. The three now form a “cut off — heard” family, rated moderate.
  • Watch for: whether the comparison is made on the Hebrew words or only on the image of the pit. Imagery alone is common to many psalms.

2.3 Psalm 35 at Lamentations 3:52–59

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 3:52 (“my enemies without cause hunted me”) and 3:59 (“You have seen, O LORD, my wrong; judge my case”) cite Psalm 35 (35:7, 19, 22–24)? Do any link the line to John 15:25?
  • Why: Audit 6 made Ps 35 the lead partner (ahead of Ps 69), on “You have seen, O LORD”, found only in Ps 35:22 and Lam 3:59. The John 15:25 route in the Christological reading rests on it.
  • Watch for: answers that cite Ps 69 alone. That was the dig’s first choice and does not test the new one.

2.4 The Song of Moses in Lamentations 4

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 4 cite Deuteronomy 32 — especially 32:13 (“produce of the field”, cf. 4:9), 32:22 (“a fire … sets on fire the foundations”, cf. 4:11) or 32:32–33 (Sodom; the cruel venom of serpents, cf. 4:3, 6)?
  • Why: Audit 6 rated the Song of Moses a partner of poem 4 above chance (moderate–high), led by 4:9 and 4:11. Deuteronomy 28 is already well documented; Deuteronomy 32 is not.
  • Watch for: answers that cite Deut 28 only. Ask a follow-up naming 32:13 if so.

3. Prophetic Partners for Poem 4

3.1 Zephaniah 3 and Ezekiel 22 at Lamentations 4:11–14

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 4:11–14 (the LORD pours out his anger; the sins of the prophets and priests who shed the blood of the righteous) compare Zephaniah 3:1–8 or Ezekiel 22:25–28?
  • Why: Audit 6 found Zeph 3 the most stable prophetic partner of poem 4 (moderate–high) and Ezek 22:25–28 the closest partner for 4:13 (moderate). Finalise would carry both.
  • Watch for: whether Mic 3:9–12 or Jer 26 are offered instead. They belong to the same family and are worth recording.

3.2 Jeremiah 14 at Lamentations 4:9–15

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 4:9 (“better the slain of the sword than the slain of famine”) and 4:13–15 (prophets and priests who wander) cite Jeremiah 14:16–18 (“if I go out into the field, behold, those slain with the sword … both prophet and priest have gone about in a land they do not know”)?
  • Why: Audit 6 found Jer 14:16–18 the leading chapter partner of 4:1–16 on every block test. Jer 14:18 at 4:9 was audited earlier; Jer 14 at the leaders (4:13–15) is new.
  • Watch for: answers that drift into authorship (“Jeremiah wrote both”). Record the textual link and leave authorship aside (overview trap 5).

4. Optional

4.1 The Greek “portion” at 4:16

  • Ask: In Lamentations 4:16 the Greek reads μερὶς αὐτῶν (“their portion”) where the Hebrew has חִלְּקָם (“He divided them”). Do commentators discuss this reading, and does anyone connect it with the priests’ portion (Num 18:20) or with Ezekiel 5:1–12?
  • Why: Audit 6 withdrew the Gen 49:7 (Levi) link and kept the Greek “portion” only as a note. The question decides whether that note is worth carrying into the overview.
  • Watch for: whether BHS’s “read the Hebrew” is mentioned.

4.2 Edom’s taunt at 4:21

  • Ask: Do commentators on Lamentations 4:21 (“Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom”) read it as irony or as a taunt, and do they compare Ezekiel 35:14–15, Obadiah 12 or Jeremiah 25:20–21?
  • Why: Audit 6 confirmed the irony (moderate) and found Ezek 35:14 and Jer 25:20 as exclusive partners.
  • Watch for: answers that cite only Ps 137:7, which was already in the dig.
