Extent. The unit is the whole of poem 1, 1:1–22. Three facts fix it:

  • it is one complete alphabet, א to ת, one verse per letter, with ע before פ (1:16, 17) — the standard order, unlike poems 2–4;
  • it opens with אֵיכָה (“How!”), as poems 2 and 4 do;
  • Leningrad closes 1:22 with a petuchah (פ), where every other verse of the poem closes with a setumah (ס). That is a fact about the Leningrad manuscript as the WLC prints it, not about the author [T] for the marker.

2:1–3 was loaded and read so that the boundary is not drawn blind: the voice changes at once from Zion’s “I” to the narrator’s account of what “the Lord” has done.

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across several tools:

  1. The poem has two voices and one absence, and the absence passes from the first voice to the second.

    • The halves. The narrator speaks of Zion as “she” in 1:1–11, and Zion breaks in twice: “See, O LORD, my affliction” (1:9c) and “See, O LORD, and look” (1:11c). Zion speaks as “I” in 1:12–22, and the narrator breaks in once: “Zion stretches out her hands; there is no one to comfort her” (1:17). Eleven verses each. [T]
    • The refrain. אֵין מְנַחֵם (“there is no comforter”) stands four times: three in the narrator’s voice, “for her” (1:2, 9, 17), and once in Zion’s, “for me” (1:21). The fifth use of מְנַחֵם (“comforter”) is Zion’s too and is not a refrain line: כִּי־רָחַק מִמֶּנִּי מְנַחֵם מֵשִׁיב נַפְשִׁי (“for far from me is a comforter, one who restores my soul”, 1:16). [T] (WLC). The overview and the sweep both count “There is none to comfort her” five times; the phrase occurs four times, and the root five.
    • The comforter described. 1:16 is the only place in the book that says what a comforter would do: מֵשִׁיב נַפְשִׁי (“restore my soul”). The poem has already tried the other way to restore life — food. “To restore life” (לְהָשִׁיב נֶפֶשׁ) by bread is sought at 1:11 and 1:19, and fails both times. Bread, comforter, bread: three uses of the phrase in poem 1, none elsewhere in the book (WLC). [T]
    • What it does. The narrator observes the absence; Zion owns it. By the end of the poem the only one left to address is the LORD. [I]

    Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Narrator’s Comment, Vocabulary, Who Am I? [T] Confidence: high.

  2. The LORD’s agency is confessed by the sufferer, not by the observer — and poem 1 is the book’s poem of reasons.

    • The count. The narrator names the LORD as the one who acts only twice: “the LORD has caused her grief” (1:5) and “the LORD has commanded concerning Jacob” (1:17). Zion names him as subject of fourteen or fifteen: ten or eleven in 1:12–15 (“the LORD inflicted … He sent fire … spread a net … turned me back … made me desolate … made my strength fail … gave me into the hands … rejected … called an appointed time … trod the winepress”) and four in 1:21–22, now as “You” (“You have done it … You have brought … You have proclaimed … You have dealt with me”). [T] (WLC, read verse by verse; listed under Tool 3).
    • Reasons. כִּי (“for, because, that”) occurs fourteen times in poem 1 — almost half of the book’s twenty-nine, in a seventh of its verses. Poem 2 has one (WLC). Poem 1 gives reasons in every direction at once: “for the LORD has caused her grief for the multitude of her transgressions” (1:5); “for the enemy has magnified himself” (1:9); “for I have rebelled” (1:18, 20); “for my groans are many” (1:22). [T]
    • Confession inside lament. צַדִּיק הוּא יְהוָה כִּי פִיהוּ מָרִיתִי (“The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against His command”, 1:18) is spoken in the same speech as “is there any pain like my pain?” (1:12) and “let all their wickedness come before You” (1:22). The poem does not choose between confession and complaint (so the sweep). [T]
    • Planted. The verb of 1:5, הוֹגָה (“he caused grief”), with רֹב (“multitude”), returns at the book’s centre: “though He causes grief (הוֹגָה), He will have compassion according to the multitude (כְּרֹב) of His lovingkindness” (3:32) — the only two verses in the Hebrew Bible with both words [S: audit].

    Surfaced by: Structure, Linking Words, Repetition, Tone, Move 4. [T] Confidence: high.

  3. “See, O LORD”: sight is the poem’s axis — and Zion asks for the sight that began the exodus.

    • Who sees. רָאָה (“see”) occurs in eight of the twenty-two verses (WLC). The adversaries see her and mock (1:7); her honourers see her nakedness (1:8); she sees the nations enter her sanctuary (1:10). Then she asks for sight: the LORD (1:9, 11, 20), the passers-by (1:12), the peoples (1:18). [T]
    • The exodus vocabulary. “Judah has gone into exile from affliction (מֵעֹנִי) and from much servitude (וּמֵרֹב עֲבֹדָה)” (1:3); the priests, the city and the people groan (1:4, 8, 11); “See, O LORD, my affliction” (1:9). These are the words of Israel in Egypt. עֳנִי (“affliction”) and עֲבֹדָה (“servitude”) stand together within a verse either side only at Deut 26:6–7 and Lam 1:3 — the creed: “the Egyptians … afflicted us and imposed hard servitude on us … and the LORD heard our voice and saw our affliction”. The root אנח (“groan”) and עֲבֹדָה stand within a verse either side only at Exod 2:23 and Lam 1:3–4: “the sons of Israel groaned because of the servitude” (WLC). See + affliction + the divine name is the formula of Exod 3:7 and Deut 26:7 (eight verses in all).
    • A later precedent. “For the LORD saw the affliction of Israel, which was very bitter … and there was no helper for Israel … so He saved them” (2 Kgs 14:26–27). עֳנִי + עוֹזֵר (“helper”) occur together only there and at Lam 1:7, “and there was no one to help her” — two verses before “See, O LORD, my affliction” (WLC).
    • What it does. Exile is told as the exodus run backwards — out of the land, into servitude, groaning — and Zion’s prayer asks God to do what he did at the beginning: see. [I]

    Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Tool 11, Biblical Theology, Original Audience. [T] for the wording. Confidence: high (the sight pattern); moderate–high (the exodus vocabulary — two exclusive window pairs and a formula; new, unaudited); moderate (2 Kgs 14:26).

  4. Zion’s word for her allies is the prophets’ word for adultery — and the betrayal turns the prophets’ charge round.

    • “My lovers”. At 1:2 the narrator says אֹהֲבֶיהָ (“those who love her”, the plain participle). At 1:19 Zion herself says מְאַהֲבַי (“my lovers”), the intensive participle. That form occurs in sixteen verses of the Hebrew Bible: all in Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Hosea (with Zech 13:6), and Lam 1:19 (WLC). It is the prophets’ word for Israel’s illicit partners. The Greek keeps the difference: ἀγαπώντων at 1:2, ἐραστάς (“lovers”) at 1:19. [T]
    • Treachery. “All her friends have dealt treacherously with her” (כָּל־רֵעֶיהָ בָּגְדוּ בָהּ, 1:2). בגד (“deal treacherously”) + רֵעַ (“friend, companion”) occur together only at Jer 3:20 and Lam 1:2: “As a woman treacherously departs from her lover, so you have dealt treacherously with Me, O house of Israel” (WLC). In Jeremiah, Israel betrays the LORD; in Lamentations, the companions she chose betray her.
    • The pursuit. “All her pursuers have overtaken her … she has found no rest” (1:3). Pursue + overtake + find occur together only at Hos 2:9 [Eng 2:7] and Lam 1:3: “She will pursue her lovers, but she will not overtake them; and she will seek them, but will not find them” (WLC). The roles are reversed: the wife who chased her lovers is now the one chased. Deut 28:65, 45 remains the closer source for 1:3 as a whole [S: audit].
    • The Greek. At 1:19 the Greek adds καὶ οὐχ εὗρον (“and they did not find”) after “they sought food” (BHS: 𝔊 adds = וְלֹא מָצָאוּ). With ἐραστάς in the same verse, it reads like Hos 2:7 in Greek: τοὺς ἐραστὰς αὐτῆς … ζητήσει αὐτοὺς καὶ οὐ μὴ εὕρῃ αὐτούς. [T] for Swete and Rahlfs.

    Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Translations, Tool 11, Original Languages. [T] for the wording; the direction of dependence is open throughout. Confidence: high (the form); moderate–high (Jer 3:20); moderate (Hos 2 as a frame) — all new, unaudited.

  5. The poem plants two of the book’s answers in its own vocabulary: “great” and “turned back”.

    • Great. The poem opens with רַבָּתִי (“great”) twice — “great with people … great among the nations” (1:1) — and closes with רַבּוֹת (“many”): “for my groans are many” (1:22). The adjective occurs in the book only in these two verses and at 3:23: רַבָּה אֱמוּנָתֶךָ (“great is Your faithfulness”). The noun רֹב (“multitude”) occurs only at 1:3 (“much servitude”), 1:5 (“the multitude of her transgressions”) and 3:32 (“the multitude of His lovingkindness”) (WLC). [T]
    • Turned back. Zion “turns back” (וַתָּשָׁב אָחוֹר, 1:8, the plain stem); the LORD “turned me back” (הֱשִׁיבַנִי אָחוֹר, 1:13, the causative). The last prayer of the book uses the same verb in the reverse order: “Turn us back (הֲשִׁיבֵנוּ) to You, O LORD, that we may turn (וְנָשׁוּבָה)” (5:21). [T] for the forms.
    • What it does. The city that was great is great now only in groans; the book finds one thing greater (3:23, 32). The God who turned her back is the one asked to turn her home. [I]

    Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Move 4, Biblical Theology. [T] for the chains. Confidence: high (the chains; the 1:1 / 1:22 inclusio); moderate (that the book is answering poem 1 by design).

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level (from the overview, confirmed by the text). Lamentations gives a ruined people ordered words — confession, protest, memory and petition — with which to keep addressing the LORD when he is silent [S] (overview); [I] as a reading of the whole. The overview’s three claims are held together throughout: the LORD has done this; the LORD is right to have done it; what has been done is beyond bearing, and he must be told so.

Poem level. Poem 1 is the only poem that holds all three in a single speech.

  • The LORD has done it. The narrator says so once in each half (1:5, 17); Zion says so in a run of verbs (1:12–15) and then to his face (1:21–22). [T]
  • He was right to. “Jerusalem sinned greatly” (1:8); “The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against His command” (1:18); “I have been very rebellious” (1:20); “for all my transgressions” (1:22). Poem 1 confesses more than any other poem: the transgressions (פֶּשַׁע) of 1:5, 14, 22 are the book’s only three, and three of the book’s four “rebel” (מרה) are here (1:18, 20 twice; 3:42) (WLC). [T]
  • It is beyond bearing. “Is there any pain like my pain?” (1:12); “my groans are many and my heart is faint” (1:22). [T]

What poem 1 adds to the book’s purpose is the act of turning. It begins as a description of Zion and ends as Zion’s prayer. The narrator’s “she” becomes Zion’s “I”, and Zion’s “I” turns to “You” (1:21–22). [T] for the sequence; [I] for the purpose.

Passage-level purpose, in one sentence. To set before God, and before anyone passing, a city that has no comforter, and to teach her to say to the God who did it both “You are righteous” and “See”. [I]

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. Nothing in the book precedes poem 1. In the Hebrew canon the book before is Ecclesiastes, which ends “fear God and keep His commandments” (Eccl 12:13). In the English and Greek order the book before is Jeremiah, which ends with the fall of the city (Jer 52). [T] for both orders as printed. So the “preceding movement” for poem 1 is the canon’s, not the book’s: the covenant curses (Deut 28) and the prophets’ warnings, which the poem presupposes and never narrates (overview, Presupposes). [I]

What poem 1 must supply, and does. A book that will spend five poems speaking to God about the fall has to establish three things first, and poem 1 does all three:

  1. The state of affairs. A city sitting alone, its feasts ended, its people exiled and starving, its sanctuary entered by the nations (1:1–11). [T]
  2. The cause. “The LORD has caused her grief for the multitude of her transgressions” (1:5). The book’s first theological statement names both the agent and the reason. [T]
  3. The speaker. Before the book can pray, someone has to start praying. Zion’s two brief cries in the narrator’s half (1:9c, 11c) are the first prayer in the book, and they open the way for her own speech (1:12–22). [T]

Why here and not elsewhere. Poem 2 begins “How the Lord has covered … in His anger!” (2:1) and makes the Lord the subject of almost every verb in 2:1–8 (2:1–22 dig, Headline 1). It can only open that way because poem 1 has already named him as the one who “caused her grief” (1:5) and has already let Zion say “He” and “You” (1:12–15, 21–22). Poem 1 is where the book’s agent is first named and where its prayer is first spoken; neither could come later without leaving the book without a ground. [I], confidence high.

Revisited after Tool 11. The poem’s partners are the texts that pictured a city’s fall as a woman’s shame — Isa 47 and Nah 3 for foreign cities, Jer 3, Hos 2 and Ezek 16 for Israel herself — and the covenant curses of Deut 28 [S: audit]; new in this dig. The poem stands where the prophets’ charge of adultery has come true, and where the curse of no rest among the nations has fallen (1:3). [I]

Immediate context

  • After. 2:1: “How the Lord has covered the daughter of Zion with a cloud in His anger!” The narrator returns, and the poem’s subject becomes the Lord. [T]
  • Wider. Poem 1 is one of the three poems of equal length (376 words in the WLC index; poems 2 and 3 have 381 each) that precede the book’s shrinking last two poems (overview, verified). It is the only acrostic in the book that keeps the standard order ע–פ. [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

The narrator’s voice is the frame of the first half and makes one intrusion into the second.

  • 1:5 — the first theological statement in the book. כִּי־יְהוָה הוֹגָהּ עַל רֹב־פְּשָׁעֶיהָ (“for the LORD has caused her grief for the multitude of her transgressions”). It gives agent and reason in one clause, and it stands among a list of facts (her adversaries rule, her children are captive) as though it were one more fact. [T]
  • 1:8 — the second. חֵטְא חָטְאָה יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“Jerusalem sinned greatly”; the noun and verb of one root, an intensifying construction). [T]
  • 1:17 — the intrusion. “Zion stretches out her hands; there is no one to comfort her; the LORD has commanded concerning Jacob that the ones round about him should be his adversaries; Jerusalem has become an unclean thing among them”. The narrator interrupts Zion’s speech, not to comfort her, but to confirm what she has said: no comforter, and the LORD’s command behind the adversaries. [T]

What the narrator never does in poem 1. He never addresses Zion (compare 2:13–19) and never addresses God. He reports. The one who speaks to anyone in poem 1 is Zion. [T] Confidence: high.

1:17 and 1:5 belong together. They are the narrator’s only two statements of the LORD’s agency (Tool 3), one in each half. 1:5 says the LORD caused grief; 1:17 says the LORD commanded the adversaries. With 1:10 (“whom You commanded that they should not enter”), צוה (“command”) occurs twice in poem 1: the LORD’s command kept the nations out of the assembly (1:10), and the LORD’s command set Jacob’s neighbours against him (1:17). [T] for the words; [I] for the irony, moderate–high.

9. Tone and Feel

  • A funeral. אֵיכָה is the dirge-cry (2 Sam 1:19, 25, 27 has its short form אֵיךְ in David’s lament). The city is mourned as a dead woman who is still alive. [T] for the word; [S] for the dirge-metre (qinah) as a technical form.
  • Shame before grief. Nakedness seen (1:8), uncleanness in her skirts (1:9), menstrual impurity (1:17), mockery (1:7). The first half of the poem is about exposure; the second about pain. [I]
  • A body. Cheeks (1:2), bones (1:13), feet (1:13), neck (1:14), eyes (1:16), hands (1:17), inner parts and heart (1:20, 22). The city is described as a woman’s body under assault. [T]
  • A voice gathering itself. Two broken cries (1:9c, 11c), then a speech to strangers (1:12–16), then a speech to the peoples and to God (1:18–22). The voice grows longer and more direct. [T] for the sequence.
  • Groaning as the poem’s sound. The root אנח (“groan”) occurs five times, and nowhere else in the book: the priests (1:4), the city (1:8), the people (1:11), Zion (1:21), and “my groans are many” (1:22). The groan passes, like the comforter line, from “her” to “me”. [T]

[T] ### 12. Genre

  • A city lament in acrostic form. The dirge-cry אֵיכָה, the personified city as a widow and a woman exposed, and the alphabet as a frame. [T] That this belongs to an ancient Near Eastern genre of city laments (the Sumerian laments over Ur and Nippur) is [S]; the parallels are real but the differences — one God, named as the agent and asked to see — are what poem 1 makes of the form. [I]
  • Two voices in one poem. The narrator’s report and the city’s speech alternate. The alternation is the genre’s means of turning observation into prayer (Tool 3). [T]
  • Lament and confession together. The poem has the elements of an individual lament — address to God, complaint, petition, imprecation (1:20–22) — and of a confession of sin (1:18, 20). It does not have a vow of praise or an expression of trust. It is lament without the turn to praise. [T] for what is absent.
  • Reading rule. The acrostic is a container: twenty-two verses, each bounded. It does not invite the reader to find a theme per letter [S: audit 4] (the Ps 119 same-letter test). [I]

13. Copycat

What, in poem 1, does a reader rightly copy?

  • Zion’s speech to God. She says “See, O LORD” three times. She gives reasons: the enemy’s pride (1:9), her humiliation (1:11), her distress and her rebellion (1:20). The reader may bring both his misery and his sin to God in the same breath. [T] for the model; [I] for the application.
  • Her confession. “The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against His command” (1:18). It is confession without bargaining: she does not say “therefore restore me”. [T]
  • Her imprecation. “Let all their wickedness come before You; and deal with them as You have dealt with me” (1:22). She hands the enemies to God and asks for no more than she herself has received. Copy it as prayer, not as permission (overview, trap 7). [I]

What does a reader not copy?

  • Her lovers. The allies she sought (1:2, 19) are the model of misplaced trust. [T]
  • Her forgetting. “She did not consider her future” (1:9; Isa 47:7) is the one thing poem 1 names as her failure of mind as distinct from her sin. [T]

14. Bible Timeline

  • Narrative time. After the fall of Jerusalem (587/586 BC; 2 Kgs 25:8–21) — the exile under way (1:3, 5, 18), the temple entered (1:10), famine still pressing (1:11, 19). [T] for the situation; [I] for the date, high.
  • Covenant time. After the curses of Deut 28 (1:3, 5, 14) and the warnings of the prophets (Jer 3; 8; 13; Hos 2), before the restoration promised in Isa 40–55 and Zech 1. [I]
  • Exodus time. The poem’s vocabulary looks back to Egypt (Headline 3). The story that began with God seeing Israel’s affliction now has Israel out of the land and asking him to see it again. [I]
  • Canonical time. In the Ketuvim, after Ruth, Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes; before Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah and Chronicles. Read in that order, the widow of Ruth (comforted) and the tears of Ecclesiastes (comfortless) stand behind the widow of poem 1. [I], low–moderate.

15. Who Am I?

  • The narrator’s position. He sees and reports, but cannot help (compare 2:13, “how shall I comfort you?”). The reader who first meets the poem stands where the narrator stands: watching. [I]
  • The passers-by. “Is it nothing to all you who pass this way?” (1:12). The reader is addressed. Poem 1 makes the reader one of those who pass by, and asks whether he will stop and look. [T] for the address; [I] for the reader’s place.
  • Zion. A reader who has sinned and is suffering for it will find his words in 1:18–22 — and will find there that confessing does not end the lament. [I]
  • Not the LORD, and not the comforter. The reader is never cast as the comforter Zion lacks. The poem does not ask him to supply what only God can. [I] Moderate.

16. So What?

  1. Name the cause rightly. The LORD did this, for her transgressions (1:5); Zion confesses it (1:18). A sermon that blames only Babylon, or only “the fallen world”, preaches less than the text. [T]
  2. Do not stop at the cause. Zion goes on to say “See, O LORD” and “is there any pain like my pain?” Confession does not silence lament. [T]
  3. Bring sin and suffering together. “See, O LORD, for I am in distress … for I have been very rebellious” (1:20). A people who believe their suffering is deserved may still ask God to look at it. [T]/[I]
  4. There is a comforter missing, and the poem will not supply one. The preacher should resist filling the gap before the text does (overview, trap 1 and pitfall below). The canon answers the poem — Isa 40:1; 51:3; Zech 1:17 — but poem 1 does not. [I]
  5. Misplaced trust betrays. The lovers and friends (1:2, 19) are not neutral: they are the prophets’ word for what Israel trusted instead of the LORD (Headline 4). [I]

Original Language Observations

  1. רַבָּתִי, שָׂרָתִי (1:1). Old construct forms with a linking ־ִי (hireq compaginis): “great of people”, “princess of provinces”. They rhyme with each other and give the opening verse its sound. [T] for the forms; [S] for the grammatical label. High.
  2. בָּכוֹ תִבְכֶּה (1:2). Infinitive absolute with the finite verb: “she weeps bitterly”. The same intensifying construction at 1:8 (חֵטְא חָטְאָה, “sinned greatly”, noun + verb) and 1:20 (מָרוֹ מָרִיתִי, “I have been very rebellious”, infinitive + verb). Weeping, sinning and rebelling are the three things poem 1 intensifies. [T] High.
  3. הוֹגָה (1:5, 12). Hiphil of יגה (“cause grief”). The narrator’s 1:5 has the object suffix (הוֹגָהּ, “caused her grief”); Zion’s 1:12 has none. [T]
  4. עוֹלַל לִי (1:12). Poal (passive) of עלל: “which was dealt to me”. At 1:22 the poel active twice: “deal with them as You have dealt with me” (עוֹלֵל … עוֹלַלְתָּ). The agent hidden at 1:12 is named at 1:22: “You”. [T] High.
  5. הֵבֵאתָ (1:21). Perfect, second person: “You have brought”. It can be read as a precative perfect (“bring!”) [S], and BHS suggests emending to the imperative הָבֵא. וְיִהְיוּ is a jussive in form (“and let them be”). The ESV reads a statement followed by a wish; the NASB95 a wish throughout. [T] for the forms. Open.
  6. מְאַהֲבַי (1:19) against אֹהֲבֶיהָ (1:2). Piel against qal participle (Headline 4). [T] High.
  7. אֵין מְנַחֵם with לָהּ / לִי. “There is no comforter for her” (1:2, 9, 17) and “for me” (1:21). The preposition carries the change of voice. [T]
  8. זוֹלֵלָה (1:11). Qal participle, feminine: “worthless, despised”. The same root as “glutton” (Deut 21:20; Prov 23:21), which is the sense the Greek did not take (ἠτιμωμένη, “dishonoured”). [T] for the forms; moderate for the root.

Textual Variants

The BHS apparatus for poem 1 (Logos export) has notes at every verse but 1:1, 3, 5, 10 and 22. Most are minor. Six bear on preaching:

1:14 — “the yoke … is bound” or “He has kept watch over my transgressions”. Triage category 2: substantive, weigh it.

  • MT (WLC). נִשְׂקַד עֹל פְּשָׁעַי (“bound is the yoke of my transgressions”). שׂקד is found only here; עֹל (“yoke”) depends on the vowels.
  • BHS. “dubious; many manuscripts נשׁקד על” — with the letter shin and the preposition עַל, “he kept watch over”. The Greek reads it so: ἐγρηγορήθη ἐπὶ τὰ ἀσεβήματά μου (“He kept watch over my ungodly deeds”). At a later word in the verse, the Lucianic Greek and Symmachus read “his yoke” (עֻלּוֹ) — probably for עָלוּ (“they came up”).
  • The other reading’s echoes. שׁקד עַל (“watch over”) is the verb of Jer 1:12 (“I am watching over My word to perform it”), Jer 31:28; 44:27, and Dan 9:14 (“the LORD has kept the calamity in store”). The Greek ἐγρηγορήθη is in nine verses of Swete, among them Jer 1:12, Dan 9:14 (Theodotion) and Lam 1:14. [T] for the texts.
  • Bearing. Both English versions read “yoke”. The overview’s echo row “1:14 yoke → 3:27; 5:5” (high) rests on the MT vowels at 1:14. 3:27 (“bear the yoke in his youth”) and 5:5 (“on our necks”) are not affected. The neck (צַוָּאר) at 1:14 is secure. [T]
  • Recommendation. Preach the yoke (the MT, followed by both translations), but do not rest a chain on it alone; rate the 1:14 → 3:27 link moderate in the Finalise pass. [I]

1:21 — statement or prayer. BHS: the Greek reads the imperative plural ἀκούσατε (“hear!”) for the opening שָׁמְעוּ (“they heard”), and the Syriac points to שְׁמַע (“hear!”); for הֵבֵאתָ BHS suggests הָבֵא (“bring!”) with the Syriac. The Greek keeps the indicative ἐπήγαγες (“You brought”) and reads καὶ ἐγένοντο (“and they became”) at the end. The ESV follows the MT’s perfect as a statement; the NASB95 reads it as a wish. Both are defensible; the overview’s pulpit note stands. [T] Preach the tension: she prays for the day she says God has brought.

1:16 — “my eye, my eye”. The MT doubles עֵינִי עֵינִי (“my eye, my eye”; the only such doubling in the Hebrew Bible, WLC). BHS: a few manuscripts, the Greek, the Syriac and the Vulgate have it once; BHS proposes deleting the second. Both English versions read “my eyes” once. The doubling may be a copying slip or a cry. [T] Low bearing; do not preach the doubling.

1:7 — the four-line verse. BHS marks a span of 1:7 as “probably added” (b–b); the export does not show which words. The natural candidate is “all her precious things that were from the days of old”, which makes 1:7 the poem’s only four-line verse [I]. Check the span on screen in Logos (as for 2:19). Low bearing.

1:19 — the Greek plus. καὶ οὐχ εὗρον (“and they did not find”), recorded by BHS (Headline 4; Tool 11). Triage category 2 for the Greek; the Hebrew stands.

1:14, 15 — the divine name. BHS at 1:14: “read with many manuscripts יהוה” for אֲדֹנָי, and refers to the same note at 1:15 and at several verses of poem 2. As in poem 2, the distribution of אֲדֹנָי (1:14, 15 twice) and יְהוָה (seven verses) is Leningrad’s. Not preached.

Minor. 1:4 נוּגוֹת (“grieving”): the Greek ἀγόμεναι (“led away”) reads נְהוּגוֹת, losing the יגה chain at 1:4 in Greek. 1:12 לוֹא אֲלֵיכֶם (“Is it nothing to you?”): the Greek tradition divides between οὐ and οἱ; the Hebrew is difficult [S]. 1:20: the Greek transposes “outside” and “in the house”. 1:6, 11, 18: ketivs (The Passage).

Historical and Cultural Background

  • The fall. Nebuchadnezzar’s army breached Jerusalem in the eleventh year of Zedekiah, burned the temple and deported the population in stages (2 Kgs 25:1–21; Jer 39; 52). The book never names Babylon (overview, verified). [T] for the narratives.
  • The excluded who entered (1:10). “Bands of the Chaldeans, bands of the Arameans, bands of the Moabites and bands of the sons of Ammon” were sent against Judah in Jehoiakim’s day, “according to the word of the LORD” (2 Kgs 24:2). Ammon jeered “Aha!” over the profaned sanctuary (Ezek 25:3). Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] excludes Ammonite and Moabite from the assembly. The poem’s “nations … whom You commanded should not enter” has named candidates in the narrative. [T] for the texts; [I] that these are meant, moderate.
  • Widowhood. A widow in Israel was without a male protector and among those the Torah singles out for care (Exod 22:22–24; Deut 10:18; 24:17–21). To call a city “like a widow” is to say it has lost its protector and its standing. [T] for the laws; [I] for the image.
  • Menstrual impurity (1:8–9, 17). Under Lev 15:19–24 a menstruant was unclean for seven days and conveyed uncleanness by contact. The image is of separation and shame, not of moral guilt in itself; poem 1 adds the moral guilt separately (1:8a). [T] for Lev 15; [I] for the reading.
  • The winepress (1:15). Grapes were trodden in a rock-cut vat (גַּת); the image of blood as juice is the prophets’ (Isa 63:2–3; Joel 4:13 [Eng 3:13]). [T]
  • Forced labour (1:1). The corvée (מַס) was the burden laid on subject peoples (Deut 20:11; Judg 1:28–35) and on Israel under Solomon (1 Kgs 5:27 [Eng 5:13]). [T]
  • Pilgrimage (1:4). The “roads of Zion” were the pilgrim roads to the three feasts (Deut 16:16; Ps 84:6–8 [Eng 84:5–7]). [T] for the feasts; [I] that these roads are meant.

Original Audience Reception

  • What the first hearers knew. The survivors knew the siege, the famine and the deportations at first hand. They also knew the covenant curses and the exodus creed (Deut 26:5–9), which were part of their worship. [I]
  • What they would have heard. A poem that begins in their own funeral cry, describes them in the third person, then lets their city speak — and speak the words of Egypt (servitude, groaning, “see our affliction”). They would hear their exile as the exodus run backwards. [I], moderate–high.
  • What would have surprised them. That their city confesses the LORD righteous (1:18) and still demands that he look (1:20); and that the poem gives no answer. [I]
  • What they would have felt. Shame (1:8–9), grief (1:16), anger at the betrayers (1:2, 19, 21–22). The poem gives each a voice. [I]
  • If the poems were used in public mourning (Zech 7:3–5; 8:19; overview, moderate), the hearers would have said “there is no one to comfort her” as a community about itself. [I]

Biblical-Theological Themes

  1. Comfort. The refrain of poem 1 is answered in Isa 40:1 (“Comfort, O comfort My people”), Isa 51:3, 12 (“I, even I, am He who comforts you”), Isa 52:9, Isa 66:13 (“As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you; and you will be comforted in Jerusalem”) and Zech 1:17. In the New Testament: “the consolation of Israel” (Luke 2:25) and “another Helper” (παράκλητος, John 14:16) (overview). [T] for the texts. High (theme).
  2. The exodus reversed and renewed. Exile is told in exodus words (Headline 3); the prophets promise a new exodus (Isa 43:16–19; 51:9–11; Hos 2:14–15 [Heb 2:16–17]). [I] Moderate–high.
  3. The unfaithful wife. Jer 3; Ezek 16; 23; Hos 1–3 portray Israel as the LORD’s unfaithful wife; poem 1 shows her betrayed by her lovers and calling to them in vain (1:2, 19). The marriage is restored in Hos 2:16–25 [Eng 2:14–23]; Isa 54:4–8 (“your husband is your Maker”); and, in the New Testament, the bride of Rev 19:7; 21:2. [T] for the texts; [I] for the line. Moderate–high.
  4. The day of the LORD. “The day of His fierce anger” (1:12) and “the day which You have proclaimed” (1:21) — the day the prophets announced (Zeph 1:14–18; 2:2–3) has come for Zion first, and is asked for her enemies. [T]
  5. Sanctuary and assembly. The nations entered the sanctuary (1:10). The prophets promised that the nations would come to Zion to learn (Isa 2:2–3; Mic 4:2 — both in the chapter scan for their shared names) and that foreigners and eunuchs would be gathered (Isa 56:3–8, which revises Deut 23). [T] for the texts; [I] for the line, moderate.

N/A — not a Torah passage. ### Christological Reading

The trap first. 1:12 (“Is it nothing to all you who pass this way?”) is sung of Christ in Christian liturgy [S]. In its own voice it is Zion’s, and the overview’s trap 6 stands: Christ is reached through categories the canon itself supplies, not by changing the speaker. [I]

Routes the text supports.

  1. The comforter — trajectory. Moderate–high (overview). Poem 1 is the refrain’s home: אֵין מְנַחֵם (“there is no comforter”), παρακαλῶν in Greek. The canon answers it with Isa 40:1; 51:3, 12; Zech 1:17, and the New Testament with “the consolation of Israel” (Luke 2:25) and “another Helper” (John 14:16). This dig adds that 1:16 describes the missing comforter: one who would “restore my soul” (מֵשִׁיב נַפְשִׁי). The only other use of the exact phrase is Ruth 4:15, of the child born to Naomi’s house, the grandfather of David — the other widow among the Megilloth. [T] for the words; low–moderate for Ruth as a route; do not build on it alone.
  2. The righteous sufferer’s prayer — typology through the psalms. Moderate. Zion’s words in 1:12–22 are close to Ps 31:8–11 (“You have seen my affliction … for I am in distress … my strength has failed”) and Ps 69:21 [Eng 69:20] (“I looked for comforters, but I found none”) (Tool 11). Jesus prays Ps 31:6 [Eng 31:5] from the cross: “Father, into Your hands I commit My spirit” (Luke 23:46, the Greek of Ps 30:6). Ps 69 is the psalm the Gospels hear in his passion (John 2:17; 15:25; 19:28–29; Matt 27:34). The difference is the point. Zion’s strength fails “because of my transgressions” (1:14), as the psalmist’s does “because of my iniquity” (Ps 31:11). Christ prays the same psalm without sin of his own. [T] for the texts; [I] for the typology.
  3. The day of the LORD’s fierce anger — trajectory. Moderate (overview). 1:12 is the first “day of His anger” in the book. The New Testament places the decisive day of wrath at the cross (Rom 3:25–26; 5:9) [S].
  4. The winepress — category. Moderate. “The Lord has trodden as in a winepress the virgin daughter of Judah” (1:15). Isa 63:2–3 has the LORD tread the winepress of the nations; Rev 14:19–20 and 19:15 have the Son do it at the end (overview). Poem 1 shows the winepress used first on God’s own people. The Christian reading is not that Christ is trodden at 1:15, but that the treading that fell on Zion will fall on the world — and the one who treads it is the Son. [I]
  5. The bride. Moderate. The city exposed, unclean and betrayed by her lovers (1:2, 8–9, 17, 19) is the unfaithful wife of Jer 3, Hos 2 and Ezek 16 (Tool 11). The New Testament’s bride is cleansed by her husband’s self-giving, “having no spot” (Eph 5:25–27), and comes down “as a bride adorned for her husband” (Rev 21:2). [T] for the texts; [I] for the line.

What not to do. Do not make 1:12 Christ’s lament, 1:15 Christ’s treading, or 1:16 a prediction of the Spirit. The poem is a city’s prayer; its Christological weight is in what it lacks and asks for. [I]

Difficult / Contested Verses

1:5, 12–15 — “The LORD has caused her grief”

  • The difficulty. Doctrinal and pastoral: the poem names God as the cause of the city’s pain, including the crushing of young men (1:15).
  • What the text says. It names both the agent (“the LORD”, 1:5, 12) and the reason (“for the multitude of her transgressions”, 1:5). It does not stop there: the same voice calls the pain unequalled (1:12) and asks God to look (1:20). The book will later say that he does not afflict “from His heart” (3:33) [S: audit].
  • Honest exegesis. The text holds sovereignty, guilt and lament together without resolving them. [T]
  • Pastoral handling. Do not explain the agency away, and do not apply 1:5 to an individual sufferer as a diagnosis. Poem 1 is a city’s confession of a covenant judgement it was warned of (Deut 28). [I]

1:8–9, 17 — nakedness, uncleanness, menstrual impurity

  • The difficulty. Pastoral and ethical: sexual and menstrual imagery used of a woman, in public worship.
  • What the text says. Shame (her nakedness seen, 1:8), uncleanness (in her skirts, 1:9) and separation (נִדָּה, 1:17). 1:8 names the sin separately (“Jerusalem sinned greatly”); the impurity images describe the city’s state and isolation. The word at 1:8 (נִידָה) may also mean an object of head-shaking (Tool 7). [T]
  • Pastoral handling. Neither sensationalise nor skip (so the sweep). Name the image plainly — shame and exclusion — and note that it is the prophets’ figure for a city, drawn from the purity laws, not a judgement on women. [I]

1:10 — the law of exclusion

  • The difficulty. Ethical: Deut 23:3 [Heb 23:4] excludes Ammonites and Moabites from the assembly, and the poem appeals to it.
  • What the text says. Zion cites the law God gave (1:10). The canon itself revises the exclusion: Ruth the Moabitess is received into Israel (Ruth 1:16; 4:13), and Isa 56:3–8 promises foreigners and eunuchs a place in the house of prayer. [T] for the texts.
  • Honest exegesis. The poem’s point is not ethnic: it is that those who came in were enemies who profaned the sanctuary (Ezek 25:3). [I]

1:21–22 — “deal with them as You have dealt with me”

  • The difficulty. Imprecation. See the overview’s trap 7.
  • What the text says. She asks for no more than she has received, and she gives the matter to God (“let all their wickedness come before You”). [T]
  • Pastoral handling. Preach it as prayer, and note the measure: “as You have dealt with me”. [I]

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Making 1:12 the voice of Christ

  • The error. Preaching “Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?” as Jesus on the cross.
  • Why it’s wrong. The speaker is Zion, and her pain is “for my transgressions” (1:14, 22). Putting the line in Christ’s mouth makes him confess sins he did not commit, or silently changes the text.
  • Corrective. Let Zion speak 1:12. Then, if the sermon reaches Christ, go through the psalm he prayed (Ps 31) or the comforter the canon promises (Isa 40:1; Luke 2:25).

Pitfall 2: Supplying the comforter the poem withholds

  • The error. Answering “there is no one to comfort her” in the first five minutes with “but Jesus is the comforter”.
  • Why it’s wrong. The poem says it four times and then has Zion say it of herself. The book does not answer it in poem 1, or anywhere (2:13 is the last use of the root). A congregation in grief needs to hear the absence named before it is filled.
  • Corrective. Sit in the refrain. When the gospel comes, let it come as the answer the canon gives later (Isa 40:1; 51:3; Zech 1:17), not as a correction of the poem.

Pitfall 3: “They deserved it” — or “it wasn’t their fault”

  • The error. Either reading 1:8 and 1:18 as the whole message, or skipping them to stay with the pain.
  • Why it’s wrong. The poem holds both: “Jerusalem sinned greatly” (1:8) and “is there any pain like my pain?” (1:12); “The LORD is righteous” (1:18) and “See, O LORD” (1:20). The overview’s trap 4 bites here.
  • Corrective. Preach the “and”: confession does not silence lament, and lament does not deny guilt.

Pitfall 4: Skipping or sensationalising 1:8–9, 17

  • The error. Omitting the impurity images, or dwelling on them.
  • Why it’s wrong. They carry the poem’s first half: exposure and exclusion before pain.
  • Corrective. Name the shame plainly; explain the purity background in one sentence (Lev 15); move on to what she does with it — she prays (1:9c).

Pitfall 5: Reading 1:16 as Jeremiah’s tears

  • The error. “Jeremiah wept, and so must we.”
  • Why it’s wrong. The speaker of 1:16 is Zion. The words she uses are Jeremiah’s (Jer 8:23; 13:17), but the book is anonymous (overview, trap 5).
  • Corrective. Say that the prophet wept in advance for what the city now weeps over — two texts, one grief.

Pitfall 6: Resting a point on an unstable reading

  • The error. Building a sermon point on the yoke at 1:14, the doubled “my eye” at 1:16, or the mood of 1:21.
  • Why it’s wrong. Each is textually or grammatically uncertain (Textual Variants).
  • Corrective. The yoke may be preached as the MT and both translations read it, but not used to carry the chain to 3:27. Do not preach the doubling. At 1:21, preach the tension between “You have brought” and “let them be”.
