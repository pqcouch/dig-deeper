The one substantive consequence is at Acts 11:20, below.

1. Acts 11:20 — the project’s note has been wrong since October, and Round 1 said so

The standing note records 11:20 as “the one place in the Acts work where NA28’s choice runs against the narrative logic”, on the ground that the internal evidence and the early external evidence both pull toward Ἕλληνας.

Round 1 already carried the correction and it was not applied: Metzger’s verdict, quoted there, is that “the preponderant weight of the external evidence combines with the strong transcriptional probability in support of Ἑλληνιστάς” [S: round 1 §3.3, via McMains, *Lexham Bible Dictionary*].

Round 2 adds the explanation Round 1 lacked, from a source new to the library. Houghton’s 2025 companion to the UBS sixth edition states that Ἕλληνας “appears to represent a replacement with a more familiar term, possibly occurring independently multiple times in the manuscript tradition” [S: Houghton, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 299]. Janse adds that Ἕλληνας is probably preferable on sense, Greeks being distinguished from the Jews of v.19 by way of life rather than language [S: Janse, in *Encyclopedia of Ancient Greek Language and Linguistics* 1:238].

So the crux is divided, and the note must be rewritten. The narrative-logic observation stands, with Robertson and Janse behind it. What the note gets wrong is the claim about external evidence, and the reason is a conflation: weight of attestation and direction of change are different questions, and on the second the argument runs the other way. Ἕλληνας is the easier, more familiar word, so a scribe meeting Ἑλληνιστάς drifts toward it — independently, in more than one place. That is why the editors print the harder reading, and it is not a case of an edition defying the evidence.

And the queue item is obsolete. “Metzger on 11:20 and 20:28” was queued on the assumption the discussion was out of reach. Round 1 had Metzger’s verdict at second hand already, and Houghton’s 2025 volume is in the library and is the modern equivalent. Retire the item; record Houghton in the holdings note.

2. Acts 20:28 — Round 1 is the better answer; Round 2 adds nothing material

Round 1 quotes the Lexham Textual Notes at 20:28 in full — so the library does have a note there, contrary to what the second round’s framing assumed — along with Metzger on ὁ ἴδιος as a papyrus term of endearment for close relatives, possibly an early Christian title for Jesus paralleling Romans 8:32 [S: round 1 §3.2].

Round 2 returned the NET notes and Cornerstone 12:574 to the same effect: the external evidence divides evenly, “church of God” has the superior internal case, and the decisive consideration is that scribes troubled by the implication that God has blood would alter θεοῦ to κυρίου.

One thing worth keeping from Round 2. Cornerstone states the inference the project had reached on its own: the Byzantine normalisation of τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου to ἰδίου αἵματος “functions as indirect evidence that the unusual word order in earlier witnesses was intentional rather than accidental” [S: Trites and Larkin, *Cornerstone* 12:574]. That is the first external corroboration of the house rule where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied, which until now rested on three internal instances. The rule can be promoted from working observation to corroborated method.

3. Isaiah 60:17 and 1 Clement 42:5 — the negative is now doubly attested

Round 2 reached exactly the result Round 1 reached, independently and from partly the same sources: 1 Clement’s use of Isaiah 60:17 is secure, but no commentary in the library discusses Acts 20:28 against Isaiah 60:17, and the treatments found handle it as functional parallel rather than textual allusion.

Both rounds reached that negative through Philippians commentaries, via ἐπίσκοπος at Philippians 1:1 — so the library simply has nothing on the question directly, and the negative shows absence of corroboration rather than considered rejection.

Verdict unchanged and now firm. The corpus fact stands — Isaiah 60:17 is the one Greek Old Testament text in which God gives ἐπισκόπους — and the allusion claim is uncorroborated and should carry that label in the 20:17–38 run and in the overview’s Isaiah row. Two independent passes have now failed to corroborate it; a third would be waste.

4. The κατηχέω / ἀσφάλεια thread — the one place Round 2 genuinely advances the project

Round 1 answered the ἀσφάλεια half thoroughly (Fitzmyer’s doctrinal-rather-than-merely-historical reading, with TDNT, O’Toole, Thompson, Spicq and Johnson). Round 2 adds a source Round 1 did not have, on the κατηχέω half:

Peters traces the preface vocabulary through Acts and argues that κατηχέω characterises information transmission as unreliable, against ἐκτίθημι as Luke’s term for secure and trustworthy knowledge transfer, with ἀκριβῶς linking the preface to the Apollos episode [S: Peters, *Luke among the Ancient Historians*, Pickwick, 2022].

Checked against the SBLGNT, and the data are better than the summary [T]:

  • ἐκτίθημι stands in four New Testament verses, all in Acts — 7:21 (Moses exposed as an infant, a different sense), 11:4, 18:26, 28:23. As a verb of knowledge-transfer it is three occurrences, all in Acts: Peter setting out the Cornelius episode καθεξῆς; Priscilla and Aquila expounding the way ἀκριβέστερον; Paul expounding the kingdom from morning to evening.
  • ἀκριβῶς stands in five New Testament verses — Matthew 2:8, Luke 1:3, Acts 18:25, Ephesians 5:15, 1 Thessalonians 5:2. Within Luke–Acts it is two, Luke 1:3 and Acts 18:25, and the claim must carry that restriction or it is false.

The contrast is staged in a single scene. At 18:25 Apollos ἦν κατηχημένος and taught ἀκριβῶς; at 18:26 Priscilla and Aquila ἀκριβέστερον … ἐξέθεντο the way of God to him. Catechised, teaching accurately, then expounded to more accurately — both verbs and both degrees of the adverb, in consecutive verses.

Consequence for the Saul-conversion study filed today. That study noted Luke’s preface vocabulary recurring at moments of retelling, including καθεξῆς at Acts 11:4. The verb at 11:4 is ἐξετίθετο — so 11:4 carries both of Luke’s terms for reliable narration at once: Peter set it out in order. Add to that study’s Headline 4; [T] on the distributions, [S: Peters] for the reliable/unreliable contrast.

A correction I made and then had to withdraw — the same failure, twice in one session

On the strength of the project’s standing note, which records the 21:1–36 run as finding the passive of κατηχέω with περί “twice in Luke–Acts, Luke 1:4 and Acts 21:21”, I verified the lemma, found three occurrences — Luke 1:4, Acts 21:21 and 21:24 — and wrote this section up as a count error caught in a filed document.

The run is not wrong. It was corrected on 11 October, and says so in its own text: “Corrected 11 October 2026: the run first said twice, and missed 21:24.” It names 21:24, quotes it, and draws the point that 21:24’s γνώσονται πάντες answers the preface’s ἐπιγνῷς as well as its κατηχήθης. It also already carries the ἀκριβῶς pair at Luke 1:3 / Acts 18:25, the ἀσφαλ- family in both of its modes across eight Luke–Acts verses, and a Logos check on the thread run the same day.

So the error was mine, and it is the same error as the stale queue — trusting a summary of a document instead of the document. The project already has the rule, learned in September on the skill’s own status table: check the installed files, not a previous document’s status note. It applies to the standing notes exactly as it applies to the skill, and I broke it twice in one session: once by re-asking five answered questions, once by reporting a corrected error as live.

What survives. The ἐκτίθημι material is genuinely new — the 21:1–36 run has the κατηχέω and ἀκριβῶς threads but not the contrasting verb, and Acts 11:4’s pairing of ἐξετίθετο with καθεξῆς appears in none of the Acts documents. That stands, and so does the Saul-study consequence above.

A gate amendment still worth drafting, on its own merits rather than on this false alarm. The Phase 10.5 chain gate verifies that every reference a report claims contains the word; nothing in it searches for occurrences the report failed to claim, so an overcount is caught and an undercount is invisible. The 21:1–36 run found its own undercount by some other route, which is luck rather than procedure. Proposed: for any load-bearing chain, run the bare lemma search once and compare its length against the stated count. One extra command, and it closes the class.

Method notes

1. Check the existing rounds before queueing a question. Five credits and a session went on re-asking what was answered in October. The queue was stale because Round 1’s answers were never folded back into the standing note.

2. Never name a single resource in the question. The first form of the 11:20 question named the Lexham Textual Notes. The Assistant scoped its search to that resource alone, found nothing, and answered from general knowledge behind a one-clause disclaimer — fluent, plausible, and agreeing with the project’s existing view, which is the worst combination, since an uncited answer that confirms you is indistinguishable from an echo. Re-asked across the whole library, the same question returned four citations including the one that settled it. Ask the whole library and let the citations show what answered.

3. The corpus still earns its place, but it cannot tell you what you already know. The lemma search did its job perfectly: it returned three occurrences of the κατηχέω construction, which is the right answer. What it could not tell me was that the run had already found them. A verification that is run against a summary rather than against the document it summarises will manufacture findings, and that is what happened here.

4. One rule would have prevented both failures in this session. Check the installed file, not the note about it. Before a Logos question: read the existing rounds. Before reporting an error in a run: open the run. Both checks cost a grep; together they would have saved five credits and a retraction.

5. The honest yield of Round 2. Houghton on 11:20 with the transcriptional explanation; Janse; Peters on the κατηχέω / ἐκτίθημι contrast; and the ἐκτίθημι distribution with the 11:4 pairing. Four items, of which one — the 11:20 explanation — changes a standing note. Everything else in this document is either Round 1’s work restated or my own correction of myself.
