One Law or Three? An Assessment of Wellum and of Green & Caneday

Papers assessed: (1) Stephen J. Wellum, “Rejection of the Tripartite Distinction of the Law”, extract from Peter J. Gentry and Stephen J. Wellum, Kingdom through Covenant, 2nd ed. (Crossway, 2018), pp. 785–788. (2) Brad Green and Ardel Caneday, “One Indivisible Law Covenant with Three Discernible Aspects: Assessing the Moral, Ceremonial, and Civil Distinction in the Mosaic Law” (undated essay, no notes).

Texts used: WLC Hebrew (Leningrad), Swete LXX and SBLGNT from the _texts/ corpus, with the Torah book overviews, the Decalogue synoptic dig, the Exodus 20:22–24:18 dig, the Leviticus overview and sweep, and the Acts sweep. English is for reference only. Every Hebrew and Greek reading quoted below was checked in the corpus on 8 October 2026; counts state the method used.

Date: 8 October 2026

1. Summary

Both papers reach the same conclusion: the Mosaic law-covenant is a single whole. Christ fulfils it and brings it to an end as a covenant, and Christian moral obligation does not rest on keeping one third of it after the other two thirds have been removed. Beyond that conclusion the two papers are quite different pieces of work.

Wellum gives a compressed summary of an argument the book develops at length. His thesis is carefully limited. He does not say the law contains no distinctions; he says the tripartite division is not the principle by which Scripture decides what carries over. He names the distinctions he accepts, gives his sources and argues from Paul and Hebrews. His weak points are the ones a summary is prone to. At the key exegetical steps he asserts where he needs to argue: what “under law” means, what “the whole law” means in James, and his reliance on Moo. Some texts that would test his position are not addressed, among them Matthew 5:17–19, Acts 15, 1 Corinthians 9:8–10 and the Torah’s own “for ever” vocabulary.

Green and Caneday have written a well-crafted popular essay that is weaker as scholarship. It has no notes. Its claim about the origin of the distinction (Aquinas) is historically wrong. It cites Deuteronomy 10:4 for something that verse does not say. Its closing claim that “no New Testament author ever tried to pull [the threads] apart” is contradicted by the New Testament’s own use of Leviticus 19. The deeper problem is that the essay is not consistent with itself. It rejects a “division”, accepts a “distinction”, and ends by recommending the threefold distinction “if used wisely”. On each of the three categories its positions match Westminster Confession 19.3–5 almost word for word. So the disagreement it announces turns out to be mostly a matter of terms. The one real dispute, the Fourth Commandment, is never mentioned.

The most useful finding for your conversations is one that neither paper uses. The Torah itself does distinguish between its laws, but it does so by who a law is addressed to and the reason it gives, not by moral, ceremonial and civil content. Some laws were broken by the nations, which were judged for breaking them (Lev 18:24–28). Some were binding on the resident foreigner as well as the native Israelite (Lev 17; 18:26). Some marked Israel’s separation from the peoples (Lev 20:24–26). The Torah’s “perpetual statute” formula (ḥuqqat/ḥoq ʿôlām) occurs 32 times in the Torah, and every occurrence attaches to cultic or priestly law. Read with Genesis 9 and Acts 15, these texts give a distinction that comes from the text itself. It explains most of what the tripartite scheme explains without importing its categories (§6). Attribution, corrected 8 October 2026: the core of this observation (Leviticus 18:26–28 set against Deuteronomy 14:21) is not original here. It is the argument of Philip Ross, From the Finger of God (2010), as presented by Justin Dillehay in “3 Parts of the Law” (see the companion assessment). What is added here is Leviticus 20:24–26, Genesis 9:3–4, the Leviticus 17 → Acts 15 link and the ʿôlām count.

2. The question and the common ground

The tripartite scheme sorts the Mosaic law into moral law (summarised in the Decalogue and binding for ever), ceremonial law (sacrifice, priesthood, purity, food and calendar, fulfilled in Christ and abrogated) and judicial or civil law (Israel’s polity, which “expired together with the State of that people; not obliging any other now, further than the general equity thereof may require”, WCF 19.4).

All three authors accept, and so does the Westminster tradition:

  • that the sacrificial and priestly system is fulfilled in Christ and no longer in force (Heb 7–10);
  • that Israel’s penal and civil code does not bind a modern state as it stands;
  • that the moral will of God endures and is grounded in God himself, before Sinai and beyond it;
  • that the law still serves the church as Scripture.

The live differences are narrower than the rhetoric on either side suggests:

  1. Is the Decalogue, as a text, the permanent summary of the moral law, or is it the Sinai covenant’s own document, so that what endures is the morality expressed in it, re-mediated through Christ and the apostles?
  2. The Fourth Commandment. The answer to the first question decides the Sabbath (or Lord’s Day) question.
  3. Method. Does the church read the Mosaic law directly, sorting it, or only through the New Testament?

Wellum takes a clear position on the third question (§3). Green and Caneday mostly skirt all three (§4).

3. Wellum: assessment

3.1 What kind of document this is

These four pages come at the end of an 800-page argument for “progressive covenantalism”. In the book they rest on long earlier chapters, including the book’s treatment of Jeremiah 31 and the new covenant. The extract has to be judged as what it is: the conclusion of a case, with that case summarised rather than restated. Some of the gaps below are gaps in the extract and not in the book. I note that where it applies.

3.2 Strengths

A precisely limited thesis. Wellum does not say the law has no internal distinctions. His words are that Scripture “does not appeal to the tripartite distinction as the principle means by which the continuity and discontinuity of moral law is established”. He then names the distinctions he accepts: weightier matters (Matt 23:23), mercy over sacrifice (Matt 9:13; cf. 5:24), sacrificial versus civil material, and “the central place of the Decalogue”. That is the right place to draw the line, and it shields him from the easy reply that “Jesus himself ranked the commandments”. Matthew 23:23 is verified: τὰ βαρύτερα τοῦ νόμου, “the weightier matters of the law”.

A genuinely textual “package” argument. Galatians 5:3 is a strong text for his case and is quoted accurately. The man who receives circumcision as covenant entry ὀφειλέτης ἐστὶν ὅλον τὸν νόμον ποιῆσαι, “is a debtor to do the whole law”. Hebrews 7:11–12 carries the main weight and it holds. ὁ λαὸς γὰρ ἐπ᾽ αὐτῆς νενομοθέτηται, “the people received the law on the basis of it [the priesthood]”, and so μετατιθεμένης γὰρ τῆς ἱερωσύνης ἐξ ἀνάγκης καὶ νόμου μετάθεσις γίνεται, “when the priesthood is changed, of necessity a change of law also takes place”. The author of Hebrews does not limit the change to “ceremonial law”. He ties the giving of the law as such to the Levitical priesthood. This is the best single text against a simple tripartite reading, and Wellum uses it correctly.

1 Corinthians 9:20–21 rightly at the centre. Paul became “as under law” (ὡς ὑπὸ νόμον) to those under law, “not being myself under law”, yet μὴ ὢν ἄνομος θεοῦ ἀλλ᾽ ἔννομος Χριστοῦ, “not being without God’s law but in-lawed to Christ”. Wellum notices what most readers miss. Paul separates God’s law from the Mosaic law without making God’s law vanish. Here “under law” plainly means Jewish covenant observance, and he exploits that correctly.

Second Temple contrast. He cites Wisdom 18:4, Jubilees, Philo (Mos. 2.14) and Josephus (Ag. Ap. 2.277) to show that much of first-century Judaism held the law to be eternal and unchangeable. Even in a summary that is real historical grounding (I have not checked the references; see §8).

Purposes of the law. His closing account of what the law was for is drawn from the texts and covers them widely: revealing God’s character, exposing sin, typological instruction, supervision (paidagōgos, Gal 3:24), and continuing use “as Scripture”. It keeps him clear of the antinomian caricature.

3.3 Weaknesses

1. The characterisation of covenant theology is slightly off target. Wellum says covenant theology “has determined what is morally binding on Christians by appealing to the tripartite distinction”. The Westminster Confession does not ground the moral law in the tripartite sorting. It grounds it in the law given to Adam and written on the heart (19.1). Sinai is a re-delivery of that law “in ten commandments” (19.2). The tripartite scheme is the confession’s way of classifying the Mosaic legislation. It is not the reason the moral law binds. Wellum’s critique applies to the popular version, which sorts laws into bins, more than to the confessional one. A well-read Reformed interlocutor will say so at once.

2. “Under law” settled by assertion. He rejects the two standard alternatives (legalism, or ceremonial law only) in a single sentence: Paul “does not equate ‘law’ (nomos) with a ‘legalistic’ misunderstanding of it”. That may be right. In this extract it is stated, not shown, and it is the point the whole section rests on. It would be fair to check whether the book argues it earlier.

3. James 2 cuts both ways and he uses only one edge. James does say that whoever “keeps the whole law but stumbles in one point has become guilty of all” (2:10). But James says this to Christians in order to hold them to the law: “you are convicted by the law as transgressors” (2:9), “so speak and so act as those who are to be judged by the law of liberty” (2:12). His “whole law” is illustrated only from the Decalogue (2:11, adultery and murder) and the “royal law” of Leviticus 19:18 (2:8). The Decalogue dig already lists James 2:11 among the forward echoes as “the indivisibility of the ten”. James is therefore a text a Reformed reader can claim with at least equal right. Wellum cites it as if it served his case alone.

4. Moo’s “source” claim cannot be squared with apostolic practice. The quoted sentence says: “Our source for determining God’s eternal moral law is Christ and the apostles, not the Mosaic law or even the Ten Commandments”. But the apostles regularly take their moral teaching from the Mosaic law, including from case law:

  • 1 Corinthians 9:8–10, in the same chapter Wellum calls “crucial”, eleven verses before his key text. Paul asks ἢ καὶ ὁ νόμος ταῦτα οὐ λέγει; “does not the law also say these things?” He then quotes Deuteronomy 25:4, “you shall not muzzle the ox while it treads out the grain”, and draws from it the minister’s right to support: δι᾽ ἡμᾶς γὰρ ἐγράφη, “for our sake it was written”. The law is also quoted at 1 Timothy 5:18 as ἡ γραφή.
  • 2 Corinthians 13:1: ἐπὶ στόματος δύο μαρτύρων καὶ τριῶν σταθήσεται πᾶν ῥῆμα. This is the judicial rule of Deuteronomy 19:15, applied to church discipline.
  • 1 Corinthians 5:13: ἐξάρατε τὸν πονηρὸν ἐξ ὑμῶν αὐτῶν, the refrain that follows capital sanctions in Deuteronomy (e.g. 17:7 וּבִעַרְתָּ הָרָע מִקִּרְבֶּךָ), now applied to excommunication.
  • Ephesians 6:2–3; Romans 13:9; 1 Peter 1:16: the Decalogue and the Holiness Code quoted to Gentile churches.

These are exactly what Westminster calls the “general equity” of the judicial law. A civil statute is carried across into a new setting by analogy. A reformulation more careful than Moo’s would say that Christ and the apostles are the authoritative interpreters of the moral law, and that they interpret it out of Moses. That reformulation, however, narrows the gap with the Reformed position considerably.

5. Texts not brought to bear in the extract:

  • Matthew 5:17–19. “I did not come to abolish (καταλῦσαι) but to fulfil (πληρῶσαι)… not one iota or one stroke shall pass from the law until all is accomplished (ἕως ἂν πάντα γένηται)”. This is the first text anyone will raise against him. Wellum’s “fulfil” framework can handle it, but the extract does not show how.
  • Acts 15. This is the one New Testament passage that formally asks the question at issue. The Pharisaic believers demand that Gentiles be told τηρεῖν τὸν νόμον Μωϋσέως, “to keep the law of Moses” (15:5). The council refuses the yoke (15:10) and imposes four abstentions (15:20, 29). See §6.4.
  • Jeremiah 31:33 and Ezekiel 36:27. The new-covenant promises are not law-free. Jeremiah writes נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם, “I will put my torah within them”. Ezekiel writes בְּחֻקַּי תֵּלֵכוּ וּמִשְׁפָּטַי תִּשְׁמְרוּ, “you will walk in my statutes and keep my judgements”, which is the exact Levitical pair of Leviticus 18:4–5, 26. Hebrews 8:10 quotes the Greek of Jeremiah 38:33 LXX, νόμους μου, with the plural for the Hebrew singular. The book discusses Jeremiah 31 at length elsewhere, but the extract’s assertion that “the law-covenant… has now come to its end” needs these texts addressed at this point, because they are the Old Testament’s own statement of what becomes of the torah in the new covenant.
  • The Torah’s own “for ever”. Wellum sets the New Testament against “Jewish thought” that the law was eternal. But “for ever” is the Torah’s own vocabulary before it is Second Temple Judaism’s (§6.3). The contrast he draws is really between the New Testament and Exodus–Numbers, and that needs to be faced directly.

6. “Christians do not ‘do’ or ‘keep’ the law” goes beyond the lexical evidence. The do/keep versus fulfil distinction (Rom 8:4; 13:8–10; Gal 5:14) is a real observation in Paul. But 1 Corinthians 7:19 says ἡ περιτομὴ οὐδέν ἐστιν… ἀλλὰ τήρησις ἐντολῶν θεοῦ, “circumcision is nothing… but keeping the commandments of God”. Romans 2:26 speaks of the uncircumcised who τὰ δικαιώματα τοῦ νόμου φυλάσσῃ, “keeps the just requirements of the law”. Whether “the commandments of God” in 1 Corinthians 7:19 are the Mosaic commandments is debated, but the word “keep” is there. And 1 Corinthians 7:19 is itself a distinction within the commandments, because circumcision was a commandment (Lev 12:3).

7. The positive criterion is underspecified. “We apply its ethical instruction to us only through the lens of the new covenant” says where to look. It does not say how to decide. Wellum has already admitted that there are distinctions within the law. Once he has to explain why the New Testament keeps Leviticus 19:18 and drops 19:19, he will be making sorting judgements again. The critics’ strongest reply is that he has rejected the label and kept the function.

3.4 Verdict on Wellum

Wellum’s piece has competent scholarly form and a defensible, carefully limited thesis. Its best exegesis is Hebrews 7:11–12 and 1 Corinthians 9:20–21. Its central weakness is selectivity: the texts are read for the package model, and the texts that show the apostles working from Mosaic case law are not engaged. As a summary, it should be read with the earlier chapters it depends on before anyone pronounces on the book.

4. Green and Caneday: assessment

4.1 What kind of document this is

This is a popular theological essay, magazine-length, without footnotes. It opens with a pastoral illustration (ham and mixed fabrics at Easter) and is written for an educated lay readership. It should not be judged as a journal article. But it makes historical and exegetical claims of a scholarly kind, and those can be tested.

4.2 Strengths

Leviticus 19:18–19 is a sound observation, and the Hebrew paragraphing supports it. In the WLC, Leviticus 19:1–19 is a single petuchah unit. The first paragraph marker in the chapter falls at the end of v. 19 (the others fall after vv. 22, 32 and 37). “Love your neighbour as yourself” (v. 18) and the mixtures law (v. 19) therefore stand in the same scribal paragraph, and the chapter’s moral and ritual material is indeed interwoven. The paper’s claim about the text is accurate.

Hebrews 7:12 and 10:1 are used correctly, with the right emphasis on redemptive-historical change.

Mark 7 is handled with some care. The authors distinguish what Jesus does to the food laws (7:19) from his reaffirmation of moral evils from the heart (7:21–22), and they refuse to turn that into a tripartite charter.

Ephesians 6:1–3 is the right test case, and the formulation is good: the commandment’s “inclusion within the Ten Words codifies morality that was true from the beginning”. This is their clearest statement of where enduring obligation is grounded, namely in God’s character and not in the Decalogue’s status as a covenant document.

The rhetorical design is effective for its audience. It is clear, concrete and pastorally motivated.

4.3 Weaknesses

1. The historical claim is wrong. “This three-part distinction is a theological construct derived from Aquinas, not from Moses, Jesus, or Paul.” Aquinas gave the classical systematic form (ST I–II q. 99, praecepta moralia, caeremonialia, iudicialia). But the distinction is much older:

  • Ptolemy’s Letter to Flora (c. 160) already divides the law three ways, though with Gnostic assumptions.
  • Justin (Dialogue 44) distinguishes precepts of piety and righteousness from those given for hardness of heart or as mystery.
  • Irenaeus (Adv. Haer. 4.15–16) separates the natural precepts of the Decalogue from the precepts of bondage.
  • Augustine (Contra Faustum 6.2) distinguishes precepts for living (vitae agendae) from precepts for signifying (vitae significandae).

Rabbinic tradition has its own two-way division between mišpāṭîm (laws reason would have required, such as murder and theft) and ḥuqqîm (decrees with no evident rationale, such as pork, šaʿaṭnēz and the red heifer) (b. Yoma 67b; Sifra on Lev 18:4). It is built on the Torah’s own pair חֻקִּים וּמִשְׁפָּטִים, and it explicitly includes the mixed-fabrics law the essay cites. So the distinction is ancient and Jewish as well as Christian. That does not make it right. It does make “derived from Aquinas” untenable, and it undermines the closing line that “no faithful Israelite… ever tried to pull them apart”. (The patristic and rabbinic references come from my general knowledge and were not checked in Logos for this assessment; see §8.)

2. A citation slip, and a text that cuts against them. The essay says the Ten Commandments are “the Old Covenant itself”, citing “Deut. 4:13; 10:4”. Deuteronomy 4:13 does identify the two: וַיַּגֵּד לָכֶם אֶת־בְּרִיתוֹ… עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים, “he declared to you his covenant… the ten words”. But 10:4 contains no word for covenant. It says only that God wrote the ten words on the tablets. The text they needed was Exodus 34:28, דִּבְרֵי הַבְּרִית עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים, “the words of the covenant, the ten words”. The Decalogue dig confirms that עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים occurs exactly three times, all of them outside the Decalogues themselves.

More seriously, the Deuteronomic setting of these verses is the strongest textual evidence for the Decalogue’s distinct status, which is the root of the tripartite intuition:

  • the Ten Words alone were spoken by God directly to the whole assembly, “and he added no more” (וְלֹא יָסָף, Deut 5:22);
  • they alone were written by “the finger of God” on stone (Exod 31:18);
  • the tablets were placed in the ark (בָּאָרוֹן, Deut 10:5), while “this book of the torah” was placed beside it (מִצַּד אֲרוֹן, Deut 31:26).

The same Torah also calls the case-law collection “the book of the covenant” (סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית, Exod 24:7). The blood is thrown “in accordance with all these words” (24:8), and the Exodus dig is right that the mišpāṭîm are “the content of the mediated covenant”. Deuteronomy speaks of a covenant in Moab “besides the covenant he made with them at Horeb” (Deut 28:69 [EVV 29:1]: מִלְּבַד הַבְּרִית). So “the Decalogue is the covenant” is one of the Torah’s ways of speaking, not its only one. The essay would have been stronger with the whole picture: the Decalogue is both distinct within the Sinai covenant and integral to it.

3. The essay is not consistent with itself, and the result looks very like Westminster. The title promises “three discernible aspects”. The introduction says theologians “artificially impose this tripartite division”. The body says “three categories are warranted”. The conclusion recommends that “the threefold distinction, if used wisely, guards us against two errors”. Taken category by category, their positions are:

Green & Caneday Westminster Confession 19
Ceremonial “reached their end in Christ’s fulfilment” “now abrogated, under the new testament” (19.3)
Civil “expired as a binding code… even as its equity continues to instruct” “expired together with the State of that people; not obliging… further than the general equity thereof may require” (19.4)
Moral “moral from the beginning, long before being codified at Sinai… God’s character” law given to Adam, “continued to be a perfect rule… delivered… upon Mount Sinai” (19.1–2); “doth for ever bind all” (19.5)

The essay quotes the confession’s “expired together with the state of that people” itself. What remains of the disagreement is a difference of vocabulary, “aspect” against “law”. The only material difference is one the essay never names: whether the Fourth Commandment, as worded in the Decalogue, binds the church. The essay’s logic implies that it does not as worded. The Sabbath is never mentioned. For a piece on this subject that is the largest single omission.

4. The proposed criterion fails on Leviticus’s own terms. On the essay’s account, what endures is what expresses “God’s character”. But Leviticus grounds the food laws in God’s character first. The full motto “you shall be holy, for I am holy” occurs five times (11:44, 45; 19:2; 20:7, 26), and its first statement is about diet: כִּי קָדוֹשׁ אָנִי וְלֹא תְטַמְּאוּ אֶת־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם, “for I am holy; and you shall not defile yourselves…” (11:44; Leviticus overview, verified). The mixtures law is introduced as “my statutes” (19:19), just as the love command is sealed with “I am the LORD” (19:18). 1 Peter 1:16 then applies the motto to Christian conduct. So “God’s character” cannot be the test that separates the enduring from the expired, because the Torah grounds both in it. A further criterion is needed, and the essay does not supply one (§6 suggests where the text supplies it).

5. “No New Testament author ever tried to pull them apart” is false on the text. Every New Testament citation of Leviticus 19:18 detaches it from 19:19: Matthew 22:39; Mark 12:31; Romans 13:9; Galatians 5:14; James 2:8. Romans 13:9 quotes four Decalogue prohibitions and then says that “any other commandment” (καὶ εἴ τις ἑτέρα ἐντολή) is summed up ἐν τῷ λόγῳ τούτῳ, Leviticus 19:18. Paul has chosen one verse out of the paragraph the essay calls inseparable. 1 Corinthians 7:19 sets a commandment (circumcision) against “keeping the commandments of God”. Matthew 23:23 ranks commandments. The New Testament does distinguish within the law. The real question is on what principle it does so, and the essay’s rhetoric closes that question when it ought to open it.

6. Their best evidence is missed. Paul’s quotation of the Fifth Commandment in Ephesians 6:3 stops short. Swete’s Greek Exodus 20:12 ends ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς τῆς ἀγαθῆς ἧς Κύριος ὁ θεός σου δίδωσίν σοι (“on the good land which the Lord your God gives you”), and Deuteronomy 5:16 is near-identical. Ephesians 6:3 ends ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς and drops the gift of Canaan. In the Hebrew of both Decalogues the promise is tied to הָאֲדָמָה אֲשֶׁר־יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ נֹתֵן לָךְ. Paul keeps the command and the promise but removes the promise from Israel’s land, so ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς can now be heard as “on the earth”. That is exactly the redemptive-historical transposition the authors argue for, made in the very verse they discuss. They quote the ESV (“in the land”) and do not notice it. (Paul also uses ἐντολὴ πρώτη ἐν ἐπαγγελίᾳ, “the first commandment with a promise”, which counts within the Ten as a recognised series. A tripartite defender will press that point, and the essay does not anticipate it.)

7. Smaller points.

  • Mark 7:19. “Jesus declared all foods clean” is the narrator’s comment. The participle καθαρίζων (masc. nom.) refers back to Jesus as subject of λέγει (7:18). The reading is also disputed: the SBLGNT marks a variant (Byzantine καθαρίζον, taking it with the digestive process). The point stands on the better text, but the essay presents a contested reading as uncontested.
  • Mark 7:10 would have served the authors better than anything they cite. Jesus quotes the Fifth Commandment and Exodus 21:17, a capital case law, in one breath, as “Moses said” and “the commandment of God” (7:8–9). That is precisely their thesis that moral and civil are interwoven.
  • Romans 3:21–26 is read as Christ’s own “faithfulness” without flagging that this is the contested subjective-genitive reading of πίστις Χριστοῦ.
  • No interlocutor is named. The essay engages no defender of the tripartite view. The obvious recent one is Philip S. Ross, From the Finger of God: The Biblical and Theological Basis for the Threefold Division of the Law (Mentor, 2010). An essay that “corrects fellow Reformed Christians” should engage at least one of them.

4.4 Verdict on Green and Caneday

This is a readable essay with a sound pastoral instinct and one strong exegetical observation (Leviticus 19). Its scholarly rigour is low: no apparatus, a mistaken history, a misapplied proof-text and a sweeping conclusion that the New Testament contradicts. Its own conclusion lands on the Westminster position, so the controversy it announces turns out largely to be a quarrel over terms. Its most important omission is the Sabbath.

6. What the Torah itself distinguishes

Both papers argue mostly from the New Testament. Your Torah digs and the corpus show that the Torah does distinguish between its laws, but along lines neither the tripartite scheme nor the “single package” model quite captures. This section was first presented as my own synthesis. That attribution is corrected (8 October 2026). The central observation in §6.1, that Leviticus 18 binds the nations while the food and carcass laws mark Israel off (there via Deut 14:21), is already argued by Philip Ross (From the Finger of God, 2010) and by Justin Dillehay, who builds on him (“3 Parts of the Law”, The Gospel Coalition), and the credit is theirs. The additions made here are Leviticus 20:24–26 and Genesis 9:3–4 (§6.1), the resident-foreigner laws and Acts 15 (§6.2), the ʿôlām count (§6.3) and the Sabbath-as-sign evidence (§6.4). Those additions are still inferences to be tested before they are relied on.

6.1 Laws the nations broke, and laws that separate Israel from the nations

  • Leviticus 18:24–28. The sexual prohibitions of chapter 18 were already being broken by “the men of the land who were before you” (אַנְשֵׁי־הָאָרֶץ), and the land “vomited out the nation that was before you” (18:28). The Canaanites were never under Sinai, yet they were held to account for these acts. These laws bind “the native and the sojourner who sojourns among you” alike (18:26: הָאֶזְרָח וְהַגֵּר).
  • Leviticus 20:24–26. The clean/unclean food distinction is grounded the other way round: “I am the LORD your God, who has separated you (הִבְדַּלְתִּי) from the peoples; you shall therefore separate between the clean beast and the unclean” (20:24–25). The food laws mark Israel off from the nations. The nations were never judged for eating pork. (The bdl chain is verified in the Leviticus overview: seven verses, 1:17; 5:8; 10:10; 11:47; 20:24, 25, 26.)
  • Genesis 9:3–4. To Noah, and so to all humanity, “every moving thing that lives shall be food for you; as the green plant I give you all” (נָתַתִּי לָכֶם אֶת־כֹּל), with one exception: “flesh with its life, its blood, you shall not eat”. The food restrictions are therefore later than and narrower than the creation-wide permission. The blood prohibition is older than both.

This is a distinction the Torah makes itself, and it answers the question people most often ask. Leviticus 18:22 and Leviticus 11 do not stand on the same footing. The text gives them different addressees and different rationales. The first is among the practices for which the land expelled its earlier inhabitants. The second is a sign of Israel’s election. This is a point about how Leviticus itself argues. It is not a claim that settles every pastoral question, and the New Testament’s own restatements (e.g. 1 Cor 6:9–11) still need their own exegesis.

6.2 The resident-foreigner laws and Acts 15

Leviticus 17 extends four requirements to “the sojourner who sojourns among them” (וּמִן־הַגֵּר הַגָּר בְּתוֹכָם): sacrifice only at the tent (17:8–9), no eating of blood (17:10–12), draining the blood of hunted game (17:13), and, with 18:26, the sexual prohibitions. The four abstentions of the Jerusalem decree are things polluted by idols, πορνεία, what is strangled, and blood (Acts 15:20, 29; τοῦ πνικτοῦ carries a variant). They correspond closely to that list. That correspondence is a well-known scholarly proposal and should be treated as an inference, not a certainty. If it holds, the apostles answered “must Gentiles keep the law of Moses?” (15:5) neither with “the moral third” nor with “none of it”. They required what the Torah itself had already required of the Gentile living among Israel. Your Acts sweep rightly warns that how far the four abstentions bind today is contested and should not be settled from the pulpit. The principle of selection, however, is drawn from the Torah’s own native/sojourner distinction. Neither paper mentions Acts 15.

6.3 What the Torah calls “for ever”

A surface phrase search of the whole WLC (accents and points stripped, maqqef and paseq normalised to spaces, prefixed forms included; Hebrew versification) finds ḥuqqat ʿôlām or ḥoq ʿôlām (“a perpetual statute / everlasting due”) 34 times in the Hebrew Bible, 32 of them in Exodus–Numbers:

Exod 12:14, 17; 27:21; 28:43; 29:9, 28; 30:21 · Lev 3:17; 6:11, 15; 7:34, 36; 10:9, 15; 16:29, 31, 34; 17:7; 23:14, 21, 31, 41; 24:3, 9 · Num 10:8; 15:15; 18:8, 11, 19, 23; 19:10, 21. (Outside the Torah: Jer 5:22, of the sea’s boundary; Ezek 46:14, of the visionary temple’s offering.)

Every Torah occurrence attaches to cultic or priestly law. They concern Passover and Unleavened Bread, the lampstand, the priests’ garments, washing and portions, fat and blood, the Day of Atonement, the feasts, the trumpets, the red heifer and the Levites’ service. None attaches to the Decalogue or to the ethical commands of Leviticus 19. The Sabbath alone is called a בְּרִית עוֹלָם and an אוֹת, “a sign between me and the sons of Israel” (Exod 31:13, 16–17).

This bears on both papers and on your likely conversations:

  • It tells against the popular tripartite instinct that “permanent” means “moral”. In the Torah’s own language the ceremonial laws are the ones labelled perpetual.
  • It sharpens Wellum’s point and shows a gap in it. Belief in the law’s eternity was not an innovation of Second Temple Judaism. It reads the Torah’s own wording. Any account in which these laws end in Christ owes an account of ʿôlām. The usual one is that it denotes indefinite duration within an order (“throughout your generations”, לְדֹרֹתֵיכֶם, its frequent companion) and not timelessness. That account is defensible, but neither paper gives it.
  • This is the first text-based objection Hebrew Roots or Sabbatarian interlocutors are likely to raise. Neither paper prepares you for it.

6.4 The Sabbath inside the Decalogue

The Decalogue dig showed that the two Decalogues ground the Fourth Word differently: in creation (Exod 20:11, the LORD rested) and in the exodus (Deut 5:15, the slave rests). Exodus 31:13–17 then makes the Sabbath Israel’s covenant sign. So the Decalogue contains within it the one command that is explicitly Israel’s covenant sign. That is the strongest evidence for Green and Caneday’s thesis that the moral and the covenantal are interwoven even inside the Ten, and they do not use it. It is also why the tripartite scheme strains at exactly this point. Colossians 2:16–17 lists σαββάτων among the things that are σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων, “a shadow of things to come”. Hebrews 4 argues from the creation rest that “a sabbath rest remains”. Any conversation on this topic will end up here.

7. For conversations: likely questions and fair short answers

“If Christians keep ‘do not steal’, why do they eat pork?” The Torah itself gives the two different grounds. The food laws separate Israel from the nations (Lev 20:24–26), and humanity had been given all food in Noah (Gen 9:3). Theft and the Leviticus 18 sins were judged in nations never under Sinai (Lev 18:24–28). The New Testament then says so explicitly (Mark 7:19; Rom 13:9).

“Doesn’t Leviticus 19 put ‘love your neighbour’ next to mixed fabrics?” It does. They share a paragraph in the Hebrew. But every New Testament writer who quotes 19:18 leaves 19:19 behind. That is evidence that the apostles did distinguish, and the honest question is on what principle.

“Didn’t Jesus say not one iota would pass?” Yes: “until all is accomplished” (Matt 5:18), and in a sermon whose antitheses go on to reinterpret a civil law (lex talionis, 5:38) and the divorce provision. On every view, “fulfil” has to carry real weight. Neither paper explains how.

“The Torah calls these laws ‘for ever’.” It does, 32 times, and always of cultic law (§6.3). The question is what ʿôlām means alongside “throughout your generations”, and how Hebrews reads the priesthood those statutes serve (Heb 7:11–12).

“Is the Sabbath moral or ceremonial?” That is the real dividing line, and both papers avoid it. The text gives it a creation ground (Exod 20:11), a redemption ground (Deut 5:15) and a covenant-sign function (Exod 31:13–17). The New Testament calls it a shadow (Col 2:16–17) and also speaks of a rest that remains (Heb 4:9).

“Should a state enforce the Old Testament penalties?” Both papers and Westminster say no, and Paul shows how civil law is carried over. Deuteronomy’s “purge the evil from your midst” becomes excommunication (1 Cor 5:13). The two-or-three-witness rule becomes church procedure (2 Cor 13:1). The ox’s muzzle becomes the minister’s wage (1 Cor 9:9).

“So who is right?” Both papers are right that Scripture treats the Sinai covenant as a whole that has reached its telos in Christ (Heb 7:12; Gal 3:24). The tripartite tradition is right that Scripture distinguishes within it: the Decalogue’s unique mode and deposit (Deut 5:22; 10:5; 31:26), weightier matters (Matt 23:23), commandments kept against commandments set aside (1 Cor 7:19). The question that remains open is which principle of distinction comes from the text. The Torah’s own categories of addressee and rationale (§6.1–6.2) are a better place to start than either “three laws” or “one package”.
