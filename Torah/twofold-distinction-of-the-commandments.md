# The Twofold Distinction of the Commandments in Old Testament Law

**Companion to:** `tripartite-law-paper.md` (§6) · **First applied:** `Exodus/twofold-distinction-exodus-20-22to23-33` · **Revised:** 29 October 2026

---

## The Distinction

Commandments in the Old Testament may be divided into two categories:

1. **Commandments expressing the created moral order**, binding on humanity as such and attested before and outside Sinai (Gen 9; Lev 18:24–28; Rom 1–2).

2. **Commandments constituting Israel as a separated and priestly people** (food, purity, cult, calendar and the covenant signs). These are characteristically marked by the language of separation (Lev 20:24–26) and of perpetuity within Israel's generations, but they can be present without those markers.

The **judicial law** is the polity's *application* and *sanction* of both kinds. Some covenant law is sanctioned by God alone and stands outside the judicial law.

The apostles carry the law into the church by analogy, which is what the Westminster Confession means by "general equity".

A position closest to the biblical evidence might therefore be called **one covenant, two kinds of commandment**.

## Applying the Distinction

*These refinements arose from applying the scheme to the Book of the Covenant (Exod 20:22–23:33, October 2026). They are meant to make the scheme usable on any passage.*

### 1. Classify the parts of a law, not the law as a whole

A single law can belong to both kinds. Interest is the clearest case (Exod 22:25 [Heb 22:24]): the duty of mercy to the poor is kind 1, while interest-free lending *within Israel* is kind 2, since the foreigner may be charged interest (Deut 23:20 [Heb 23:21]). Classify each part of a law separately:

| Part | Question |
|---|---|
| **Norm** | What does the law require or forbid? |
| **Ground** | What reason does the text give for it? |
| **Form** | In what shape is it given to Israel (cultic act, calendar, land, polity)? |
| **Sanction** | Who enforces it, and how? |

The category is decided by the **norm**. The other three are recorded beside it. This extends the move the scheme already makes for the judicial law, which divides one law into rule and sanction.

### 2. Classify by the norm, not the motive

The stated motive for a universal norm is often Israel's own history: *"You shall not wrong a sojourner… for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt"* (Exod 22:21; 23:9). If the motive decided the category, this law would be kind 2. But the norm is kind 1: outsiders are held to it (Job 31:32; Ezek 16:49). **The motive is Israel's; the norm is creation's.** A motive drawn from Israel's history does not place a law in kind 2.

### 3. Tests for kind 1 — and say which one carries the weight

| Test | What counts | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1a. Before Sinai** | The norm is stated or assumed before Sinai | Gen 9:5–6 (bloodshed; the beast that kills a man); Gen 31:39 (custody of animals); Gen 34:12 (bride-price); Gen 40:15 (kidnapping) |
| **1b. Outsiders held to it** | The nations are judged for it, or a non-Israelite setting assumes it | Lev 18:24–28; Deut 18:9–12; Amos 1:6, 9; Ezek 16:49; Job |
| **1c. Creation ground** | The norm is grounded in creation, not in Israel's history | Gen 9:6 (*"in the image of God"*); Job 31:15 |
| **1d. Apostolic reading** | The New Testament binds Gentile believers by it, or condemns Gentiles for breaking it | Rom 1:30; 13:9; 1 Tim 1:9–10 |

The evidence varies in strength. For some laws only 1d applies; honour of parents in the Pentateuch is one example, since Gen 9:22–25 is narrative rather than stated norm. **Say which test is carrying the classification.** 1d is a legitimate warrant, but it is not the same as 1a.

**On Job:** Job's setting outside Israel (*"in the land of Uz"*, Job 1:1) is in the text, so it counts for 1b. Its date and its relation to Sinai are reconstruction, so it should not be cited for 1a.

### 4. Tests for kind 2 — the markers are characteristic, not required

| Test | What counts | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **2a. Content** | Cult, calendar, food, purity or a covenant sign | Exod 20:24–26 (altar); 23:14–17 (feasts) |
| **2b. Separation or holiness given as the ground** | *"I have separated you from the peoples"*; *"you shall be holy men to Me"* | Lev 20:24–26; Exod 22:31 [Heb 22:30] |
| **2c. Perpetuity in Israel's generations** | *ḥuqqat ʿôlām* ('a perpetual statute'); "throughout your generations"; a covenant sign | Exod 31:13, 16; the 32 Torah occurrences of the formula |
| **2d. The line drawn at the foreigner** | The Torah elsewhere permits outsiders what it forbids Israel | Deut 14:21 (the carcass and the kid in its mother's milk); Deut 23:20 [Heb 23:21] (interest) |

A passage may have none of the 2b or 2c vocabulary and still contain kind-2 law. The Book of the Covenant has no "separate", no *ḥuqqat ʿôlām* and no "throughout your generations". Its altar, firstborn and festival laws are identified by **content (2a)** and by **parallels elsewhere (2d)**. When a classification rests only on 2a or 2d, say so.

**A caution at food and purity.** The Torah's own line is not perfectly sharp here. Deut 14:21 lets the sojourner eat a carcass, but Lev 17:15 makes the sojourner who eats one unclean like the native.

### 5. Three roles in the judicial law

| Role | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Application** | Specifying how a norm works in Israel's circumstances: the case, the measure, the distinction of intent, **and procedure** (before God or the judges, the oath, the evidence) | Exod 21:13 (no intent); 21:28–29 (the ox already known to gore); 22:8–11 (the deposit and the oath) |
| **Sanction by the community** | A penalty or remedy the polity enforces: death, restitution, ransom, compensation — **including release** | Exod 21:12–17; 22:1–4; 21:26–27 (the slave freed for an eye or a tooth) |
| **Sanction by God alone** | God is the enforcer and no court could act | Exod 22:22–24 (*"I will kill you with the sword"*); 22:27; 23:7; 23:21 |

**Sanction by God alone is not judicial law** on the scheme's definition, but it is not mere exhortation either. Call it **covenant law under divine sanction**, and keep it apart from the judicial law.

### 6. Check whether a body of law sanctions both kinds

The scheme holds that the judicial law applies and sanctions *both* kinds, and across the Torah it does. The Sabbath carries death (Exod 31:14–15; Num 15:32–36), and blood and fat carry being *"cut off"* (Lev 7:25–27). **But a given body of law may sanction only one.** In the Book of the Covenant every human sanction enforces a kind-1 norm. The one exception is Exod 22:20, idolatry in cultic form, with a ḥērem sanction; there the norm is kind 1 and the form and sanction are Israel's. Report what each corpus actually does, rather than assuming the general claim applies to it.

### 7. The Sabbath remains the hinge

The Fourth Word belongs to both kinds, and its texts ground it differently:

- **creation** (Exod 20:11; 31:17);
- **covenant sign and perpetuity** (Exod 31:13, 16);
- **the rest of the dependent**: *"so that your ox and your donkey may rest, and the son of your female slave… may refresh themselves"* (Exod 23:12), with neither the creation ground nor the sign.

Record which grounding each Sabbath text gives. The evidence remains genuinely divided, and should be presented that way.

### 8. Procedure for a passage

1. List the commandments.
2. For each, record the norm, ground, form and sanction (§1).
3. Classify by the **norm** (§2), naming the test that carries it (§§3–4).
4. Mark the judicial role: application, sanction by the community, sanction by God alone, or none (§5).
5. Count, then report proportions rather than exact figures, since the counts depend on how the laws are divided.
6. Note where the kinds cluster in the passage's structure. In the Book of the Covenant, kind 2 stands at the ends and kind 1 in the middle.
7. Check which kinds the passage actually sanctions (§6).
8. Close with where the scheme strained on this passage.
