## The Passage

Four chapters, most of it case law. The frame and the ratification are quoted; the case law is summarised by category with ranges.

> **20:22** And the LORD said to Moses, "Thus you shall say to the people of Israel: '**You have seen for yourselves that I have talked with you from heaven.** **23** You shall not make gods of silver to be with me, nor shall you make for yourselves gods of gold. **24** An altar of earth you shall make for me and sacrifice on it your burnt offerings and your peace offerings… **In every place where I cause my name to be remembered I will come to you and bless you. 25** If you make me an altar of stone, you shall not build it of hewn stones, for if you wield your tool on it you profane it. **26** And you shall not go up by steps to my altar, that your nakedness be not exposed on it.'"

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple converging tools:

1. **The whole block is framed by an altar: it opens with the law of the altar (20:24–26) and closes with an altar actually built at the foot of the mountain (24:4), and the frame's stated purpose is presence and blessing — "In every place where I cause my name to be remembered I will come to you and bless you" (20:24).** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Positional Necessity, Linking Words, Internal echo. **The case law is enclosed by worship at both ends.** Israel's civil and criminal statutes are not free-standing legislation; they are the interior of a covenant whose outer walls are sacrifice. *High confidence.*

2. **The blood is divided in half — half thrown against the altar, half thrown on the people (24:6, 8) — and the sentence that interprets it is "Behold the blood of the covenant."** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Narrator's Comment, Christological Reading. **One blood, two parties, one covenant.** This is the verse Jesus takes onto his own lips at the Supper ("this is my blood of the covenant", Matt 26:28) and the verse Hebrews quotes when explaining why "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" (Heb 9:18–20). *High confidence — and the single most important verse in the unit.*

3. **The exodus is written into the case law as its motive. "You shall not wrong a sojourner… **for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt**" (22:21); "**You know the heart of a sojourner**, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt" (23:9); and when the oppressed cry out, God says "**I will surely hear their cry**" (22:23) and "**I will hear, for I am compassionate**" (22:27).** `[T]` Surfaced by: Linking Words, Repetition, Author's Purpose, Internal echo, So What?. Those are the verbs of 2:23–25 — Israel cried, God heard — now redeployed to protect the vulnerable *inside* Israel. **The rescued are legislated into becoming rescuers, and if they do not, God will hear the same cry against them.** *High confidence.*

4. **The people vow three times, and the third time after the terms have been read aloud. 19:8 "All that the LORD has spoken we will do"; 24:3 the same; 24:7, after Moses reads the Book of the Covenant, "All that the LORD has spoken we will do, **and we will be obedient**."** `[T]` Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Internal echo, Positional Necessity. The consent is informed, unanimous and escalating — which is precisely what makes chapter 32 what it is. *High confidence.*

5. **Seventy-four men eat a meal in the presence of God and are not killed, and the narrator says so explicitly: "he did not lay his hand on the chief men of the people of Israel; they beheld God, and ate and drank" (24:11).** `[T]` Surfaced by: Narrator's Comment, Tone, Original Audience, Christological Reading. The aside tells the reader what did *not* happen, which means the reader was expected to fear that it would — and against 19:21 and 33:20 that fear is well founded. **The covenant is sealed not only in blood but at a table.** *High confidence.*

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** One thing dominates: **20:18–21.** The people heard God's voice, could not bear it, stood far off, and asked for a mediator — "You speak to us, and we will listen; but do not let God speak to us, lest we die." Moses drew near to the thick darkness where God was.

**Necessity answer.** Why here? Because **this is the first thing Moses mediates.** The Book of the Covenant is the discharge of the request of 20:19: the people asked for God's word at one remove, and 20:22 begins "Thus you shall say to the people of Israel." Every statute in these chapters comes to Israel through the arrangement they themselves asked for. `[T]` *High confidence.* The 19:9–20:21 dig found that the theophany existed to authenticate the mediator (19:9 → 20:19); this unit is what the authenticated mediator then delivers.

It must also come **before** ch. 24's ratification, because a covenant cannot be ratified until its terms are stated and heard — hence the order: terms (20:22–23:33) → recital → consent → blood (24:3–8). And it must come **before** 25–31, because the forty days it ends with (24:18) are the forty days of the tabernacle instructions and of the calf (32:1).

**Implication for this passage.** Three things follow. First, the case law is not a digression between the Decalogue and the tabernacle; it is **the content of the mediated covenant**, and 24:8's blood is thrown over *these words* ("in accordance with all these words"). Second, this unit supplies the ratification that everything downstream depends on — what ch. 32 breaks and ch. 34 remakes is the covenant cut here. Third, its ending is the book's hinge: 24:15–18's cloud, six days, seventh-day call and forty days are the door into 25–31.

**Re-weighting.** The overview lists this unit as "Standard (⭐ if the covenant is the series hinge)". On the evidence above — the mediator's first delivery, the ratification in blood, the meal, and the hand-off into the tabernacle block — **it is the hinge, and should be ⭐ HIGH.** Carried back below.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 4 sharpens it further: 20:23's "gods of silver… gods of gold" is the precise clause the calf breaks, and 23:20–23's angel — a *promise* here — becomes a *penalty* at 32:34 and 33:2–3. Both are only visible from this unit's side.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** The overview's grace-before-law rule meets its hardest test in a block of case law — and survives, because the text supplies the indicative inside the statutes rather than only in the preamble (22:21; 23:9).

**Passage-level.** Three things this unit is doing:

- **Delivering the mediated covenant.** The people asked for a mediator; here is what he mediates. The block's content is what "covenant" actually means in practice: how you treat a slave, a pregnant woman, an ox, a widow, an enemy's donkey.
- **Ratifying it.** 24:3–8 is the legal act the whole book has been building toward since 19:5. Everything from ch. 32 onward presupposes that this happened.
- **Turning rescued slaves into a just society.** The recurring motive — you were sojourners, you know the heart of a sojourner — makes Israel's own experience of oppression the ground of its law. `[T]` *High confidence.*

---

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6.* Short form: this is the first thing Moses mediates after 20:19, it must precede its own ratification, and its closing verses are the door into the tabernacle block.

#### General contextual notes

**Immediately before (20:18–21).** The people at a distance; Moses in the thick darkness. The transition is seamless and load-bearing: 20:22 is God's answer to a request made three verses earlier.

**Immediately after (25:1).** "The LORD said to Moses, 'Speak to the people of Israel, that they take for me a contribution.'" The first of the seven tabernacle speeches, spoken inside the forty days of 24:18.

**Section.** Third movement (19:1–40:38). The Sinai block now reads: proposal and vow (19:1–8) → the LORD comes down (19:9–20:21) → **the Book of the Covenant, ratified in blood (20:22–24:18)** → instructed (25–31) → broken (32) → renewed (33–34) → built (35–40).

**Commonly read out of context.** Three passages travel badly. **21:22–25** is quoted in the abortion debate by both sides, usually without the case-law frame. **21:2–6** (the slave who loves his master) is preached as a Christ-type on a lexical basis that will not bear it (see Preaching Pitfalls). And **23:20–21** ("my name is in him") is used as a stand-alone Christophany proof-text, detached from the fact that this same angel becomes a demotion at 33:2–3.

**Devices.**

- **An altar inclusio.** 20:24–26 legislates an altar; 24:4 builds one. `[T]` *High confidence — the block's clearest structural signal, and easy to miss because the two ends are four chapters apart.*
- **Casuistic law between two hortatory frames.** The interior (21:1–23:19) is "if… then" case law; the two ends (20:22–26; 23:20–33) are direct divine address with promises attached. The shape is: *promise → rules → promise*, then ratification.
- **The ratification's own sequence is a chiasm-adjacent doubling**: words spoken (24:3) → consent → words *written* (24:4) → altar and offerings → blood halved → words *read* (24:7) → consent again → blood applied. **Consent is given twice, before and after the reading**, with the writing and the blood in between. `[T]` *High.*
- **The block ends where the next begins.** 24:15–18 belongs structurally to this unit and functionally to the next; the six-days-and-seventh-day pattern of 24:16 is the shape 25:1–31:18 then takes in seven speeches.

### 4. Linking Words

**20:22 — "Thus you shall say."** The mediation formula. Everything in the block is *reported* speech.

**20:24 — "In every place where I cause my name to be remembered **I will come to you and I will bless you**."** Two promises in the apodosis of an altar law. The whole legal block sits under this sentence.

**20:25 — "**for** if you wield your tool on it you profane it."** ←*for*← A reason attached to a prohibition about human workmanship at the altar.

**21:1 — "Now these are the rules."** The formal heading; everything to 23:19 hangs off it.

**22:21 / 23:9 — "**for** you were sojourners in the land of Egypt."** The block's most important connector, twice. ←*for*← The law's ground is the hearers' biography.

**22:23, 27 — "**if** they cry out to me, **I will surely hear**"; "**if** he cries to me, **I will hear, for I am compassionate**."** Conditional plus divine commitment plus a stated ground in God's character. Three clauses that turn a civil statute into a covenant threat.

**23:21 — "**for** he will not pardon your transgression, **for** my name is in him."** Two causal clauses stacked, and the second is the more startling.

**24:8 — "the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you **in accordance with all these words**."** The blood is explicitly tied to *the words just read*. The rite does not float free of the content.

### 5. Parallels

Not poetry — **N/A** at verse level, with one exception and one structural note.

**The exception: 21:23–25 is formally a poetic list.** "Life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot, burn for burn, wound for wound, stripe for stripe" — a seven-fold series in tight parallel, the only passage of its kind in the block. **The form matters:** it is a *formula*, and formulas function as principles (proportionality) rather than as itemised sentencing instructions. See Difficult Verses.

**The structural parallel: 22:21–27 and 23:1–9.** Both move from a prohibition against oppressing the vulnerable to a statement about God hearing or judging, and both are anchored to Egypt. The second is not a repetition but a return, and it is what makes the "sojourner" theme a deliberate thread rather than an isolated clause.

---

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Almost the entire block is divine speech, so the narrator is nearly silent — which makes his three appearances in ch. 24 decisive.

- **24:3, 7 — the people's answer, reported twice.** The narrator does not editorialise; he simply records the same consent twice, once before the reading and once after. On a second reading the repetition is unbearable.
- **24:10 — "and they saw the God of Israel."** Flat, unqualified, and followed by a description that describes only what was *under his feet*. The narrator tells us they saw and then declines to say what.
- **24:11 — "and he did not lay his hand on the chief men of the people of Israel."** `[T]` **The unit's most revealing aside.** A narrator only says what did not happen when the reader expects it might. Set against 19:21 ("lest they break through to the LORD to look and many of them perish") and 33:20, this half-verse is the narrator quietly registering an escape.

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory.** Moses, Aaron, **Nadab and Abihu**, the seventy elders, **Joshua**, **Hur**; Israel and the twelve tribes; Egypt; Sinai; the six nations of 23:23. Triage: mostly routine at this point in the book. Two carry freight.

- **Nadab and Abihu (24:1, 9)** — **outstanding-word check fires.** They eat in God's presence and live here; they are consecrated as priests at 28:1; they die for unauthorised fire at Leviticus 10:1–2. This verse is where their story starts, and its point is that God *did not* strike them. `[T]` for the appearances; `[I]` for the trajectory. *Moderate–high.*
- **Hur (24:14)** — left in charge with Aaron during the forty days. Aaron's half of that pairing produces the calf; Hur's grandson Bezalel builds the tabernacle (31:2). The 25–31 dig noted this from the other end; 24:14 is its source. `[T]`/`[I]` *Moderate.*

**The words that carry the unit.**

**(a) *mišpāṭîm* — "rules, judgments, case-decisions" (21:1)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
The heading-word for 21:1–23:19, distinguishing this material from the *dĕbārîm* ("words") of the Decalogue. *Mišpāṭîm* are judicial precedents — the kind of law a court applies, phrased casuistically ("if a man…"). The distinction matters: the Ten Words state the covenant's shape; the *mišpāṭîm* show what it looks like in a dispute about an ox.

**(b) *sēper habbĕrît* — "the Book of the Covenant" (24:7)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
The block names itself. Moses **writes** (24:4) and then **reads** (24:7) — so the covenant Israel consents to is a *documented* one, and the document is the thing the blood is applied "in accordance with" (24:8). This is also the phrase 2 Kings 23:2, 21 uses of the scroll found in the temple.

**(c) *dam habbĕrît* — "the blood of the covenant" (24:8)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
Three words that Jesus takes over almost verbatim at the Supper (Matt 26:28; Mark 14:24, "this is my blood of the covenant"). See the Christological Reading.

**(d) *gēr* — "sojourner, resident alien" (22:21; 23:9)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
Not a foreigner passing through but a long-term resident without kinship protection — structurally the most vulnerable person in an ancient society, because there is no clan to avenge them. Israel *was* one. The law's protection of the *gēr* is therefore autobiographical.

**(e) *ḥannûn* — "gracious, compassionate" (22:27)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
"If he cries to me, I will hear, **for I am gracious**." The same adjective stands second in the great self-declaration of 34:6 ("merciful and **gracious**"). **Its first occurrence in Exodus is in a law about a poor man's coat.** `[T]` for both texts; `[I]` for the deliberate anticipation. *Moderate–high — and a genuinely striking preaching point.*

**Also worth a line:** ***rāṣaʿ*** (21:6), "bore/pierce" the ear with an awl — a single-occurrence verb here; note that Psalm 40:6's "ears you have dug for me" uses a different verb (*kārâ*), which matters for a popular typological reading (see Preaching Pitfalls). *Moderate–high; verify.*

### 8. Translations

**21:22 — "so that her children come out" / "so that she has a miscarriage."** ESV: "so that her children come out, but there is no harm." NASB (older): "so that she gives birth prematurely." KJV: "so that her fruit depart from her." NIV: "she gives birth prematurely." **The renderings diverge sharply and the divergence is doctrinally weaponised** — whether the Hebrew describes a *miscarriage* or a *premature live birth* is the hinge of a standard argument about the unborn's legal status. The Hebrew is literally "her children come out". *The moderns do **not** agree here, so the ancient-versions check is triggered by disagreement rather than agreement.* **Preaching implication:** if this verse comes up, say plainly that translations differ and why; do not build a case on one rendering. *Routed to Open Questions.*

**21:23–25 — "life for life, eye for eye."** All versions agree; the rendering is exegetically weighty; **ancient check fires.** *Routed to Open Questions.*

**23:19 — "You shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk."** All versions agree. Notable only because it recurs verbatim at 34:26 and Deut 14:21 — a triple repetition of an otherwise obscure prohibition.

**24:8 — "Behold the blood of the covenant."** ESV/NASB/KJV agree, and the agreement carries the maximum possible exegetical weight, since the NT quotes it. **The LXX reads *idou to haima tēs diathēkēs*** — and **Matthew 26:28 / Mark 14:24 use *to haima mou tēs diathēkēs*, and Hebrews 9:20 *touto to haima tēs diathēkēs***. The Greek of the NT citation tracks the Greek of Exodus. *Moderate–high confidence on the LXX wording — routed to Open Questions, but the NT wording is certain and the correspondence is unmistakable.*

**24:16 — "the glory of the LORD dwelt (*šākan*) on Mount Sinai."** ESV "dwelt"; KJV "abode". As noted at 25–31, the ESV's choice makes the link to 25:8 audible in English and the KJV's does not.

**Pulpit edition note:** Pulpit text matches report default.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Three registers, and the shift between the second and third is the sermon.**

- **20:22–26 — direct, warm, and surprisingly generous.** "I will come to you and I will bless you" is not the tone one expects at the head of a law code.
- **21:1–23:19 — flat, procedural, unhurried.** The case law has no rhetorical heightening at all: an ox gores, a fire spreads, a garment is pledged. **And then, twice, the temperature changes** — at 22:21–27 and 23:9, where the prose stops describing procedures and starts describing God ("I will surely hear their cry"; "I am gracious"). Those are the passages where the legislator's voice becomes audible inside the legislation.
- **24:1–18 — solemn, ceremonial, and then astonished.** The ratification is liturgy: altar, pillars, offerings, basins, recital, response. Then 24:9–11 breaks into something the prose can barely hold — seventy-four men on a mountain, a sapphire pavement, and a meal. Then the cloud, six days of silence, and a voice.

**Soundtrack.** A long, level, administrative passage — and then, twice in the middle and once at the end, something breaks through it.

**Note for the preacher.** The temptation is to preach the interesting bits (24) and skip the law. But the tonal architecture requires the flatness: 22:27's "for I am gracious" only lands because it arrives in the middle of a paragraph about a coat.

**Idea-level repetition: the protection of those who cannot protect themselves.** The sojourner, the widow, the orphan, the poor debtor, the injured slave, the man whose coat is his blanket, and — twice — the *enemy's* animal. Different cases, one concern, and it is the concern the exodus itself was about. `[T]` *High confidence — the strongest idea-level convergence in the block, and the answer to any charge that the case law is morally arbitrary.*

**The six/seventh rhythm is worth naming.** The Hebrew slave goes free in the seventh year (21:2); the land rests in the seventh year (23:10–11); work stops on the seventh day (23:12); and God calls Moses on the seventh day (24:16). **The Sabbath principle governs labour, land, liberty and revelation alike.** `[T]`/`[I]` *Moderate–high.*

### 12. Genre

**Covenant document.** The block has the shape of an ANE treaty text: a statement of the suzerain's prior act (20:22, "you have seen for yourselves"), stipulations (21–23), promises and warnings (23:20–33), a written deposit (24:4, 7), and a ratification ceremony (24:3–8). The interior is **casuistic law** ("if a man…"), the same form as the great Mesopotamian collections; the two ends are **apodictic** ("you shall not").

Reading rules: (i) casuistic law states *precedents*, not exhaustive codes — it is a judge's training material, and reading it as a complete legislative system misunderstands the genre; (ii) the *mišpāṭîm* are covenant terms for this people at this stage, and the Bible Timeline tool governs their application; (iii) the ratification narrative at 24:3–11 is history, not liturgy-in-the-abstract, and should be preached as something that happened; (iv) the genre's ANE parallels are illuminating and should be handled as background, not as a source-claim (see Historical Background).

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** Sinai, the third month after the exodus, before the tabernacle. The covenant being ratified here is the Mosaic covenant, and this is its founding legal act.

**What has happened since that changes the reading.**

- **This covenant is broken in three chapters** (32) and remade in the same words (34:1, 10–26 ≈ 23:14–19).
- **Deuteronomy restates and expands the *mišpāṭîm*** for the land.
- **Jeremiah 31:31–34** announces a new covenant "not like the covenant that I made with their fathers… **my covenant that they broke**" — a direct reference to the covenant cut here.
- **Jesus takes 24:8 onto his own lips** and calls the cup "the new covenant in my blood" (Luke 22:20; cf. Matt 26:28).
- **Hebrews 8–10** argues the whole case: 24:8 quoted at 9:20; the first covenant "obsolete" (8:13); "we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all" (10:10).
- **The moral concerns are carried forward, the case law is not** — the NT reuses the sojourner-and-widow ethic and drops the goring-ox statutes without comment.

**Timeline errors to avoid.** Preaching the case law as directly binding legislation; and, in the opposite direction, dismissing it as obsolete when the NT quotes its ratification verse at the institution of the Lord's Supper. Also: reading 23:20–33's conquest promises without the rest of the canon's handling of them.

### 15. Who Am I?

- **The people of Israel** — the reader's place: freed slaves being taught to run a society, and consenting twice to terms they will break. Both halves matter.
- **Moses** — mediator, writer, reader and blood-thrower. Type of Christ at 24:8 in particular, where he does what Christ will do at the Supper. Not a model.
- **The seventy elders, Nadab and Abihu** — recipients of an unrepeatable privilege, and (for two of them) a fuse. Not examples.
- **The slave, the sojourner, the widow, the poor debtor** — **the figures the law is *about*, and the ones a comfortable congregation will not instinctively identify with.** The text's own move is to tell Israel that it *was* these people (22:21; 23:9), and the sermon should make the same move.

**The misreading to flag.** Reading the case law from the position of the one who might be sued rather than the one being protected. The block is legislation for the powerful about the powerless, and the motive clause is addressed to people who were once powerless themselves.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** Consent — informed, corporate, and sealed. And then a settled way of life in which rescued people do not become oppressors.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

*Worldview.* Three shifts:

1. **Covenant is detailed.** Being God's people turns out to mean specific things about oxen, coats, wages, courts and slaves. A spirituality that never reaches the case law has not reached where this covenant lives.
2. **Rescue creates obligation, and the obligation is shaped like the rescue.** You were a sojourner; therefore protect the sojourner. You cried and were heard; therefore do not make someone else cry.
3. **Law and blood belong together.** The covenant is not concluded by agreement but by sacrifice — and the blood is applied to both parties. The words are not enough on their own, which is the point Hebrews later presses.

*Behaviour.* **Start** reading the ethical weight of your own position — who is the sojourner, the widow, the pledged-coat debtor in your economy, and what does your treatment of them say about your covenant? **Start** the impartiality of 23:3 and 23:6, which cuts against partisanship in both directions. **Stop** treating enemies' interests as forfeit (23:4–5). **Stop** assuming consent is the hard part; Israel consented twice, unanimously, in writing.

*Motive.* Every hard command in the block is grounded either in Israel's own rescue (22:21; 23:9) or in God's character (22:27, "for I am gracious"). The indicative is *inside* the statutes, not just in the preamble — which is the strongest possible answer to the charge that this block is law divorced from grace.

**Four audiences.**

- *For me:* Where do I read this law as the defendant rather than as the person it protects — and what does that reveal?
- *For a Christian friend:* For anyone who thinks the Old Testament law is arbitrary — read 22:21–27 aloud and ask what kind of legislator writes "for I am gracious" into a statute about a coat.
- *For the church community:* Consent is not the hard part. Israel said yes twice. The question is what happens forty days later — which is why the blood, not the agreement, is what holds.
- *For an unbeliever:* Here is a legal code whose stated reason for protecting immigrants is that its own people had been immigrants; and whose ratification ceremony was quoted, at a supper, by a man who said the blood would be his.

**Prayer.** *You wrote your rescue into your law and told us to remember what it felt like to be strangers. You did not leave the covenant standing on our promise but sealed it in blood — half on the altar, half on us. We said we would do all that you had spoken, and we did not. Thank you that another has said it and kept it, and that the cup we drink is the blood of the covenant.*

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**

- **Casuistic conditionals.** The interior runs on *kî* ("when/if") introducing a principal case and *ʾim* ("if") introducing sub-cases — the standard structure of ANE case law. Recognising it prevents the commonest misreading: these are *precedents to reason from*, not a complete penal code. `[T]` *High.*
- **Apodictic address at the frames.** 20:22–26 and 23:20–33 use second-person direct address with promises attached; the interior does not. The genre-shift is the structural signal (Tool 3).
- **The doubled consent at 24:3 and 24:7.** The second adds *wĕnišmāʿ* — "and we will **hear/obey**". The verb is *šāmaʿ*, so the escalation is from *doing* to *hearing-and-doing*. `[T]` **The people commit not only to compliance but to attentiveness** — which makes 32:1's "we do not know what has become of him" the more damning. *Moderate–high on how much weight the addition bears; the lexical fact is certain.*
- **The infinitive absolute at 22:23.** "If they cry out to me **I will surely hear** (*šāmōaʿ ʾešmaʿ*)" — the doubled verb form marking emphasis, the same construction as 3:7's "I have surely seen". The two emphatic hearings bracket the exodus and its law. `[T]` *High confidence on the construction; moderate–high on the deliberate pairing.*
- **21:22's "her children come out" (*wĕyāṣĕʾû yĕlādêhā*).** Literally "and her children come out", with no verb of death — which is why the translations divide (Tool 8). The subsequent "if there is harm / no harm" (*ʾāsôn*) governs the penalty, and *whose* harm is the interpretive crux. `[T]` for the wording; the referent genuinely contested. *Routed to Difficult Verses and Open Questions.*

### Textual Variants

**No variant destabilises the Hebrew of this block.** Two matters for awareness:

- **21:22–23 in the LXX — verified, and it is a substantial doctrinal divergence.** `[revised: logos-research]` The Greek reads: **"…καὶ ἐξέλθῃ τὸ παιδίον αὐτῆς μὴ ἐξεικονισμένον, ἐπιζήμιον ζημιωθήσεται"** — "and her child comes out **not fully formed**, he shall be fined" — **"ἐὰν δὲ ἐξεικονισμένον ᾖ, δώσει ψυχὴν ἀντὶ ψυχῆς"** — "but if it **is fully formed**, he shall give life for life" (Rahlfs; the Lexham English Septuagint renders "her child comes out unformed"). *High confidence — read directly from the Logos LXX.*

  **What this means.** The MT makes the penalty turn on **whether harm followed** (*ʾāsôn*); **the LXX makes it turn on the developmental stage of the child.** These are different laws. The Greek reading is the root of the long "formed / unformed" tradition in patristic and later moral theology on abortion — which means a great deal of historic Christian reasoning about this passage descends from a translation, not from the Hebrew. **A preacher touching 21:22–25 should know this**, both because it explains the tradition and because it shows how much weight the verse has been asked to bear.
- **The Book of the Covenant's boundaries.** Some traditions and commentators bound the unit at 20:22–23:33, treating 24 as a separate ratification narrative. Nothing textual is at stake; it is a division question. The overview's unit (20:22–24:18) keeps them together, and this dig's positional argument supports that.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Sinai, third month of the first year, before the tabernacle. `[T]`

**What the original audience knew.**

- **What a law collection was.** The great Mesopotamian collections — Ur-Nammu, Lipit-Ishtar, Eshnunna, and above all **Hammurabi** (c. 1750 BC) — share the casuistic form, and often the very cases: the goring ox, the deposited property, the injured pregnant woman. An audience with any exposure to the wider legal world would find the *form* entirely familiar. `[S]` *Moderate–high on the general parallel.*
- **What was different.** Three things are commonly noted and are worth the preacher's attention: (i) the collection is embedded in a **covenant with a god**, not promulgated by a king in his own name; (ii) penalties are markedly **less stratified by class** than in Hammurabi, where compensation varies by the victim's rank; and (iii) **property crimes are not capital** here, whereas Hammurabi executes for certain thefts — the valuation of human life over property runs the opposite way. `[S]` *Moderate — flagged for verification, and the comparison should be made carefully rather than triumphantly.*
- **What slavery was.** Debt-servitude, not chattel slavery on the later Atlantic model: a time-limited arrangement entered under economic duress, here capped at six years for a Hebrew (21:2), with mandated release, protection against permanent injury (21:26–27), and — for a daughter sold as a servant — three guaranteed provisions and free release if they fail (21:10–11). None of this makes the institution good; the text regulates and limits something it did not invent. `[S]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Why the *gēr* was vulnerable.** In a kinship society, protection came from your clan. A resident alien had none — no avenger, no inheritance, no standing. That is why the *gēr* appears in the same breath as the widow and the orphan.
- **The covenant meal.** Shared eating in the presence of the deity, following sacrifice, is the standard ANE way of concluding a treaty. 24:9–11 is doing something recognisable — with the unrecognisable addition that they *saw* him and lived.

**What this changes about how we read.** Two things. First, the case law's familiarity of form makes its differences of substance visible: the audience would notice what was *not* there as much as what was. Second, the ratification is a legal act in a known genre — which is why Jesus' adoption of its language at a supper is not a poetic flourish but a claim about a new treaty.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Torah — compositional and canonical audiences coincide: wilderness Israel, with the tabernacle standing and the covenant already broken once and remade. **They are hearing the terms they broke.**

**The first hearers.**

- *Who they were:* recently freed slaves being handed a legal order for a land they had not yet entered.
- *Their situation:* between the theophany that terrified them and the tabernacle that would house God among them.
- *What they brought:* an intimate, bodily knowledge of what it is to be the unprotected person in someone else's economy — which is exactly the knowledge 23:9 appeals to ("you know the heart of a sojourner").

**Where the passage fits their world.** The case law is not abstract to them. Every category — the debtor whose coat is his blanket, the servant released in the seventh year, the resident alien with no clan — describes someone they had recently been. **The law is their own biography turned into legislation.**

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** 24:11 — that seventy-four men saw God and lived, told in a half-verse; and 23:21's "he will not pardon your transgression".
- **Surprising:** that the very first case law concerns **freeing slaves** (21:2); and that an enemy's straying ox must be returned (23:4).
- **Comforting:** "I will come to you and I will bless you" (20:24); "I will hear, for I am gracious" (22:27); and the meal.
- **Disturbing:** the blood thrown on *them* (24:8) — this is not a spectator's rite; and the conquest promises of 23:23–33.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A modern legislative expectation (comprehensive codes, uniform sentencing) that misreads casuistic law; a discomfort with slavery legislation that can make the passage unpreachable rather than difficult; and a distance from vulnerability that makes 22:21's motive clause land as sentiment rather than memory.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. Covenant, and its ratification in blood.** *(Primary.)*

- *Where this passage sits:* the founding legal act of the Mosaic covenant — terms, consent, writing, blood.
- *What comes before:* the Noahic and Abrahamic covenants (Gen 9; 15; 17 — note Gen 15's cutting rite); the proposal at 19:5–6.
- *What comes after:* the covenant broken (32) and remade (34); Deuteronomy's restatement; Jeremiah 31:31–34's "new covenant… not like the covenant that I made with their fathers… **which they broke**"; Luke 22:20's "the new covenant in my blood"; Hebrews 8–10, which quotes 24:8 at 9:20.
- *Contribution:* it supplies the *category* and the *words* the NT uses for the cross. Without 24:8 there is no "blood of the covenant" for Jesus to take over.
- *Confidence: high.*

**2. Justice and the memory of rescue.**

- *Where this passage sits:* the point at which the exodus becomes an ethic.
- *What comes before:* Israel's own oppression (1:8–14), cry (2:23–25) and rescue.
- *What comes after:* Deuteronomy's expansions; the prophets' indictments, which prosecute Israel on precisely these statutes (Amos 2:6–8 on the pledged garment; Isa 1:17 on the orphan and widow); James 1:27 and 5:4.
- *Contribution:* it establishes that the God who hears the cry of the oppressed will hear it *against his own people*, and that the ground of social ethics is remembered grace.
- *Confidence: high.*

**3. Presence and the table.**

- *Where this passage sits:* 24:9–11 — the only meal eaten in God's visible presence in the Torah.
- *What comes before:* Eden; the covenant meal's ANE analogues.
- *What comes after:* the parody at 32:6; the Passover kept annually; "this is my blood of the covenant" at a table (Matt 26:28); the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9).
- *Contribution:* it establishes eating-with-God as the shape of covenant fellowship, which is the shape the Supper then takes.
- *Confidence: moderate–high.*

**Confluence — (c) climactic convergence.** The three meet at a single point: **a covenant ratified in blood produces a people whose law is shaped by their rescue, and it is concluded at a table in God's presence.** That is also the shape of the Supper — blood, covenant, a people, a table — which is why Jesus needed only to add one pronoun to 24:8.

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton.** Exodus · third division, "at the mountain" (19–40) · leading idea: the progressive proximity of God. This unit is the **legal instrument** of that proximity: the covenant that makes the dwelling possible, and the meal that anticipates it.

**Findings.**

1. **Framing (step 3): the altar inclusio.** Altar legislated (20:24–26) → altar built (24:4). Cross-reported to Tool 3. `[T]` *High.*
2. **Narrative braking and acceleration (step 1).** Three chapters of case law at a uniform, unhurried pace — then ch. 24 slows further for a ceremony narrated action by action, then accelerates through forty days in one verse. The proportions put the weight on the *ratification*, not on the statutes. `[T]`/`[I]` *Moderate–high.*
3. **Narrative space (step 2): the graded mountain.** 24:1–2 sets three zones — Moses alone near, seventy-four at a middle distance, the people below — the same gradient the 25–31 dig found transposed into the tent. **This verse is where the gradient is stated most explicitly.** `[T]` *High.*
4. **Repetition and linkage (step 4).** The six/seventh rhythm at four scales (slave-release, sabbath year, Sabbath day, seventh-day call); the doubled consent; the twice-stated Egypt motive. `[T]` *High.*
5. **First vs second reading (step 5).** On a first reading 24:3–8 is a solemn conclusion. On the re-read — knowing ch. 32 — the doubled "we will do" is almost unbearable, 20:23's prohibition of gold gods is a loaded gun, and 24:11's "he did not lay his hand on them" reads as a reprieve rather than a right. `[T]`/`[I]` *High.*
6. **Polyacoustic intertextuality (step 6).** The block sounds forward more than backward: 24:8 into the Supper and Hebrews; 22:21–27 into the prophets and James; 23:20–21's angel into 32:34. Heard together, this is the covenant whose breaking and remaking the rest of Scripture is about. *Moderate–high.*
7. **Irreducibility.** Do not reduce this to "God's law is just" or "the Old Testament cares about the poor". What is told is that God gave a people their rescue back to them as a way of life, that they agreed twice, and that the agreement was sealed by blood thrown over them — and then they ate with him.

**Echoes / signals.** Leading words: *mišpāṭîm*, *gēr*, *dam habbĕrît*. Structural devices: the altar inclusio; apodictic frames around casuistic interior; doubled consent around the reading; the six/seventh rhythm. Intertextual links: forward to Matt 26:28 and Heb 9:20 (cross-reported to Tool 11).

**Nil returns.** *Chiasm* — none proposed at block level; the ratification's doubling is not a chiasm. *Type-scene* — the covenant meal is a recognised ANE form rather than a biblical type-scene in Schnittjer's sense. *Wordplay* — none advanced.

### Christological Reading

**Type(s) of connection: one direct verbal takeover, one institutional typology, one trajectory, and one popular reading to decline.**

- **24:8 → the Lord's Supper (direct takeover).** "Behold **the blood of the covenant** that the LORD has made with you" → "this is **my blood of the covenant**, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (Matt 26:28; cf. Mark 14:24; Luke 22:20 "the new covenant in my blood"). *Tests:* category (covenant, blood, atonement) — pass; NT precedent — **explicit and verbal, on Jesus' own lips**; escalation — pass (a new covenant, poured out, for forgiveness); authorial pattern — pass (Exodus itself makes the blood the instrument of ratification). **This is as strong as OT→NT connections get, and it is the reason this unit is the series hinge.** *High confidence.*
- **The whole ratification → Hebrews 9:18–22 (institutional typology).** Hebrews quotes 24:8 and builds from it the argument that "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" (9:22), then contrasts the "copies" purified with these rites against "the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices" (9:23). The NT does the typological work; the preacher follows it. *High confidence.*
- **The meal in God's presence (24:9–11) → the Supper and the marriage supper (trajectory).** Seventy-four men ate before God and were not struck. *Tests:* category (covenant fellowship) — pass; NT precedent — the Supper's own shape, and Rev 19:9 — moderate rather than explicit; escalation — pass. *Moderate–high; a trajectory, not a typology.*
- **The altar law (20:24–26) → the cross (trajectory, held lightly).** No tool on the stone; no steps, lest nakedness be exposed. Two prohibitions about human contribution and human shame at the point of approach. Suggestive, and preachable as illustration; but the text gives no interpretation and no NT writer picks it up. *Moderate — label it as illustration, not typology.*
- **23:20–21, "my name is in him" → a Christophany?** The angel who bears the divine name and does not pardon transgression is a genuine candidate, and the 3:1–4:17 dig rated the angel-of-the-LORD material moderate–high. **But note the trajectory:** this same angel becomes at 32:34 and 33:2–3 a *substitute* for God's presence that Moses refuses. **Do not preach the angel here as straightforwardly Christ, when three chapters later his coming instead of God is treated as a disaster.** *Moderate, with the caution attached.*
- **A connection to decline: 21:5–6, the slave who loves his master.** "I love my master… I will not go out free", followed by the ear bored through at the doorpost, is very widely preached as a type of Christ via Psalm 40:6 ("ears you have dug for me") and Hebrews 10:5–7. **The lexical basis is weak:** Exodus 21:6 uses *rāṣaʿ* (bore, pierce) and Psalm 40:6 uses *kārâ* (dig) — **different verbs describing different actions**, and Hebrews 10:5 follows the LXX's quite different "a body you have prepared for me". The two texts also emphasise different things: the Psalm's opened ears are about *receptivity to God's will*, the Exodus boring is about *permanent marked ownership*. The *theological* picture — willing, permanent, loving servitude — is genuinely attractive and genuinely Christ-shaped; the *verbal* bridge is not there. **Recorded and declined as typology; usable as illustration if labelled as such.** `[revised: logos-research]` **Note the status of this verdict honestly:** a Study Assistant query returned **no library sources** on the question, so the checking stage confirmed my lexical reasoning but could not cite a commentator either way. The verdict rests on the verbs, which are checkable.

**Moralism check.**

- *The "be like X" temptations:* "keep the law like Israel promised"; "be generous to the poor"; "be a willing bondservant like the pierced-ear slave". The first two are the block's natural sermons and both can be preached without a gospel.
- *The gospel grounding:* the law's own motive clauses do the work — you were sojourners (22:21; 23:9), and "I am gracious" (22:27). And the covenant is not concluded on the strength of Israel's twice-given word but by blood applied to them.
- *Christ as hero, not example:* the people said "we will do, and we will be obedient" and did not. The blood, not the promise, is what holds — and the one who said the same words and kept them took this verse onto his own lips over a cup.

*Confidence: high (24:8 → Matt 26:28; the Heb 9 typology); moderate–high (the meal); moderate (the altar law, as illustration); declined (the pierced ear as typology).*

### Difficult / Contested Verses

This block contains more ethically difficult material per chapter than anything else in Exodus. Six need advance thought.

**21:2–11, 20–21, 26–27 — the slave laws.**
- *Category:* ethical — the hardest in the block, and the one a thoughtful non-Christian will raise.
- *The difficulty:* Scripture regulates rather than abolishes an institution we regard as intrinsically wrong; 21:20–21's "he is his money" is especially jarring.
- *Rhetorical function in context:* **the very first case law given to a nation of freed slaves is about setting slaves free** (21:2). That placement is not accidental.
- *Honest exegesis:* the institution is debt-servitude, time-limited (six years), with mandated release, protection of the injured (manumission for the loss of an eye or a tooth — a penalty found nowhere in the surrounding codes), and specific guarantees for a daughter (21:10–11). It regulates, limits and humanises. **It does not endorse.** The Torah's own trajectory (Deut 15:12–15's generous release, grounded again in Egypt) and the NT's (Philemon; 1 Tim 1:10, which lists slave-traders among the lawless) run one way.
- *Handling:* do not skip it and do not defend more than the text does. Name the regulation-not-endorsement structure, name the trajectory, and be honest that the passage does not say what we would want it to say.

**21:22–25 — the fighting men and the pregnant woman.**
- *Category:* ethical / weaponised — quoted by both sides in the abortion debate.
- *The difficulty:* whether "her children come out" describes a miscarriage or a premature live birth, and whether the "harm" (*ʾāsôn*) that triggers *lex talionis* is the mother's or the child's. The translations divide (Tool 8), and the LXX differs materially (Textual Variants).
- *Honest exegesis:* the Hebrew says "her children come out" with no verb of death; the penalty structure distinguishes a fine (no harm) from life-for-life (harm). **Both major readings are exegetically available**, and a great deal of confident preaching on this verse is confident beyond the evidence in both directions.
- *And the versions do not agree.* **The LXX replaces the harm/no-harm distinction with a formed/unformed one** — the fine applies if the child comes out "not fully formed", life-for-life "if it is fully formed" (verified; see Textual Variants). So the Greek makes the penalty turn on the child's development, and the Hebrew on whether harm followed. **Much of the historic Christian argument from this verse descends from the Greek.** `[revised: logos-research]`
- *A negative result worth recording:* a Study Assistant query on *ʾāsôn*'s referent and the versions **returned no library sources** — it could not cite commentary either way. So the uncertainty is not merely my own. `[revised: logos-research — no sources found]`
- *Handling:* if the sermon touches it, state the options, the versional split and the uncertainty plainly. Do not build a doctrine of the unborn's status on a contested clause in a disputed text when better ground exists (Ps 139:13–16; Luke 1:41–44).

**21:23–25 — "eye for eye."**
- *Category:* ethical / commonly misused.
- *Rhetorical function:* a **formula of proportionality** limiting vengeance, not a licence for it — and note its immediate neighbour: 21:26–27, where an eye or a tooth destroyed in a *slave* is compensated by **freedom**, not by reciprocal injury. The block itself declines to apply the formula literally in the very next case.
- *Handling:* preach it as a cap on retribution, with 21:26–27 alongside; and note Matt 5:38–42, where Jesus addresses its use as a personal ethic rather than a judicial one.

**22:18 — "You shall not permit a sorceress to live."**
- *Category:* ethical / apologetic; commonly cited against Scripture.
- *Honest exegesis:* a capital sanction within a theocratic covenant community for a practice understood as covenant treason. The Bible Timeline tool governs: nothing here transfers to a modern state, and the church is given no sword (John 18:36; 2 Cor 10:4).
- *Handling:* one honest sentence; do not linger, and do not pretend it is not there.

**23:20–33 — the conquest promises.**
- *Category:* ethical / pastoral.
- *The difficulty:* driving out nations, breaking their pillars, no covenant with them.
- *Honest exegesis:* the stated ground is religious, not ethnic ("lest they make you sin against me", 23:33); the process is explicitly gradual (23:29–30); and the wider canon frames it as judgement on a specific people at a specific point (Gen 15:16). It remains hard.
- *Handling:* acknowledge; route the fuller treatment to Joshua; do not moralise it into "conquer your spiritual enemies", which trivialises both the text and the ethics.

**24:9–11 — "they saw the God of Israel… and ate and drank."**
- *Category:* doctrinal / apologetic (apparent contradiction).
- *The difficulty:* it appears to contradict 33:20 ("man shall not see me and live") and John 1:18 ("no one has ever seen God").
- *Rhetorical function:* the narrator's "**he did not lay his hand on them**" signals that survival was the surprise, not the entitlement — the text is aware of the tension it is creating.
- *Honest exegesis:* the description is carefully limited to what was *under his feet*; the encounter is mediated, graded (24:1–2) and, on the narrator's own telling, an act of restraint. Compare 33:23's "you shall see my back".
- *Handling:* name the tension; let the narrator's aside do the resolving; and note that John 1:18's answer is not to deny the OT theophanies but to say that the Son "has made him known".

**Also flag briefly:** 21:15, 17 (striking or cursing a parent as capital); the goring-ox laws and the owner's liability; and 22:29–30's firstborn language read without 13:13's redemption provision.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching the pierced ear as a type of Christ

- **What it looks like:** "'I love my master… I will not go out free' — here is Christ, the willing servant, whose ear was opened, Psalm 40, Hebrews 10."
- **Why it's wrong:** the verbs do not connect. Exodus 21:6 has *rāṣaʿ* (bore, pierce); Psalm 40:6 has *kārâ* (dig, open); Hebrews 10:5 quotes the LXX's "a body you have prepared for me". Three different things. The reading is thematic, and it is usually preached as though it were lexical.
- **The corrective:** if you use it, label it as illustration — "a picture of the kind of servitude Christ rendered" — and do not claim the texts are quoting one another. The genuine Christ-connection in this block is 24:8, and it needs no help.

### Pitfall 2: Preaching the case law as a moral quarry

- **What it looks like:** a tour of interesting statutes with a modern application bolted onto each.
- **Why it's wrong:** casuistic law states precedents to reason from, and the block's own logic is thematic, not itemised — protection of those who cannot protect themselves, grounded in Israel's memory of Egypt.
- **The corrective:** preach the frame and the theme. If you preach individual cases, preach the ones the text itself emphasises with motive clauses (22:21–27; 23:1–9).

### Pitfall 3: Skipping the slave laws

- **What it looks like:** starting at 22:21, or at ch. 24.
- **Why it's wrong:** the congregation has read them, or will; and a preacher who never addresses them concedes the field. It also loses a striking fact — **the first case law given to freed slaves is about freeing slaves** (21:2).
- **The corrective:** name the regulation-not-endorsement structure, name the trajectory through Deuteronomy 15 to Philemon, and be honest about what the text does not say.

### Pitfall 4: Using 21:22–25 as a settled proof-text

- **What it looks like:** either "the Bible says a fetus is worth only a fine" or "the Bible prescribes life-for-life for causing a miscarriage."
- **Why it's wrong:** the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous, the translations divide, and **the LXX has a different law altogether** (formed/unformed). Confident preaching here outruns the evidence in both directions.
- **The corrective:** if the sermon must touch it, say what is certain (the case protects a pregnant woman and penalises harm) and what is not. Argue the anthropology from clearer ground.

### Pitfall 5: Preaching 23:20–21's angel as straightforwardly Christ

- **What it looks like:** "'My name is in him' — here is the pre-incarnate Christ, the guide of Israel."
- **Why it's wrong:** it may well be a genuine Christophany candidate, but the *same angel* becomes at 32:34 and 33:2–3 the thing Moses refuses — an angel offered **instead of** God's presence. A sermon that makes the angel simply Christ here has to explain why his coming three chapters later is a disaster.
- **The corrective:** preach the promise as a promise, note that it is later downgraded, and let the presence-thread carry the weight.

### Pitfall 6: Treating "we will do" as the sermon's application

- **What it looks like:** "Israel said 'all that the LORD has spoken we will do' — will you make the same commitment today?"
- **Why it's wrong:** the text records that vow three times and then narrates its collapse within forty days. Inviting the congregation to repeat it as a decisive act is inviting them into chapter 32.
- **The corrective:** preach the blood, not the vow. The covenant held because of what was thrown over them, not because of what they said.
