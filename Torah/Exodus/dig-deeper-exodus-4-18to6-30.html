Dig Deeper: Exodus 4:18–6:30

The Passage

Two and a half chapters. The narrative frame, the confrontation and the “I am the LORD” speech are quoted; the straw-decree sequence and the genealogy are summarised with ranges.

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple converging tools:

  1. The unit is bracketed by belief and unbelief, and the only thing that changes between them is that obedience made everything worse. “The people believed… and worshipped” (4:31) → “they did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery” (6:9). [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Narrator’s Comment, Positional Necessity, Tone. Between those two verses stands chapter 5 — the first obedience to God’s command, and its consequence is heavier bricks and beaten foremen. The unit exists to establish that the deliverance will not come by negotiation, and that faith can be crushed out of people by circumstance without being their fault — the narrator blames the bondage, not them. High confidence.

  2. Pharaoh asks the question the rest of the book answers: “Who is the LORD… I do not know the LORD” (5:2). [T] Surfaced by: Linking Words, Repetition, Author’s Purpose, Internal echo, Biblical Theology. The plague-cycle’s drumbeat refrain — “that you may know that I am the LORD” (7:5, 17; 8:10, 22; 9:14, 16, 29; 10:2; 11:7) — is the answer to this sentence, and God’s own reply begins three chapters later with “you shall know that I am the LORD your God” (6:7). The book’s central question is posed by its villain. High confidence.

  3. God’s answer to the mediator’s complaint is a name and seven promises. “I am the LORD” opens (6:2, 6) and closes (6:8) a speech containing exactly seven first-person “I will” commitments — bring out, deliver, redeem, take, be, bring in, give. [T] — counted, not recalled. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Vocabulary, Linking Words. Moses says “you have not delivered your people at all” (5:23); God replies with his name three times and seven undertakings. The reply to “why?” is “who”. High confidence.

  4. The firstborn-son announcement is planted here and detonates at the Passover: “Israel is my firstborn son… let my son go… if you refuse, I will kill your firstborn son” (4:22–23). [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Vocabulary, Internal echo, Christological Reading, Biblical Theology. The whole logic of the tenth plague and of Passover is stated before a single plague falls — and stated as a matter of sonship, not merely of power. High confidence.

  5. “Burdens” is the word that runs from the oppression to the promise. Siblôt — the noun of 1:11 and 2:11 — is thrown back by Pharaoh (“get back to your burdens”, 5:4) and then taken up by God: “I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians” (6:6, 7). [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Internal echo. God’s promise is worded to answer the book’s opening description exactly. High confidence.

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. The call (3:1–4:17) has commissioned Moses, answered five objections, given him signs and given him Aaron. Crucially, God has already told him how it will go: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand. So I will stretch out my hand and strike Egypt with all the wonders that I will do in it; after that he will let you go” (3:19–20).

Necessity answer. Why does this unit exist here?

  • Because a commission has to be tested, and this is the test. The word is spoken, and the immediate result is that the oppression worsens. The narrative cannot move from the burning bush to the plagues without showing what the first obedience actually produced, or the plagues would look like an escalation God chose rather than a demonstration Pharaoh compelled.
  • Because Pharaoh’s question has to be asked before the answer can be given. “Who is the LORD?” (5:2) is the hinge on which the entire plague cycle turns; the refrain of chs. 7–11 is its reply. This unit poses the book’s question.
  • Because the people’s faith has to fail once. 4:31’s belief is genuine and 6:9’s collapse is not blamed on them — and the exodus therefore proceeds with a people who are not, at this point, on board. God’s action is made independent of Israel’s confidence.
  • And because the mediator has to complain and be answered. 5:22–23 is the first of the complaints that will recur (17:4; 32:11–13; Num 11:11–15); the answer he gets — the Name and the seven promises — is the pattern for all of them.

Implication for this passage. Three things. First, the unit’s shape is a descent: belief → obedience → worse suffering → rejection by the people → complaint → and then, at the lowest point, the fullest self-disclosure God has yet given. Second, the “I am the LORD” speech is placed after the failure, not before it, which is the point. Third, the unit ends with the mediator still objecting (6:30) and the genealogy having interrupted him — so the reader arrives at the plagues with nothing resolved except who God is.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 sharpens it: 6:3–4 names the Abrahamic covenant explicitly and 6:8 grounds the land-promise in the oath — so this speech is the theological centre of the book’s first movement, and it works by putting the Name against the covenant. The Genesis material the book has been drawing on since 1:1 is here argued rather than alluded to.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. The overview’s presenting situation and pressure are addressed head-on: how can the God of the fathers keep his sworn word? 6:2–8 is the answer, stated at the moment the evidence is worst.

Passage-level. Four things this unit is doing:

  • Establishing that deliverance is not negotiation. The first audience with Pharaoh achieves nothing but harm. Everything after this is God’s doing.
  • Posing the book’s question through Pharaoh’s mouth (5:2), so that the plagues can answer it.
  • Grounding the rescue in the covenant and the Name (6:2–8) rather than in Israel’s faith, which has just collapsed (6:9).
  • Certifying the messengers. The genealogy (6:14–27) exists to say who these two men are — “these are the Aaron and Moses to whom the LORD said…” (v. 26) — at the point where their credibility has been destroyed with both Pharaoh and Israel.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

See Phase 0.6. Short form: the commission must be tested and fail; Pharaoh must ask the question the plagues answer; and the Name must be given at the low point, not the high one.

General contextual notes

Immediately before (4:17). “And take in your hand this staff, with which you shall do the signs.” The staff appears again at 4:20 (“the staff of God in his hand”) — the commission is complete and the journey begins.

Immediately after (7:1–5). “See, I have made you like God to Pharaoh… and the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD.” The plague cycle begins by restating the answer to 5:2.

Section. First movement (1:1–15:21, “from Egypt”). Within it: the emergency (1), the deliverer (2), the call (3–4:17), the first confrontation and the covenant-name (4:18–6:30), the plagues (7–11).

Commonly read out of context. Three. 5:22–23 is quoted as an example of honest lament, which it is — but usually without noticing that God’s answer does not address the “why” at all. 6:3 is quoted in debates about the composition of the Pentateuch, usually without its own context (a speech about covenant-keeping). And 6:6–8 is quoted devotionally without the verse that follows it — “but they did not listen” (6:9), which is the point of its placement.

Devices.

  • A belief-inclusio, inverted. 4:31 (“the people believed… and worshipped”) and 6:9 (“they did not listen”). The frame runs from faith to its collapse, and chapter 5 is what happens in between. [T] High confidence — the unit’s clearest structural signal.
  • A resumptive repetition around the genealogy. 6:12’s objection (“I am of uncircumcised lips; how then shall Pharaoh listen to me?”) is repeated at 6:30 after the genealogy intervenes — a standard Hebrew technique for resuming a narrative after an insertion, and a sign that 6:14–27 is a deliberate interruption rather than a displacement. [T] High confidence.
  • The descent, and the turn. Belief (4:31) → obedience (5:1) → worse suffering (5:6–19) → rejection by the people (5:21) → the mediator’s complaint (5:22–23) → and then the fullest revelation of the Name so far (6:2–8). The lowest point in the movement is where the Name is given.
  • Two questions that frame the middle. Pharaoh’s “Who is the LORD?” (5:2) and Moses’ “Why did you ever send me?” (5:22). The unit’s two challenges come from opposite directions and receive one answer: “I am the LORD.” [T]/[I] Moderate–high.

4. Linking Words

4:21 — “but I will harden his heart.” An adversative attached to the commission itself: do the wonders, but they will not work — yet. The programmatic announcement the plagues dig identified as framing chs. 7–11 is made here.

4:23 — “Let my son go, that he may serve me: and if you refuse… behold, I will kill your firstborn son.” Purpose, then condition, then consequence. The tenth plague is announced conditionally before the first is performed.

5:2 — “Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?” A rhetorical question with a result clause. Pharaoh’s refusal is grounded in ignorance, and he says so.

5:8 — “for they are idle; therefore they cry.” ←for← →therefore→ Pharaoh’s diagnosis of the problem, and the logic of his cruelty: worship is idleness, and the cure is more work.

5:23 — “For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name, he has done evil to this people, and you have not delivered your people at all.” ←for← Moses grounds his complaint in the evidence, and the last clause is a flat accusation.

6:1 — “But the LORD said to Moses, ‘Now you shall see…’” The adversative that turns the unit. Note the temporal marker: now.

6:6 — “Wherefore (lākēn) say to the people of Israel.” →therefore→ The seven promises are drawn as a consequence from vv. 2–5 — the Name, the covenant established, the groaning heard, the covenant remembered. The promises rest on the covenant, not on the circumstances. [T] High confidence.

6:9 — “but they did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery.” The adversative and the causal clause together. The narrator supplies the reason and it is not sin — it is crushed spirit and cruel bondage.

6:12 — “Behold, the people of Israel have not listened to me. How then shall Pharaoh listen to me?” An argument from the lesser to the greater, and Moses’ one genuinely logical objection.

5. Parallels

Not poetry — N/A at verse level. Two structural parallels carry weight.

The seven “I will”s of 6:6–8 stand in a cumulative series bracketed by “I am the LORD”: bring out → deliver → redeem → take → be → bring in → give. The movement is from rescue from (vv. 6) to relationship with (v. 7) to inheritance in (v. 8) — out of Egypt, into covenant, into the land. The series is the whole Pentateuch in one sentence.

5:15–21 ↔︎ 5:22–23 — two complaints in the same shape. The foremen appeal to Pharaoh and are rebuffed, then turn on Moses (“the LORD look on you and judge”); Moses then turns on God (“why did you ever send me?”). The complaint travels up the chain, and the same grievance is voiced twice in successive scenes.

6. Narrator’s Comment

  • 4:31 — “and the people believed… they bowed their heads and worshipped.” The narrator vouches for the faith. This matters for how 6:9 is read.
  • 4:26 — “It was then that she said, ‘A bridegroom of blood,’ because of the circumcision.” An explanatory aside that explains almost nothing — the narrator tells us why she said it and leaves everything else dark. See Difficult Verses.
  • 6:9 — “but they did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery.” [T] The unit’s most pastorally important sentence. The narrator does not say they were faithless; he names the cause as qōṣer rûaḥ (“shortness of spirit”) and hard bondage. This is Scripture describing faith failing under duress without condemning it.
  • 6:26–27 — “These are the Aaron and Moses… this Moses and this Aaron.” An emphatic double identification closing the genealogy, with the order reversed between the two verses. The narrator is underlining the credentials of two men whose stock has just collapsed.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. Jethro; Midian; Zipporah; Aaron; the mountain of God; Pharaoh; the elders; and, in the genealogy, Reuben, Simeon, Levi and his line — Gershon, Kohath, Merari, Amram and Jochebed (v. 20, naming Moses’ parents for the first time), Aaron’s wife Elisheba, Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, Ithamar, and Phinehas (v. 25). Triage: most are routine or handled elsewhere. Amram and Jochebed discharge the anonymity of 2:1 (the 2:1–25 dig flagged it as planted → 6:20). Nadab and Abihu appear here for the first time in a list — the fuse the 20:22–24:18 dig traced to Leviticus 10. Phinehas is named three generations ahead of his own story (Num 25).

The words that carry the unit.

(a) siblôt — “burdens” (5:4, 5; 6:6, 7; ← 1:11; 2:11) [T] — High confidence. The noun the book used for the forced labour in ch. 1 and for what Moses “looked on” in ch. 2. Pharaoh throws it at Moses — “get back to your burdens” (5:4) — and God takes it back: “I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians” (6:6, 7). The promise is worded to answer the description.

(b) gāʾal — “redeem” (6:6) [T] — High confidence. Its first occurrence in Exodus, and it is the kinsman-redeemer word: to buy back what belongs to the family, by one who has the right and the obligation. Not merely to rescue but to reclaim as one’s own. The word will run to Ruth, to Isaiah’s “your Redeemer” (Isa 41:14; 43:14), and into the NT’s redemption vocabulary.

(c) “I am the LORD” — ʾănî YHWH (6:2, 6, 8, 29) [T] — High confidence. Four times in the unit, three of them bracketing the seven promises. The formula is a self-identification that functions as a guarantee: the promises are underwritten by who is making them. It becomes the refrain of Leviticus.

(d) bēn bĕkōr — “firstborn son” (4:22–23) [T] — High confidence. Israel called God’s firstborn son, and Pharaoh’s firstborn threatened in the same breath. The category is family, not merely politics — and it makes the tenth plague a matter of sons for a son.

(e) ʿăral śĕpātayim — “uncircumcised lips” (6:12, 30) [T] — Moderate–high. An unusual idiom for inadequate speech, using the language of circumcision — four chapters after a crisis about a circumcision that had not been performed (4:24–26), and anticipating Deuteronomy and Jeremiah’s “circumcise your heart” (Deut 10:16; Jer 4:4). [T] for the vocabulary; [I] for the link to 4:24–26. Moderate — a genuine lexical thread, but do not over-press.

(f) ʾĒl Šadday — “God Almighty” (6:3) [T] — High confidence on the term. The patriarchal designation (Gen 17:1; 28:3; 35:11; 48:3), set here against the name YHWH. What the contrast means is the crux — see Difficult Verses.

Also worth a line: qōṣer rûaḥ (6:9), literally “shortness of spirit” — the phrase the narrator uses for why Israel could not listen. Not unbelief; something closer to what we would call being at the end of one’s tether.

8. Translations

6:3 — the crux. ESV: “I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty, but by my name the LORD I did not make myself known to them.” NASB similar. KJV: “but by my name JEHOVAH was I not known to them.” NIV: “but by my name the LORD I did not make myself fully known to them” — NIV supplies “fully”, which is an interpretation, not a translation. [T] The Hebrew has no adverb. Some render the clause as a rhetorical question (“and by my name the LORD did I not make myself known to them?”), which the consonantal text permits and which resolves the difficulty differently again. The translations are doing exegesis here, and the preacher should know which one they are standing on. High confidence that the moderns diverge; the resolution contested — routed to Difficult Verses and the Logos pass.

Ancient versions check (triggered: exegetically weighty, and the moderns do not agree, which makes the ancient witnesses more interesting still). Not run directly — routed to Open Questions.

4:25 — “touched Moses’ feet with it” / “cast it at his feet.” ESV “touched Moses’ feet”; KJV “cast it at his feet”; NASB “threw it at his feet”. The Hebrew says “his feet” without specifying whose, and “feet” is a recognised Hebrew euphemism in some contexts, which is one of several reasons the verse is obscure. ESV’s insertion of “Moses’” is an interpretive supply. High confidence that the pronoun is unspecified.

4:25–26 — “bridegroom of blood.” All the major versions render similarly; the phrase is opaque in Hebrew too. The difficulty is not translational but exegetical.

5:2 — “Who is the LORD?” No divergence, and none needed. Note only that the versions render the divine name as “the LORD”, so the force of Pharaoh’s ignorance of a name is slightly muted in English; he is not asking “who is God?” but “who is YHWH?”

6:6 — “redeem.” ESV/NASB/KJV all “redeem”; NIV “redeem”. Good — the kinsman-redeemer overtone is preserved.

Pulpit edition note: Pulpit text matches report default.

9. Tone and Feel

A long descent, and one sentence that stops it.

  • 4:18–23 — brisk and ominous. Leave-taking in a sentence; then two announcements that the reader will remember for eleven chapters.
  • 4:24–26 — abrupt and terrifying. Three verses, no explanation, no context, and the LORD trying to kill his own newly-commissioned messenger. The narrative simply stops, does this, and moves on. The tonal shock is deliberate and should not be smoothed.
  • 4:27–31 — warm. A brother’s kiss, elders gathered, signs done, a people bowing to worship. This is the unit’s high point and it lasts five verses.
  • 5:1–21 — grinding. Pharaoh’s contempt (“you are idle, you are idle”), the scattering for stubble, the beatings, the failed appeal, and then Israel’s own leaders cursing Moses in the street. The register is bureaucratic cruelty, exactly as in ch. 1.
  • 5:22–23 — raw. Moses’ complaint is not decorous. “You have not delivered your people at all.”
  • 6:1–8 — solemn, formal, overwhelming. The register changes completely: a self-identification, a covenant recital, seven undertakings, and the Name again. After twenty verses of grievance, God does not argue; he announces himself.
  • 6:9 — one flat sentence that takes it all away. And then a genealogy.

Soundtrack. Something ominous, then briefly warm; then the sound of a workyard; then a shout; then, at 6:2, a single voice speaking slowly and not raising itself.

Note for the preacher. The sermon’s emotional architecture must let chapter 5 be as bad as it is. If the congregation does not feel that obeying God made things worse, 6:2–8 will land as a platitude instead of as the thing that holds when nothing else does.

11. Quotation/Allusion

Genesis 17 → Exodus 6:3–8 (high confidence — named source with near-verbatim phrasing) [T]

Source context. Genesis 17 is the covenant of circumcision: God appears to Abram as God Almighty (ʾĒl Šadday, 17:1), changes his name, promises to be God to him and his offspring, gives “the land of your sojournings, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession” (17:8), and institutes circumcision as the sign, with the penalty that the uncircumcised is “cut off from his people” (17:14). Its function in Genesis is to bind the promise to a sign in the flesh.

Book usage. The Abrahamic covenant is Exodus’s most-worked source — activated in its dark half at 1:11–12, named at 2:24, invoked at 3:6, 15–16, and here argued: this is the passage where Exodus states the relation between the God of the fathers and the God of the exodus. It runs on to 12:40–41 and 32:13.

OT-to-OT. Genesis 17 and Genesis 15 are the two covenant chapters, and Exodus draws on both — 15’s affliction clause at 1:11–12, 17’s name, land-formula and sign here.

What it adds. Three things. The vocabulary is Genesis 17’s — ʾĒl Šadday, “the land of their sojournings”, “I will be their God”. The argument is that the God now speaking is the God who swore then, which is precisely what Israel needs and cannot hear (6:9). And — a connection the unit makes and rarely gets preached — Genesis 17 is the circumcision covenant, and this unit contains both a circumcision crisis (4:24–26) and a man who calls himself “uncircumcised of lips” (6:12, 30). [T] for the vocabulary; [I] for the circumcision thread as design. Moderate–high on the thread.

Genesis 17:10–14 → Exodus 4:24–26 (moderate confidence — shared institution, no verbal quotation) [T] for both texts

Source context. Circumcision is the covenant sign; the uncircumcised male “shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant” (17:14).

Zipporah’s act removes the ground of the threat. This is the most widely-held reading and it is an inference from Genesis 17, not a statement of Exodus 4. Moderate — see Difficult Verses.

12. Genre

Hebrew historical narrative with three embedded forms: prophetic messenger-speech (“Thus says the LORD”, 4:22; 5:1), a covenant self-disclosure oracle (6:2–8, with the self-identification formula bracketing a series of undertakings), and a genealogy (6:14–27).

Reading rules: (i) the messenger-speech formula marks the words as God’s, not Moses’; (ii) 6:2–8 should be read as a formal declaration, not as conversation — it does not answer Moses’ question, it supersedes it; (iii) the genealogy is functional, not decorative — it certifies the messengers, and its placement (after their rejection by both parties) is the point; (iv) 4:24–26 is narrated with extreme compression, and compression is not the same as obscurity by accident.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits. Between the call and the plagues; the exodus has not begun and the first attempt has made things worse.

What has happened since that changes the reading.

  • The plague cycle answers 5:2 directly and repeatedly.
  • Passover discharges 4:22–23’s firstborn logic.
  • Sinai fulfils 6:7’s “I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God”.
  • The land fulfils 6:8 — beyond the book, in Joshua.
  • Romans 9:17–18 takes up the hardening announced at 4:21.
  • The covenant formula of 6:7 runs to Jeremiah 31:33 and lands at Revelation 21:3, “they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God.”
  • 1 Peter 1:18–19 develops gāʾal’s redemption vocabulary: “you were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ.”

Timeline errors to avoid. Preaching 6:6–8 as a set of promises available directly to the hearer (the land-promise in particular runs through Christ, per Heb 11:16); and reading 5:22–23 as a template for demanding explanations from God, when the answer Moses received was not an explanation.

15. Who Am I?

  • Israel in 5:20–21 and 6:9 — the reader’s most honest place. People who believed a fortnight ago, whose obedience has cost them, who can no longer hear good news because they are too tired and too frightened. The text does not scold them, and neither should the sermon.
  • Moses — mediator, and here specifically the mediator who complains. Not a model of leadership technique; a figure whose protest is answered by God’s self-disclosure.
  • The Israelite foremen — caught between an impossible quota and their own people; beaten by Egyptians and cursed by Hebrews. A recognisable position for anyone in middle leadership.
  • Pharaoh — the one who asks the book’s question in unbelief, and will be answered whether he likes it or not.
  • Zipporah — the chapter’s one decisive actor at 4:25, and largely unexplained. Not an identification point.

The misreading to flag. Identifying with Moses-the-frustrated-leader and preaching 5:22–23 as a leadership sermon about perseverance. The unit’s centre of gravity is 6:2–8, and its subject there is God.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. For the first hearers: to know that the God who brought them out is the God who swore to their fathers, that his promises rest on his name and not on their circumstances, and that the worst stretch of their history was inside his plan rather than outside it.

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.

Worldview. Three shifts:

  1. Obedience can make things worse before it makes them better. The first act of obedience in this unit produces beatings, and the narrator does not apologise for it. Any theology in which faithfulness reliably improves circumstances has not read Exodus 5.
  2. God’s answer to “why” is often “who”. Moses asks two questions (5:22) and receives no answer to either. What he gets is a name, a covenant recital, and seven undertakings. That is not evasion; it is the only foundation that would still be standing in chapter 14.
  3. Faith can be crushed out of people by suffering, and Scripture says so without blame. 6:9 is the verse to have ready for anyone who has stopped being able to hear.

Behaviour. Start taking complaints to God rather than about him — Moses’ protest is the model, and it is far blunter than most Christian prayer. Start measuring God’s faithfulness by his name and his covenant rather than by the week’s evidence. Stop treating a fellow believer’s inability to hear encouragement as a failure of faith; the narrator calls it broken spirit and hard slavery.

Motive. The seven “I will”s are all first-person and none is conditional. Nothing in 6:6–8 depends on Israel listening — which is fortunate, because 6:9 says they didn’t.

Four audiences.

  • For me: When I ask God why, do I actually want an explanation, or would his name be enough? And which am I praying for?
  • For a Christian friend: For the one who has stopped being able to take in good news — 6:9 is in the Bible, and it is not a rebuke.
  • For the church community: A congregation whose obedience has cost it something is standing exactly where Israel stood in chapter 5. The answer given there is the one still on offer.
  • For an unbeliever: Pharaoh’s question — “Who is the LORD, that I should obey him? I do not know the LORD” — is a fair question, honestly put, and the rest of the book is the answer.

Prayer. We have obeyed and watched things get worse, and we have asked you why and received no answer. Be to us what you were to Moses: not an explanation but a name — I AM the LORD, who brings out, delivers, redeems, takes, and is God to his people. And where we are too crushed to listen, be patient with us, as you were with them.

Grammatical and syntactical features.

  • The sevenfold wĕ- + first-person imperfect at 6:6–8. Seven cohortative-force undertakings in sequence — wĕhôṣēʾtî (I will bring out), wĕhiṣṣaltî (I will deliver), wĕgāʾaltî (I will redeem), wĕlāqaḥtî (I will take), wĕhāyîtî (I will be), wĕhēbēʾtî (I will bring in), wĕnātattî (I will give). Counted against the text. [T] High confidence. The series is bracketed by ʾănî YHWH at both ends.
  • The unspecified pronouns of 4:24–26. “The LORD met him and sought to put him to death”; “touched his feet”. Hebrew does not say whether the object is Moses or the son at any point, which is a principal reason the passage resists resolution. [T] High confidence on the ambiguity — and it should be stated from the pulpit rather than concealed by a translation’s supply.
  • The clause structure of 6:3. “And I appeared… as ʾĒl Šadday, and my name YHWH — I was not known to them.” The negative is unqualified: no “fully”, no “in its full significance” — those are supplied by translators and commentators, not by the Hebrew. Whether the clause can be read as a rhetorical question is a genuine grammatical dispute. [T] for the wording; the construal contested.
  • The resumptive repetition at 6:28–30. A standard technique: the narrative resumes after the genealogical insertion by repeating the last thing said (6:12 → 6:30). Recognising it prevents the misreading that 6:28–30 is a second, separate objection. [T] High.
  • The infinitive absolute is absent where one might expect it. At 5:23 Moses says simply wĕhaṣṣēl lōʾ-hiṣṣaltā — “and delivering, you have not delivered” — which is an infinitive-absolute construction, and it intensifies: “you have not delivered your people at all.” The bluntness is grammatical. Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. Egypt, the delta brickfields and the royal court. [T]

What the original audience knew.

  • Brick-making and straw. Chopped straw was mixed into Nile mud to bind the brick and speed drying; removing the straw while holding the quota is a technically informed cruelty, not an arbitrary one. Egyptian records attest brick quotas and the supply of straw. [S] Moderate–high — the general practice is well attested; verify specifics before citing figures.
  • “Stubble instead of straw” (5:12). Gathering stubble from harvested fields is far more labour for far worse material. The decree does not merely add work; it degrades the product while holding the standard.
  • Israelite foremen (šōṭĕrîm). A supervisory layer drawn from the enslaved population and beaten when quotas failed — a documented administrative pattern, and the source of the unit’s most poignant scene.
  • The three-days’ journey to sacrifice (5:3). A request for a religious festival requiring travel out of the land would be a recognisable category; Pharaoh’s response treats it as a labour-relations problem (“you are idle”), which is exactly how a state values worship it does not share.
  • “The God of the Hebrews” (5:3). The designation used to a foreigner; the messengers identify their God by the people he belongs to, which is the only handle Pharaoh has.
  • Circumcision in Egypt. Egyptians practised a form of circumcision, so the rite itself would not be alien — but the covenantal meaning of Genesis 17 would be. [S] Moderate.

What this changes about how we read. Two things. First, the oppression in ch. 5 is competent — a well-designed measure by an administration that knows exactly how brick-making works. Second, the audience would recognise Pharaoh’s category error: he hears a request for worship and answers with a labour policy, which is the same misreading that runs through the plagues (“let my people go, that they may serve me”).

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Torah — compositional and canonical audiences coincide: wilderness Israel, hearing why the exodus began badly and what God said when it did.

The first hearers.

  • Who they were: the people who had since seen the plagues, the Sea and Sinai — and who were, in the wilderness, repeatedly doing again what 5:21 records.
  • Their situation: free, grumbling, and prone to remember Egypt as better than it was (16:3).
  • What they brought: Genesis and the patriarchal covenant; and a living memory of straw and quotas.

Where the passage fits their world. It tells them that their parents’ generation once cursed Moses in the street and that God’s response was not to punish but to name himself — which is both a rebuke and a comfort to a people currently grumbling. And it tells them that the demand Pharaoh refused was for worship (“that they may hold a feast to me in the wilderness”, 5:1), which is what they are now free to do and keep failing to prioritise.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.

  • Shocking: 4:24 — the LORD seeking to kill the man he has just commissioned; and Israel’s leaders invoking God against Moses (5:21).
  • Surprising: that God’s answer to a bitter complaint is neither rebuke nor explanation; and that the genealogy appears exactly where it does.
  • Comforting: 6:2–8 entire — and 6:9’s refusal to blame them.
  • Disturbing: that obedience produced beatings; and 4:21’s “I will harden his heart”, stated before any of it begins.

What we bring that they didn’t. An expectation that obedience is rewarded within the story arc; an impatience with genealogies; a tendency to read 5:22–23 as a leadership problem; and — because we know how the book ends — a difficulty in feeling how total the collapse at 6:9 was.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. The Name, and knowing the LORD.

  • Where this passage sits: Pharaoh’s “I do not know the LORD” (5:2) and God’s “you shall know that I am the LORD your God” (6:7) — the question and the promised answer, set three chapters apart.
  • What comes before: the Name given at the bush (3:14–15); ʾĒl Šadday in Genesis.
  • What comes after: the plague refrain (7:5 through 11:7); Ezekiel’s seventy-odd “then they shall know that I am the LORD”; and “this is eternal life, that they know you” (John 17:3).
  • Contribution: it establishes that the exodus is an epistemological event — God acts so that he may be known — and it puts the question in the mouth of the man who most needs the answer.
  • Confidence: high.

2. Covenant remembered and promises given.

  • Where this passage sits: 6:2–8 — the covenant with the patriarchs named, and seven undertakings drawn from it.
  • What comes before: Gen 15 and 17; Exod 2:24.
  • What comes after: 12:41; 19:4–6; 20:2; 32:13; Jer 31:33’s covenant formula; Rev 21:3.
  • Contribution: it supplies the book’s clearest statement that the rescue rests on an oath sworn generations earlier, and it does so at the moment the oath looks worthless.
  • Confidence: high.

3. Redemption (gāʾal).

  • Where this passage sits: 6:6 — the kinsman-redeemer verb’s first Exodus appearance.
  • What comes before: the Genesis family narratives in which kinship obligation operates.
  • What comes after: the redemption of the firstborn (13:13); Leviticus 25’s redemption laws; Ruth; “your Redeemer” in Isaiah 40–55; and 1 Pet 1:18–19’s “ransomed… with the precious blood of Christ”.
  • Contribution: it defines the exodus not as escape but as a claim of ownership by one who has the right to make it — which is why 6:7 pairs redemption immediately with “I will take you to be my people”.
  • Confidence: high.

Confluence — (c) climactic convergence. All three meet in 6:6–8: the Redeemer who is bound by covenant acts so that his people may know his Name. Redemption is the action, covenant the ground, and knowing the Name the goal — which is also the shape the NT gives to salvation.

Christological Reading

Type(s) of connection: trajectory and contrast, with one strong vocabulary line. No forced typology — the unit’s Christology is mostly in what it plants.

  • “Israel is my firstborn son” → the Son given (trajectory). 4:22–23 makes the exodus a matter of sonship: God’s firstborn is held, and Pharaoh’s firstborn will die. Tests: category (sonship, firstborn, substitution) — pass; NT precedent — Israel-as-son is applied to Christ at Matt 2:15 (quoting Hos 11:1), and “firstborn” becomes a Christological title (Col 1:15; Rom 8:29) — moderate–high; escalation — pass, decisively: at the Passover another firstborn dies in place of Israel’s, and at the cross God’s own firstborn is not spared (Rom 8:32); authorial pattern — the text plants and the book detonates. High confidence on the trajectory.
  • Gāʾal, “redeem” (6:6) → the Redeemer (vocabulary trajectory). The kinsman-redeemer verb enters Exodus here and runs to Isaiah’s “your Redeemer” and to 1 Peter 1:18–19. Redemption means being bought back by one with the right and the obligation to do it — which is the logic the incarnation supplies (Heb 2:14–17, “he had to be made like his brothers in every respect”). Moderate–high.
  • The mediator who complains and is answered (5:22–6:8) → Gethsemane and Hebrews 5:7 (contrast and trajectory). Moses protests and is given a name; Christ protests and is given a cup. The pattern of the mediator bearing the weight of a rescue that is going wrong is genuine; the escalation is that Christ’s “why have you forsaken me?” receives no answer at all. Moderate — a trajectory, and it should be labelled as such rather than pressed as typology.
  • “I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God” (6:7) → Revelation 21:3. The covenant formula’s first full statement in Exodus, and its terminus. High confidence on the formula’s canonical career.
  • A connection to decline. Zipporah’s circumcision (4:25) as a type of atonement — blood applied, wrath averted. The pattern is suggestive and the passage is too obscure to bear it. Recorded and declined; see Difficult Verses.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptations: “persevere like Moses when ministry gets hard”; “don’t lose faith like the Israelites”; “be bold like Moses before Pharaoh”. The second is the worst, because the text explicitly declines to blame them (6:9).
  • The gospel grounding: every promise in 6:6–8 is first-person and unconditional, given to people who cannot hear it. Nothing in the unit turns on Israel’s response.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the subject of 6:2–8 is God identifying himself; the subject of 4:22–23 is a Father and a son. Preach the Redeemer who claims what is his, and the firstborn given up so that the firstborn might live.

Confidence: high (the firstborn trajectory; the covenant formula); moderate–high (gāʾal); moderate (the mediator’s complaint); declined (Zipporah as atonement-type).

Difficult / Contested Verses

4:24–26 — “the LORD met him and sought to put him to death.” - Category: doctrinal / apologetic — one of the hardest passages in the Pentateuch, and flagged as a landmine in the book overview since v0.1.0. - The difficulty: God attempts to kill the man he has just commissioned; the reason is not stated; the pronouns never specify whether “him” is Moses or the son; Zipporah’s words and actions are opaque; and “bridegroom of blood” is obscure in Hebrew. - Rhetorical function in context: it stands between the firstborn-son announcement (4:22–23) and the arrival in Egypt. God has just said “I will kill your firstborn son” — and immediately a firstborn son is in mortal danger and is saved by the application of blood. That placement is almost certainly deliberate and is the strongest interpretive clue the passage gives. [I] Moderate–high. - The main options: (i) Moses had failed to circumcise his son, and the covenant sanction of Gen 17:14 (“cut off… he has broken my covenant”) falls on the covenant mediator — the most widely held reading; (ii) the danger is to the son rather than Moses; (iii) the episode functions primarily as a foreshadowing of Passover — blood applied, death passed over; (iv) an older tradition whose original sense is now irrecoverable. - Checking stage. On the referent, the answer is Moses: the LORD seeks to put Moses to death, “though the narrative’s opacity has generated interpretive debate”, and the threat ceases when Zipporah circumcises the son and touches Moses’ feet. On the phrase, three readings are in play: the covenant-obligation reading (Zipporah’s act fulfils what Moses had neglected and reincorporates him into the covenant community through a bloody rite — the most straightforward); a wordplay linking the bridegroom’s blood to the blood of the covenant, so that Moses is consecrated through blood as a bridegroom is through marriage; and a sardonic redefinition of the bond between Zipporah and Moses. And a fourth observation worth keeping: “the ambiguity may be intentional — the text resists neat resolution, leaving readers with the visceral image of a woman performing a priestly act to save her husband’s life.” [revised: logos-research] Note honestly: this portion of the answer came back uncited — no library sources were attached to it — so treat it as a summary of the standard options rather than as sourced, and verify before leaning on any one reading. - What an honest exegesis surfaces: reading (i), with (iii) as the narrative function, accounts for the most data — the Genesis 17 sanction explains the threat, the placement immediately after 4:22–23 explains the timing, and the blood explains the deliverance. But the passage withholds so much that any reading is an inference, and the preacher should say so. - Handling: do not skip it and do not pretend to solve it. Name the placement (it comes immediately after 4:23), name the likeliest reading, name what the text refuses to tell us, and let the strangeness stand — including the fact that the one who acts is Zipporah. A congregation is better served by an honest three minutes than by a confident ten.

6:3 — “by my name the LORD I did not make myself known to them.” - Category: doctrinal / apologetic — the overview’s second flagged landmine here, and the verse most often used in debates about the Pentateuch’s composition. - The difficulty: Genesis uses the name YHWH repeatedly and puts it in the patriarchs’ mouths (e.g. Gen 15:2, 7; 28:13), so the plain sense of 6:3 appears to contradict Genesis. - The main options — checked, and there are four live ones. [revised: logos-research] 1. The character-versus-name distinction. The patriarchs had the name but not the character behind it; the Hebrew preposition is taken as “in the character of the name”. Rashi distinguishes ʾĒl Šadday as God’s promise-giving character from YHWH as fulfilment; Rambam contrasts providential power with miracle-working power (Kaiser, The Old Testament Documents, 139–40). This medieval Jewish reading is older and more precise than the usual pulpit version of it. 2. The rhetorical-question construal. W. J. Martin proposed reading the clause as an implicit affirmation — “Did I not make myself known to them?” — arguing that the following clause about establishing the covenant requires a positive sense (Kaiser). So the construal has a named proponent, and my provisional note that “the consonantal text permits it” understated its standing. 3. The experiential-knowledge reading. “Knowing YHWH means experiencing him as YHWH” (Pröbstle); and, most precisely, the verb “make oneself known” refers not to announcing a previously unknown name but to God’s actions consistent with the name’s meaning (T. D. Alexander, Apollos OTC, 2:114–15). That formulation is better than mine and should be the one preached. 4. The source-critical account. E and P avoid the name in Genesis, so 6:2–3 is the Priestly account of its revelation. Alexander notes this “enjoyed widespread twentieth-century support” but that it struggles to explain why the final redactor overlooked the apparent contradiction — a fair objection from within a conservative commentary. Name it as existing; this report does not adopt it. - What an honest exegesis surfaces: readings 1 and 3 converge and fit the verse’s own context — the speech is about covenant-keeping (vv. 4–5) and about what God is now about to do (vv. 6–8), which is exactly the register of “knowing” throughout this book (5:2; 6:7; and the plague refrain). Alexander’s formulation is the sharpest available. It is also worth saying plainly that the NIV’s “fully” is supplied by translators, not present in the Hebrew. - Handling: one careful paragraph, and name at least the rhetorical-question option as well — a hearer with a study Bible may have met it, and it is not a fringe suggestion. A hearer who has met the source-critical version elsewhere will be glad it was addressed rather than avoided.

4:21 — “I will harden his heart.” - Category: doctrinal. - Announced here, executed through chs. 7–11, and taken up at Romans 9:17–18. The fuller treatment belongs to the plagues dig, which worked the three-verb pattern and the agency shift at 9:12. Flag here; route there.

5:3 — the three days’ journey. - Category: ethical / apologetic. - The difficulty: the request is for a three-day festival, not for permanent departure — and God himself framed it that way (3:18). Is this a deception? - Honest exegesis: the options are that it is a genuine first request whose refusal escalates the conflict; a formal diplomatic opening; or a test that exposes Pharaoh’s refusal to grant even a modest concession. Note that God had already said Pharaoh would refuse even this (3:19) — so the request functions to demonstrate the refusal rather than to secure the concession. Moderate. - Handling: name it briefly if the sermon touches ch. 5. It is a fair question and there is a fair answer.

5:22–23 — Moses accuses God. - Category: pastoral / doctrinal. - The complaint is blunt: “you have not delivered your people at all.” God does not rebuke it. The difficulty is for readers who think such speech is impious — and the passage is the answer to them. Handling: preach it as permission, with the caution that Moses takes it to God.

6:9 — “they did not listen… because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery.” - Category: pastoral, and the most useful verse in the unit. - Not a rebuke. Scripture here describes faith failing under duress and assigns the cause to the duress. Handling: say it plainly; someone in the room needs it.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Preaching 5:22–23 as a leadership sermon

  • What it looks like: Moses the discouraged leader; a message about perseverance in ministry when things go wrong.
  • Why it’s wrong: it puts the preacher and congregation at the centre of a unit whose centre is 6:2–8, where the subject is God identifying himself. The complaint is real and is not rebuked — but it is the set-up, not the point.
  • The corrective: preach the complaint honestly, then let God’s answer be the sermon. The answer to “why” is “who”.

Pitfall 2: Blaming Israel for 6:9

  • What it looks like: “If only they’d had more faith they’d have believed the promises.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the narrator gives the reason and it is not sin: “because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery.” Scripture describes faith failing under duress and assigns the cause to the duress.
  • The corrective: say what the text says. Someone in the room has stopped being able to hear good news, and this verse is for them.

Pitfall 3: Skipping 4:24–26

  • What it looks like: starting the reading at 4:27, or reading it and moving on without comment.
  • Why it’s wrong: the congregation will have read it, and silence looks like evasion. It is also placed deliberately — immediately after “I will kill your firstborn son” — and that placement is the strongest clue the text gives.
  • The corrective: three honest minutes. Name the likeliest reading, name that the LORD’s target is Moses, name what the text withholds, and let it stand. Do not manufacture certainty.

Pitfall 4: Resolving 6:3 too quickly, or in one direction only

  • What it looks like: either “they knew the name but not the character — next verse”, or a lecture on the Documentary Hypothesis.
  • Why it’s wrong: there are four live options, two of them convergent and one of them a rhetorical-question construal with a named proponent. The NIV’s “fully” is a translator’s supply, and a hearer using it may not know that.
  • The corrective: one careful paragraph. Alexander’s formulation is the cleanest: the verb refers not to announcing an unknown name but to God’s acting in a way consistent with what the name means.

Pitfall 5: Treating chapter 5 as a detour

  • What it looks like: summarising the straw decree to get to the good bit in chapter 6.
  • Why it’s wrong: 6:2–8 only lands if chapter 5 has been felt. The unit is built as a descent, and the promise arrives at the bottom of it.
  • The corrective: let the brickfields be as bad as they are. The sermon’s emotional architecture depends on it.

Pitfall 6: Reading the seven “I will”s as promises to the hearer

  • What it looks like: claiming 6:6–8 directly — “God will bring you out, redeem you, bring you into the land.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the land-promise in particular runs through Christ (Heb 11:16), and the covenant formula of 6:7 reaches its terminus at Revelation 21:3. Applied flat, it becomes a prosperity reading of a text about Egypt.
  • The corrective: preach the promises as the covenant’s own logic, and trace them forward — out of slavery, into covenant, into the inheritance — as the NT does.
