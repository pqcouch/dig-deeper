## The seven remaining

**1. The angel-of-the-LORD Christophany at Exodus 3:2, 4.**
Held at *moderate–high* and never asserted as settled. **Verify against a commentary that treats OT Christophany, particularly the angel/LORD apposition** — the narrator moves between "the angel of the LORD" and "the LORD"/"God" without marking the shift, which is the whole question.
*Why it is not urgent:* the report already refuses to assert it. Resolving it would let you preach it with more or less confidence, not differently. *(§ 3:1–4:17.)*

**2. The rear boundary of the 19:1–8 unit.**
This project ends the unit at v. 8 (the vow and Moses' report), with v. 9 opening the sequel. **A defensible alternative keeps 19:1–9 together, with v. 9 as the pivot.**
*Why it matters more than the others:* **it determines where your first Sinai sermon stops.** Worth ten minutes with two commentaries before you plan that pair. *(§ 19:1–8.)*

**3. Hebrews 12:21's "I tremble with fear", attributed to Moses.**
Drawn from tradition (cf. Deuteronomy 9:19), **not quoted from Exodus 19–20**. Confirm the source before using it in a Sinai sermon — it is the kind of detail that gets repeated confidently and is not in the text being preached. *(§ 19:9–20:21.)*

**4. The resumptive 19:9b** — *"When Moses told the words of the people to the LORD."*
A known compositional seam. Nothing preaching-critical depends on it, but note it if you teach the chapter's flow. *(§ 19:9–20:21.)*

**5. Do commentators treat 20:22–24:18 as one unit, or divide at 23:33?**
The positional argument (the altar inclusio at 20:24–26 → 24:4) supports keeping them together, and the dig makes that case from the text. **A commentary check would tell you whether you are with the field or against it.** *(§ 20:22–24:18.)*

**6. The Reuel / Jethro double-naming** in Exodus 2 and 18.
Standard explanations exist; the Research Assistant explicitly did not address it. **A hearer will notice**, so it is worth a sentence's worth of preparation. *(§ 2:1–25; § 18:1–27.)*

**7. Two loose ends.**
Whether the **Hur** of 17:10 and 24:14 is Bezalel's grandfather (31:2) — used in a minor internal-echo finding; and whether **"a sign on your hand and frontlets between your eyes"** (13:9, 16) is literal or metaphorical. *(§ 15:22–17:16; § 12:1–13:16.)*

## Two questions that stay open by nature

Not awaiting research. **Both are better informed than they were, and neither will close.**

- **Exodus 4:24–26 — the "bridegroom of blood."** The Septuagint and the Targums read the circumcision as a sacrificial rite and the attacker as **an angel of death rather than Yahweh**, tracing the assault to Moses' failure under Genesis 17:9–14 — and **the rabbinic tradition itself divides over whether Moses or the child is the "bridegroom of blood."** **But the versions resolve what the Hebrew leaves open, and that is itself the finding.** Preach the options and say the text withholds a verdict.
- **The ethics of the midwives' answer at 1:19.** Commentators distinguish the deception from the ground of the blessing, which the text states as the fear of God. Open by nature.

## One contested reading to hold in view

Not a question so much as a live disagreement, recorded so it is not forgotten.

**Exodus 18:23 — condition or assertion?** This project reads Jethro's *"If you do this, and God so commands you"* as a **condition the narrative never reports as met**, which supports the finding that the chapter withholds divine endorsement of the plan. **Ross and Oswalt read it as a flat assertion — *"God commands you to do it"* — citing Moses' immediate compliance as evidence.** The English versions render a conditional and the chapter's divine silence is undisputed, so the reading stands; **but if you preach the silence, know that a good commentary reads the verse the other way.**
