## The Passage

Twenty-five verses, quoted in full.

> **1** Now a man from the house of Levi went and took as his wife a Levite woman. **2** The woman conceived and bore a son, and when she saw that he was a fine child, she hid him three months. **3** When she could hide him no longer, she took for him **a basket** made of bulrushes and daubed it with bitumen and pitch. She put the child in it and placed it among the reeds by the river bank. **4** And his sister stood at a distance to know what would be done to him.
>
> **5** Now the daughter of Pharaoh came down to bathe at the river, while her young women walked beside the river. She saw the basket among the reeds and sent her servant woman, and she took it. **6** When she opened it, she saw the child, and behold, the baby was crying. She took pity on him and said, "This is one of the Hebrews' children." **7** Then his sister said to Pharaoh's daughter, "Shall I go and call you a nurse from the Hebrew women to nurse the child for you?" **8** And Pharaoh's daughter said to her, "Go." So the girl went and called the child's mother. **9** And Pharaoh's daughter said to her, "Take this child away and nurse him for me, and **I will give you your wages**." So the woman took the child and nursed him. **10** When the child grew older, she brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. She named him Moses, "Because," she said, "**I drew him out of the water**."
>
> **11** One day, when Moses had grown up, he went out to his people and looked on their burdens, and he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his people. **12** He looked this way and that, and seeing no one, he struck down the Egyptian and hid him in the sand. **13** When he went out the second day, behold, two Hebrews were struggling together. And he said to the man in the wrong, "Why do you strike your companion?" **14** He answered, "**Who made you a prince and a judge over us?** Do you mean to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?" Then Moses was afraid, and thought, "Surely the thing is known." **15** When Pharaoh heard of it, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from Pharaoh and stayed in the land of Midian. And he sat down by a well.
>
> **16** Now the priest of Midian had seven daughters, and they came and drew water and filled the troughs to water their father's flock. **17** The shepherds came and drove them away, but Moses stood up and saved them, and watered their flock. **18** When they came home to their father Reuel, he said, "How is it that you have come home so soon today?" **19** They said, "**An Egyptian** delivered us out of the hand of the shepherds and even drew water for us and watered the flock." **20** He said to his daughters, "Then where is he? Why have you left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread." **21** And Moses was content to dwell with the man, and he gave Moses his daughter Zipporah. **22** She gave birth to a son, and he called his name **Gershom**, for he said, "**I have been a sojourner in a foreign land.**"
>
> **23** During those many days the king of Egypt died, and the people of Israel groaned because of their slavery and cried out for help. Their cry for rescue from slavery came up to God. **24** And **God heard** their groaning, and **God remembered** his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob. **25** **God saw** the people of Israel — and **God knew.**

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple converging tools:

1. **The basket is an ark. *Tēbâ* — the word used at 2:3 and 2:5 — occurs in the Hebrew Bible only here and in the flood narrative (Genesis 6–9), and both vessels are waterproofed with pitch to carry life through waters of death.** `[T]` — verified: Gen 6:14 "make yourself an ark… and cover it inside and out with **pitch**"; Exod 2:3 "a basket made of bulrushes and daubed it with **bitumen and pitch**". Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion (Move 1), Schnittjer Pass, Biblical Theology. **The narrator has narrated Moses' rescue as a second flood-deliverance in miniature**, and the reader who knows Genesis is told, in one noun, that this child is being preserved for a new beginning. *High confidence — the most distinctive verbal allusion in the chapter.* **Commentators give it "considerable weight":** both figures "escape waters of judgment in an ark sealed with pitch, and both survive to found a new world — in Moses' case the nation of Israel" (Hahn, *Catholic Bible Dictionary*, 68); the river ordeal carries "overtones of judgment and salvation" (Dozeman, *Commentary on Exodus*, ECC, 81–82); the restricted usage marks a vessel "capable of floating to deliver its occupants from danger" (Hartley, *Genesis*, UBC, 102). `[revised: logos-research]`

   **But the link is Hebrew-only — verified.** **LXX Exodus 2:3 reads θῖβιν (*thibin*), a loan-word; LXX Genesis 6:14 reads κιβωτόν (*kibōton*).** The Greek uses two different nouns where the Hebrew uses one, so **a Greek-reading hearer does not meet the allusion at all.** (The *pitch* survives in both — ἀσφαλτοπίσσῃ at Exod 2:3, ἀσφάλτῳ at Gen 6:14 — so the sealing detail carries in Greek even though the vessel-word does not.) `[revised: logos-research]` *High confidence — read directly from the Logos LXX.* **Say "in the Hebrew".**

2. **The chapter's deliverers are women, and Moses is entirely passive throughout the first ten verses.** A Levite mother, a watching sister, Pharaoh's daughter and her maids — and then seven daughters in Midian. Moses does not act until v. 11, and when he does, it goes wrong. `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Narrator's Comment, Who Am I?, Copycat, Original Audience. **Chapter 1's one concession — "every daughter you shall let live" (1:22) — is the loophole through which the deliverer survives.** *High confidence.*

3. **Moses' first attempt at deliverance is a private act, done after checking that no one is watching, and it fails on both counts: it is discovered, and his own people reject him.** "He looked this way and that, and seeing no one, he struck down the Egyptian" (2:12); "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (2:14). `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Linking Words, Tone, Copycat, Christological Reading. **Both Acts 7:25 and Hebrews 11:24–27 read this scene, and Stephen makes it a paradigm of Israel's rejection of its deliverers** ("he supposed his brethren would have understood… but they understood not"). *High confidence.*

4. **The chapter ends by breaking the silence of chapter 1 with four verbs in two verses: God *heard*, *remembered*, *saw*, and *knew* (2:24–25).** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Narrator's Comment, Positional Necessity, Internal echo. Chapter 1 made God the subject of two clauses in twenty-two verses; here he is the subject of four in two. And the last has no object in the Hebrew — "**and God knew**", full stop. **The turn of the whole book happens in a sentence with a missing word.** *High confidence.*

5. **Moses names his son for exile, and the name becomes the ethical ground of Israel's law. "Gershom, for he said, 'I have been a *sojourner* (*gēr*) in a foreign land'" (2:22)** — and the Book of the Covenant will twice ground its protection of the vulnerable on exactly that experience: "you were **sojourners** in the land of Egypt" (22:21; 23:9). `[T]` for both texts; `[I]` for the connection as design. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Internal echo, Biblical Theology, So What?. **The mediator's own displacement becomes the law's memory.** *Moderate–high confidence.*

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Chapter 1 has done three things this chapter requires. (i) It established the **blessing outrunning the policy** (1:7, 12, 20). (ii) It escalated to a **public decree of drowning** — "every son that is born you shall cast into the Nile, but you shall let every daughter live" (1:22). (iii) It **withheld God**, making him the subject of only two clauses in twenty-two verses.

**Necessity answer.** Why does this chapter exist here, and in this shape?

- **Because 1:22 requires a basket.** The chapter's opening scene is unintelligible without the decree; the mother hides the child for three months and then puts him *in the very river* the decree named. **The instrument of death becomes the means of rescue** — which is the same move the book will make at 7:20 and 14:28.
- **Because 1:22's loophole requires daughters.** Pharaoh spared girls, and girls save the deliverer. The irony is only available because ch. 1 stated the policy twice (1:16, 22).
- **Because the silence has to break somewhere, and not too soon.** Twenty-two verses of ch. 1 and twenty-two of ch. 2 pass with God almost unmentioned; then four verbs. **Had 2:24–25 come earlier, the covenant would look responsive rather than remembered.**

And it must come **before** ch. 3, because the burning bush answers this chapter twice over: the four verbs of divine attention here (heard, remembered, saw, knew) are matched by four verbs of divine action there — "I have **seen**… I have **heard**… I **know**… I have **come down**" (3:7–8). The 3:1–4:17 dig identified that pairing as a headline finding; **this chapter is its other half.** `[T]`

**Implication for this passage.** Three things follow. First, the chapter is structured as **rescue → failed rescue → exile → and then God**, so that the deliverer is off the stage when deliverance finally begins to move. Second, Moses' forty years in Midian are narrated in seven verses and his failure in five: **the narrator is not interested in his formation but in his disqualification.** Third, the chapter's last word — "and God knew" — is the hinge of the whole book, and it is placed after every human attempt has failed.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 1 sharpens it: the *tēbâ* allusion means the chapter opens with a flood-rescue and closes with a covenant remembered — **Noah at one end, Abraham at the other.** The chapter is bracketed by the two great covenants of Genesis.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** The overview's presenting situation gets its answer here: the God of the fathers has not forgotten, and the deliverer exists.

**Passage-level.** Three things this unit is doing:

- **Preserving the deliverer through the decree** — and doing it by the hands of women, under the nose of the court, at the expense of the crown ("I will give you your wages", v. 9).
- **Disqualifying him.** The narrator gives five verses to Moses' attempt and its rejection, and forty years to a sentence. **The point of 2:11–22 is not that Moses was being prepared but that Moses had failed and fled.**
- **Turning the book.** vv. 23–25 are the pivot: the human story has run into the sand (literally, v. 12), and God moves.

A fourth belongs to the narrator: **naming.** Moses is named at v. 10 and Gershom at v. 22, both with explanations, and the explanations are about *water* and *exile* — the two things the book is about.

---

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6.* Short form: 1:22 requires the basket and supplies the loophole; the silence of ch. 1 makes 2:24–25 land; and 2:24–25 is answered by 3:7–8.

#### General contextual notes

**Immediately before (1:22).** The decree. Everything in vv. 1–10 is a response to it.

**Immediately after (3:1–6).** "Now Moses was keeping the flock of his father-in-law, Jethro, the priest of Midian" — the situation this chapter has just established, and the bush.

**Section.** First movement (1:1–15:21, "from Egypt"). Chapter 1 sets the emergency; chs. 2–4 raise and commission the deliverer.

**Commonly read out of context.** Two passages. **2:11–15** is the standard text for leadership sermons about "God's timing" and "acting in the flesh" — readings that are not baseless (Acts 7:25 supports something like the first) but that usually proceed without the chapter's own emphasis, which is Moses' rejection rather than his impatience. And **2:24–25** is quoted as a devotional assurance divorced from the covenant it names: God remembers *his covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob*, not a general benevolence.

---

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 2:1–10 | **Preserved through the water** | Birth, concealment, the ark, the daughter, the nursing, the naming. Moses is passive throughout |
| 2:11–15 | **The failed deliverance** | Moses acts, kills, is rejected, is discovered, flees |
| 2:16–22 | **Midian: a well, a rescue, a marriage, a name** | A betrothal type-scene; Moses does successfully for foreigners what he could not do for his own |
| 2:23–25 | **God hears** | The king dies; Israel cries; four divine verbs |

**Devices.**

- **A three-part human sequence answered by a divine one.** vv. 1–22 are entirely about what people do — hiding, watching, drawing out, striking, fleeing, watering, marrying. vv. 23–25 are entirely about what God does. **The structural break at v. 23 is the chapter's argument.** `[T]` *High confidence.*
- **The water-thread runs the whole chapter.** The river that kills (1:22) carries the ark (2:3–5); Moses is named for being **drawn out** of water (2:10); he sits by a **well** (2:15) and **draws water** for the daughters (2:17, 19). *The Hebrew of v. 10's "I drew him out" and v. 19's "he drew water" are not the same root, so this is a thematic thread rather than a verbal one — but it is unmistakable and it is the chapter's dominant image.* `[T]` for the occurrences; `[I]` for the thread as design. *Moderate–high.*
- **The betrothal type-scene at the well (vv. 15–21).** The recognised pattern — a man arrives in a foreign land, comes to a well, meets young women, water is drawn, hospitality follows, a marriage results — is the same pattern as Genesis 24 (Isaac's bride) and Genesis 29 (Jacob and Rachel). **The variation is the point:** here the man does the drawing and the rescuing, and he is a fugitive rather than an emissary. `[T]` for the elements; `[S]` for the type-scene category. *Moderate–high; see the Schnittjer Pass.*
- **Two naming-etymologies frame the chapter's middle** — Moses (v. 10) and Gershom (v. 22) — and both explain a name by an experience.

---

### 4. Linking Words

**2:2 — "and when she *saw* that he was a fine child."** The causal link between perception and action; Hebrews 11:23 takes the parents' seeing as *faith*.

**2:3 — "*When* she could hide him no longer."** The temporal clause marks the point at which concealment fails and a riskier trust begins.

**2:11 — "*One day, when* Moses had grown up."** A time-jump of decades in five words; Acts 7:23 supplies "forty years old".

**2:12 — "and *seeing no one*, he struck down the Egyptian."** A circumstantial clause that condemns: the deed is conditioned on not being observed. **He looked this way and that — and not up.** `[T]` for the clause; the homiletical turn is `[I]` and should be labelled.

**2:14 — "*Who made you* a prince and a judge over us?"** A rhetorical question that the NT treats as programmatic (Acts 7:27, 35). **Its force is a challenge to authority Moses had not been given** — and 3:10 is where he is given it.

**2:22 — "Gershom, *for* he said, 'I have been a sojourner in a foreign land.'"** ←*for*← The name's stated ground.

**2:23 — "*During those many days*… *and* the people of Israel groaned… *and* their cry came up to God."** A chain of *waw*-consecutives with no causal marker: the narrative simply stacks events until the cry reaches God. **The absence of a "therefore" is itself notable** — the text does not say the cry *caused* God to act; the next verse says he *remembered his covenant*.

**2:24 — "and God remembered his covenant *with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob*."** The three-fold naming is the ground of everything that follows in the book. Not "God felt sorry for them".

---

### 5. Parallels

Not poetry — **N/A** at verse level. Two structural parallels carry meaning.

**vv. 11–15 ↔ vv. 16–22 — two interventions, two outcomes.** Moses intervenes on behalf of the oppressed twice: for a Hebrew against an Egyptian (v. 11), and for seven Midianite women against shepherds (v. 17). **The first gets him rejected and exiled; the second gets him a home, a wife and a son.** The parallel is exact enough to be deliberate — and its irony is that the outsider's welcome exceeds his own people's.

**The four verbs of vv. 24–25** stand in a cumulative series: heard → remembered → saw → knew. Not synonymous parallelism but escalation, moving from perception to relationship, and ending on a verb with no object.

---

### 6. Narrator's Comment

The narrator is spare here, and three moments matter.

- **2:2 — "she saw that he was a fine child" (*kî-ṭôb hûʾ*).** Literally "that he was **good**". **The phrase is the refrain of Genesis 1** ("and God saw that it was good"), and its appearance at the birth of the deliverer is at least a resonance. `[T]` for the wording; `[I]` for the allusion. *Moderate — worth naming, not worth building on.*
- **2:11 — "he went out to his people and looked on their burdens."** The narrator supplies Moses' identification with Israel before any action. Hebrews 11:24–26 turns this into a deliberate choice ("refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter… choosing rather to suffer affliction with the people of God").
- **2:14 — "Then Moses was afraid, and thought, 'Surely the thing is known.'"** Inside information: his fear and his reasoning. **The narrator lets us hear that Moses' concern is exposure, not conscience.**
- **2:23–25 — the four verbs.** The chapter's only theological statement, delivered flat, with no adjectives and no explanation. **The most consequential narratorial move in the first two chapters, and the plainest sentence in them.**

---

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory.** Levi (twice, vv. 1); Pharaoh and his daughter (unnamed); **Moses**; the Hebrews; **Midian**; **Reuel**; **Zipporah**; **Gershom**; Abraham, Isaac, Jacob (v. 24); Egypt. Triage: **Moses** and **Gershom** are named *with etymologies by the text itself* and are handled below; **Reuel** is worth a line (he is called **Jethro** from 3:1 — a genuine double-naming the preacher should be ready for); **Midian** carries Genesis freight (a son of Abraham by Keturah, Gen 25:2 — so Moses marries within the wider Abrahamic family); **Abraham, Isaac and Jacob** are the chapter's theological anchor. Pharaoh and his daughter remain unnamed, continuing ch. 1's pattern.

**The words that carry the chapter.**

**(a) *tēbâ* — "ark, basket" (vv. 3, 5)** `[T]` — *High confidence. The chapter's key word.*
Used in the Hebrew Bible **only here and of Noah's ark** (Gen 6–9). Both are sealed with pitch; both carry life through waters that are killing everything else; both result in a new beginning for a people. English translations render "basket" here and "ark" there, which is defensible (the objects differ wildly in scale) but **hides the single most distinctive lexical link in the chapter.** See Tool 8 and Tool 11.

**(b) *mōšeh* — "Moses" (v. 10)** `[T]` for the text's own explanation; **the etymology settled at the checking stage.**
Pharaoh's daughter names him "Because I **drew him out** (*mĕšîtihû*) of the water." **The scholarly consensus decisively favours an *Egyptian* origin** — the verb *msy*, "to be born", and the noun *ms*, "son", as in Thut-mose (Dozeman, ECC, 81–82). **The Hebrew explanation at v. 10 is a wordplay, not a derivation** (NET notes), and Garrett puts the mechanism well: the Egyptian princess, recognising the congruence between the Egyptian name and the Hebrew verb describing how she found the boy, adopted the Hebrew verb as an explanation — **without claiming it was the name's origin** (*A Commentary on Exodus*, Kregel, 170–71). `[revised: logos-research]` *High confidence.*

  **Which sharpens, and limits, the observation.** The Hebrew form is **active** ("one who draws out") while the gloss is **passive** ("I drew him out") — and "the drawn-out one becomes the drawer-out" is a genuine and preachable irony. **But it is an irony in the text's Hebrew gloss, not in the name's etymology**, and it should be preached as the narrator's wordplay rather than as what the name means. *Moderate on the homiletical turn; the lexical facts now certain.*

**(c) *gēr* — "sojourner" (v. 22, in *Gershom*)** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
The word for a resident alien with no kinship protection. Moses names his firstborn for his own displacement — and the Book of the Covenant will twice ground Israel's duty to the vulnerable on the same word: "you were **sojourners** in the land of Egypt" (22:21; 23:9). See Headline 5.

**(d) The four verbs of vv. 24–25** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
*wayyišmaʿ* (heard) · *wayyizkōr* (remembered) · *wayyarʾ* (saw) · *wayyēdaʿ* (knew). **The fourth has no direct object in the Hebrew** — the sentence is simply "and God knew". Most English versions supply something ("God knew"; KJV "had respect unto them"). *The elision is the striking feature and should be preached as such.* *Moderate–high on how much weight the elision bears; the grammatical fact is certain.*

**(e) *zākar* — "remember"** `[T]` — *High confidence.*
Not a mental event but a covenant-action word: when God "remembers", he acts on an obligation (cf. Gen 8:1, "**God remembered** Noah" — again the flood). **The chapter's two Genesis contacts, the *tēbâ* and *zākar*, are both flood-narrative words.** `[T]` *Moderate–high on the pairing as significant.*

**Name-hyperlinks.**

- **Midian.** A son of Abraham by Keturah (Gen 25:2), so the Midianites are relatives. Moses' refuge is with the extended Abrahamic family, and his father-in-law is a **priest** (v. 16) — of what, the text does not say. `[T]` for Gen 25:2; `[I]` for the significance. *Moderate.*
- **Reuel / Jethro.** Called Reuel here and Jethro at 3:1 and 18:1. Explanations vary (a personal name and a title; a clan name; two traditions). *Flag for the preacher — a hearer will notice. Routed to Open Questions.*
- **Levi (v. 1).** Both parents are Levites, and the text says so before naming either of them (they are Amram and Jochebed at 6:20). **The tribe that takes the sword at 32:26–29 and is ordained for it is the tribe the deliverer comes from.** `[T]` for the tribal notice; `[I]` for the link. *Moderate.*

### 8. Translations

**2:3, 5 — "basket" or "ark"?** ESV, NASB and NIV all read "basket"; **KJV reads "ark"** in both verses, using the same English word it uses for Noah. **The moderns agree, the rendering is exegetically weighty, and the ancient-versions check therefore fires** — *not run directly; routed to Open Questions.* `**A translation-driven loss:**` the modern renderings are more natural English, but they conceal that Exodus 2:3 uses **the same rare noun as Genesis 6:14** and no other passage in the Hebrew Bible. **The preacher must supply what the translation cannot: say the word is *ark*.** *High confidence — this is the most important translation note in the chapter.*

**2:2 — "a fine child" / "goodly" / "beautiful."** ESV "fine"; NASB "beautiful"; KJV "goodly"; NIV "fine". The Hebrew is simply *ṭôb*, "good" — the Genesis 1 refrain-word. Every English rendering makes an aesthetic judgement the Hebrew does not force. *Moderate; worth a sentence if the Genesis 1 resonance is used.*

**2:25 — "and God knew."** ESV "and God knew"; NASB "and God took notice of them"; KJV "and God had respect unto them"; NIV "and was concerned about them". **The Hebrew has no object**, and the versions divide between preserving the abruptness (ESV) and supplying a sense (the rest). *High confidence on the grammatical fact.* **Preach from a version that leaves it open**, or say so.

**2:14 — "a prince and a judge."** ESV "a prince and a judge"; NASB "a prince or a judge"; KJV as ESV. Acts 7:27 quotes it as "a ruler and a judge", following the LXX. No issue; note the NT wording if you quote both.

**Pulpit edition note:** Pulpit text matches report default.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Four registers, one per section, and the last is the shock.**

- **vv. 1–10 — quiet, domestic, and almost unbearably tense.** No dialogue for four verses; a mother hiding a baby, a sister standing "at a distance", a princess bathing. The narrator lets the reader wait. And then the small human detail that breaks it: "**behold, the baby was crying**" (v. 6). The scene turns on an infant's cry, and it is the same word-family that will reach God at v. 23.
- **vv. 11–15 — abrupt and furtive.** Short clauses, quick verbs: he looked, he saw, he struck, he hid. Then the exposure, the fear, the flight. **The prose moves at the speed of a man doing something he should not.**
- **vv. 16–22 — pastoral, generous, faintly comic.** A well, seven daughters, bullying shepherds, a stranger who intervenes; a father saying "Where is he? Why have you left the man? Call him, that he may eat bread." **The chapter's only warmth is in a foreign country.**
- **vv. 23–25 — flat, enormous.** Four verbs, no adjectives, no explanation. After twenty-two verses of human effort, the register changes entirely and the subject changes to God.

**Soundtrack.** A held breath; then something hurried and furtive; then, briefly, a folk tune at a well; and then, at v. 23, a single low sustained note that does not resolve.

**Note for the preacher.** The baby's cry (v. 6) and the people's cry (v. 23) are the chapter's two sounds. **A sermon that connects them has understood the chapter.**

### 10. Repetition

| Repeated | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **"see / look"** | vv. 2, 5, 6, 11 (×2), 12, 25 | Human seeing throughout, and then **God** sees (v. 25) |
| **water / river / well / draw** | vv. 3, 5, 10, 15, 16, 17, 19 | The chapter's dominant image, from the killing river to the well |
| **"cry"** | vv. 6 (the baby), 23 (the people) | Two cries; the second reaches God |
| **"his people / his brothers"** | vv. 11 (×2) | Moses' identification stated twice before he acts |
| **"went out"** | vv. 11, 13 | Two days, two interventions |
| **"Egyptian"** | vv. 11, 12, 14, 19 | Moses kills an Egyptian and is then *called* one |
| **"God" (*ʾĕlōhîm*)** | vv. 23, 24 (×2), 25 (×2) | Five times in three verses, after twenty-two verses of absence |
| **the four verbs** | vv. 24–25 | heard, remembered, saw, knew |

**Idea-level repetition: rescue by drawing out.** Moses is drawn out of the water (v. 10); he draws water for the daughters (v. 19); and the verb of his name will describe what he does to a nation. **The chapter says the same thing three ways: the deliverer is first the delivered.** `[T]`/`[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**The "Egyptian" irony.** Moses kills **an Egyptian** (v. 12) and is then identified by the Midianite women as **an Egyptian** (v. 19). `[T]` **He is a Hebrew to the Egyptians, an Egyptian to the Midianites, and a sojourner to himself (v. 22).** The man who will define a nation's identity has none.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Book-level source inventory.** Genesis is the live source; this chapter makes two contacts, and both are with the **flood narrative** — which is a finding in itself.

**Independent candidate-finding.** From the chapter's own vocabulary: *tēbâ* + pitch → Gen 6:14; *zākar* with God as subject → Gen 8:1; *kî-ṭôb* → Gen 1; the well-betrothal sequence → Gen 24; 29; Midian → Gen 25:2.

---

**Genesis 6:14 (with 6–9) → Exodus 2:3, 5** *(high confidence — a rare noun shared with only one other passage in the Hebrew Bible)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Genesis 6–9 is the flood: a world under judgement, one family preserved in a pitch-sealed vessel, and afterwards a covenant and a re-issued blessing (9:1, 7). The *tēbâ*'s function in Genesis is to be the sole means of survival through waters that destroy everything else — and God's remembering of Noah (8:1) is what turns the tide.

*Book usage.* Genesis is Exodus's live source throughout. **The specific flood-material is used only here** — but note that Exodus 1:7 already drew on Genesis 9:1, 7, the *post-flood* re-issue of the blessing. **So the book's first two chapters both reach for the flood narrative**, ch. 1 for its blessing and ch. 2 for its vessel. `[T]` *Moderate–high on the pairing.*

*OT-to-OT.* Genesis 6–9 and Genesis 1 are already in conversation within Genesis (the flood as un-creation and re-creation; 9:1, 7 as the re-issued 1:28). Exodus 1–2 activates both ends of that conversation in consecutive chapters.

*What it adds.* Three things. **The child is being carried through waters of judgement in the one vessel Scripture reserves for exactly that.** **A remnant is being preserved for a new beginning** — which is what the flood ark was for. And, with 2:24's *zākar* ("God remembered", the verb of Gen 8:1), **the chapter is bracketed by the two decisive moments of the flood story: the ark and the remembering.** *The preaching payoff: say the word "ark", and the congregation who know Genesis will do the rest.*

---

**Genesis 24 and 29 → Exodus 2:15–21** *(moderate–high confidence — type-scene, no verbal quotation)* `[T]` for the elements; `[S]` for the category

*Source context.* Genesis 24: Abraham's servant comes to a foreign land, stops at a well, meets Rebekah, water is drawn, hospitality is extended, a marriage follows. Genesis 29: Jacob arrives in Haran, comes to a well, meets Rachel, rolls away the stone and waters the flock, is welcomed into Laban's house, marries. **The pattern is stable enough across the Torah to be recognised as a convention.**

*Book usage.* This is Exodus's only betrothal scene.

*What it adds.* The variations carry the meaning. **Moses is not an emissary or an heir but a fugitive with blood on his hands**; he does the drawing himself; and he *rescues* the women before he waters the flock — the verb at v. 17 is a save-word. **The type-scene tells the reader that this displaced killer is nonetheless in the line of the patriarchs**, and that his instinct, even in exile and even for strangers, is deliverance. *Moderate–high; and see the Schnittjer Pass on type-scenes.*

---

**Genesis 1:31 → Exodus 2:2** *(uncertain — a common word in a suggestive position)* `[T]` for the wording

"She saw that he was **good** (*ṭôb*)" echoes Genesis 1's refrain. **But *ṭôb* is among the commonest adjectives in Hebrew and the construction is ordinary.** *Recorded and largely declined:* worth a sentence as resonance, not defensible as allusion. An honest negative.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew historical narrative, in three scenes with dialogue plus a summary conclusion. The birth-and-rescue account (vv. 1–10) belongs to a recognisable ancient category of **exposed-infant stories** about figures who rise to greatness (the Sargon birth legend is the standard comparison) — a comparison worth knowing and worth handling carefully (see Historical Background).

Reading rules: (i) it is presented as having happened, and the narrator's asides (vv. 2, 11, 14, 23–25) are authoritative in a way the characters' speech is not; (ii) the type-scene at the well is a convention, so **the variations are where the meaning is**; (iii) vv. 23–25 shift from scene to summary and from human to divine subject — the genre-change marks the turn.

**Posture note.** The chapter's effective agents are women who take risks, a foreign princess who takes pity, and God who remembers a covenant. **The one man who tries to fix things by his own hand makes it worse and leaves the country.** Any application that reverses that emphasis has inverted the chapter.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** The closing years of the sojourn foretold at Genesis 15:13, spanning roughly eighty years — Moses' birth, forty years in Egypt (Acts 7:23), forty in Midian (Acts 7:30) — and ending with a king's death and a cry.

**What has happened since that changes the reading.**

- **Exodus 3–4** answers 2:24–25 directly, and 3:7–8's four verbs are its counterpart.
- **Acts 7:23–29, 35** makes Moses' rejection the pattern for Israel's treatment of its deliverers, and applies it to Christ.
- **Hebrews 11:23–27** reads the whole sequence as faith, and reads Moses' choice as valuing "the reproach of Christ".
- **Matthew 2:13–15** repeats the shape: a decree against infants, a child preserved, an escape, and a return from Egypt.
- **1 Peter 3:20–21** reads Noah's ark — the *tēbâ* — as a figure of baptism, which sits interestingly alongside a second *tēbâ* carrying a child through water. *Suggestive; do not press.*

**Timeline errors to avoid.** Preaching Moses' Midian years as a template for "God's waiting room" (the text says nothing about his formation there); and reading Acts 7:25's sympathetic gloss as though Exodus had supplied it.

---

### 15. Who Am I?

- **The Levite mother and sister** — genuine examples, and Hebrews 11:23 says so. Faith under a lethal decree, expressed as a practical, dangerous act.
- **Pharaoh's daughter** — not an example but a demonstration: God's provision arrives through the household of the man trying to kill the child.
- **Moses** — **type of Christ by the NT's own reading, and not a model.** Stephen makes the rejection programmatic (Acts 7:35); Hebrews makes the choice exemplary (11:24–26). **The reader is not Moses.**
- **The Hebrew who says "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?"** — **an uncomfortable and important identification.** Acts 7:35 makes this rejection the type of the rejection of Christ. **A congregation should be invited to sit here**, not with the deliverer.
- **Israel groaning (v. 23)** — the readers' place at the end of the chapter: people whose situation has not changed and whose God has just been said to know.

**The misreading to flag.** "Be like Moses, willing to stand up for the oppressed" makes a model of the chapter's one failure. The text's own emphasis falls on his rejection and flight, and the NT's on his rejection as a pattern fulfilled in Christ.

---

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** For the first hearers: to know that the deliverer they follow was preserved by God's providence before he was anything else, that his first attempt failed, and that the exodus began not with a hero's plan but with God remembering a promise.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

*Worldview.* Three shifts:

1. **Providence works through the powerless and the unlikely.** A basket, a watching sister, an enemy's daughter, and a court paying a Hebrew mother to raise her own son. Not one of those was anyone's strategy.
2. **God's remembering is not the start of his caring but the start of his acting.** *Zākar* is a covenant word: he had not forgotten, and 2:24 names *why* he moves — "his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob".
3. **Deliverance attempted by our own hand, in secret, fails.** Moses looked this way and that; he did not look up. The chapter is not against courage but against self-appointment.

*Behaviour.* **Start** looking for God's work in what looks like coincidence and other people's kindness. **Start** letting Gershom's name shape an ethic — "I have been a sojourner" is the ground Exodus later gives for how the vulnerable must be treated (22:21; 23:9). **Stop** self-appointing to deliverances God has not commissioned; the same man does the same thing at 3:10 with a call, and it works.

*Motive.* Not "try harder in God's timing" but: the covenant God remembered is the covenant he keeps, and the deliverer who failed on his own is the one God sends back with a commission.

**Four audiences.**

- *For me:* Where am I looking this way and that instead of up — solving something by my own hand because I cannot bear to wait?
- *For a Christian friend:* For someone whose one big attempt to do good went badly and who thinks that disqualifies them — Moses' forty years in Midian are narrated in seven verses, and then God calls him.
- *For the church community:* The chapter's rescue is a chain of small, risky, unglamorous acts by people with no power. That is what most congregational faithfulness looks like.
- *For an unbeliever:* Here is a national hero introduced as a baby in a basket, a murderer, and a refugee — and the story insists the turning point was not anything he did.

**Prayer.** *You heard a baby crying in a basket and a people crying under their burdens, and you remembered what you had promised long before either. Where we have looked this way and that instead of up, forgive us. Where we are groaning and nothing has changed, be the God who hears, remembers, sees — and knows.*

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**

- **The elided object at v. 25.** "And God saw the people of Israel, and God **knew**" — *wayyēdaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*, with nothing following. English versions supply an object or a sense; the Hebrew stops. `[T]` **The abruptness is the effect**, and it is the last thing the chapter says. *High confidence on the grammar.*
- **The four *wayyiqtol* verbs of vv. 24–25.** Standard narrative-sequence forms, stacked without connectives beyond *waw*: heard, remembered, saw, knew. **The plainness is deliberate** after twenty-two verses of eventful prose.
- **The circumstantial clause at v. 12.** "and he saw that there was no man" — a subordinate observation on which the main verb depends. Grammatically, the killing is conditioned on the absence of witnesses. `[T]`
- **The infinitive of purpose at v. 4.** The sister stood "**to know** (*lĕdēʿâ*) what would be done to him" — **the same root as v. 25's "God knew".** *A quiet link: the sister watches to know; at the chapter's end, God knows.* `[T]` for the roots; `[I]` for the connection. *Moderate — attractive, and flagged as inference.*
- **The name-formula at vv. 10 and 22.** Both follow the Hebrew convention *she/he called his name X, for/because…* — the text supplying its own etymology, as it does with many Genesis names. Note that such glosses are **wordplays, not lexicography**, which matters for the Egyptian derivation of "Moses".

**Wordplay and sound-features.**

- ***mōšeh* / *māšâ*** (v. 10) — the name and the verb "draw out". The gloss is paronomastic; **whether it is also the name's actual origin is a separate question** (see Open Questions).
- **No claim is advanced on *Gēršōm*'s morphology** beyond the text's own gloss (*gēr* + a second element variously explained). *An explicit limit.*

**Recommended verification.** The LXX of 2:3, 5 (does it use *kibōtos*, the Noah's-ark word?) — **this is the key check, since it would show whether the Greek preserved the link**; the Egyptian etymology of Moses; the range of *ṣāʿaq* as a legal-plea term; and whether the Reuel/Jethro double-naming has a standard explanation. Routed to the Logos pass.

### Textual Variants

**No significant variants affect the preaching of this chapter.** One note: **v. 25's abrupt "and God knew"** has attracted variant renderings in the ancient versions (the LXX supplies a phrase along the lines of "and God was made known to them"), which is a translational rather than a textual matter but has the same practical effect — the versions were uncomfortable with the ellipsis too. *Moderate confidence; routed to the Logos pass.* `[S]`

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Egypt (the delta) and Midian (north-west Arabia / the eastern side of the Gulf of Aqaba, broadly). `[T]` for the text's geography; precise locations are `[S]`.

**What the original audience knew.**

- **Papyrus vessels on the Nile.** Light boats of bundled reeds sealed with bitumen were ordinary Nile craft. **The mother did not improvise something bizarre; she made a small version of a familiar thing.** `[S]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Royal women bathing at the river.** Not implausible for the period; the Nile's associations were both practical and religious. *Moderate.*
- **Wet-nursing contracts.** Paid wet-nursing was a recognised arrangement with documented contracts in the ancient Near East, which makes v. 9's "I will give you your wages" a piece of ordinary legal life — **and the irony sharper: the crown pays a Hebrew mother to raise the child it decreed should die.** `[S]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Exposed-infant stories.** The Sargon of Akkad birth legend — a child placed in a reed basket sealed with bitumen, set on a river, drawn out and raised to greatness — is the standard comparison, and it is close. **Handle with care in both directions:** it shows the narrative uses a recognisable form, and it does not follow that the Exodus account is derivative. `[S]` *Moderate — and see Preaching Pitfalls.*
- **Midian.** A trading and pastoral people descended from Abraham through Keturah (Gen 25:2). **Moses' refuge is among relatives**, and his father-in-law is called a **priest** — of what god, the text does not say, which is itself notable given 18:10–12.
- **Shepherds bullying women at a well.** Water rights were a standard flashpoint; the scene is ordinary rural conflict, not melodrama.

**What this changes about how we read.** Two things. First, **almost every element of the rescue is mundane** — a reed boat, a bathing princess, a nursing contract — which is the point: providence is not narrated as miracle here. Second, the exposed-infant convention means the first hearers would have recognised the *shape* of the story as a story about someone destined for greatness, and would have been listening for how this one differed.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Torah — compositional and canonical audiences coincide: **wilderness Israel**, hearing the origin story of the man standing in front of them. This chapter is a biography of their own leader, and not a flattering one.

**The first hearers.**

- *Who they were:* the people Moses led, who had themselves said things like "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (cf. 5:21; 14:11; 16:3; 17:3; Num 16:13).
- *Their situation:* out of Egypt, in the wilderness, repeatedly disputing Moses' authority.
- *What they brought:* Genesis, including the flood and the covenant with Abraham; and their own recent experience of crying out under burdens.

**Where the passage fits their world.** It tells them **where their leader came from and how badly his first attempt went** — which is a strange thing for a national epic to include, and which quietly answers the challenge they keep making. **v. 14's "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" is a question the wilderness generation had asked in their own words**, and the book puts it in the mouth of a man in the wrong.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** the murder, narrated without comment; and the detail that Moses checked no one was watching.
- **Surprising:** that Pharaoh's own daughter rescues and adopts him, and that the court funds his Hebrew upbringing.
- **Comforting:** vv. 24–25 — and the fact that Moses' failure did not disqualify him.
- **Disturbing:** the eighty years of nothing between v. 22 and v. 23; and the rejection by his own people, which the hearers would recognise as their own habit.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A hero-biography expectation that makes 2:11–15 read as backstory rather than disqualification; a modern interest in Moses' interior life, which the text refuses to supply; and the Acts 7 and Hebrews 11 glosses, which are true but which the first hearers did not have — **so the sermon should let the Exodus text speak before importing them.**

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. Preservation through the waters.**

- *Where this passage sits:* the second and last *tēbâ* in Scripture.
- *What comes before:* Gen 6–9 — one family carried through judgement, and God remembering (8:1).
- *What comes after:* the Sea (14:21–29), where the same waters kill the oppressor and save the people; the *tēbâ* read as baptism (1 Pet 3:20–21); and Matthew 2:13–15, where a child is again preserved from a decree and comes out of Egypt.
- *Contribution:* it establishes that God's method is to bring his people *through* deadly water rather than around it — which is the shape of the exodus and of baptism.
- *Confidence: high on the *tēbâ* link; moderate on pressing the baptismal line.*

**2. God remembers his covenant.**

- *Where this passage sits:* 2:24 — the first time in Exodus that the covenant with the patriarchs is **explicitly named as the reason God acts**.
- *What comes before:* Gen 15:13–14 (activated at Exod 1:11–12); Gen 8:1's remembering of Noah.
- *What comes after:* 3:6, 15–17; 6:3–8; 12:40–41 ("on that very day"); 32:13, where **Moses throws 2:24 back at God**; and the canon's long habit of grounding hope in what God has sworn (Luke 1:72, "to remember his holy covenant").
- *Contribution:* it names the engine of the entire book. **Everything from ch. 3 onward happens because of v. 24.**
- *Confidence: high.*

**3. The rejected deliverer.**

- *Where this passage sits:* v. 14 — the first rejection, and the pattern's origin.
- *What comes before:* Joseph rejected by his brothers and made their saviour (Gen 37; 45:4–8) — the same shape, one book earlier.
- *What comes after:* the wilderness disputes (5:21; 14:11; 16:2; 17:3; Num 14; 16); **Acts 7:35's "This Moses whom they refused… the same did God send to be a ruler and a deliverer"**, applied by Stephen to Christ; and John 1:11.
- *Contribution:* it gives the canon its Torah instance of the deliverer disowned by those he comes to save, and the NT names it as such.
- *Confidence: high — the NT does the work.*

**Confluence — (b) mutual illumination.** The preservation theme explains *how* there is a deliverer at all; the covenant theme explains *why* God moves; and the rejection theme explains *what kind* of deliverer he will be. **The middle one is causally prior: without v. 24 the other two are just biography.**

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton.** Exodus · first division, "from Egypt" (1:1–15:21) · leading idea: the progressive proximity of God — here at its second-furthest point, with God silent for twenty-two verses and then named five times in three.

**Findings.**

1. **Type-scene (step 3): the betrothal at the well.** The convention — foreign land, well, young women, water drawn, hospitality, marriage — is shared with Gen 24 and Gen 29, and **the variations carry the meaning**: Moses is a fugitive rather than an emissary, he draws the water himself, and he *rescues* before he waters. `[T]` for the elements; `[S]` for the category. *Moderate–high — this is the pass's clearest fire in the chapter.*
2. **Narrative selectivity and time (step 1).** Eighty years pass in this chapter. Moses' birth and rescue get ten verses; his failure five; forty years in Midian get seven; the rest is a phrase ("during those many days"). **The narrator brakes for the basket and for the killing, and accelerates through everything else** — including the entire formative period a modern biography would dwell on. The proportions say the chapter is about preservation and disqualification, not development. `[T]` *High.*
3. **Narrative space (step 2): river, sand, well.** The chapter moves from water that kills (the Nile), through **sand** (v. 12, where the body is hidden — the place things are buried and found), to water that gives life and a home (the Midianite well). **The spatial arc is the chapter's argument in geography.** `[I]` *Moderate.*
4. **Repetition and linkage (step 4).** The seeing-thread (vv. 2, 5, 6, 11, 12 → 25); the crying-thread (v. 6 → v. 23); the water-thread throughout. `[T]` *High.*
5. **First vs second reading (step 5).** On a first reading, v. 14's "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" is an insult. On the re-read — knowing 3:10, and knowing Acts 7:35 — **it is the exact question God is about to answer.** Also re-read: the *tēbâ* at v. 3, once Genesis is in view; and the Levite parentage of v. 1, once ch. 32 is. `[T]`/`[I]` *High.*
6. **Polyacoustic intertextuality (step 6).** Genesis 6–9 (the ark and the remembering), Genesis 24 and 29 (the well), Genesis 25:2 (Midian) and Genesis 37/45 (the rejected saviour) all sound here; forward, Acts 7 and Hebrews 11 read the chapter explicitly, and Matthew 2 repeats its shape. **Heard together: a preserved-and-rejected deliverer, carried through water, in a story God turns by remembering.** *Moderate–high.*
7. **Irreducibility.** Do not reduce this to "God prepares his servants" (the text says nothing about preparation) or to "stand up for justice" (the one man who does is rejected and exiled). What is told is that a child was carried through a killing river in an ark, that his own attempt at rescue failed, and that the story turned when God remembered a promise.

**Echoes / signals.** Leading words: *tēbâ*, *zākar*, the seeing-verbs. Structural devices: human sequence answered by divine (vv. 1–22 → 23–25); the betrothal type-scene; the two name-etymologies. Intertextual links: Gen 6:14 with 8:1; Gen 24; 29 (cross-reported to Tool 11).

**Nil returns.** *Chiasm* — none proposed. *Leading word across the whole unit* — no single *Leitwort* governs all four sections; the water-image is thematic rather than lexical, and the report says so. *Symbolic numbers* — "three months" (v. 2) and "seven daughters" (v. 16) are the only candidates and neither is evidently symbolic; **do not preach them as such.**

### Christological Reading

**Type(s) of connection: trajectory, contrast, and one NT-authorised typology. The NT does most of the work here — follow it rather than improvising.**

- **The rejected deliverer → Christ (typology, NT-authorised).** "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (v. 14) is quoted at **Acts 7:27**, and Stephen draws the conclusion at **7:35**: "**This Moses whom they refused**, saying, Who made thee a ruler and a judge? the same did God send to be a ruler and a deliverer." Stephen's whole speech then turns this into the pattern his hearers have repeated with "the Righteous One" (7:52). *Tests:* category (deliverer/mediator) — pass; NT precedent — **explicit, and the connection is Stephen's own**; escalation — pass; authorial pattern — the Exodus text stages the rejection and the later commissioning. **This is the chapter's principal Christological connection and it is handed to us.** *High confidence.*
- **Preservation from a decree against infants → Matthew 2:13–15 (pattern).** A king kills male children; one child is preserved; the child is in Egypt and is called out of it (Matt 2:15, quoting Hos 11:1, which is reading the exodus). *Category and escalation pass; NT precedent is a narrative frame rather than a citation of Exodus 2.* *Moderate–high — the same verdict the ch. 1 dig reached.*
- **The *tēbâ* → salvation through water (trajectory).** One vessel-word, two rescues, and 1 Peter 3:20–21 reads the first as a figure of baptism. **The line from Exodus 2:3 to baptism runs through Genesis 6, not directly** — say so if you use it. *Moderate; do not press.*
- **Moses' choice → the reproach of Christ (Hebrews' own move).** Hebrews 11:26 says Moses esteemed "the reproach of **Christ** greater riches than the treasures in Egypt". **The NT has already made this passage Christological in a way the preacher need not invent.** *High confidence that Hebrews says it; how to explain it is a further question.*
- **A connection to decline.** Moses as a deliverer-figure drawn out of water and *therefore* a baptism-type in his own person. **The *tēbâ* carries the water-typology; Moses' name does not.** *Recorded and declined.*

**Moralism check.**

- *The "be like X" temptations:* "be brave like Moses' mother"; "have Moses' passion for justice"; "wait for God's timing like Moses in Midian". The second is the standard sermon on 2:11–15 and it makes a virtue of the chapter's failure.
- *The gospel grounding:* the chapter's agents are a mother, a sister, a foreign princess and a remembering God. **Moses' one initiative is the thing that goes wrong.** And the NT reads his rejection, not his zeal, as the point.
- *Christ as hero, not example:* Acts 7:35 supplies the move — the rejected one is the one God sends. **Preach the pattern the NT names**, and Moses stays a signpost rather than a model.

*Confidence: high (Acts 7's rejection typology; Hebrews 11's reading); moderate–high (the Matthew 2 pattern); moderate (the *tēbâ* baptismal trajectory); declined (Moses himself as a water-type).*

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**2:11–14 — Moses kills the Egyptian.**
- *Category:* ethical — the chapter's main difficulty.
- *The difficulty:* the deliverer commits what looks like murder, hides the body, and Scripture never explicitly condemns it.
- *Rhetorical function in context:* the narrator supplies two damning circumstantial details — he **looked this way and that**, and he **hid him in the sand** — and then reports his fear of exposure rather than any remorse. **The framing is not approving.**
- *The main options:* (i) a sinful act of self-appointed vengeance, from which God nonetheless brings good; (ii) a rash but well-intentioned act of solidarity, which Acts 7:25 partially explains ("he supposed his brethren would have understood how that God by his hand would deliver them"); (iii) a legitimate defence of the oppressed, botched in method and timing.
- *What an honest exegesis surfaces:* **Exodus narrates it without a verdict; Acts supplies a motive; Hebrews supplies a frame of faith. None of the three calls it right.** The safest reading is that the act was wrong in method and the impulse real — and that the chapter's interest is in its *failure*, not its ethics. *Moderate–high.*
- *Handling:* do not exonerate and do not moralise. Name that Scripture is restrained here, and move to what the text emphasises: the rejection.

**2:15 vs Hebrews 11:27 — did Moses fear or not?**
- *Category:* apologetic (apparent contradiction).
- *The difficulty:* Exodus says "Moses was afraid" (v. 14) and "fled from Pharaoh" (v. 15); Hebrews 11:27 says "By faith he forsook Egypt, **not fearing the wrath of the king**."
- *Honest exegesis:* many take Hebrews 11:27 to refer to the **later** departure at the exodus rather than this flight, which resolves it cleanly; others read Hebrews as characterising the whole movement of Moses' life by faith rather than by the fear of a particular moment. **Both are defensible.**
- *Handling:* one sentence, honestly. A hearer with a study Bible will have seen the note.

**2:19 — "An Egyptian delivered us."**
- *Category:* minor, and interesting.
- Moses is identified by his clothing and speech as an Egyptian, four verses after killing one. Not a difficulty so much as a datum about his divided identity — and it sits with v. 22's "sojourner in a foreign land".

**2:22 / 18:3–4 — how many sons?**
- Exodus 2:22 names one (Gershom); 18:3–4 names two (Gershom and Eliezer); Acts 7:29 says "two sons". No contradiction — the second is simply born later — but a hearer may notice. *One sentence if raised.*

**2:25 — "and God knew."**
- *Category:* interpretive.
- The Hebrew has no object. **Do not smooth it.** Whatever the versions supply, the sentence's abruptness is the last thing the chapter says and it is doing work.

**The Sargon parallel.**
- *Category:* apologetic.
- The Akkadian birth legend of Sargon — infant in a reed basket sealed with bitumen, set on a river, drawn out — is close enough that a sceptical hearer may raise it as evidence of borrowing.
- *Handling:* acknowledge the similarity of form; note that shared narrative conventions do not establish literary dependence in either direction, and that the differences (the mother's identity, the sister, the adoptive household, the nursing) are substantial. **Do not overclaim in either direction, and do not be caught unaware.** `[S]` *Moderate.*

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: "Be like Moses, standing up for the oppressed"

- **What it looks like:** 2:11–15 as a call to courageous action for justice, with Moses as the model.
- **Why it's wrong:** it makes a model of the chapter's one failure. The narrator supplies two condemning details — he looked to see that no one was watching, and he hid the body — and reports his fear of exposure rather than remorse. **Neither Exodus nor Acts nor Hebrews calls the method right.**
- **The corrective:** preach the rejection, which is what the NT does (Acts 7:35). The impulse was real; the self-appointment was the problem, and 3:10 is where it is fixed.

### Pitfall 2: The Midian years as "God's waiting room"

- **What it looks like:** forty years of hidden preparation, God shaping Moses in obscurity, a sermon about seasons of waiting.
- **Why it's wrong:** **the text says nothing whatever about Moses' formation in Midian.** It gives seven verses to a well, a marriage and a son, and then moves to God. The preparation theme is imported.
- **The corrective:** if you want to preach waiting, preach v. 23 — "during those many days" — where the waiting is Israel's and the text does dwell on it.

### Pitfall 3: Reading the chapter as Moses' biography

- **What it looks like:** a character study, with the basket as an origin story and the killing as a flaw to be redeemed.
- **Why it's wrong:** the chapter's proportions refuse it — eighty years in twenty-five verses, and the last three about God. **Moses is off the page when the story turns.**
- **The corrective:** let the chapter end where it ends. The subject of vv. 23–25 is God, and that is the chapter's point of arrival.

### Pitfall 4: Sentimentalising the basket

- **What it looks like:** a nursery-friendly retelling — the sweet baby, the kind princess, the clever sister.
- **Why it's wrong:** the scene is a mother's last resort under a decree of infanticide, and the vessel is named with the word for the ark that carried the only survivors of the flood. **It is a scene about a child being put into the river that was killing children.**
- **The corrective:** say the word "ark". The congregation who know Genesis will feel the weight immediately.

### Pitfall 5: Preaching "God knew" as warm reassurance

- **What it looks like:** v. 25 as a comforting close — God saw them, God cared.
- **Why it's wrong:** not false, but it smooths what the Hebrew leaves rough: **the sentence has no object.** And v. 24 names *why* God moves, and it is not their suffering as such but "his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob".
- **The corrective:** preach the abruptness and preach the covenant. The ground of hope here is a promise, not a feeling.

### Pitfall 6: Importing Acts 7 and Hebrews 11 too early

- **What it looks like:** reading v. 12 through Acts 7:25's "he supposed his brethren would have understood", or v. 15 through Hebrews 11:27's "not fearing the wrath of the king", before the Exodus text has been heard.
- **Why it's wrong:** both are true and both should be used — but Exodus narrates a furtive killing and a frightened flight, and the congregation should feel that before the NT's frame arrives. **The NT interprets; it does not overwrite.**
- **The corrective:** let Exodus speak, then bring Acts and Hebrews in as the apostolic reading.
