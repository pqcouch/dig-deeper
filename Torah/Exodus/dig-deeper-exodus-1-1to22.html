Dig Deeper: Exodus 1:1–22

Translation: ESV (unless specified). Passage text verified in full against a public-domain text (KJV) before the tools were worked, together with the Genesis parallels. Pulpit translation: ESV (Anglicised) — same text as the report default Date: 5 August 2026 Book-overview context: In-conversation — book-overview-exodus.md (v.1.0.0, finalised) front-loaded at Phase 0.5; the whole-book sweep (unit 1) and the ten completed ⭐ HIGH digs consulted at the checking stage only Series context: Exodus (series). Unit 1 of fifteen — the book’s opening unit. Standard weight in the overview. Note its special status in the toolkit: the opening unit is a permanent neighbour of every other passage in the book, because it is where the differentiated parties are named and the quiet anticipations planted. Every previous dig in this series has had to reconcile against it from memory; this report supplies the thing they were reconciling against.

The Passage

Twenty-two verses, quoted in full.

1 These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob, each with his household: 2 Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah, 3 Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin, 4 Dan and Naphtali, Gad and Asher. 5 All the descendants of Jacob were seventy persons; Joseph was already in Egypt. 6 Then Joseph died, and all his brothers and all that generation. 7 But the people of Israel were fruitful and increased greatly; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong, so that the land was filled with them.

8 Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph. 9 And he said to his people, “Behold, the people of Israel are too many and too mighty for us. 10 Come, let us deal shrewdly with them, lest they multiply, and, if war breaks out, they join our enemies and fight against us and escape from the land.” 11 Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with heavy burdens. They built for Pharaoh store cities, Pithom and Raamses. 12 But the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied and the more they spread abroad. And the Egyptians were in dread of the people of Israel. 13 So they ruthlessly made the people of Israel work as slaves 14 and made their lives bitter with hard service, in mortar and brick, and in all kinds of work in the field. In all their work they ruthlessly made them work as slaves.

15 Then the king of Egypt said to the Hebrew midwives, one of whom was named Shiphrah and the other Puah, 16 “When you serve as midwife to the Hebrew women and see them on the birthstool, if it is a son, you shall kill him, but if it is a daughter, she shall live.” 17 But the midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but let the male children live. 18 So the king of Egypt called the midwives and said to them, “Why have you done this, and let the male children live?” 19 The midwives said to Pharaoh, “Because the Hebrew women are not like the Egyptian women, for they are vigorous and give birth before the midwife comes to them.” 20 So God dealt well with the midwives. And the people multiplied and grew very strong. 21 And because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.

22 Then Pharaoh commanded all his people, “Every son that is born to the Hebrews you shall cast into the Nile, but you shall let every daughter live.”

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple converging tools:

  1. Verse 7 stacks the creation blessing onto a family: four verbs of increase in close succession — fruitful, swarmed, multiplied, grew mighty — plus “exceedingly” and the clause “and the land was filled with them”, the words of Genesis 1:28 and 9:1, 7. [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Quotation/Allusion (Move 1–2), Author’s Purpose, Schnittjer Pass. The book opens by narrating Israel as the creation-blessing coming true. Everything Pharaoh subsequently does is an attempt to reverse Genesis 1, and the reader is told in the first paragraph that he will fail. High confidence — and commentary-confirmed: the four-verb succession is “a pattern unique to this verse”, all of it mirrors Gen 1:28 and 9:1–2, and “Israel fulfils in microcosm what God intended for the whole of creation” (T. D. Alexander, Exodus, Apollos OTC, 2:42–43). [revised: logos-research] A gain from the checking stage: the same vocabulary also carries the patriarchal promises of numerous descendants — so v. 7 is simultaneously creation-mandate and Abrahamic-promise language, which is why the chapter can serve both Genesis 1 and Genesis 15 at once.

  2. The chapter runs on a single irony, stated flatly at v. 12: “the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied and the more they spread abroad.” [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Linking Words, Narrator’s Comment, Tone, Repetition. Pharaoh proposes to “deal shrewdly” (v. 10) and every measure produces the opposite of its intention. The oppression is the means of the blessing’s advance, and the narrator says so without comment. High confidence.

  3. Two Hebrew midwives are named; the king of the greatest empire on earth is not. Shiphrah and Puah are given names, speech, motive (“they feared God”) and a divine reward; Pharaoh is “a new king”, “the king of Egypt”, “Pharaoh” — a title, never a name. [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary (proper-noun inventory), Narrator’s Comment, Structure, Who Am I?, Original Audience. The narrator’s allocation of names is a verdict on who matters. High confidence.

  4. The fear of God is introduced into Exodus as civil disobedience. “The midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them” (v. 17), repeated as the ground of their reward (v. 21). [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Linking Words, Copycat, So What?. The book’s first instance of anyone fearing God is two foreign-titled women refusing an order to kill, and the narrative rewards them explicitly. High confidence.

  5. The chapter’s escalation ends in the Nile — and the Nile is where the book’s judgements begin and end. Pharaoh’s policy escalates from forced labour (11–14) to covert infanticide (15–16) to a public decree of drowning (22). The river he makes a grave for Hebrew sons becomes blood at 7:20 and the grave of his own army at 14:28. [T] for the texts; [I] for the design. Surfaced by: Structure, Internal echo, Biblical Theology, Schnittjer Pass. High confidence — and the strongest single line from this chapter to the rest of the book.

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. Exodus opens with a conjunction: wĕʾēlleh — “And these are the names”. The book begins mid-sentence, grammatically continuing Genesis. The specific preceding material is threefold: Genesis 46:8–27 (the list of those who came down to Egypt, seventy persons), Genesis 50 (Joseph’s death and his oath about his bones), and — decisively — Genesis 15:13–14: “Know for certain that your offspring will be sojourners in a land that is not theirs and will be servants there, and they will be afflicted for four hundred years. But I will bring judgment on the nation that they serve, and afterward they shall come out with great possessions.”

Necessity answer. Why does the book open here, and this way? Because this chapter is the announced fulfilment of Genesis 15:13. The oppression is not an accident that interrupts the promise; it is a stage of the promise that God specified to Abram centuries before. [T] — the word “afflict” (ʿānâ) at Exod 1:11–12 is the verb of Gen 15:13. The reader who has come through Genesis meets Exodus 1 not as a disaster but as a clock running.

And the chapter must open with names and numbers (vv. 1–7) before it opens with oppression (vv. 8–14), because the whole of what follows depends on the reader knowing that the seventy have become a nation before Pharaoh notices. The blessing is established as a fact of the narrative before it becomes a threat to the empire.

Implication for this passage. Three things follow. First, the chapter is not “the situation Exodus needs to fix”; it is the covenant on schedule, in its dark phase. Second, the narrator’s refusal to editorialise — no lament, no theodicy, no “but God” until 2:23–25 — is deliberate: the reader is meant to feel the silence. Third, everything Pharaoh does is set up to fail before he acts, because v. 7 has already happened.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 sharpens it: Genesis is not merely alluded to here but re-cited (1:1–5 ≈ Gen 46:8–27) and stacked (1:7 draws on both Gen 1:28 and Gen 9:1, 7). The opening is a deliberate hinge, and its density of Genesis material is the highest anywhere in Exodus.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. The overview’s presenting situation and pressure are constructed by this chapter: a family of seventy under a death sentence, and a God who has sworn.

Passage-level. Three things this unit is doing:

  • Establishing the promise as fact before establishing the threat. v. 7 precedes v. 8, and the order is the argument.
  • Showing that the opposition is doomed by its own methods. v. 12’s flat statement is the chapter’s thesis; Pharaoh’s shrewdness is narrated as futility.
  • Introducing the fear of God as the only force in the chapter that stops the killing — and locating it in two women with no power.

A fourth belongs to the narrator: withholding God. God is not named as an actor until v. 17 (“the midwives feared God”), and does not act until v. 20. In a chapter about a covenant people being crushed, the divine name is almost absent — which is what makes 2:24–25 land.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

See Phase 0.6. Short form: the chapter is the fulfilment of Genesis 15:13, and it opens with a conjunction that makes Exodus grammatically continuous with Genesis.

General contextual notes

Immediately before (Genesis 50:22–26). Joseph dies at 110, and makes the sons of Israel swear: “God will surely visit you, and you shall carry up my bones from here.” Exodus 1:6 (“Then Joseph died”) picks up that thread — and the oath is discharged at Exod 13:19, when Moses takes the bones out. The book’s first and near-last narrative acts are tied by a Genesis promise.

Immediately after (2:1–10). “Now a man from the house of Levi went and took as his wife a Levite woman.” The decree of 1:22 is what makes ch. 2’s basket necessary — and it is women again who defeat it.

Section. First movement (1:1–15:21, “from Egypt”). Within it, ch. 1 is the setting of the problem and chs. 2–4 the raising of the deliverer.

Commonly read out of context. 1:17 and 1:21 are lifted as a proof-text in debates about civil disobedience and about whether lying can be right (the midwives’ answer at v. 19). Both discussions are legitimate; both usually proceed without noticing that the text’s stated ground for the reward is the fear of God (v. 21), not the deception.

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
1:1–5 The names Re-cites Gen 46:8–27; seventy persons; the link to Genesis made explicit
1:6–7 The generation dies; the blessing explodes Five verbs of increase; the hinge from family to nation
1:8–10 A king who did not know Joseph The threat perceived; “let us deal shrewdly”
1:11–14 Policy one: forced labour Taskmasters, store cities, bitterness — and v. 12’s reversal
1:15–21 Policy two: covert infanticide The midwives named; the fear of God; the interrogation; the reward
1:22 Policy three: public drowning The decree that sets up ch. 2

Devices.

  • A three-stage escalation, each stage more desperate and less private than the last. Labour → secret killing by two women → open killing by “all his people”. [T] The escalation is the structure. High confidence.
  • An inclusio of increase around the oppression. v. 7 (“multiplied and grew exceedingly strong”) and v. 20 (“the people multiplied and grew very strong”). The same two verbs bracket everything Pharaoh does — so his entire programme is framed by the thing it was designed to prevent. [T] High confidence — and easy to miss.
  • The sons/daughters reversal. Pharaoh twice orders sons killed and daughters spared (vv. 16, 22). In the next chapter it is daughters — the midwives, Moses’ mother, his sister, Pharaoh’s own daughter — who save the son. The policy’s one concession is the instrument of its defeat. [T] for both texts; [I] for the irony as design. Moderate–high.
  • The narrator’s silence about God until v. 17. Structural by omission (see Tool 6).

4. Linking Words

1:1 — “And (wĕ-) these are the names.” The book’s first word is a conjunction. Exodus does not begin; it continues. [T]

1:7 — “But the people of Israel were fruitful.” (ESV’s “but” renders a simple wĕ-; the contrast with v. 6’s deaths is contextual rather than lexical, though real.) Death of a generation, and then explosion.

1:10 — “Come, let us deal shrewdly with them, lest they multiply.” A negative purpose clause that the narrative immediately falsifies. Pharaoh’s stated fear (“lest they multiply”) is precisely what his policy produces.

1:11 — “Therefore they set taskmasters over them.” →therefore→ The policy follows from the fear.

1:12 — “But the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied.” The chapter’s hinge, and the book’s first great reversal. The Hebrew construction is a proportional comparative — as they afflicted, so they multiplied. [T] High confidence.

1:17 — “But the midwives feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded.” The adversative that turns the chapter. Two allegiances named in one sentence, and one of them loses.

1:21 — “Because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.” ←because← The stated ground of the reward. Note carefully what it is not: the text does not say “because the midwives deceived Pharaoh”. See Difficult Verses.

5. Parallels

Not poetry — N/A at verse level. Two structural parallels carry weight.

The five verbs of v. 7 stand in a cumulative series rather than a parallelism, but the effect is the same: each adds to the last, and the sentence ends with the whole land full. It is the nearest thing to a hymn in the chapter, and it is about population.

vv. 16 and 22 in parallel. “If it is a son, you shall kill him, but if it is a daughter, she shall live” ↔︎ “Every son… you shall cast into the Nile, but you shall let every daughter live.” The same antithesis, escalated from a private instruction to a public decree — and given to a different agent (two midwives → all his people).

6. Narrator’s Comment

The narrator’s most eloquent move in this chapter is what he withholds.

  • God is not mentioned until v. 17, and then only as the object of the midwives’ fear. He does not act until v. 20. In twenty-two verses about the crushing of the covenant people, God is the subject of exactly two clauses (vv. 20, 21). [T] High confidence. The silence is not an oversight; it is the condition the reader is meant to feel, and it is broken at 2:24–25 (“God heard… God remembered… God saw… God knew”).
  • 1:12b — “And the Egyptians were in dread of the people of Israel.” An interior report about the Egyptians’ state of mind, which the reader could not otherwise have. The oppressors are afraid.
  • 1:17 — “But the midwives feared God.” The narrator supplies the motive. Their speech at v. 19 does not; without v. 17 the reader could not tell whether they were pious or merely evasive.
  • 1:20–21 — “So God dealt well with the midwives… because the midwives feared God, he gave them families.” The narrator’s verdict, twice, with the ground stated. This is the chapter’s only evaluative comment, and it is entirely positive about two women who disobeyed a king.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. Israel/Jacob; the eleven sons named (Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Benjamin, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher) and Joseph; Egypt; Pithom and Raamses; Shiphrah and Puah; the Nile; Pharaoh, “the king of Egypt”. Triage: the tribal names are the Genesis-recitation and are handled under Tool 11. Pithom and Raamses are the chapter’s historical anchor. Shiphrah and Puah carry the chapter’s argument. Pharaoh is significant by absence of a name.

The words that carry the chapter.

(a) The verbs of v. 7 [T] — High confidence. Four verbs of increase in succession — pārû (were fruitful) · wayyišrĕṣû (swarmed, teemed) · wayyirbû (multiplied) · wayyaʿaṣmû (grew mighty) — reinforced by bimʾōd mĕʾōd (“exceedingly”) and closed by wattimmālēʾ hāʾāreṣ ʾōtām (“and the land was filled with them”). The first, third and the filling-clause are Genesis 1:28’s creation mandate; šāraṣ is the word used of the swarming water-creatures in Genesis 1:20–21 and of Noah’s descendants in 9:7. Alexander notes the four-verb succession is a pattern unique to this verse in the Hebrew Bible. [revised: logos-research] The stacking is deliberate excess, and it doubles as patriarchal-promise vocabulary. See Tool 11.

(b) ʿānâ — “afflict, oppress” (vv. 11, 12) [T] — High confidence. The verb of Genesis 15:13 (“they will be afflicted four hundred years”). Its appearance here is the prophecy clocking on. It recurs at 3:7 (“I have seen the affliction of my people”) and 3:17.

(c) yārēʾ ʾĕlōhîm — “feared God” (vv. 17, 21) [T] — High confidence. The first occurrence of God-fearing in Exodus, and it belongs to the midwives. The phrase describes a settled orientation that overrides a royal command; the same root will run to 20:20’s two fears (“do not fear… that the fear of God may be before you”).

(d) ḥākam — “deal shrewdly / wisely” (v. 10) [T] — High confidence on the root; moderate on pressing the irony. Pharaoh proposes wisdom. The narrative treats his wisdom as the engine of his defeat, and the true wisdom in the chapter belongs to two midwives who fear God — which is the Old Testament’s own definition of where wisdom starts (Prov 1:7; 9:10). The link is thematic rather than verbal, and should be preached as illumination rather than as a claim about vocabulary.

(e) bāttîm — “houses / families” (v. 21) [T] — Moderate–high. “He gave them houses” — i.e. households, families of their own. The women who protected Israelite households are given households. English versions rightly render “families”, which is the sense, but the wordplay with the bêt the midwives were serving in is audible in Hebrew and worth a sentence.

Name-hyperlinks.

  • Shiphrah and Puah. The names are Semitic rather than Egyptian, and plausibly mean something like “beauty/fairness” (šiprâ) and “splendour” or “girl” (pûʿâ) — but the etymologies are not load-bearing and are contested; what matters is that they have names. Moderate; do not build on the meanings.

    Their nationality is genuinely open — checked. The Hebrew permits both “Hebrew midwives” and “midwives of the Hebrews”, and the etymology of the names settles nothing (Faro & Dalrymple, Redeeming Eden, 75). The Septuagint and other ancient versions treat them as Egyptians (Junior, “Exodus,” Women’s Bible Commentary, 58). Arguments for Hebrew identity: the Semitic name-roots, and the logic that Pharaoh would use women with access to Hebrew births. Arguments for Egyptian: that Pharaoh would hardly trust the mission to Hebrew women, and that they seem familiar with births in both communities. [revised: logos-research] The sermon changes materially depending on the answer — if Egyptian, the chapter’s only faithful actors are foreigners who fear Israel’s God — so preach the uncertainty rather than picking a side.

  • Pithom and Raamses (v. 11). Store cities in the eastern Nile delta. The name Raamses is the chapter’s one hard chronological datum and its most contested — see Historical Background. [T] for the naming.

  • Pharaoh, unnamed. Pattern-break absence, and it clears the hard gate: the chapter names eleven sons of Israel, two midwives, two cities and a river. In a paragraph that hands out names freely, the king of Egypt does not get one — and the pattern is established by the text itself, not guessed. Weighting: moderate, per the absence-argument ceiling. Cross-reported to Tool 11.

8. Translations

1:7 — the five verbs. ESV: “were fruitful and increased greatly; they multiplied and grew exceedingly strong”. NASB: “were fruitful and increased greatly, and multiplied, and became exceedingly mighty”. KJV: “were fruitful, and increased abundantly, and multiplied, and waxed exceeding mighty”. All three preserve the pile-up; NIV compresses (“were exceedingly fruitful; they multiplied greatly, increased in numbers and became so numerous”), losing the šāraṣ (“swarmed”) flavour that ties the verse to Genesis 1:20–21 and 9:7. Use a literal version for this verse, or the Genesis link goes quiet. High confidence.

Ancient versions check (triggered: the literal moderns agree and the rendering carries the chapter’s main allusion). The LXX of 1:7 was not read directly — routed to Open Questions.

1:19 — “they are vigorous” / “lively”. ESV “vigorous”; NASB “vigorous”; KJV “lively”; NIV “vigorous”. The Hebrew ḥāyôt is unusual here and has been taken as “lively”, “vigorous”, or even “like animals” (a reading some find in the word’s resemblance to ḥayyâ, “living creature/beast”). The moderns agree on “vigorous”; the possibility that the midwives are flattering Pharaoh’s prejudices is an interpretive judgement, not a translation one. Moderate; routed to Open Questions.

1:21 — “he gave them families” / “he made them houses.” ESV “he gave them families”; NASB “He established households for them”; KJV “he made them houses”. The KJV is literal and the moderns interpret. Both are right; the preacher should say the Hebrew word is houses. High confidence.

1:16 — “on the birthstool.” ESV/NASB “birthstool”; KJV “upon the stools”. The Hebrew ʾobnayim is a dual form, literally something like “the two stones” — the birthing arrangement. Nothing doctrinal turns on it; it is a vivid detail worth naming.

Pulpit edition note: Pulpit text matches report default.

9. Tone and Feel

Two registers, and the join between them is at v. 8.

  • vv. 1–7 — genealogical, then exuberant. A list of names in the flat register of a record, and then a sentence that runs away with itself: fruitful, swarmed, multiplied, mighty, and the land full. The prose enacts the increase by refusing to stop at one verb.
  • vv. 8–22 — cold, procedural, and increasingly desperate. Pharaoh’s speech is the language of policy (“come, let us deal shrewdly”); the oppression is described in the vocabulary of administration (taskmasters, store cities, quotas of mortar and brick). The horror is bureaucratic, which is exactly why it lands. And it escalates: the last verse is a public order to drown babies, delivered without a raised voice.

A note on the midwives’ scene. vv. 15–21 has a different feel again — almost a folk-tale beat: a king, two women, a summons, an answer, a reward. The congregation will find it satisfying, and it is meant to be. But it sits between two atrocities, and v. 22 wipes the smile off.

Soundtrack. A census-clerk’s monotone; then a swelling; then the sound of an office; then, at the end, a river.

Note for the preacher. Do not let the midwives’ scene set the emotional tone of the whole sermon. The chapter ends at v. 22, not v. 21.

Idea-level repetition: futility. Pharaoh’s fear (v. 9), his plan (v. 10), his labour policy (vv. 11–14), his instruction to the midwives (vv. 15–16) and his public decree (v. 22) all aim at one outcome, and the narrator reports the opposite three times (vv. 7, 12, 20). The chapter says the same thing five ways: it will not work. [T] High confidence — the strongest idea-level convergence.

A note on ʿābad. The service-root is hammered in vv. 13–14 and becomes the book’s key demand — Israel will serve Pharaoh or serve the LORD, and the exodus is a transfer of service, not an escape from it. That thread starts here. [T]/[I] Moderate–high.

11. Quotation/Allusion

Book-level source inventory. Genesis is Exodus’s only earlier source and this chapter is its densest concentration in the entire book — a re-citation (vv. 1–5), a stacked allusion (v. 7) and a fulfilment (vv. 11–12).

Independent candidate-finding. From the chapter’s own vocabulary: the eleven names + seventy persons → Gen 46:8–27; pārû … wayyirbû … wattimmālēʾ hāʾāreṣ → Gen 1:28; wayyišrĕṣû → Gen 1:20–21; 9:7; ʿānâ + “serve” + “four hundred years” → Gen 15:13; “Joseph died” → Gen 50:26.

Genesis 46:8–27 → Exodus 1:1–5 (high confidence — direct re-citation) [T]

Source context. Genesis 46 records Jacob’s descent into Egypt with his household at Joseph’s invitation, with a full name-list and the total of seventy persons. Its function in Genesis is to close the patriarchal narrative’s movement: the family that was promised a land goes down into another country to survive a famine.

Book usage. This is Exodus’s first use of Genesis and it is a re-citation rather than an allusion — the list is reproduced. Genesis will remain the book’s live source throughout (2:24; 3:6, 15–17; 6:3–8; 12:40–41; 13:19; 32:13).

OT-to-OT. Genesis 46’s list and Genesis 15’s prophecy are already connected within Genesis — the descent into Egypt is the beginning of the sojourn Gen 15:13 announced. Exodus 1 activates both at once.

What it adds. The book’s opening word is a conjunction and its opening paragraph is a quotation. Exodus insists it is not a new story. And the number seventy, set against v. 7’s “the land was filled with them”, measures the distance the blessing has travelled.

Genesis 1:28 (with 1:20–21 and 9:1, 7) → Exodus 1:7 (high confidence — distinctive verbal stacking) [T]

Source context. Genesis 1:28 is the creation mandate to humanity: “Be fruitful and multiply and fill the earth.” Genesis 1:20–21 uses šāraṣ of the waters swarming with living creatures. Genesis 9:1, 7 reissues the mandate to Noah after the flood, adding šāraṣ (“bring forth abundantly / swarm”). The function of all three in Genesis is the same: God’s blessing of life, given at creation and re-given after judgement.

Book usage. Genesis 1–2 becomes Exodus’s most sustained conversation partner — the plagues as de-creation, the rûaḥ over the waters at 14:21, Sabbath at 16 and 20:11, the seven speeches of 25–31 and the finished/blessed tabernacle of 39–40. This verse is where that conversation opens, and everything downstream is reading back to it.

OT-to-OT. Genesis 1:28 and 9:1, 7 are already a pair within Genesis — the second is the deliberate re-issue of the first. Exodus 1:7 draws on both, so the verse is not simply “creation language” but creation language as re-given after judgement.

What it adds. Three things. The blessing is being fulfilled, not merely promised — the reader watches Genesis 1 come true in a family. Israel is narrated as a new humanity, which is why the plagues can later be narrated as the un-making of Egypt. And Pharaoh’s programme is set against the creation mandate itself, which is why it is doomed before it starts: he is not fighting a people but a word spoken over the world.

Genesis 15:13–14 → Exodus 1:11–14 (high confidence — thematic fulfilment with the key verb shared) [T]

Source context. In the covenant-cutting ceremony of Genesis 15, a dreadful darkness falls on Abram and God tells him: “your offspring will be sojourners in a land that is not theirs and will be servants there, and they will be afflicted for four hundred years. But I will bring judgment on the nation that they serve, and afterward they shall come out with great possessions.” Its function in Genesis is to attach a timetable and a guarantee to the covenant — and to say, at the moment of the covenant’s ratification, that it will pass through slavery.

Book usage. The Abrahamic covenant is Exodus’s most-worked source (2:24; 3:6, 15–17; 6:3–8; 12:40–41 with its “430 years… on that very day”; 32:13). This is its first activation, and it is the dark half.

OT-to-OT. Gen 15:13–14 and Gen 46 are sequential moments of the same programme; Exodus 1 puts them in the same chapter.

What it adds. The oppression is inside the promise. A reader arriving from Genesis knows before Pharaoh speaks that this affliction has been foretold, that it has an end, and that the oppressing nation will be judged and Israel will leave with possessions — all of which the book delivers (12:35–36). It reframes the chapter from tragedy to countdown. And it should temper any sermon that treats ch. 1 as unrelieved darkness: the darkness is scheduled.

Moderate–high on the Matthew resonance — it is a narrative pattern rather than a citation, and Matthew’s Egypt-and-out-of-Egypt frame (2:15) makes it more than coincidence.

12. Genre

Hebrew historical narrative, opening with a genealogical list (vv. 1–5) and proceeding to summary narration (vv. 6–14) and a scene with dialogue (vv. 15–21). The mixture is typical of a book-opening: the list connects, the summary compresses decades, the scene slows to real time.

Reading rules: (i) the summary sections cover a very long period in a few verses — do not read v. 7 as a single event; (ii) the midwives’ scene is narrated with dialogue and a verdict, which marks it as the passage’s interpretive centre; (iii) it is presented as having happened, and the narrator’s verdicts (vv. 12b, 17, 20–21) are authoritative in a way the characters’ speeches are not — which matters for v. 19.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits. Between the patriarchs and the exodus: the four-hundred-year sojourn of Genesis 15:13, at its bitter end. Before the law, the covenant at Sinai, the land, the monarchy.

What has happened since that changes the reading.

  • The exodus itself answers the chapter: the Nile of v. 22 becomes blood (7:20) and then Egypt’s grave (14:28).
  • Acts 7:17–19 makes this chapter part of the church’s own founding story, told by a martyr to a hostile council.
  • Hebrews 11:23 commends the faith of Moses’ parents in defying the edict — the NT’s own verdict on the resistance this chapter begins.
  • Matthew 2:13–18 repeats the pattern: a king kills infant boys, a child escapes to Egypt, and is called out of it (2:15, quoting Hos 11:1, which is itself reading the exodus).
  • Revelation 12:4 gives the pattern its cosmic form: a dragon waiting to devour a child at birth.

Timeline errors to avoid. Reading the chapter as a general lesson about persecution while missing that it is the fulfilment of a specific promise (Gen 15:13); and applying the midwives’ civil disobedience to modern politics without the canonical framing (Rom 13 alongside Acts 5:29).

15. Who Am I?

  • Israel under oppression — the reader’s place in one sense, but with care: for most Western congregations the honest identification is not with the enslaved. The chapter’s first hearers were the descendants of these slaves, hearing why they exist.
  • The midwives — the nearest thing to a direct example the chapter offers, and the NT-supported one. Identification is legitimate here: powerless people, in an evil system, who feared God and refused.
  • Pharaoh — foil, and a warning about the shape power takes when it fears a people it can neither assimilate nor destroy. A congregation with any power at all should be invited to consider this identification, and will not want to.
  • Joseph — absent, and that is the point: the man who saved Egypt is forgotten by it within a generation.
  • God — nearly silent, and the chapter’s real subject by absence.

The misreading to flag. Reading the chapter as a straightforward “God is with the oppressed” without noticing that God is barely mentioned in it. The comfort here is structural (the blessing outruns the policy) rather than experiential; the experiential comfort arrives at 2:24–25.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. For the first hearers: to understand their own existence as the outworking of a promise that survived a state’s attempt to end it, and to know that the affliction was foretold and finite. Confidence, and a particular kind of memory.

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.

Worldview. Three shifts:

  1. God’s word outlasts policy. Pharaoh has an army, an economy and a river; Israel has a sentence spoken over creation. The chapter’s flat verdict — “the more they were oppressed, the more they multiplied” — is a claim about which of those is stronger.
  2. The darkness was scheduled. Genesis 15:13 means the oppression is not evidence that God has failed. This is the chapter’s pastoral gift and it should not be given cheaply: it does not make the mortar and brick less bitter, and the text does not pretend it does.
  3. Faithfulness often looks like refusal by people with no leverage. The only human beings who change anything in this chapter are two midwives who declined to obey.

Behaviour. Start naming the places where obedience will mean refusal, and count the cost as the midwives did. Start remembering — the chapter’s disaster begins with a king who “did not know Joseph”. Stop measuring God’s faithfulness by present conditions; ch. 1 is what covenant faithfulness looked like from inside, and it looked like nothing.

Motive. Not “be brave like the midwives” but: the blessing spoken over creation is being kept by God against an empire, and the fear of God is the reasonable response of people who know which side is going to win.

Four audiences.

  • For me: Where am I reading present darkness as evidence that God has stopped? And what have I been calling prudence that is actually the fear of a king rather than the fear of God?
  • For a Christian friend: For the believer in a long, unrelieved stretch — this chapter is what a promise looks like from the middle. God is scarcely named in it, and he is keeping every word.
  • For the church community: Two women without office or power did the only faithful thing in the chapter, and the narrator gave them names and a reward. That is a comment on where a congregation’s real work usually happens.
  • For an unbeliever: Here is a national epic that opens by admitting its people were slaves, that their god appeared to do nothing for four centuries, and that the heroes were two midwives. It is not the way empires tell their stories.

Prayer. You spoke a blessing over the world, and no policy has ever been able to stop it. Give us the fear of God where we have had the fear of men, and the memory that keeps us from becoming the king who did not know Joseph. And where we are in the dark and you seem to be saying nothing — remind us that you had already said it, to Abram, long before.

Grammatical and syntactical features.

  • The opening wĕ-. Exodus begins with a conjunction (wĕʾēlleh šĕmôt, “And these are the names”) — the same construction that opens Leviticus, Numbers and Joshua. The book is grammatically a continuation, and the Hebrew title of the book is its first two words: Šĕmôt, “Names”. [T] High.
  • The proportional comparative at v. 12. “Ka’ăšer … kēn …” — “as they afflicted them, so they multiplied and so they spread”. A construction of direct proportion: the affliction is not merely failing but driving the increase. English “the more… the more” catches it. [T] High confidence — this is the chapter’s thesis and its grammar is the argument.
  • The negative purpose clause at v. 10 (pen-yirbeh, “lest they multiply”) set against v. 12’s kēn yirbeh (“so they multiplied”). The same root in the fear and in the outcome. [T] High.
  • The Hithpael at v. 10 (nitḥakkĕmâ) — a reflexive/reciprocal form: “let us make ourselves shrewd”, “let us outwit”. The stem carries a self-congratulatory note the English struggles with.
  • The ambiguity of “the Hebrew midwives” (v. 15). The Hebrew construct (mĕyallĕdōt hāʿibriyyōt) can mean midwives who are Hebrew or midwives to the Hebrew women. Both are grammatically available, and the question of whether Shiphrah and Puah were Israelites or Egyptians turns on it. [T] for the ambiguity. Routed to Open Questions — and note that the sermon changes if they are Egyptians.

Wordplay and sound-features.

  • bāttîm at v. 21. The midwives who preserved Israelite houses are given houses. Audible in Hebrew; flattened by every English rendering that reads “families”. Moderate–high.
  • No claim is advanced on Shiphrah/Puah’s etymologies. An explicit negative — the meanings are contested and nothing should rest on them.

Recommended verification. The LXX of 1:7 (does it preserve the five-verb pile-up?); the precise range of pārek; whether the “Hebrew midwives” ambiguity is resolvable; the etymologies of Shiphrah and Puah. Routed to Open Questions and the Logos pass.

Textual Variants

No significant textual variants affect the preaching of this chapter. One note: the number of persons at v. 5. The MT reads seventy; the LXX and a Qumran fragment read seventy-five, and Acts 7:14 (Stephen) says seventy-five, following the Greek. The difference arises from how many of Joseph’s descendants are counted. Moderate–high confidence that the divergence exists and that Acts follows the LXX. Preaching implication: if anyone notices that Stephen’s number differs from Exodus’s, this is why — a translation tradition, not an error in either. Routed to the Logos pass for confirmation. [S]

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. The eastern Nile delta (Goshen), during a period of Egyptian state construction. [T] for the text’s own geography; the absolute dating is [S] and contested.

What the original audience knew.

  • Store cities and corvée labour. Pithom and Raamses (v. 11) are supply or storage centres; corvée — compulsory state labour on royal projects — was a normal instrument of Egyptian administration, not an innovation. The audience would recognise the policy as ordinary statecraft, which is part of the horror.
  • Mortar and brick (v. 14). Mud-brick construction was the standard delta building technique (stone being scarce there and reserved for temples and tombs). Egyptian tomb paintings depict brick-making gangs under overseers. [S] Moderate–high.
  • The name Raamses. The city name is associated with the Ramesside period, and it is the chapter’s one hard chronological datum and its most contested — it sits at the centre of the early-date (fifteenth-century) versus late-date (thirteenth-century) exodus debate, since a city named for Rameses II raises questions for an earlier dating (to which the standard reply is an editorial updating of an older place-name). [S] Do not settle the exodus date from this verse in a sermon. Moderate; flagged.
  • The birthstool (v. 16). The ʾobnayim — two stones or bricks on which a woman squatted to deliver — is attested in Egyptian practice. The detail is domestic and precise.
  • Midwifery as a profession. Two named women for a whole people is implausible as a literal headcount; they are most plausibly supervisors or representatives of a guild. [I]/[S] Moderate.
  • Semitic peoples in the delta. Groups from Canaan settling in the eastern delta, sometimes in large numbers, are well attested across this period. [S] Moderate–high.

What this changes about how we read. Two things. First, the oppression is bureaucratically normal — taskmasters, quotas, state building projects — which is why the chapter’s horror is administrative rather than melodramatic, and why it translates so readily into modern analogues. Second, the genocidal turn is not normal: from v. 15 the policy stops being economic and becomes exterminatory, and the text marks the shift by moving from summary narration to a scene with names.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Torah — compositional and canonical audiences coincide: wilderness Israel, hearing the account of why they exist and what their grandparents came out of. For them this is not ancient history; it is the generation before last.

The first hearers.

  • Who they were: the descendants of these slaves, now free, encamped around a mountain or moving toward a land.
  • Their situation: free, but with the memory recent enough to sting — and, in the wilderness, prone to remember Egypt fondly (16:3).
  • What they brought: the family names in vv. 2–4 as their own tribal identities; the knowledge of what mortar and brick feel like; and Genesis, if they had it, with its promise to Abram.

Where the passage fits their world. It answers “why were we there?” with a promise (Gen 15:13) rather than an accident, and “why are we here?” with a blessing no policy could stop. And it puts their tribal names in the first four verses, so that every hearer finds their own family in the list before the trouble starts.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.

  • Shocking: the escalation to drowning, delivered as a public decree in the last verse, with no comment.
  • Surprising: that two midwives are named and the king is not; and that God’s first recorded action in the book is to give them families.
  • Comforting: v. 12 — and the fact that the reader already knows how the book ends.
  • Disturbing: the near-silence of God; and, for a wilderness generation tempted to nostalgia, the reminder of what Egypt actually was.

What we bring that they didn’t. A modern reflex to read the chapter as a template for political resistance without the covenantal frame; a distance from state violence that can make the horror abstract; and an expectation that God will be seen to act, which this chapter refuses for twenty-two verses.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. The creation blessing, and the assault on the seed. (Primary.)

  • Where this passage sits: the blessing of Genesis 1:28 visibly operating in a family, and a state’s attempt to stop it.
  • What comes before: Gen 1:28; 3:15 (enmity between the seeds); 9:1, 7; the patriarchal promises of offspring.
  • What comes after: the plagues as de-creation; Israel as “my firstborn son” (4:22); and the pattern’s recurrences — Haman, Herod (Matt 2:16), the dragon before the woman (Rev 12:4).
  • Contribution: it establishes that the opposition to God’s people is opposition to a word spoken over creation, which is why it cannot succeed.
  • Confidence: high.

2. Covenant faithfulness in the dark.

  • Where this passage sits: the bitter phase of Gen 15:13’s announced sojourn.
  • What comes before: the covenant with Abram, cut in darkness (Gen 15:12, 17).
  • What comes after: 2:24–25’s four verbs; 6:3–8; the exodus as covenant-keeping (12:40–41, “on that very day”); and the canon’s long habit of promise-in-delay (Hab 2:3; 2 Pet 3:9).
  • Contribution: it gives the canon its paradigm case of a promise operating invisibly, and it refuses to resolve the tension early.
  • Confidence: high.

3. The fear of God.

  • Where this passage sits: the first instance in Exodus, in two women defying a king.
  • What comes before: the God-fearing of the patriarchal narratives (Gen 22:12; 42:18).
  • What comes after: 20:20’s definition (dread transposed into the reverence that keeps from sin); Proverbs’ “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom” (1:7; 9:10) — set pointedly against Pharaoh’s self-styled shrewdness here; and Acts 5:29.
  • Contribution: it introduces the theme by showing what it costs and what it refuses, before the book ever defines it.
  • Confidence: moderate–high.

Confluence — (b) mutual illumination. The creation-blessing theme explains why the opposition fails; the covenant-faithfulness theme explains why the darkness is bearable; and the fear-of-God theme explains what human beings contribute in the meantime, which is a refusal. Not three parallel tracks: the second is why the third is reasonable, and the first is why both are safe.

Schnittjer Pass

Skeleton. Exodus · first division, “from Egypt” (1:1–15:21) · leading idea: the progressive proximity of God, here at its furthest point — God is barely named. This unit sets the book’s presenting problem.

Findings.

  1. The opening conjunction and the name-list (step 0/6). Exodus begins wĕʾēlleh, “And these are the names”, quoting Gen 46:8–27. The book announces itself as a continuation before it announces anything else, and the Hebrew title of the book is its opening words. [T] High.
  2. Narrative time: extreme acceleration (step 1). Four centuries pass in vv. 6–7, and then the narrative slows to a scene with dialogue for the midwives. The narrator’s braking marks vv. 15–21 as the chapter’s interpretive centre, and it is the only place anyone in the chapter does the right thing. [T] High.
  3. Narrative space: the land filled (step 2). “The land was filled with them” (v. 7) — the land of Egypt, not the promised land. The blessing is overflowing in the wrong country, which is the chapter’s spatial problem and the book’s whole plot. [T]/[I] Moderate–high.
  4. Repetition and leading word (step 4). rābâ (“multiply”) in the blessing (v. 7), the fear (vv. 9–10), the reversal (v. 12) and the reward (v. 20) — the same root in Pharaoh’s nightmare and God’s blessing. [T] High.
  5. First vs second reading (step 5). On a first reading, v. 22’s decree is the chapter’s grim close. On the re-read — knowing ch. 2 — the sparing of daughters is the loophole that saves Moses; and knowing ch. 14, the Nile is already Egypt’s grave. [T]/[I] High.
  6. Polyacoustic intertextuality (step 6). Genesis 1, Genesis 9, Genesis 15 and Genesis 46 all sound at once in seven verses; forward, the chapter sounds again in Acts 7, Hebrews 11 and Matthew 2. Heard together: the seed under threat and the blessing that will not be stopped — without flattening into a single equation. Moderate–high.
  7. Irreducibility. Do not reduce this to “God protects his people” (he is scarcely mentioned) or to “stand up to tyrants” (two women do, and it is not the chapter’s engine). What is told is that a blessing spoken at creation kept advancing through four centuries of state violence while God said almost nothing.

Echoes / signals. Leading word: rābâ. Structural devices: the increase-inclusio (v. 7 → v. 20); the three-stage escalation; the sons/daughters antithesis. Intertextual links: Gen 46:8–27; Gen 1:28 with 9:1, 7; Gen 15:13–14 (cross-reported to Tool 11).

Nil returns. Chiasm — none; the shape is linear escalation. Type-scene — none fires; the midwife scene is not a recognised type-scene. Janus — no single verse faces both ways in the technical sense, though v. 7 comes close.

Christological Reading

Type(s) of connection: trajectory and pattern, not typology. Handle with restraint — this chapter is thin Christologically and forcing it produces allegory.

  • The assault on the seed → Matthew 2:16 (pattern). A king, threatened by a coming deliverer, kills infant boys; the child escapes; and in Matthew the escape is into Egypt, and the coming-out is quoted from Hosea 11:1, which is itself reading the exodus. Tests: category (the seed of the woman under threat, Gen 3:15) — pass; NT precedent — Matthew’s frame is explicit about the exodus pattern, though he does not cite Exodus 1 — moderate; escalation — pass; authorial pattern — the OT text does not itself signal a type. Classify as pattern/trajectory, not typology. Moderate–high.
  • The blessing that cannot be suppressed → the growth of the church (trajectory). Acts repeatedly reports that under persecution “the word of God increased and multiplied” (Acts 6:7; 12:24; 19:20) — the same idea as Exodus 1:12, in a book that has just retold Exodus 1 in Stephen’s speech (7:17–19). Moderate–high; the verbal link is Luke’s, not a citation.
  • The midwives → obedience to God over men (trajectory). Acts 5:29 states the principle the midwives embody; Hebrews 11:23 commends the same defiance in Moses’ parents. Moderate.
  • A connection to decline. Moses-as-Christ is sometimes read back into this chapter through the infant-under-decree motif. The chapter has no Moses in it — he is not born until 2:2 — and reading him in here is anticipation, not exegesis. Recorded and declined.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptations: “be brave like the midwives” — the overview names this one explicitly (Trap: moralising the midwives), and it is the sermon this chapter almost always gets.
  • The gospel grounding: the chapter’s effective agent is not the midwives but the blessing (vv. 7, 12, 20) — and behind it, the oath sworn to Abram (Gen 15:13–14) which God is keeping on schedule while saying nothing. The midwives are commended, but they are not what makes the chapter work.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the honest move here is not to manufacture a Christ-figure but to preach the promise-keeping God whose word outlasts an empire — and to name that the pattern begun here (a king killing children to stop a deliverer) recurs at Bethlehem and is defeated at the cross.

Confidence: moderate–high (the Matthew 2 pattern; the Acts growth-language); moderate (the midwives/Acts 5:29 principle); declined (Moses read back into ch. 1).

A note of restraint. This is the thinnest Christological chapter in Exodus. Say less rather than more, and let the trajectory do the work: the seed is under threat, the blessing advances, and the one who finally comes through the same pattern is the point.

Difficult / Contested Verses

1:19 — the midwives’ answer to Pharaoh. - Category: ethical / doctrinal — the chapter’s live question, and one orthodox Christians genuinely divide on. - The difficulty: did the midwives lie? And if so, does Scripture commend a lie? - Rhetorical function in context: the reply is a response under interrogation by a king who has ordered infanticide. The narrator has already given the reader the midwives’ motive (v. 17, “they feared God”) and then gives the verdict (vv. 20–21). - The main options: (i) it is a lie, and God rewards the women despite it, for their fear of him; (ii) it is a lie, and Scripture here recognises a case where deceiving a murderer is not sin (cf. Rahab, Josh 2, commended at Heb 11:31 and Jas 2:25); (iii) it is substantially true — Hebrew women in hard labour may well have delivered fast and unattended — and is at most a partial disclosure. - What an honest exegesis surfaces: the text states the ground of the reward and it is not the answer: “because the midwives feared God, he gave them families” (v. 21). That does not settle the ethics, but it does tell the preacher where the emphasis lies. Confirmed at the checking stage, and this is the standard commentary move: the reply “appears to be a lie”, but commentators distinguish the deception from the basis of the blessing — Exodus 1:21 blesses them because they feared God, not because they lied; “the text does not endorse everything the women did or said… they were obligated both to sustain and save life and to honour the truth” (Kaiser et al., Hard Sayings of the Bible, 137). The narrative commends their fear of God and their resistance to infanticide, not their deception. [revised: logos-research] High confidence on the emphasis; the ethical question itself genuinely open. - Handling: name the debate briefly and honestly; say where the text puts the weight; do not resolve the ethics from the pulpit unless the sermon is about it; and do not let it eat the chapter.

1:15–16, 22 — infanticide, and preaching it. - Category: pastoral. - The difficulty: the chapter contains a state programme of killing infant boys, described twice. - Handling: a congregation will include people for whom infant death is not abstract. Do not use the horror for rhetorical effect, and do not skate over it. Name it plainly, once, and let the chapter’s own restraint set the tone.

1:11 — “Raamses” and the date of the exodus. - Category: apologetic / historical. - The difficulty: the city name bears on the early-date/late-date debate, and a well-read hearer may raise it. - Handling: one sentence — the name is associated with the Ramesside period, the standard reply for an early date is an editorial updating of an older place-name, and the sermon does not depend on the answer. Do not adjudicate.

1:5 — seventy or seventy-five? - Category: apologetic (apparent contradiction). - MT seventy; LXX and Acts 7:14 seventy-five. See Textual Variants. Handling: one sentence if raised.

Also flag briefly: the absence of God as an actor for sixteen verses, which is pastorally significant and should be named rather than smoothed; and the temptation to draw direct modern political applications from vv. 17–21 without Romans 13 in view.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: “Be brave like the midwives”

  • What it looks like: the whole sermon on vv. 15–21, with the midwives as models of courage and a call to be like them.
  • Why it’s wrong: it makes two minor characters the chapter’s engine when the engine is a blessing spoken at creation (vv. 7, 12, 20) and an oath sworn to Abram. The midwives are genuinely commended — but the chapter works without them and they know it.
  • The corrective: preach the promise that outruns the policy, and let the midwives be what the text makes them: the one place where a human being does the right thing, rewarded by God, in a chapter where God is otherwise silent.

Pitfall 2: Making the sermon a debate about lying

  • What it looks like: twenty minutes on whether v. 19 was a lie and whether lying to a murderer is permissible.
  • Why it’s wrong: it is a real question, but it is not this chapter’s question — and the text answers a different one, telling us plainly that the reward was for the fear of God (v. 21).
  • The corrective: name it in two sentences, say where the text puts the weight, and move on.

Pitfall 3: Preaching the chapter as unrelieved darkness

  • What it looks like: a sermon on suffering and oppression that stops at v. 22.
  • Why it’s wrong: the reader has Genesis 15:13 and knows the affliction was foretold, timed and guaranteed to end in judgement on Egypt and departure with possessions. The darkness is scheduled.
  • The corrective: preach the countdown as well as the cruelty — without using the schedule to make the mortar and brick less bitter than the text says they were.

Pitfall 4: Skating over God’s silence

  • What it looks like: “God was with them all along” said warmly, as though the chapter said it.
  • Why it’s wrong: the chapter is remarkable for how little it says about God — he is the subject of two clauses in twenty-two verses, and the four great verbs of divine attention do not arrive until 2:24–25. Asserting felt divine presence over a text that withholds it costs the preacher credibility with anyone currently in the dark.
  • The corrective: name the silence. It is the chapter’s most pastorally useful feature.

Pitfall 5: Modern political application without the canonical frame

  • What it looks like: vv. 17–21 as a straightforward mandate for civil disobedience in a contemporary dispute.
  • Why it’s wrong: the principle is real and canonically supported (Dan 3; 6; Acts 4:19; 5:29) — but it is being drawn from a case of refusing to commit murder, which is the clearest possible instance and does not transfer smoothly to contested policy questions. Romans 13 belongs in the same frame.
  • The corrective: state the principle at the level the text supports it, and let the application be careful.

Pitfall 6: Reading Moses into the chapter

  • What it looks like: treating 1:22 as being about Moses’ escape, or finding a Christ-figure in the threatened infants.
  • Why it’s wrong: Moses is not born until 2:2. The decree of v. 22 is general, and its point here is the escalation, not an individual.
  • The corrective: let ch. 1 be the setting of a national emergency; the individual deliverer arrives next week.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

Two resolved at the checking stage; four remain.

  1. Whether commentators read v. 7 as deliberate Genesis-1:28 stacking. Resolved: yes, with weight — a four-verb succession unique to this verse, mirroring Gen 1:28 and 9:1–2, and doubling as patriarchal-promise vocabulary (T. D. Alexander, Apollos OTC, 2:42–43). Folded into Headline 1 and Tool 7. High.
  2. Shiphrah and Puah’s nationality, and the ethics of v. 19. Resolved as far as it can be: the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous, the names settle nothing, and the LXX and other versions treat them as Egyptians; on v. 19, commentators distinguish the deception from the ground of the blessing, which the text states as the fear of God. Folded into Tool 7 and Difficult Verses. High on the state of the question; the ethics remain open by nature.
  3. The LXX of 1:7 — does the Greek preserve the four-verb pile-up, or compress it? Bears on whether a Greek-reading hearer would catch the Genesis link. Moderate — not pursued.
  4. Seventy or seventy-five (1:5) — the MT/LXX/Acts 7:14 divergence; the mechanism is understood in outline but was not verified here. Moderate.
  5. The range of pārek (“ruthlessly”, vv. 13–14) — a rare word, three times in two verses. Moderate; nothing load-bearing depends on it.
  6. The ANE background claims — corvée labour as ordinary statecraft, mud-brick construction, the birthstool, Semitic settlement in the delta, and the Raamses dating question. All standard, all [S], none verified here. Moderate — check before asserting specifics from the pulpit, particularly anything touching the exodus date.
