Point and Purpose: Exodus 20:22–23:33 — The Book of the Covenant

Pulpit text: ESV (Anglicised) — Patrick’s own church Study text: NASB95 Date: 29 October 2026 Built from: dig-deeper-exodus-20-22to23-33 (v1.1); dig-deeper-exodus-claim-audit-2 (the two load-bearing claims, now audited); twofold-distinction-exodus-20-22to23-33; book-overview-exodus (Finalised)

Scope Decision

A single sermon on the whole code, as the preacher intends. At 105 verses the passage exceeds the usual rule of thumb for one sermon. It is still one unit: one divine speech, bounded by paragraph markers at 20:22 and 23:33, with worship at both ends. A single sermon can do it justice if it preaches the code’s shape and motives rather than its cases one by one. The structure below does that. Individual laws appear as instances, not as a catalogue.

Alternative split, if a series is ever wanted:

  1. 20:22–21:36 — worship and the release of the slave
  2. 22:1–23:9 — the neighbour and the God who hears
  3. 23:10–33 — sacred time, and the way ahead

Not in scope: 24:1–18. The sermon should close by pointing to 24:7–8, where these terms are read and sealed in blood, because that is where the code lands. The ratification itself belongs to another sermon.

Fallen Condition Focus

Rescued people forget their rescue. With power of their own, they treat those under their hand as they were once treated. And they divide their service between the God who freed them and other masters.

Anchors in the text:

  • “You shall not wrong a sojourner or oppress him, for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt” (22:21; 23:9). The law has to tell a freed people to remember.
  • The verbs of Egypt turned on Israel: the piel of עָנָה (‘afflict’, 22:22–23 ← 1:11–12) and לָחַץ (‘oppress’, 22:21; 23:9 ← 3:9).
  • The code’s closing danger, “if you serve their gods, it will surely be a snare to you” (23:33).
  • The recoil of 20:19, “let not God speak to us”.

[T] Anchored.

Shared with us: the same forgetfulness, the same misuse of power over those weaker than us, the same divided worship. Different: we hear it after the covenant blood of Matt 26:28 and the release announced at Nazareth (Luke 4:18).

Teaching Point

The God who heard Israel’s cry and set them free claims their whole life for his name — so that a released people must release, protect and judge justly as he did for them, because he still hears the cry of the afflicted.

  • Subject: what does the God who set his people free require of their life together?
  • Complement: their whole common life under his name — worship undivided, the weak treated as he treated them — because he is the God who hears.

Derived from: dig Headlines 2–4 (release as the code’s first law; Egypt’s verbs turned on Israel; “for I am gracious” and “I will not justify the wicked”), and Headline 1 as reframed by claim audit 2 (worship at both ends; the slave’s “service” turned into the service of God). God is the actor throughout: he speaks, hears, claims, releases, judges.

Purpose Statement

To move people whom Christ has set free to live their freedom the way the code describes it. That means giving God undivided worship, and treating the vulnerable under their hand as God treated them. The motive is remembering their own release, not earning it. Differentiated:

  • The comfortable believer — employer, landlord, lender, voter, anyone with power over another — is warned that God hears the cry against them (22:23, 27).
  • The afflicted believer is assured that the same God hears them, and calls himself gracious (22:27).
  • The church together — the “you” is plural — is called to be a people among whom the sojourner, the widow and the poor are safe.
  • The unbeliever is shown that the God who says “I will not justify the wicked” (23:7) is the God the gospel says justifies the ungodly. That is possible only because the covenant’s own blood has been shed by the one who announced release.

Candidate Big Question

“If God has set you free, what does your freedom look like on Monday — and whose cry is he hearing about you?”

A candidate. The downstream one-sentence-sermon step, or the preacher, should settle the final wording.

Proposed Structure

Four movements, following the code’s own shape (dig Tool 3). Each lands on the Purpose Statement.

1. The God who speaks and comes (20:22–26)

Function: worship before law.

  • “You yourselves have seen that I have spoken to you from heaven.” These are the same words that opened the covenant proposal at 19:4 (אַתֶּם רְאִיתֶם, ‘you yourselves have seen’).
  • No gods with Me.
  • An altar of earth, and the promise: “in every place where I cause My name to be remembered, I will come to you and bless you” (20:24).
  • Plant the bookend here. The code begins with God’s name remembered and ends (23:13, 24–25, 33) by forbidding the names and the service of other gods.

FCF surfaced: divided worship.

2. Freedom written into law (21:1–22:20)

Function: the created moral order applied to a freed people.

  • The first law is a release: “he shall serve six years, and in the seventh he shall go out free” (21:2).
  • The first section ends with a slave set free for an eye or a tooth (21:26–27). חָפְשִׁי (‘free’) stands in Exodus only in these four verses, and the verb at 21:26–27 is Pharaoh’s — “let go”.
  • Then life, body and property under God’s justice: the image of God behind 21:12 (Gen 9:6); the talion as a ceiling in court, turned at once into liberty for the slave.
  • The twofold analysis is useful here: these are the norms of the created order, applied and sanctioned in Israel’s courts. What has changed since is the sanction, not the norm. The movement ends with the three capital laws of 22:18–20 (sorcery, bestiality, sacrifice to other gods). These are the last of the court’s sanctions, and the third brings the first commandment back into view.

TP established: the God who released them claims their whole common life.

3. The God who hears (22:21–23:19)

Function: the hinge of the sermon. It opens and closes on the same sentence: “for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt” (22:21; 23:9). That is a bracket Alexander and Johnstone both recognise as a section, before the sacred-time laws of 23:10–19.

  • The verbs of Egypt turned on Israel: “You shall not afflict any widow or orphan”. The same verb was used of Pharaoh’s taskmasters (1:11–12).
  • “If they cry to Me, I will surely hear” (22:23), as at the bush (3:7).
  • The poor man’s coat: “What else shall he sleep in?… I will hear, for I am gracious” (22:27). חַנּוּן (‘gracious’) appears in Exodus only here and at 34:6.
  • The court: “I will not justify the wicked” (23:7).
  • Rest for “the son of your female slave and the sojourner” (23:12).

PS pressed: warning and comfort together.

4. Serve the LORD alone — and the way ahead (23:20–33)

Function: the close, and Christ.

  • The angel sent “before you… on the way”.
  • “Serve the LORD your God”, against the snare of other gods (23:24–25, 33).
  • The bookend completes: the slave who served his master (21:2, 6) has become the people who must serve God and no other.
  • Then Christ, on the New Testament’s own warrant:
    • The Gospels take 23:20’s words, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face” (Matt 11:10). In the Gospels that saying is fused with Malachi and Isaiah’s “prepare the way” and spoken of John the Baptist. The messenger is John; the one he goes before — standing where Israel stood — is Jesus.
    • At Nazareth Jesus announces the release this code began with (Luke 4:18, quoting Isa 58:6).
    • At the table he takes the words that sealed these very terms — “the blood of the covenant” (24:8) — and says “My blood of the covenant” (Matt 26:28).

Land on the PS: the code’s demands are lived by people released at that cost.

Keep Honest

  1. Preach the shape and the motives, not a catalogue. The code itself grounds its commands in Egypt (“you were sojourners”), in God’s hearing, and in his grace. Grace before law is not imposed on this text; it is written into the statutes (overview Trap 4).

  2. Bookends, not engineered frames. Claim audit 2 found the “nested frames” design no more than modestly above chance. What stands is the reframed claim:

    • worship at both ends;
    • two verbal turns — God’s name remembered (20:24) against other gods’ names (23:13);
    • the slave who serves (21:2, 6) against the people who must serve the LORD (23:24–25, 33).

    Say that much, and no more. The first is in print: Johnstone (Eerdmans Commentary on the Bible, p. 93) matches the altar law of 20:22–26 with the worship law of 23:10–19, and reads 23:9 as bracketing 22:21. The fuller concentric scheme (Halbe) is rejected by Alexander (Apollos 2:437–38). Do not present it as settled. The two verbal turns are your own reading, so preach them as observation, not as consensus.

  3. The angel is not Christ, and Malachi is not Exodus alone. The New Testament makes John the messenger and puts Jesus in Israel’s place. Malachi 3:1 fuses Exodus 23:20 with Isaiah 40:3 (claim audit 2). Say “Exodus, Malachi and Isaiah meet in the Gospels”, not “Malachi rewrote Exodus”.

  4. Read the slave laws and do not apologise for them. Read 21:16 (kidnapping a man is a capital crime). Name the hard line at 21:21 (“for the slave is his money”) honestly. The code’s first and last words to slaves are release.

  5. Do not promise health or fertility from 23:25–26. These are corporate, conditional promises to Israel in the land (overview Trap 14). Someone in the room has been told their illness is a failure of faith.

  6. Do not call these chapters “what Exodus calls the Book of the Covenant”. Say “the covenant terms Moses read aloud” (24:7). Exodus 24:3–4 does not say whether the Ten Words were in the book.

Christ-Centred / Anti-Moralism Check

Test Result
“Be like X,” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes. No human figure is held up. The hearers are addressed as the released, whose obedience is the fruit of a release already given
Is God / Christ the subject? Passes. In the TP God speaks, hears, claims and releases. In movement 4 Christ fulfils, as the one brought in, the one who announces release, and the one whose blood seals the covenant
Is the good news grounded in saving work? Passes. The motive is the exodus, and in Christ the greater release (Luke 4:18) and the covenant blood (Matt 26:28). Danger point: movement 3 could become “be kind to the poor”. Keep “for you were sojourners” and “for I am gracious” attached to every application

Pulpit-Text Note (ESV against NASB95)

The movement hinges are carried by the ESV, with four exceptions the preacher must cover:

Point in the sermon What the ESV reads What the congregation will miss Remedy
Movement 3’s hinge (22:22–23) “mistreat” That this is the verb of Egypt’s taskmasters. The ESV renders it “afflict” at 1:11 Say so in one sentence
The bookend (20:24 / 23:13) “remembered” / “mention” That both verses use the same causative verb with “name” Name it
Movement 4 (23:20 / Matt 11:10) “angel” at 23:20; “messenger… before your face” at Matt 11:10 The link between the two verses Read Matt 11:10 aloud beside 23:20
23:21 / 34:7 “pardon” / “forgiving” That both are the same Hebrew verb Only if the sermon reaches forward to 34:7

Agreements worth using:

  • At 21:26–27 the ESV keeps “let… go”, so the echo of “Let my people go” is audible.
  • At 21:21 the ESV’s “for the slave is his money” is the more literal rendering, and the harder one to hear.
