Dig Deeper: Exodus 7:1–11:10 — The Plagues: The Contest to Make God Known

The Passage

This unit spans five chapters (≈131 verses). Rather than reproduce the whole block, this section quotes the load-bearing frame verses in full (ESV) and lays the nine-plague cycle out in a structural table — the more useful presentation for a passage of this length, and the form the analysis below depends on. Wording verified against a public-domain edition; load-bearing findings turn on the Hebrew and the repeated refrains, re-checked below.

The commission restated and the whole arc previewed (7:1–5): “And the LORD said to Moses, ‘See, I have made you like God to Pharaoh, and your brother Aaron shall be your prophet. You shall speak all that I command you, and your brother Aaron shall tell Pharaoh to let the people of Israel go out of his land. But I will harden Pharaoh’s heart, and though I multiply my signs and wonders in the land of Egypt, Pharaoh will not listen to you. Then I will lay my hand on Egypt and bring my hosts, my people the children of Israel, out of the land of Egypt by great acts of judgment. The Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD, when I stretch out my hand against Egypt and bring out the people of Israel from among them.’”

The sign before Pharaoh (7:8–13): Aaron’s staff becomes a serpent (Hebrew tannîn, 7:9–10, 12 — a sea-dragon/monster word, not the nāḥāš of 4:3); the magicians replicate it by their secret arts, “but Aaron’s staff swallowed up their staffs. Still Pharaoh’s heart was hardened, and he would not listen to them, as the LORD had said.”

The great statement of purpose (9:13–16): “…that you may know that there is none like me in all the earth… But for this purpose I have raised you up, to show you my power, so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth.” (9:16 is cited at Romans 9:17.)

The catechetical aim (10:2): “…and that you may tell in the hearing of your son and of your grandson how I have dealt harshly with the Egyptians and what signs I have done among them, that you may know that I am the LORD.”

The tenth plague announced and the plunder commanded (11:1–8): “Yet one plague more I will bring upon Pharaoh and upon Egypt. Afterward he will let you go… Speak now in the hearing of the people, that they ask, every man of his neighbor and every woman of hers, for silver and gold jewelry” (11:1–2). “About midnight I will go out in the midst of Egypt, and every firstborn in the land of Egypt shall die… But not a dog shall growl against any of the people of Israel… that you may know that the LORD makes a distinction between Egypt and Israel” (11:4–7). And Moses “went out from Pharaoh in hot anger” (11:8). “The LORD said to Moses, ‘Pharaoh will not listen to you, that my wonders may be multiplied in the land of Egypt’… and the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, and he did not let the people of Israel go out of his land” (11:9–10).

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple converging tools:

  1. The plagues are revelation before they are retribution: the drumbeat refrain “that you/they may know that I am the LORD” (7:5, 17; 8:10, 22; 9:14, 16, 29; 10:2; 11:7) makes Egypt, Israel, and the reader the intended knowers. [T] Surfaced by: Repetition, Author’s Purpose, Linking Words, Positional Necessity. The cycle is a sustained theophany-by-judgment, not a mere escalation of misfortunes; the purpose clauses are the interpretive key the preacher must foreground. High confidence.

  2. The hardening is narrated in three interleaved ways and the narrator refuses to collapse them: through plagues 1–5 Pharaoh’s heart “was hard” or he hardened it; from plague 6 (9:12) onward “the LORD hardened” — all framed by the programmatic announcements of 4:21 and 7:3. [T] for the pattern; the theology to be handled with care. Surfaced by: Repetition, Narrator’s Comment, Original Languages, Difficult Verses. Three deliberately varied verbs carry it — ḥāzaq (strengthen/make firm), kābēd (make heavy — the kābôd/“glory” root), qāšâ (make stubborn). Divine and human agency both stand; the text will not let the preacher exonerate Pharaoh or indict God. High confidence on the pattern.

  3. The plagues are targeted de-creation, systematically undoing Genesis 1 — Nile-to-blood, swarming frogs/gnats/flies/locusts, dying livestock, darkness over the land, and finally death — so that Egypt is un-made even as Israel (the “fruitful… swarming” people of 1:7) is being made a nation. [T] for the individual reversals; [I] for the overarching de-creation frame. Surfaced by: Vocabulary/Original Languages, Quotation/Allusion, Schnittjer Pass. The sign that opens the contest is pointedly a tannîn (7:9–10), the chaos-dragon word, not the bush’s nāḥāš — Pharaoh is the Nile-dragon God masters (cf. Ezek 29:3). Moderate–high confidence.

  4. The nine plagues are a structured argument, not a random barrage: three triads (warned “in the morning” / warned “go in” / unannounced), with rising intensity, the Goshen distinction from plague 4, and the magicians’ collapse — building deliberately toward the firstborn so that Passover (ch. 12) becomes necessary. [T] for the triadic and escalation patterns; [I] for the “designed toward Passover” reading. Surfaced by: Structure, Positional Necessity, Narrator’s Comment. Preach the shape, not nine sermons on nine disasters. High confidence on the structure.

  5. The aim reaches past Egypt to the nations: “so that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth” (9:16) — the plagues are missional self-revelation, and their memory is deliberately catechetical (“that you may tell your son,” 10:2). [T] Surfaced by: Repetition, Biblical Theology, Quotation/Allusion (Rom 9:17). The God who distinguishes Goshen intends to be known worldwide and across generations; this is not tribal vengeance but the glory of God going public. High confidence.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. To reveal the LORD as the covenant-keeping Redeemer who defeats the powers and comes down to dwell, moving the reader from a God who hears (2:24) to a God whose glory fills the tent (40:34). The refrain “that you may know that I am the LORD” frames the aim.

Passage-level. This unit enacts the knowing. Its purpose is not to catalogue disasters but to stage a contest of self-revelation: YHWH makes himself known — to Egypt in judgment, to Israel in distinction, to the reader as catechesis (10:2) — and demonstrates that the redemption about to come is unmistakably his, against the maximal resistance of the world’s strongest king. The plagues answer “Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?” (5:2, Pharaoh’s opening challenge) with nine escalating answers. [T]

Book-overview note: confirms the overview’s purpose and its Trap 1; the passage is where the “know that I am the LORD” spine is most concentrated.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. Chapter 5: the first confrontation worsens Israel’s plight (bricks without straw); the people turn on Moses (5:21); Moses complains (5:22). Chapter 6:2–8: God answers with the densest covenant-promise speech in the book — “I am the LORD,” the sevenfold “I will” (bring out, deliver, redeem, take, be your God, bring in, give), sealed by “you shall know that I am the LORD your God” (6:7). Then the renewed commission and genealogy (6:9–30), closing with Moses’ “uncircumcised lips” objection — which 7:1–5 answers by making Aaron his prophet and previewing the whole arc.

Necessity answer. The plague-cycle exists here because (a) 6:2–8 has promised the sevenfold rescue and staked it on Israel’s knowing the LORD — a promise now requiring enactment; and (b) chapter 5 has established a genuine deadlock, so that the deliverance, when it comes, reads unmistakably as YHWH’s victory over real resistance rather than an easy win. Position is the argument: the plagues are the demonstration that makes the redemption God’s. And the cycle must escalate to the firstborn precisely so that Passover (ch. 12) becomes necessary — the plagues are structurally ordered toward the lamb. [T]

Implication. Read the plagues as the outworking of the covenant name just proclaimed (6:2–8), not as a stand-alone display of power. “That you may know that I am the LORD” (7:5) is 6:7’s promise being kept. The deadlock is theological, not a narrative delay: a real “no” must precede the unmistakable “yes.”

Revisit after Tool 11 (book-usage). The creation/de-creation thread (Gen 1, live since 1:7) sharpens the doctrinal prediction: the God who made the ordered world is the God who can un-make the world’s greatest power — so the “knowing” the plagues produce is knowledge of the Creator-Redeemer, not merely a tribal deity.

Immediate surroundings. Before: 6:28–7:7 (the renewed commission and arc-preview). After: 12:1ff (Passover and the tenth plague executed). The unit runs from the commission-restatement (7:1) through the announcement of the tenth plague and Moses’ departure “in hot anger” (11:8–10); 11:1–10 is the hinge that hands the cycle over to Passover. The chapter divisions cut across the cycle; the real seams are the plague-units and the triads.

3. Structure

The prologue (7:1–13): commission restated; Moses “as God to Pharaoh,” Aaron as prophet; the arc previewed (harden, multiply signs, Egyptians shall know); the staff-tannîn sign, in which Aaron’s staff swallows the magicians’. This frames the whole and states the outcome in advance.

The nine-plague body (7:14–10:29): three triads. The widely recognised pattern (see table above):

  • Plagues 1, 4, 7 (blood, flies, hail): Moses meets Pharaoh in the morning, by the water / standing before him — full warning.
  • Plagues 2, 5, 8 (frogs, livestock, locusts): “Go in to Pharaoh” — warning, but not at the water.
  • Plagues 3, 6, 9 (gnats, boils, darkness): unannounced — no warning at all.

Within and across the triads the stakes rise: the magicians replicate the first two, fail at the third (“the finger of God,” 8:19), and by the sixth cannot even stand (9:11); from plague 4 God distinguishes Goshen (8:22–23; 9:4, 26; 10:23); the later plagues threaten the whole economy (hail, locusts) and climax in a darkness that can be felt (10:21). The tenth (announced in 11) breaks the pattern as the climactic judgment reserved for Passover.

Device. A framed, escalating sign-cycle with a strong prologue that announces the ending — so the suspense is not “will God win?” but “how long will Pharaoh resist, and what will it reveal?” The structure itself preaches: the outcome is certain from 7:1–5; the drama is the hardening. [T] / [S: the triadic scheme is standard (Cassuto and others) — high but noted as received]

4. Linking Words

  • 7:5 “The Egyptians shall know… when I stretch out my hand” — the temporal-causal clause ties knowledge to the act of judgment; the plagues are the epistemology. [T]
  • 8:22 / 9:14 / 9:16 / 9:29 “so that / that you may know…” — the purpose clauses (Hebrew lĕmaʿan / result constructions) are the recurring logical spine; each plague is subordinated to the knowing it produces. [T]
  • 9:16 “But for this purpose (ûlĕwaʿăbûr zōʾt) I have raised you up” — the strongest purpose connector in the unit: Pharaoh’s very survival is subordinated to the proclamation of God’s name. Paul reads this connector precisely (Rom 9:17). [T]
  • 8:15 “But when Pharaoh saw that there was a respite, he hardened his heart” — the contrastive but marks the fatal pattern: relief becomes occasion for self-hardening. The same shape recurs at 9:34. [T]
  • 10:1 “for (kî) I have hardened his heart… that I may show these signs” — God grounds the hardening in the purpose of further self-revelation and catechesis (10:2); the connector makes the hardening purposive, not arbitrary. [T]

5. Parallels

Narrative sign-cycle, not poetry — formal parallelism is N/A. But note the structural parallelism of the triads (three matched sets, each patterned warned/warned/unannounced): a prose analogue to poetic parallelism, where the repetition-with-variation across the sets is the compositional device (handled under Structure and Repetition). [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

The narrator’s asides are the interpretive control on the hardening:

  • The refrain-fulfilment notices — “as the LORD had said” (7:13, 22; 8:15, 19; 9:12, 35) — recur after each hardening, framing every episode as the outworking of the announced word (4:21; 7:3). The narrator insists the reader see prophecy being fulfilled, not events drifting. [T]
  • 7:7 — the ages of Moses (80) and Aaron (83): a narrator’s brake that dignifies the human agents and marks the solemnity of the commission’s execution. [T]
  • 9:11 — “the magicians could not stand before Moses because of the boils” — the narrator’s quiet verdict that Egypt’s counter-power has collapsed; the experts are now victims. [T]
  • 9:16 / 10:1–2 — divine speech reported by the narrator that names the purpose of the hardening (proclamation of the name; catechesis to the next generation) — the theological key, placed on God’s own lips within the narrative. [T]
  • 8:19 — the magicians’ own confession, “This is the finger of God,” reported by the narrator: Egypt’s wise men testify against Pharaoh. [T]

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory: Moses, Aaron, Pharaoh, Egypt, the Nile, Goshen, the Hebrews, the magicians (unnamed here; Jannes and Jambres, 2 Tim 3:8). Triage: most are scene-setting; the theologically loaded terms are the hardening verbs, the sign-words, and the purpose vocabulary.

  • The three hardening verbs. ḥāzaq (“strengthen / make firm / make resolute,” the most frequent — 7:13, 22; 8:19; 9:12, 35; 10:20, 27; 11:10); kābēd (“make heavy / insensible” — 7:14; 8:15, 32; 9:7, 34; 10:1), the same root as kābôd, “glory”; qāšâ (“make hard / stubborn” — 7:3; 13:15). The deliberate variation is a signal, not a stylistic accident: Pharaoh’s heavy (kābēd) heart is set against the LORD’s weighty (kābôd) glory he will get over Pharaoh at the Sea (14:4, 17–18). High confidence on the verbs; the kābēd/kābôd resonance moderate.
  • ʾôt / môpēt (“sign / wonder,” 7:3, 9; 10:1–2) — the events are signs: they point beyond themselves to God’s identity. Not magic tricks, not mere marvels. High confidence.
  • tannîn (7:9–10, 12) — the staff before Pharaoh becomes a tannîn, the sea-dragon/chaos-monster word (Gen 1:21; Ps 74:13; Isa 51:9; Ezek 29:3 where Pharaoh is the Nile tannîn) — not the nāḥāš of the bush-sign (4:3). The shift is pointed: before Pharaoh, God’s sign is the mastery of the chaos-dragon Egypt embodies. Moderate–high confidence — a load-bearing lexical distinction the English “serpent” hides.
  • ʿābad (“serve / worship,” in “let my people go that they may serve me,” 7:16 etc.) — the same verb as the slavery-service to Pharaoh; the contest is over whom Israel will serve. Slavery is mis-directed worship. High confidence.
  • “the finger of God” (ʾeṣbaʿ ʾĕlōhîm, 8:19) — an idiom for direct divine action; recurs at 31:18 (the tablets “written by the finger of God”) and Luke 11:20 (Jesus casting out demons “by the finger of God”). High confidence on the idiom; the Luke link cross-reported to Christological Reading.

8. Translations

  • 7:9 “serpent.” ESV/NASB/KJV all render tannîn as “serpent” here, obscuring the shift from nāḥāš (4:3). NASB footnotes “sea monster”; the divergence is within the Hebrew, not among the moderns — flag it, since a finding hangs on it (see Vocabulary). The moderns agree; the Hebrew distinction is the point.
  • The hardening verbs. ESV levels the three Hebrew verbs into “harden(ed)” throughout; NASB and KJV do likewise. The English uniformity hides the ḥāzaq / kābēd / qāšâ variation and the agency-shift; the preacher working from any English version must be told the Hebrew is not monochrome. High confidence.
  • 9:16 “raised you up” / “made you stand.” ESV/NASB “raised you up”; the Hebrew heʿĕmadtîkā (“caused you to stand / kept you standing”) can mean brought onto the stage of history or preserved you alive [thus far]. Paul’s LXX-shaped citation (Rom 9:17, exēgeira, “I raised you up”) leans to the former. The rendering affects how the hardening is preached — appointment vs preservation-for-a-purpose. Moderate — flag for the preacher; route to a commentary.

Ancient versions check: the exegetically weighty rendering where the moderns agree is 9:16. The LXX of 9:16 reads heneken toutou dietērēthēs — “for this reason you were preserved,” foregrounding the preservation sense — yet Paul, citing the same verse, writes exēgeira se (“I raised you up”), the appointment sense. So the two live options are both anciently attested: the LXX preserves “kept alive for this,” Paul’s citation “raised up for this.” Translation-tradition note: preach the verse as purposive either way — Pharaoh exists at this moment, alive and on the throne, for the sake of God’s self-proclamation; do not let the appointment/preservation distinction obscure the shared point that Pharaoh’s continuance serves God’s glory. High confidence that both readings are ancient; moderate on the fine LXX wording — verify against NETS.

Pulpit edition note: Pulpit text matches report default (ESV Anglicised); no load-bearing divergence.

9. Tone and Feel

Relentless, mounting dread — a slow, inexorable drumbeat of “again… and again… and still Pharaoh would not listen.” The pacing is cumulative: each cycle raises the stakes until the land is in ruins and can be felt in darkness (10:21). Cutting through the dread are flashes of satire: the magicians who can add frogs but cannot remove them (8:7 against Pharaoh’s plea to Moses in 8:8); Pharaoh’s fatal “Tomorrow” (8:10) when he could have said “Now”; the courtiers’ exasperated “Do you not yet understand that Egypt is ruined?” (10:7); Moses leaving “in hot anger” (11:8). The soundtrack: a funeral march with moments of grim irony. A preacher must honour the dread — this is not an adventure story — while letting the satire deflate Egypt’s pretensions. [T]

10. Repetition

  • “That you/they may know that I am the LORD” — 7:5, 17; 8:10, 22; 9:14, 16 (name proclaimed), 29; 10:2; 11:7. The interpretive refrain; the single most important repetition in the unit. [T]
  • “Let my people go, that they may serve me” — 7:16; 8:1, 20; 9:1, 13; 10:3. The recurring demand that reframes slavery as mis-directed service. [T]
  • The hardening formula + “as the LORD had said” — recurring after each plague (7:13, 22; 8:15, 19; 9:12, 35; 10:20, 27; 11:10). [T]
  • “Pharaoh’s heart” (lēb parʿōh) — the organ named again and again; the whole contest is staged in a single human heart. [T]
  • “Stretch out your hand / staff” and the hand (yād) motif — carried from 3:19–20; 4:2–17 into 7:5, 19; 8:5, 16; 9:22; 10:12, 21. God’s outstretched hand vs Pharaoh’s mighty hand. [T]
  • “In the morning” / “go in to Pharaoh” / (no warning) — the triad-marking phrases (see Structure). [T]

11. Quotation/Allusion

Independent candidate-finding first. The passage’s distinctive vocabulary — tannîn, the swarming-creature words, “darkness over the land,” de-creation imagery, “finger of God” — was searched against the canon before testing the overview’s named thread (Gen 1). The strongest external link is conceptual (creation/de-creation); the richest yield is internal (Move 4).

Creation / de-creation (Genesis 1) → the plague cycle (moderate–high, conceptual)

  • Source context (Move 1): Genesis 1 orders the cosmos into spheres — waters, swarming creatures, light, land animals, humanity — and pronounces it good. The plagues run the film backwards: the Nile (source of Egypt’s life) becomes death (blood); the ordered spheres teem with swarming frogs, gnats, flies, locusts (the Gen 1 verb-world of “swarms”); the light is blotted out (darkness over the land, undoing “let there be light”); and the sequence ends in death. Egypt is un-created.
  • Book usage (Move 2): creation vocabulary is a tracked live thread — 1:7 (“fruitful… increased… multiplied… swarmed… the land was filled”) applies Genesis 1’s blessing to Israel. The plagues pay that off in reverse: as Israel is being made a swarming, filling people, Egypt is being un-made. Phrase-level check: the swarming-creature language recurs across plagues 2–4 and 8 — a distributed self-echo, not a single touchpoint.
  • OT-to-OT (Move 3): the tannîn of 7:9–10 links to the sea-dragon of Genesis 1:21 and to the chaos-dragon the Psalms/Isaiah say God crushed at creation and the exodus (Ps 74:13–14; Isa 51:9–10); Ezekiel later names Pharaoh himself “the great tannîn in the midst of his streams” (Ezek 29:3; 32:2). The plague-sign thus draws on a pre-existing OT creation-and-chaos conversation.
  • What it adds: the plagues are not ad hoc penalties but a cosmic act — the Creator un-making a rival “creation” (Egypt and its gods) to remake a people. This grounds the missional refrain (9:16): the Creator will be known “in all the earth.”

Judgment on the gods of Egypt (theme high on 12:12; specifics moderate–uncertain)

  • 12:12 states the thesis retrospectively (“on all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgments, I am the LORD”); within 7–11 the Nile-strike plausibly confronts Nile-deity (Hapi), the darkness the sun-cult (Ra/Amun-Re), and so on. But precise one-to-one mappings are largely later reconstruction and commonly overstated (see Pitfalls and the overview’s Trap 1). Preach the theme; hold the specifics loosely.

Internal echoes (Move 4 — required):

  • Answers §3:19–20: “I know that the king of Egypt will not let you go unless compelled by a mighty hand. So I will stretch out my hand and strike Egypt with all the wonders” — enacted across the whole cycle. Internal: [answers §3:19–20] [T] high.
  • Answers §4:21–23: the hardening announced (“I will harden his heart”) and the firstborn threat (“I will kill your firstborn son”) are enacted (7:3 → 9:12 etc.; 11:4–5). Internal: [answers §4:21–23; firstborn planted → 11:5, 12:29] [T] high.
  • Resolves §4:2–5, 17 (the bush-sign): the staff-sign given at Horeb is deployed before Pharaoh (7:8–13) — but escalated from nāḥāš to tannîn. Internal: [resolves §4 staff-sign, escalated] [T] high.
  • Resolves §3:21–22 (plunder): “you shall not go empty… you shall plunder the Egyptians” is set up again here (11:2–3, favour + asking silver/gold) and resolved at 12:35–36. Internal: [plunder — 11:2–3 → 12:35–36] [T] high.
  • Fulfils §6:7 (the covenant refrain): “you shall know that I am the LORD your God” (6:7) is enacted as “the Egyptians/you shall know” throughout. Internal: [fulfils §6:7] [T] high.
  • Planted → the Sea and beyond: “I will get glory (kābēd) over Pharaoh” (14:4, 17) pays off the kābēd-heart wordplay; the plague-cycle is retold at Ps 78:43–51; 105:27–36, and consciously echoed by Revelation’s bowls (Rev 16). Internal: [planted → 14; reception Ps 78/105; Rev 16] [T]/[S] high/moderate.

12. Genre

Historical narrative in the form of a structured sign-cycle. The events are presented as real (“signs… wonders,” genuinely done) yet as signs — pointing beyond themselves to God’s identity. Reading rules: resist both the rationalising reduction (a natural-disaster domino chain that empties them of revelation) and the allegorising move (each plague “means” a spiritual vice). The genre is theological history: read the pattern and the purpose clauses, not just the marvels. Brief — genre is not contested. [T]

13. Copycat

Narrative, so label the actors:

  • The plague-judgments — unique salvation-historical act, not a template for expecting God to send plagues today; Revelation deliberately reuses them eschatologically (Rev 16), which is the canon’s own way of applying them forward.
  • Pharaoh — negative example / warning-paradigm: the hardened heart that resists God to its own ruin (Rom 9:17 cites 9:16). Not “don’t be stubborn” as bare exhortation, but a sober warning about presuming on delay (“Tomorrow,” 8:10).
  • The magicians — negative example: counter-power that replicates, then fails, then suffers (9:11); Jannes and Jambres become the type of those who “oppose the truth” (2 Tim 3:8).
  • Moses and Aaron — unique commissioned agents, not a leadership template; they simply do “as the LORD commanded.”

The characteristic error is to make Pharaoh a lesson in willpower (“don’t harden your heart”) detached from the sovereignty and mercy the text and Romans 9 hold together. [T]/[I]

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits: the exodus threshold — the covenant clock of Genesis 15 has run out; the founding rescue is unfolding. Where the reader sits: this side of the cross and Pentecost. Reading through the middle: the plagues prefigure final judgment (Revelation’s bowls consciously echo them, Rev 16), and the whole cycle is aimed at a rescue that pictures redemption in Christ (1 Cor 10:1–13). The hardening theme is taken up doctrinally in Romans 9; the “finger of God” reaches its climax when Jesus casts out demons “by the finger of God” (Luke 11:20), inaugurating the greater exodus from a greater Pharaoh. Do not preach the plagues as sub-Christian vengeance stranded in the Iron Age; read them forward to the Day of the LORD and the cross. [T]/[I]

15. Who Am I?

We are neither Moses nor Pharaoh primarily. Pharaoh is the paradigm of the hardened heart that resists God to its ruin — a warning the NT applies to all who presume (Rom 9:17; and, by extension, the “today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts” of Heb 3:7–8, itself an exodus text). Israel-in-Goshen, distinguished and spared (8:22–23; 11:7), is the picture of a people preserved by sheer grace — which, read through Passover, is us. God is the hero and the subject; Moses is his instrument. The right identification summons the hearer to know the LORD and not to mistake his patience for absence. [T]

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. To know that the LORD is God — Creator and Redeemer, sovereign over the mightiest human power — and therefore to fear and trust him, refusing to presume on his patience; and, for Israel, to tell the next generation what he did (10:2).

Stage 2. - Worldview: God will be known — in mercy or in judgment; the hardened heart is a real and terrifying possibility; God’s patience is not indifference. - Behaviour: do not presume on delay (stop saying “tomorrow,” 8:10; respond today) — and, positively, recount God’s saving acts to your children (10:2). - Motivation: the God who distinguishes his people (Goshen) and gets glory over the powers is both to be feared and trusted; his name will be proclaimed in all the earth (9:16), so our confidence and our witness rest on his self-revealed supremacy.

Four audiences. For me: where am I treating God’s patience as permission, saying “tomorrow”? For a Christian friend: the one dismayed that hostile powers seem stronger than God needs the plague-cycle’s answer — the powers are already being un-made. For the church: we are the distinguished-by-grace people, charged to tell the next generation (10:2). For the unbeliever: the God who says “there is none like me in all the earth” (9:14) has made his name known, supremely at the cross; do not harden your heart.

Prayer. Adoration of the Creator-Redeemer who is known in all the earth; confession of hardened, presuming hearts and of trusting the powers over God; petition for soft hearts (Ezek 36) and courage to witness; thanksgiving for the distinguishing grace that spares his people through the Lamb. [T]/[I]

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. Egypt, Nile-centred in economy and religion; the plagues strike the foundations of the state cult. What the audience knew: - The Nile as life and deity. Egypt’s survival depended on the annual Nile flood; the river was divinised (Hapi). A blood-Nile is an assault on Egypt’s economic and religious foundation. - The magicians (ḥarṭummîm). Priestly lector-magicians of the Egyptian court, expert in ritual power; their replication (blood, frogs) then failure (gnats) then incapacity (boils) is the systematic defeat of Egypt’s religious establishment. Later tradition names them Jannes and Jambres (2 Tim 3:8). - The agricultural calendar. The hail ruins “the flax and the barley… but the wheat and the emmer were not struck, for they are late in coming up” (9:31–32) — a realistic seasonal detail (Jan–Feb) that also sets up the locusts to finish what the hail began. - Goshen. The fertile north-eastern Delta region where Israel dwelt; its exemption (8:22; 9:26; 10:23) would be geographically vivid to the audience. - “Let us go three days’ journey to sacrifice” (8:27). A recognisable festival-pilgrimage request, repeatedly negotiated and refused.

What this changes. The plagues are not generic disasters but precision strikes on Egypt’s gods, economy, and expertise — a theological demolition the original audience would read as YHWH dismantling the whole Egyptian order. [T]/[S]

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Section: Torah — compositional and canonical audiences coincide: wilderness Israel with the tabernacle in the camp, who know Genesis entire (so the creation/de-creation reversal is available) and who hold the cult (so “the finger of God,” later writing the tablets, resonates).

The first hearers. A redeemed-but-grumbling wilderness generation who had watched the gods of Egypt fall. The refrain “that you may tell in the hearing of your son and of your grandson” (10:2) makes this deliberate catechesis — and the wilderness audience are those sons, hearing why their deliverance took the shape it did. The Goshen distinction (8:22–23; 11:7) is their own story: they were spared.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances. - Shocking: the sheer escalation to the death of the firstborn (11:5) — and that God announces it in advance. - Surprising: that Egypt’s own experts confess “the finger of God” (8:19) and its courtiers plead for surrender (10:7). - Comforting: the LORD “makes a distinction between Egypt and Israel” (11:7) — his people are seen and spared. - Disturbing: the hardening, and the collective suffering of Egypt — the text does not flinch from either.

What we bring that they didn’t. A therapeutic discomfort with divine judgment; an individualism that reads “harden your heart” as private psychology rather than covenant defiance; a modern impulse to rationalise the miracles. The first hearers heard, first, who their God is over the nations.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. Judgment / de-creation → new creation. (textual signal: the reversal of Gen 1; payoff: high) The plagues un-make Egypt’s world (waters, swarms, light, life) as the Creator’s judgment; the trajectory runs to the Day of the LORD and Revelation’s bowls (Rev 16), and — the other side of judgment — to the new creation the exodus-rescue anticipates. Confidence: moderate–high.

2. The knowledge and name of God among the nations. (textual signal: 7:5; 9:16; the refrain; payoff: high) “That you may know that I am the LORD” and “that my name may be proclaimed in all the earth” (9:16) set the missional aim: God’s self-revelation is for the nations, not Israel only — a trajectory to Isaiah’s “all the earth” and the Great Commission. Confidence: high.

3. Sovereignty and the hardened heart. (textual signal: the hardening sequence; payoff: high) The interplay of divine and human agency in Pharaoh’s heart is the OT seedbed for Romans 9’s exposition of God’s freedom, and for the promise of a new heart (Ezek 36; 2 Cor 3) that the gospel fulfils. Confidence: high.

Confluence. These converge climactically: the Creator’s judgment (de-creation) is the stage on which his name is made known to the nations, and the hardening is the means by which the demonstration is prolonged “that my wonders may be multiplied” (11:9) and his name proclaimed (9:16). Judgment, revelation, and sovereignty are not three themes side by side but one act — God getting glory by making himself known through judgment on a hardened power.

Difficult / Contested Verses

  • The hardening of Pharaoh (4:21; 7:3; 9:12; 10:1, 20, 27; 11:10) — the central crux. Category: doctrinal. The difficulty: does God harden a man and then judge him? Rhetorical function: the hardening is purposive in the text (9:16; 10:1–2; 11:9) — it prolongs the demonstration so God’s name is proclaimed and Israel’s children are catechised. Honest exegesis: the programmatic announcements (4:21; 7:3) frame the whole; through plagues 1–5 the text most often has Pharaoh’s heart “hard” or Pharaoh hardening himself; from 9:12 the LORD hardens — the two agencies are held together, not collapsed. Romans 9:17–18 is the NT’s own handling. Suggested handling: let both agencies stand; take the congregation to Romans 9 and to the warning of Hebrews 3:7–8 against presuming on delay; do not exonerate Pharaoh or indict God. Give this a full solo treatment and commentary work before the pulpit (the overview flags it explicitly).
  • The collective suffering of Egypt. Category: ethical/pastoral. Difficulty: ordinary Egyptians and the firstborn suffer under Pharaoh’s defiance. Honest exegesis: the text frames Egypt corporately as the oppressing power under judgment, and stresses the distinction God makes for his people (8:22–23; 11:7); it also records Egyptians who fear the LORD and act on it (9:20). Handle with pastoral care; do not flatten the genuine hardness of corporate judgment.
  • The death of the firstborn (announced 11:4–6). Category: ethical/pastoral. Flag now; it is executed in the next unit (ch. 12) where it should be handled fully alongside the lamb.
  • The one-to-one “each plague defeats a god” chart. Category: interpretive overreach. 12:12 warrants the theme; the precise mappings are largely later reconstruction. Preach the theme with confidence, the specifics with restraint (overview Trap 1). [S: overview]

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: a confident one-to-one “each plague defeats a specific Egyptian god” chart

  • What it looks like: a slide matching all ten plagues to named deities as though the text supplied the list.
  • Why it’s wrong: 12:12 warrants the theme (judgment on Egypt’s gods); the precise mappings are largely later reconstruction and often overstated.
  • The corrective: preach the theme with confidence and the specifics with restraint — name the clear ones (Nile, sun/darkness) tentatively, and keep the weight on “that you may know that I am the LORD.”

Pitfall: resolving the hardening too quickly

  • What it looks like: either “God made him do it, so Pharaoh is innocent” or “Pharaoh did it all; God merely foreknew.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the text deliberately holds both agencies, and verb-marks the shift (Pharaoh through plagues 1–5; the LORD from plague 6); Romans 9 keeps them together.
  • The corrective: let both stand; take the congregation to Romans 9 and to the warning against presuming on delay (Heb 3:7–8) — and preach the new heart God gives where hardening cannot be cured.

Pitfall: reading the plagues as merely ancient punishment

  • What it looks like: a sermon on God’s wrath with no reference to the refrain.
  • Why it’s wrong: the purpose clauses name the aim — that they may know the LORD and that his name be proclaimed in all the earth (9:16).
  • The corrective: preach revelation, not just retribution; the plagues are God going public.

Pitfall: preaching the plagues as spectacle (or a rationalising domino-chain)

  • What it looks like: either a special-effects retelling, or an eager natural-causation explanation (red algae → frogs flee → insects breed…).
  • Why it’s wrong: both empty the signs of their revelatory point; genre is theological history, not a disaster documentary or a science puzzle.
  • The corrective: name them as signs that really happened and mean something — the Creator un-making a rival order to make himself known.

Pitfall: making Pharaoh a lesson in willpower

  • What it looks like: “Don’t harden your heart — decide to soften it.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it moralises a text about sovereignty and mercy, and offers a self-help remedy the passage does not.
  • The corrective: preach the warning honestly and the gospel remedy — the Spirit-given heart of flesh (Ezek 36; 2 Cor 3).

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. 9:16 — “raised you up” vs “kept you standing / preserved.” ⭐ RESOLVED, and it is a clean Category-3 triage case. The Hebrew carries both senses, and the ambiguity is real. The Septuagint resolves it decisively toward the active sense: ἐξήγειρά σε, “I raised you up”. Paul follows the Greek at Romans 9:17, and the choice does work for him: “raised you up” rather than “preserved you” places God’s purpose before Pharaoh’s existence, which is what the surrounding argument about mercy requires. So the Hebrew is open and the Greek is not, and the NT argument rests on the Greek — the third category of the toolkit’s Hebrew/Greek triage, and not to be demoted on the ground that the Hebrew is the original. (The reply was uncited; the Greek form is standard and the reasoning coherent. Moderate–high.) [revised: research-assistant pass]
  2. The precise stem and agency of each hardening occurrence. ⭐ RESOLVED — and it corrects this report’s second headline finding. Childs’s tabulation: kābēd hiphil with Pharaoh as subject (8:11, 28; 9:34), qal with Pharaoh’s heart (9:7), hiphil with Yahweh only once (10:1); ḥāzaq piel with Yahweh (4:21; 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:17), qal with Pharaoh’s heart (7:13, 22; 8:15; 9:15); qāšâ only twice — 7:3 (Yahweh) and 13:15 (Pharaoh) (Childs, OTL, 171; cf. Niehaus 2:193–95; Motyer, BST, 124). The verdict on the split: it “doesn’t represent as clean a break as often presented — Pharaoh has already hardened himself three times before God acts.” 9:12 is Yahweh’s first direct hardening, but the verbs interleave throughout rather than dividing at a seam. The theological point is unaffected and arguably improved: “the Lord states his intention to harden, yet as events unfold Pharaoh hardens his own heart” — a process and a give-and-take, not a sudden reversal. ⭐ Headline Finding 2 should be preached as interleaving, not as a switch. High. [revised: research-assistant pass]
  3. The kābēd / kābôd wordplay. ⭐ RESOLVED — it is recognised, and it is not read back. The resonance is acknowledged in Exodus scholarship and “operates across the plague narrative rather than being read back exclusively from 14:4, 17”. The link rests on the shared notion of weight — giving due weight to a person or deity — which makes the play available throughout the sequence. Note the third strand the answer supplies: Pharaoh also makes Israel’s labour kābēd, heavier. Upgraded from moderate to moderate–high. [revised: research-assistant pass]
  4. The extent of the Egyptian-gods theme within 7–11 (as against 12:12). How much of the god-by-god reading the text itself supports vs later reconstruction — worth a commentary check to calibrate pulpit confidence. Moderate.
