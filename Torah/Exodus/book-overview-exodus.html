A standing note on the versions. Three of the digs turned up places where a load-bearing count or wordplay is a Hebrew-tradition feature that the Greek does not carry: the LXX lacks 32:9, so “stiff-necked” is fourfold in Hebrew and threefold in Greek; the LXX resolves pānîm to autos at 33:14, breaking the face/presence hinge; and the LXX relocates the priestly-garments section (MT 39:2–31) to the front of its pericope, displacing the first of the two sevenfold compliance-refrains. Exodus 35–40 diverges from the MT more than any other stretch of the Pentateuch, and the explanation is genuinely unsettled. Where this overview reports a count, read “in the Hebrew”. [S: digs 32:1–34:35; 35:1–40:38, Logos-checked]

And how to read that note — three kinds of divergence, not one. It would be easy to turn the cases above into a standing preference for the Hebrew over the Greek, and that would be a mistake. They are not all the same kind of finding.

  1. Translation loss. Tēbâ→θῖβις and pānîm→αὐτός are Hebrew literary features that no translation carries — the ESV breaks them too. These say nothing about the LXX’s quality; they are why the standing formula reads “in the Hebrew”.
  2. Substantive variants. The absence of 32:9, the different law at 21:22–25, and the whole different edition of chs. 35–40 are genuine text-critical divergences — and here there is no default. The LXX translates a Hebrew text sometimes older than the MT: LXX Jeremiah is about an eighth shorter with 4QJer-b behind it, 4QSam-a repeatedly sides with the LXX against the MT, and Deut 32:8’s “sons of God” now stands in the ESV text. For Exodus 35–40 specifically, which edition is prior is disputed, and this overview does not settle it.
  3. The Greek as the NT’s own text. Kappōret→hilastērion (Rom 3:25), tabnît→typos (Heb 8:5) and qāran→δεδόξασται (driving 2 Cor 3:7–18) are load-bearing whichever text is prior, and this overview’s intertextual map and Christological trajectory both depend on them. They are not to be demoted on the ground that the Hebrew is the original.

Working from the Hebrew is already the default — the ESV translates the MT — so no rule is needed to secure it. WCF 1.8’s “kept pure in all ages” is a claim about the Hebrew original and its providential preservation, not a ruling on any particular variant; the Reformed kept those apart, and so should this document. Presenting Situation

Narrator / author. The book narrates in the third person, with large blocks of first-person divine speech (the law, the tabernacle instructions). The text states that Moses is commanded to write — the Amalek memorial (17:14), the words of the covenant (24:4), and the renewed covenant words (34:27) — and that he read the Book of the Covenant aloud (24:7). [T] That the book as a whole is Mosaic, and its date and compositional history, are reconstruction, not text-observation: hold them as received and hang nothing on them. [S]

Audience. Narrative-world: the enslaved-then-redeemed generation, addressed directly at points (12:24–27; 13:8, 14). [T] Compositional: wilderness Israel with the tabernacle standing in the camp, hearing how that tent — and the God who fills it — came to be among them. [I] Canonical: later Israel, and the church, reading the paradigm of redemption. [I] Default to the canonical audience; keep the wilderness camp in the ear, because Exodus explains the reader’s own institutions (Passover, Sabbath, tabernacle, covenant).

Occasion. The founding of Israel as the LORD’s redeemed, covenant, God-indwelt people — from a family of seventy under a death-sentence to a nation with God dwelling in its midst. [T] for the movement the book narrates; [I] for “occasion” as a compositional judgement.

Pressure. How can the God of the fathers keep his sworn word (Gen 15:13–16) when his people are enslaved and targeted for death — and, once redeemed, how can a holy God dwell among a stiff-necked people without consuming them? The book’s own refrain frames the aim: “that you may know that I am the LORD” (6:7; 7:5, 17; 8:10, 22; 9:14, 16, 29; 10:2; 11:7; 14:4, 18; 29:46). [T] The plagues dig established that this refrain is the interpretive key of the whole contest, not a decorative tag: the cycle is theophany-by-judgment, and Egypt, Israel and the reader are all the intended knowers. [S: dig 7:1–11:10]

Note where the refrain lands the last time it is used: 29:46, at the tabernacle. Its final object is not a plague or a sea but a residence.

And note where the question is asked. The refrain is an answer, and the 4:18–6:30 dig locates the question that provokes it: Pharaoh’s “Who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice…? I do not know the LORD” (5:2). Everything from 7:5 onwards answers that sentence, and Pharaoh is himself made a knower of it (7:5; 14:4, 18). Read alongside 3:13–14 (Moses asks the same thing from the other side, and gets the Name) and 6:2–8 (the Name given again, to a people who cannot hear it), the book’s whole first half is a sustained reply to a question two men ask for opposite reasons. [T] [S: dig 4:18–6:30, Logos-checked] High confidence. New in v1.0.3.

⭐ And the book has a second question, asked by the insiders — which is the key to its whole second half. At a dry camp between the Sea and the mountain, Israel says: “Is the LORD among us or not?” (17:7) — a sentence the narrator thinks important enough to preserve as the reason two places got their names. The two questions divide the book between them:

Question Asked by Answered by The refrain
1 “Who is the LORD?” (5:2) Pharaoh — the outsider, about God’s identity the plagues and the Sea (7–15) “that you may know that I am the LORD”
2 “Is the LORD among us or not?” (17:7) Israel — the insider, about God’s presence Sinai and the tabernacle (19–40) “that I may dwell in their midst” (25:8; 29:45–46)

And 29:46 — already this overview’s thesis sentence — answers both in one breath: “they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt that I might dwell among them.” Knowing and dwelling; identity and presence; question one and question two. This is the strongest single organising claim in the document, and it was only visible once both wilderness units were dug. [T] for both questions and for 29:46 — all three re-verified against the text at the claim audit; [I] for the pairing. [S: digs 4:18–6:30; 15:22–17:16, both Logos-checked] High confidence. One honesty note, added at the audit and ⭐ refined by the Research Assistant pass. The two questions and 29:46 are quoted text and the answering refrains are countable. On the pairing, the position is now precise, and it is half-attested:

  • The first half has a named scholar behind it. Blackburn treats Pharaoh’s “Who is the LORD…? I do not know the LORD” as the introduction to Exodus 5–15, with the rest of the exodus account functioning as the answer to it — and holds that “knowing the Lord is the theme and ultimate goal of the plagues” (The God Who Makes Himself Known, NSBT 28, 39–40). That is our claim, in print.
  • The second half remains ours. No source was found developing 17:7 as a parallel structural question, or citing 29:46 as the synthesising answer to both. The commentaries reach the neighbouring idea by a different route — God moving from distant deity to one who dwells among his people — but do not make it a two-question architecture.

The people’s own pressure, stated by the narrator. The same dig supplies the book’s most exact pastoral datum: Israel believes and worships at 4:31, and by 6:9 “they did not listen to Moses, because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery.” The inversion is deliberate — the unit opens with faith and closes with faith crushed — and Scripture assigns the cause to the duress, not to sin. This is the human floor from which the seven “I will”s of 6:6–8 are spoken. [T] [S: dig 4:18–6:30] High confidence. New in v1.0.3.

Purpose. To reveal the LORD as the covenant-keeping Redeemer who defeats the powers, binds his people to himself at Sinai, and comes down to dwell — moving the reader from a God who hears and remembers (2:24) to a God whose glory fills the tent (40:34). The digs sharpen the goal twice over: the rescue’s stated end is not liberation but God himself — “I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to myself” (19:4) [S: dig 19:1–8] — and the book states its own purpose outright at 29:46: “they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them out of the land of Egypt that I might dwell among them.” Grammatically the dwelling is a purpose clause hanging off the exodus, not a sequel to it. That is Exodus’s own thesis sentence about its own plot, and every other statement of purpose in this overview should be read as a gloss on it. [T] / [S: dig 25:1–31:18]

Device. A linear, spatial build — from Egypt → through the wilderness → at the mountain — mapping the progressive proximity of God: deliverance → journey → dwelling. The proportions are the point: nearly half the book stays at one mountain, because the weight of Exodus is not the escape from Egypt but how a holy God can dwell with a sinful people, the question chs. 32–34 nearly answer in the negative and chs. 35–40 answer in grace. Two large command→fulfilment doublings (instructions 25–31 ↔︎ construction 35–40) and an ascent→descent pattern (Moses up into glory, ch. 24 ↔︎ down into the calf, ch. 32) shape the Sinai block. [T] / [S: Schnittjer confirms the three-movement spatial shape]

The descent-spine (sweep-confirmed; ⭐ reframed at the claim audit). The spatial arc is carried by two interlocking verb-chains, not one — and the audit corrected this document’s earlier claim that “a single verb” runs through.

  • Yārad, “come down”: “I have come down to deliver” (3:8), planted at the bush → “the LORD will come down… upon Mount Sinai” (19:11), “the LORD descended upon it in fire” (19:18), “the LORD came down upon Mount Sinai” (19:20). Verified. But the chain stops at Sinai — there is no yārad in chapter 40.
  • Šākan, “dwell”: “the glory of the LORD abode (šākan) upon Mount Sinai” (24:16) → “that I may dwell in their midst” (25:8) → “I will dwell among the people of Israel” (29:45) → 40:35, “the cloud abode (šākan) thereon.”
  • Note the subject-shift at the end, which this document previously obscured: at 40:34 the glory fills (mālēʾ) the tabernacle; the verb šākan appears at 40:35, and there its subject is the cloud, not the glory.

The theology is unaffected — God comes down and God dwells, and the bush is the first move of a movement that ends in the tent. What the audit corrected is the presentation of it as a single lexical chain, which a hearer with a concordance could falsify. [T] — re-verified against the text at the audit; [S: dig 3:1–4:17, Headline 2] High confidence on each chain. [revised: claim audit]

Refinement at the Sinai seam. The digs re-cut the head of the Sinai block: 19:1–8 (proposal and consent) and 19:9–20:21 (the LORD comes down), dividing where the text divides (19:9 opens the descent it announces) and ignoring the 19/20 chapter break. The theophany-frame at 19:16 and 20:18 — the same thunder, lightning, trumpet and smoking mountain — brackets the Ten Words, so the Decalogue is spoken inside the terror, not after it. [S: digs 19:1–8; 19:9–20:21] High confidence. The Preaching Units table below is revised accordingly.

Law arrives before Sinai — so Sinai is where law is covenanted, not where it is introduced. The arc map above has read the mountain as the place law is given. The 15:22–17:16 dig shows that is a half-truth: a “statute and a rule” is made at Marah (15:25); the manna test is “whether they will walk in my tôrâ” (16:4); the Sabbath is given, kept and violated (16:23–30); and God asks, “How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?” (16:28) — all before chapter 19. Two consequences. (a) When the Decalogue arrives, Israel is not receiving law for the first time; they are receiving it as covenant, having already demonstrated they cannot keep it. (b) The Sabbath legislation of 31:12–17 and 35:1–3 presupposes a Sabbath already in use and already broken. This is a structural argument about law and grace built into the itinerary rather than into a sentence. [T] [S: dig 15:22–17:16] High confidence. New in v1.0.4.

Why the tabernacle block sits where it does. The instructions do not merely follow the covenant; they answer a problem chapter 24 has just created. At 24:16 “the glory of the LORD dwelt (šākan) on Mount Sinai” — the glory has taken up residence on a mountain that Israel must leave. Chapters 25–31 make the dwelling portable: poles through rings, carried on shoulders, going where the camp goes. The same verb moves from the mountain (24:16) to the people (25:8) to the tent (40:35, of the cloud — see the descent-spine note above). The tabernacle is Sinai made portable. [T] for the verb-chain. High confidence.

⭐ But separate the two claims here, as the audit required. The positional argument — the glory takes up residence on a mountain Israel must leave, and 25–31 makes the dwelling portable — is textual, carried by the šākan chain, and high confidence. The further reading that the mountain’s vertical holiness-gradient is “laid on its side” into the tent’s three horizontal zones is a commentators’ construct, not something Exodus states (Morales, NSBT 37; Köstenberger & Goswell). It is mainstream and attractive, and graded access is genuinely present in both — but it is [S], not [T], and this document previously gave both the same confidence. [S: dig 25:1–31:18, Logos-checked] Moderate–high on the three-zone mapping; attribute it when preaching it. [revised: claim audit]

And the calf is set inside it. Chapters 25–31 are spoken during the forty days of 24:18, and ch. 32 opens “when the people saw that Moses delayed.” The apostasy is placed as a jarring break between God’s instructions (25–31) and their communication to Israel (35:1–19), and Israel’s making of the calf parodies the making of the tabernacle. A Sabbath inclusio encloses it: the Sabbath speech that ends the instructions (31:12–17) and the Sabbath command that opens their delivery (35:1–3). So the Sinai block’s real shape is instructed (25–31) → broken (32) → interceded and renewed (33–34) → built (35–40), with the sign of covenant fellowship framing the breach. [T] for the placement; [S: dig 25:1–31:18, Logos-checked] High confidence.

The Sinai block’s driving question: who goes with whom. The presence-spine is not a steady ascent. Chapters 32–34 nearly lose it: “make us gods who shall go before us” (32:1) is answered by “my angel shall go before you” (32:34) and “I will not go up among you” (33:3) — a granted request that is in fact a sentence — and it is reversed only by petition: “if your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up” (33:15), “my presence will go with you” (33:14), “let the Lord go in the midst of us” (34:9). The same prepositional phrase carries the promise (25:8; 29:45–46), the threat (33:3) and the plea (34:9). [T] [S: dig 32:1–34:35] High confidence. New in v0.2.2 — and it is the Sinai block’s real crisis.

And the book ends in motion, one verse short of its answer. The construction account renders obedience in a form the calf had made necessary: “as the LORD commanded Moses” seven times in the making of the garments (39:1, 5, 7, 21, 26, 29, 31) and seven times in the erection (40:19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29, 32) — each seven followed by a blessing, Moses’ at 39:43 and God’s own at 40:34. Then the glory fills the tent and shuts the mediator out (40:35), and the last three verses shift to habitual time: the cloud, the fire, “throughout all their journeys” (40:36–38). Exodus does not conclude; it becomes a routine, and hands the problem of 40:35 straight to Leviticus 1:1, where God calls. [T] [S: dig 35:1–40:38, Logos-checked] High confidence. New in v0.3.0.

A three-zone gradient, transposed. Sinai had graded access — summit (Moses alone), boundary (priests and elders, 19:22; 24:1, 9), base (the people behind a limit, 19:12). The tent has the same gradient — Most Holy Place, Holy Place, court — so the holiness that was vertical becomes horizontal, and the axis of approach rotates from up to in. [T] for both structures; [I] for the transposition. [S: dig 25:1–31:18] Moderate–high.

Microscript

The God of the fathers keeps his sworn word: hearing his enslaved people, he comes down to redeem them by the blood of a lamb and through the sea, binds them to himself in covenant at Sinai, and — the true summit — comes down to dwell in their midst, so that the glory that filled creation now fills a tent among a stiff-necked people who deserve to be consumed and instead are graced. Keynotes: “come down” / “dwell” (yārad / šākan); “stiff-necked” (qĕšēh-ʿōrep, 32:9; 33:3, 5; 34:9 — the book’s diagnosis of its own people); the refrain “that you may know that I am the LORD.” Structural pulse: deliverance → covenant → dwelling; and grace before law, always. [T]

The microscript’s last clause is now text-warranted. “A stiff-necked people who deserve to be consumed and instead are graced” is not a gloss: it is the passage’s own vocabulary, and its pivot is 34:9, where Moses turns the diagnosis into the reason for the presence — “let the Lord go in the midst of us, for it is a stiff-necked people” — using the same causal kî that grounded God’s withdrawal at 33:3. [S: dig 32:1–34:35] (Note: the LXX lacks 32:9, so the count is fourfold in Hebrew, threefold in Greek.)

Test against the movements: from-Egypt (redemption) → wilderness (formation) → mountain (covenant and dwelling). Each serves the presence-spine; the calf (32–34) is the crisis that makes the final dwelling an act of sheer grace.

Sweep confirmation. Two digs independently placed dwelling at the end of the arc from inside the first half of the book. The Song of the Sea already looks past the rescue to “your holy abode… the sanctuary, O Lord, which your hands have established” (15:13, 17) — freedom is for God, not merely from Egypt. [S: dig 13:17–15:21, Headline 2] And the covenant proposal names the destination as God himself: “brought you to myself” (19:4). [S: dig 19:1–8, Headline 4] The microscript stands; it is now demonstrated from the text rather than projected onto it.

Christological Trajectory

  • The Name and the Angel (3:2–15). The narrator identifies the appearer as “the angel of the LORD” and then simply as “the LORD”/“God” — a strong Christophany candidate, to be held at moderate–high confidence and not asserted as settled. The Name itself runs a direct line: the “I AM” of the bush is claimed by Jesus (John 8:58), and Jesus cites 3:6 (Mark 12:26). Crucially, the Name is glossed by its own context — “I will be with you” (3:12) and “I AM” (3:14) are the same Hebrew verb — so the Name asserts self-existence as covenant presence. The book’s whole later question is already compressed into it. [S: dig 3:1–4:17] Confidence: high (the Name); moderate–high (Christophany).
  • Passover / substitution. The firstborn-son claim (4:22–23) sets a fuse that burns to the Passover, where blood substitutes for the firstborn (12), completed at the Sea (14) and sealed in “the blood of the covenant” (24:8). Two sharpenings from the dig: the danger passing through the land is God himself — “I will pass through… I will strike” (12:12) — so Passover is rescue from the judgment of God, not merely escape from Egypt; and the distinction on the night is applied blood, not ethnicity — Israel’s firstborn stood under the identical threat. “Christ our Passover lamb has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7); no bone broken (John 19:36); the firstborn Son who was not spared (Rom 8:32). [S: dig 12:1–13:16] Confidence: high.
  • The seven “I will”s, and the covenant formula (6:6–8). Bracketed by “I am the LORD” at 6:2 and 6:8, God speaks seven first-person promises — bring out, deliver, redeem (gāʾal), take you to be my people, be your God, bring you in, give it to you. Two things run forward. (a) Gāʾal makes its first Exodus appearance here — the kinsman-redeemer verb, the one that presumes a relative with both the right and the will to pay; it is the vocabulary behind “you were ransomed… not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ” (1 Pet 1:18–19), and behind Isaiah’s Redeemer (Isa 43:1; 44:6). (b) The covenant formula — “I will take you to be my people, and I will be your God” — is stated here in full for the first time, and runs to Jer 31:33, 2 Cor 6:16, and its terminus at Rev 21:3, “he will dwell with them, and they will be his people.” Exodus states its own destination at 6:7, thirty-four chapters before 40:34. [T] [S: dig 4:18–6:30, Logos-checked] Confidence: high. New in v1.0.3.
  • ⭐ Gôy at 19:6 is pointed, and it alludes to Genesis 12:2. The Research Assistant pass confirms what the 19:1–8 dig flagged for checking: gôy denotes a political entity where ʿam is a kinship term, and its use at 19:6 is “an intentional allusion to Genesis 12:2” — the promise to make Abram a great nation. The force is deliberate: “although gôy highlights the similarity between Israel and the nations, Israel was to be different, a holy nation… the use of gôy draws out the force of qādôš.” Israel is a nation among the nations, distinguished not politically but by holiness and priestly vocation — inheriting the responsibility conferred on Abraham, that of mediating blessing to the earth (Blackburn, NSBT 28, 93–94; Williamson, NSBT 23, 97). [S: Research Assistant] New in v1.2.0.
  • The nations, modelled before they are mentioned. Before Israel is told “all the earth is mine… you shall be to me a kingdom of priests” (19:5–6), a priest of Midian hears the report of the exodus, blesses the LORD by name, offers the burnt offering, and is joined at table by Aaron and all the elders (18:9–12). Commentators read the placement as deliberate: Jethro is a proleptic model of the vocation Israel is about to receive. The trajectory runs to the mixed multitude (12:38), the sojourner under one law (12:48–49), and on to Acts 10, Ephesians 2:11–22 and Revelation 7:9. But preach the last verse too: “Moses let his father-in-law depart, and he went away to his own country” (18:27). The chapter has no way to keep him — and that unresolved departure is the sharpest pointer forward in it, answered only at “no longer strangers and aliens, but fellow citizens” (Eph 2:19). Note also the limit on warrant: Exodus 18 is not quoted anywhere in the NT, so every Christ-connection here is trajectory or contrast, not apostolic typology. [T]/[S: dig 18:1–27, Logos-checked] High confidence. New in v1.0.5.
  • The mediator’s office begins to fragment. “You are not able to do it alone” (18:18) is the first division of Moses’ office in the canon, and the pattern continues — seventy elders (Num 11:16–17), a successor (Num 27), priests, prophets, kings. The office keeps subdividing because no man can hold it, which is exactly the argument Hebrews 7:23–25 makes: the former priests “were many in number, because they were prevented by death from continuing in office, but he holds his priesthood permanently.” Set Moses, heavy-laden and dividing the load, against “Come to me, all who labour and are heavy laden” (Matt 11:28) — the Mediator who invites the burden rather than distributing it. [T]/[S: dig 18:1–27] High confidence as contrast. New in v1.0.5.
  • The redeemed people already non-ethnic. A mixed multitude goes up (12:38) and the circumcised sojourner keeps the feast under “one law” (12:48–49) — the redeemed congregation is bounded by covenant sign, yet open to the outsider who comes under it, from the very first night. A trajectory to the gospel’s welcome of the nations. [S: dig 12:1–13:16] Confidence: moderate–high.
  • Redemption / new exodus. The whole rescue becomes the template: Jesus’ death is an exodos (Luke 9:31); Isaiah 40–55 reads Exodus forward; Israel-brought-from-Egypt is applied to Christ (Matt 2:15 ← Hos 11:1). The Sea is read as baptism (1 Cor 10:1–2), and its Song confesses “he has become my yĕšûʿâ” (15:2) — the noun behind the name Jesus. The Divine Warrior who “will reign for ever” (15:18) is the Christ who “disarmed the rulers and authorities” (Col 2:15) and whose song is sung again by the Lamb’s people (Rev 15:3). [S: dig 13:17–15:21] Confidence: high.
  • The mediator who points beyond himself — and whose institution is born at Sinai. Moses is commissioned at the bush by a God who answers “who am I?” with “I will be with you” (3:11–12); he shuttles between God and people before any theophany (19:3, 7, 8); and when God speaks, the people beg for him — “you speak to us… but do not let God speak to us, lest we die” (20:19). The narrative endorses the request: “the people stood far off, while Moses drew near to the thick darkness where God was” (20:21). Deuteronomy has God commend it (5:28) and turn it into the prophet-like-Moses promise (18:15–19). The mediator-gap is made physical here, and filled at 1 Tim 2:5; Heb 3; 8–9; 12:24. After the calf Moses offers to be blotted out (32:32) — an offer that exposes its own limit, and the Christ-shaped vacancy Rom 9:3 gestures at. [S: digs 3:1–4:17; 19:1–8; 19:9–20:21] Confidence: high.
  • The vow that cannot be kept. “All that the LORD has spoken we will do” (19:8) is genuine consent, not folly — but the next unit already undoes it (20:19), and ch. 32 shatters it. The human “yes” is made good only by the covenant-keeper who obeyed and bore the curse (Gal 3:13). [S: digs 19:1–8; 19:9–20:21] Confidence: moderate–high.
  • The wilderness test, taken again and passed. The manna is given “that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law” (16:4), and Deuteronomy 8:3 supplies the lesson: “he humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna… that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone.” Jesus quotes that verse — hungry, in a wilderness, after forty days (Matt 4:4; Luke 4:4). The correspondence is the NT’s own, made by citation, not ours by construction: Israel was tested by hunger and demanded bread; Christ was tested by hunger and refused to demand it, quoting the lesson Israel failed to learn. Alongside it: the manna as the bread of life (John 6:31–58, where Christ makes the escalation himself — “your fathers ate the manna… and died”), the rock as Christ (1 Cor 10:4), and the rest that remains (Heb 4:9). Handle the rock polyacoustically — Num 20:8–11; Ps 78:15–16; Ps 105:41; Isa 48:21; 1 Cor 10:4 build the note over five texts — rather than flattening it to a single struck-rock type. [T]/[S: dig 15:22–17:16] High confidence on the wilderness-test contrast and the manna; moderate–high on the rock. New in v1.0.4.
  • The hardened heart → the new heart. The heart no plague can soften (7–11) is answered by the Spirit who gives a heart of flesh (Ezek 36:26; 2 Cor 3:3) — Christ softens what judgment only hardens. [S: dig 7:1–11:10] Confidence: high.
  • Presence / tabernacle. God comes down to dwell (25:8; 40:34) → the Word “tabernacled among us” (John 1:14) → “the dwelling of God is with man” (Rev 21:3). The Song of the Sea already names this destination (15:13, 17). The glory that fills the tent is the goal the whole book climbs toward. Confidence: high. The 25–31 dig sharpens the typology: the text signals its own provisionality — it calls itself a copy of a tabnît shown on the mountain (25:9, 40) — which is why Hebrews 8:5 can quote it without importing anything foreign. [S: dig 25:1–31:18] Remaining gap: chs. 32–40 still undug.
  • “The blood of the covenant” (24:8). Moses divides the blood — half thrown against the altar, half thrown on the people — and names it: “Behold the blood of the covenant that the LORD has made with you in accordance with all these words.” One blood, two parties, applied to a documented covenant. Jesus takes the phrase onto his own lips over a cup (“this is my blood of the covenant”, Matt 26:28; cf. Mark 14:24; “the new covenant in my blood”, Luke 22:20), and Hebrews 9:20 quotes the verse to argue that “without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness” (9:22). Tests: category, NT precedent, escalation and authorial pattern all pass. [S: dig 20:22–24:18] Confidence: high — as strong as OT→NT connections get, and the reason 20:22–24:18 is the series hinge. New in v1.0.0.
  • The covenant meal (24:9–11). Seventy-four men “beheld God, and ate and drank”, and the narrator adds that “he did not lay his hand on” them — telling the reader what did not happen, which is how we know it might have. Eating with God is the shape covenant fellowship takes, and the shape the Supper takes after it; parodied at 32:6. [S: dig 20:22–24:18] Confidence: moderate–high (trajectory, not typology). New in v1.0.0.
  • The sanctuary stands on ransom money. The hundred talents of census silver — the half-shekel paid “as a ransom for his life” (30:12–16) — “were for casting the bases of the sanctuary and the bases of the veil; a hundred bases for the hundred talents, a talent a base” (38:25–27). Every upright of God’s house is footed on atonement, and the arithmetic checks. Commentators give it exactly this weight: the sanctuary’s structural stability rests on the ransom paid for communal life before God (T. D. Alexander, Apollos OTC, 2:603). → 1 Pet 1:18–19, which names silver and gold precisely to say the real ransom was not made of them. [S: dig 35:1–40:38, Logos-checked] Confidence: high on the text; moderate–high on the 1 Peter resonance. New in v0.3.0.
  • Arrival and exclusion, in one scene. 40:34 is the book’s destination — and 40:35 is its unfinished business: “Moses was not able to enter.” If the mediator cannot go in, no one can, and that is where Exodus stops. Hebrews names this as the point — “the way into the holy places was not yet opened” (9:8) — and answers it: “we have confidence to enter… by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh” (10:19–20). Preach 40:34 without 40:35 and the gospel has nothing to open. [S: dig 35:1–40:38, Logos-checked] Confidence: high. New in v0.3.0.
  • The mercy seat and the empty throne. The kappōret is where blood is applied and where God speaks from (25:22) — law inside the box, atonement on the lid, voice above it. The LXX calls it the hilastērion, and Paul calls Christ a hilastērion whom God put forward by his blood (Rom 3:25; cf. Heb 9:5). Note also what is not there: the throne-furniture of an ANE sanctuary is kept and the image that normally sat on it is abolished — Israel “saw no form” (Deut 4:12, 15). The most important thing in God’s throne room is an absence, and the Christian claim is that it was finally filled by a person. [S: dig 25:1–31:18, Logos-checked] Confidence: high on the lexical chain; moderate–high on pressing the empty-throne point. New in v0.2.1.
  • The high priest who carries names in and guilt out. Aaron bears the tribes’ names on his shoulders and over his heart “for regular remembrance before the LORD” (28:12, 29) and bears their guilt on his forehead “that they may be accepted” (28:38) — and must first be atoned for himself, seven days over (29:1, 35–37). Likeness and insufficiency in one rite: Heb 7:25, 27. [S: dig 25:1–31:18] Confidence: high. New in v0.2.1.
  • The character of God at 34:6–7. “Merciful and gracious… forgiving iniquity… who will by no means clear the guilty” — a tension the OT states but cannot resolve, answered at the cross where guilt is borne, not cleared (Rom 3:25–26). The formula is anticipated in the Decalogue’s own jealous-God/ḥesed clause (20:5–6), and it becomes the most re-used text within the OT (Num 14:18 — Moses quoting it back at the next rebellion; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; 145:8; Joel 2:13; Jonah 4:2; Nah 1:3; Mic 7:18). Note the two clauses stand side by side with no adversative in the Hebrew — the tension is not softened, and a sermon that dissolves it has thrown away the passage’s best gift. [S: dig 32:1–34:35] Confidence: high.
  • Moses’ refused substitution. “If you will forgive their sin— but if not, blot me out of your book” (32:32); “Whoever has sinned against me, I will blot out” (32:33). The offer is right and the offerer is not qualified — and Paul makes the identical unfulfillable wish (Rom 9:3) before naming the one who was made a curse (Gal 3:13). The passage’s most important Christological datum is a refusal. [S: dig 32:1–34:35] Confidence: high. New in v0.2.2.
  • John 1:14–18 as a compressed retelling of Exodus 33–34. “Tabernacled among us… we have seen his glory… full of grace and truth” (1:14 ← ḥesed weʾĕmet, 34:6); “the law was given through Moses” (1:17 ← 34:1, 28); “no one has ever seen God” (1:18 ← 33:20); “the only Son… has made him known” — where Moses was given a back and a rock-cleft. The four markers arrive in the Exodus narrative’s own order, and the request of 33:18 is answered in John 1:14. [S: dig 32:1–34:35, Logos-checked] Confidence: high. New in v0.2.2.
  • 34:29–35 → 2 Corinthians 3:7–18, and note why: the LXX renders qāran (34:29) as δεδόξασται, “had been glorified” — so Paul’s whole doxa argument is reading the word the Greek translator supplied. [S: dig 32:1–34:35, Logos-checked] Confidence: high. New in v0.2.2.

Moralism check. Resist “be brave like the midwives,” “be a bold leader like Moses,” “overcome your excuses like Moses,” “conquer your Red Sea,” “make and keep your vow like Israel,” “be willing to sacrifice like the givers of ch. 35.” Grace precedes every demand — and the digs found the rule to be textual, not merely narrative: the covenant proposal grounds its first “if” in what God has already done (“now therefore,” 19:4–5), and the Ten Words open “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out… of the house of slavery” (20:2) before a single command. And it operates inside the case law too, which is the strongest form of the claim: “You shall not wrong a sojourner… for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt” (22:21); “You know the heart of a sojourner” (23:9); and, in a statute about a poor man’s pledged coat, “if he cries to me, I will hear, for I am gracious” (22:27) — the first occurrence in Exodus of the adjective that stands second in the self-declaration of 34:6. [S: dig 20:22–24:18] The indicative is not only in the preamble; it is in the statutes. At the Sea Israel’s part is to “stand firm… and be silent” (14:13–14); at the door, there is nothing in the lamb to imitate, only something to receive. Preaching Traps

  1. Preaching the plagues as a spectacle (or a one-to-one map onto Egyptian gods). Bites at: 7:1–11:10. Why wrong: 12:12 warrants the theme of judgment on Egypt’s gods, but the specific one-to-one mappings are largely later reconstruction; the point is “that you may know that I am the LORD.” Corrective: preach the self-revelation of God in judgment, and preach the shape — three triads, rising intensity, the Goshen distinction, the magicians’ collapse — not nine sermons on nine disasters. [S: dig 7:1–11:10 confirms]

  2. Moralising Moses into a leadership seminar. Bites at: 2:11–15 and 18:13–27 hardest of all, then 3–4; 32. Why wrong: the book keeps exposing the mediator’s limits so we look past him — the call is answered not by upgrading Moses but by the sufficiency of God, and the fifth objection is marked by the narrator as sin (4:14). At 2:11–15 the narrator supplies two condemning details (he checked no one was watching; he hid the body) and reports his fear of exposure rather than remorse — so “be like Moses, standing up for the oppressed” makes a model of the chapter’s one failure. Corrective: preach the greater Mediator the book reaches for — and at ch. 2, preach the rejection, which is what Acts 7:35 does. Two further passage-level forms named by the 2:1–25 dig: the Midian years as “God’s waiting room” (the text says nothing about Moses’ formation there), and reading the chapter as Moses’ biography when its last three verses change the subject to God. [S: digs 3:1–4:17; 2:1–25]

  3. Treating the tabernacle chapters (25–31; 35–40) as tedious detail to skip. Bites at: the whole Sinai block. Why wrong: the length is the emphasis — Exodus gives more space to this tent than to the plagues, the Sea or the Ten Words — and the creation-shaped, glory-filled climax is the book’s destination. Corrective: preach the tent as the answer to Eden’s lost presence and the anticipation of Immanuel; preach the seven-speech shape and the dwelling purpose rather than the fittings. Tested and confirmed by the 25–31 dig, which adds a passage-level twin: preaching the furniture — assigning a symbolic meaning to each item when the text glosses only a few (the pattern, the testimony, the meeting-place, the remembrance-stones, the guilt-bearing plate). [S: dig 25:1–31:18]

  4. Divorcing law from grace (Sinai as merit). Bites at: 19:5 (“if you will indeed obey…”) and the whole 19–24 block, and hardest at the case law of 21–23. Why wrong: the “if” conditions vocation and status for an already-rescued people, not the rescue itself; v. 4 forecloses the works-reading before the “if” arrives; 20:2 puts the indicative inside the Decalogue’s own grammar; and the case law carries its own motive clauses — “for you were sojourners in the land of Egypt” (22:21; 23:9) and “for I am gracious” (22:27). Corrective: the imperative rides on the indicative — always, and the statutes say so themselves. Tested in the block most exposed to it and confirmed. [S: digs 19:1–8; 19:9–20:21; 20:22–24:18]

  5. Sentimentalising the Red Sea (“name your Red Sea”). Bites at: 13:17–15:21. Why wrong: Israel is silent and helpless; the LORD fights (14:13–14). The Sea also inverts the Nile-as-grave of ch. 1, and the Song is a janus looking back to the rescue and forward to the sanctuary. Corrective: preach accomplished salvation and the reversal; let the matched patterns (1↔︎14; 24↔︎32; 25–31↔︎35–40) shape a series. [S: dig 13:17–15:21 confirms]

  6. Reading the calf (32) as merely Israel’s lapse. Bites at: 32–34. Why wrong: it exposes that the deepest problem is the redeemed people themselves, which no exodus cures — the pressure that makes 34:6–7 and the later new-heart promises necessary. Corrective: preach the stiff neck and the God who stays. Tested and confirmed by the 32–34 dig, with three passage-level forms named there (the Levites’ sword as a model of zeal; “be an intercessor like Moses”; making the calf a sin the congregation has never committed). [S: dig 32:1–34:35]

    ⭐ “A great sin” — the covenant-adultery register is attested. The 32–34 dig flagged ḥeṭeʾ gĕdōlâ (32:21, 30, 31) as possibly carrying an adultery register but could not confirm it. Confirmed: the phrase occurs five times in the OT — once of adultery (Gen 20:9) and four times of idolatry (Exod 32:21, 30, 31; 2 Kgs 17:21) — and the same expression appears in Egyptian marriage contracts and in a Ugaritic text, of adultery. So the idiom is genuinely marital, and the calf is narrated as covenant adultery: “idolatry as adultery at a religious level, leaving the God with whom you have yoked yourself to have an affair with another deity.” [S: Research Assistant] New in v1.2.0.

Warrant correction, v0.2.2: this trap previously read “preach the stiff-necked heart”. Exodus 32–34 never uses the word “heart” — the diagnosis throughout is qĕšēh-ʿōrep, a stiff neck, an animal that will not turn to the rein. The heart language is Deuteronomy’s, and Deuteronomy supplies it while retelling this very episode: “stiff-necked” at Deut 9:6, 13, and then, in the immediate aftermath, “Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart, and be no more stiff-necked” (Deut 10:16), leading to 29:4 and 30:6. Exodus’s own next use of “heart” runs the other way — the willing hearts of 35:5, 21, 22, 26, 29. If nearness were safe, none of those seven chapters would be necessary. Corrective: hold both; the cost is the measure of the desire. [S: dig 25:1–31:18] New in v0.2.1. 8. The Bezalel vocation sermon, detached from its object. Bites at: 31:1–11. Why wrong: the Spirit-filling is the sixth of seven speeches and is for this building; lifted out, it becomes a general encouragement about creativity that the text is not giving. (It is, though, the first place in the canon where anyone is said to be filled with the Spirit of God — Gen 41:38 describes Spirit-gifting in other words.) Corrective: God specifies, then God equips. ⭐ The hardening of Pharaoh — and a correction to this document. This overview and the plagues dig have described a “verb-marked shift at 9:12” from Pharaoh hardening himself to God hardening him. The distribution is real but the shift is less clean than we have presented it. Childs’s tabulation: kābēd in the hiphil with Pharaoh as subject (8:11, 28; 9:34), once in the qal with Pharaoh’s heart as subject (9:7), and only once in the hiphil with Yahweh (10:1); ḥāzaq typically piel with Yahweh (4:21; 9:12; 10:20, 27; 11:10; 14:17) but also qal with Pharaoh’s heart (7:13, 22; 8:15; 9:15); qāšâ only twice — 7:3 (Yahweh) and 13:15 (Pharaoh). 9:12 is Yahweh’s first direct hardening, but Pharaoh has already hardened himself three times before God acts, and the verbs interleave rather than divide. The theological point stands and is better for the correction: “the Lord states his intention to harden, yet as events unfold Pharaoh hardens his own heart” — a process and a give-and-take, not a sudden reversal (Niehaus; Motyer; Childs, OTL, 171). Preach the interleaving, not a switch. [S: Research Assistant] [revised: research-assistant pass]

The identity of “the destroyer” (12:23) — worked. The ambiguity is deliberate and unresolved by the text: 12:23 presents an angelic agent, while 12:13 and 12:27 have God himself destroying. This is the OT’s broader pattern of mediated divine action (2 Sam 24:16; the angel against Sennacherib; 1 Cor 10:10, probably invoking Num 16). Early thought regarded the destroyer as God’s emissary, not clearly distinguished from God himself; later Judaism increasingly gave it a distinct identity (Watson, AYBD 2:159–60; Edwards, ISBE). Preach the ambiguity — it is the text’s, not ours. [S: Research Assistant]

The 430 years (12:40) — worked. MT: 430 years in Egypt. LXX and Samaritan: 430 years in Canaan and Egypt, giving roughly 215 in Egypt. Paul follows the LXX/Samaritan reckoning at Galatians 3:16–17, dating the 430 years from the promise to Abraham to the giving of the law — as did Josephus and most Christian interpreters before the twentieth century. Kitchen and Mitchell prefer the MT on the ground that 12:41 implies 430 from Jacob’s entry. Both readings are live; Paul’s is the one a congregation will meet. [S: Research Assistant]

The aspect of ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh (3:14) — worked, and the ambiguity is the point. Grammar favours the future (the imperfect ʾehyeh), making the statement soteriological — a promise of continuing activity — while the LXX and Vulgate read it as present, making it ontological. Context tilts toward “I AM”, since 3:14 echoes 3:12’s “I will be with you”. Blackburn’s conclusion is the one to preach: the response is intentionally open, refusing to confine the name within fixed boundaries and leaving it oriented toward further revelation. The tension may be intentional rather than resolvable. [S: Research Assistant]

21:22–25 — the ʾāsôn referent, worked and still contested. Three positions, now named: (a) ʾāsôn = death of the mother (the traditional reading, with ANE parallels and rabbinic roots); (b) ʾāsôn refers to the child — a live premature birth versus a stillbirth (Jackson), which would give the fetus a status close to that of a person; (c) Westbrook’s “unknown perpetrator” reading, and Sprinkle’s “an injury requiring medical attention”. Since ʾāsôn occurs elsewhere only at Genesis 42:38 and 44:29, precision is not available; Alexander’s verdict is that it most likely denotes “serious injury”, which could include death but need not (Greengus, AYBD 4:248; Alexander, Apollos OTC 2:484–85). Name the options; do not settle it from the pulpit. [S: Research Assistant]

⭐ The ANE background is now citable rather than assumed (Research Assistant pass). Across the sweep, the historical-background claims were tagged [S] and unverified, with a standing instruction to check before asserting specifics. Most are now verified, and several are sharper than expected:

  • Corvée and the two-tier supervision of 5:6–19. Well attested in New Kingdom Egypt. The tomb of the vizier Rekhmire (c. 1450 BC) depicts a mixed-race gang making bricks under Egyptian overseers with batons, roughly ten men per overseer. [S] High.
  • Brick quotas, with a figure. A leather scroll from Year 5 of Rameses II (c. 1275 BC) lists forty junior taskmasters, each with a target of 2,000 bricks for his gang. Papyri record that workers “are making their quota of bricks daily”. This is the citable number the 4:18–6:30 dig said not to assert without a check. [S] High.
  • The straw shortage of 5:7–13. Straw was standard in 19th–20th Dynasty brick-making because it made stronger bricks, and the complaint is documented: an official at Qenqenento reports “there are neither men to make bricks nor straw in the neighbourhood.” [S] High.
  • The tabernacle’s construction technology. Tutankhamun’s four nested gilded shrines — demountable wooden panels, mortice-and-tenon joints, sliding bolts, with protective cherub-like figures on the innermost — are the closest known parallels, and the technique goes back to the bedroom suite of Queen Hetepheres (c. 2500 BC). The commentators are careful about what this demonstrates: shared craft technology, not borrowed theology. [S] Moderate–high.
  • One qualification on the Hammurabi comparison. Of the three differences this document has carried, two hold securely — the covenant framing (Hammurabi’s laws do not treat religious affairs) and the absence of class-stratified penalties (Hammurabi grades by freedman / state dependent / slave; the Book of the Covenant does not, and protects the lower orders). The third — that property crimes are non-capital — “requires important qualification” and should not be asserted flatly. [S] [revised: research-assistant pass]
  • The Sargon birth-legend. Real surface similarities (reed basket, bitumen, river), but scholarly opinion divides sharply on literary dependence. The dig’s instruction to check before raising it from the pulpit was right; raise it as a parallel, not a source. [S] Moderate.
  • The Song of the Sea’s archaic date. Rests on linguistic method — chiefly verbal patterns and the longer form of the 3rd-person masculine plural verbal suffix, which runs through 15:1b–18 and is entirely replaced by the shorter form in later standard poetry. Individual features carry weight only when clustered. [S] Moderate–high.

⭐ And one caution to strengthen: the four cups. The Passover seder’s four cups are popularly linked to the four “I will” statements of 6:6–7. The link is not ancient. The four cups were added to the seder over time, and the exact shape of the first-century Passover cannot be established. The neat correspondence is a popular-source claim, not a documented one — do not present it as ancient practice. [S: Research Assistant] [revised: research-assistant pass]

Two landmines now worked (v1.0.3), and how to preach them.

  • “Bridegroom of blood” (4:24–26). Worked, not solved. The LORD’s target is Moses, not the son — the syntax and the sense both run that way. For the series as a whole: read across fifteen runs, Exodus is a more unified and more pessimistic book than a standard series treats it as. Unified, because its shape is carried by a few devices working at book scale — a verb descending from a bush to a tent, a thesis planted six times before it is stated, a creation frame closed in the final chapter, and two questions that divide the book cleanly. Pessimistic, because the sweep’s most persistent finding is not Egypt’s power but Israel’s: a “yes” that collapses four times over, a stiff neck no exodus cures, and a mediator who is authenticated, then subdivided, then shut out of the tent he built. The book’s own answer is not a better people but a nearer God — it ends on a cloud over a tent, a ransom under every upright, and a way in that has not yet opened. Three consequences for preaching: the tabernacle block is not optional (it answers 17:7); grace-before-law should be preached from the architecture, not from 20:2 alone; and the sweep consistently refused the sermon that makes a human being the hero — not Moses, not the midwives, not Aaron and Hur, not Jethro.

Two honest nils from the sweep: Exodus quotes no earlier Scripture (it is a source, not a borrower — which is why internal echo produced the richest material in nearly every run), and no macro-argument emerged from type-scene or symbolic-number patterning — this book’s structure runs on repetition and placement.
