Book Overview: Exodus

Translation: ESV Genre: OT historical narrative with embedded law and liturgy; the second volume of the Torah Mode: Finalise/Upgrade — built on the completed Exodus whole-book sweep (fifteen units) and the Torah master overview; Schnittjer Torah Story companion consulted at the checking stage Date: 27 July 2026 Sweep status: Whole-book sweep in hand; ⭐ HIGH units await solo digs before the pulpit

Sits beneath book-overview-torah.md (the master). Inherit from it: the presence-spine, Genesis-as-live-source, the grace-before-law rule, and the mediator trajectory. This overview works Exodus in that frame.

Presenting Situation

Narrator / author. Mosaic narrative in the third person, with large blocks of first-person divine speech (the law, the tabernacle instructions); Moses is told to write (17:14; 24:4; 34:27). Retrospective in final form. [T] / [S] (composition debates — flag, hang nothing on them).

Audience. Narrative-world: the enslaved-then-redeemed generation. Compositional: wilderness Israel with the tabernacle standing in the camp, hearing how that tent — and the God who fills it — came to be among them. Canonical: later Israel (and the church) reading the paradigm of redemption. Default to the canonical audience; keep the wilderness camp in the ear, because Exodus explains the reader’s own institutions (Passover, Sabbath, tabernacle, covenant). [T] / [I]

Occasion. The founding of Israel as the LORD’s redeemed, covenant, God-indwelt people — from a family of seventy under a death-sentence to a nation with God dwelling in its midst. [T]

Pressure. How can the God of the fathers keep his sworn word (Gen 15:13–16) when his people are enslaved and targeted for death — and, once redeemed, how can a holy God dwell among a stiff-necked people without consuming them? The book’s own refrain frames the aim: “that you may know that I am the LORD” (6:7; 7:5; 14:4, 18; 29:46). [T]

Purpose. To reveal the LORD as the covenant-keeping Redeemer who defeats the powers, binds his people to himself at Sinai, and comes down to dwell — moving the reader from a God who hears and remembers (2:24) to a God whose glory fills the tent (40:34). [I]

Structural Arc Map

Verses Movement Function
1:1–15:21 From Egypt — bondage to deliverance The assault on the seed (1), the deliverer (2), the call (3–4), the contest of the plagues (7–11), Passover (12), the Sea and its Song (14–15): God redeems his people by blood and water
15:22–18:27 Through the wilderness — the journey to the mountain Testing at the water, manna, the rock, and Amalek (15–17); Jethro’s confession and the delegation of judges (18): the redeemed are formed and begin to grumble
19:1–40:38 At the mountain — covenant and dwelling Sinai’s terror (19), the Ten Words (20), the Book of the Covenant sealed in blood (21–24), the tabernacle instructions (25–31), the calf and the revelation of glory (32–34), the dwelling built and filled (35–40): God covenants with Israel and comes to dwell

Device. A linear, spatial build — from Egypt → through the wilderness → at the mountain — mapping the progressive proximity of God: deliverance → journey → dwelling. The proportions are the point: nearly half the book stays at one mountain, because the weight of Exodus is not the escape from Egypt but how a holy God can dwell with a sinful people, the question chs. 32–34 nearly answer in the negative and chs. 35–40 answer in grace. Two large command→fulfilment doublings (instructions 25–31 ↔︎ construction 35–40) and an ascent→descent pattern (Moses up into glory, ch. 24 ↔︎ down into the calf, ch. 32) shape the Sinai block. [T] / [S: Schnittjer confirms the three-movement spatial shape]

Microscript

The God of the fathers keeps his sworn word: hearing his enslaved people, he comes down to redeem them by the blood of a lamb and through the sea, binds them to himself in covenant at Sinai, and — the true summit — comes down to dwell in their midst, so that the glory that filled creation now fills a tent among a stiff-necked people who deserve to be consumed and instead are graced. Keynotes: “come down” / “dwell” (yārad / šākan); the refrain “that you may know that I am the LORD.” Structural pulse: deliverance → covenant → dwelling; and grace before law, always. [T]

Test against the movements: from-Egypt (redemption) → wilderness (formation) → mountain (covenant and dwelling). Each serves the presence-spine; the calf (32–34) is the crisis that makes the final dwelling an act of sheer grace.

Intertextual Map

Exodus is carried by internal repetition and by Genesis, not by external citation (there is no earlier Scripture for it to quote except Genesis, which it re-cites at 1:1–5). For the passage digs: track Genesis and the Abrahamic oath as the confirmed live source, and lean on Move 4 (internal echo) for the book’s large split patterns.

Consequence for the sweep. Exodus supplies more of the NT’s salvation vocabulary than almost any other book; teaching it well equips a congregation to read most of the rest of Scripture. Externally source-less, internally dense — so give Genesis echoes a raised prior and hunt the internal split-patterns. [T] / [S: reception]

Christological Trajectory

  • Passover / substitution. The firstborn-son claim (4:22–23) sets a fuse that burns to the Passover, where blood substitutes for the firstborn (12), completed at the Sea (14) and sealed in “the blood of the covenant” (24:8). “Christ our Passover lamb has been sacrificed” (1 Cor 5:7); the Supper is the new covenant blood (Matt 26:28). Confidence: high.
  • Redemption / new exodus. The whole rescue becomes the template: Jesus’ death is an exodos (Luke 9:31); Isaiah 40–55’s new exodus reads Exodus forward; Israel-brought-from-Egypt is applied to Christ (Matt 2:15 ← Hos 11:1). Confidence: high.
  • The mediator who points beyond himself. Moses is commissioned (3), begged for by a terrified people (20:19), and stands between wrath and Israel after the calf, offering to be blotted out (32:32) — an offer that exposes its own limit (he cannot be blotted out for them). The Christ-shaped vacancy the NT fills (1 Tim 2:5; Heb 3; 8–9; and the one who could be accursed, Rom 9:3). Confidence: high.
  • Presence / tabernacle. God comes down to dwell (25:8; 40:34) → the Word “tabernacled among us” (John 1:14) → “the dwelling of God is with man” (Rev 21:3). The glory that fills the tent is the goal the whole book climbs toward. Confidence: high.
  • The character of God at 34:6–7. “Merciful and gracious… forgiving iniquity… who will by no means clear the guilty” — a tension the OT states but cannot resolve, answered at the cross where guilt is borne, not cleared (Rom 3:25–26). Confidence: high.

Moralism check. Resist “be brave like the midwives,” “be a bold leader like Moses,” “be willing to sacrifice like the givers of ch. 35.” Grace precedes every demand: God redeems before he legislates; the Decalogue opens “I brought you out of the house of slavery” (20:2) before a single command; the tabernacle offerings are the grateful response of the already-redeemed. God is the Redeemer-hero; Moses points to the greater Mediator; the people receive grace as we do. Preach the indicative of rescue under every imperative. [T]

Preaching Traps

  1. Preaching the plagues as a spectacle (or a one-to-one map onto Egyptian gods). Bites at: 7:1–11:10. Why wrong: 12:12 warrants the theme of judgment on Egypt’s gods, but the specific one-to-one mappings are largely later reconstruction; the point is “that you may know that I am the LORD.” Corrective: preach the self-revelation of God in judgment, and route the hardening question (divine/human agency, Rom 9) to careful study.
  2. Moralising Moses into a leadership seminar. Bites at: 3–4; 18; 32. Why wrong: the book keeps exposing the mediator’s limits so we look past him. Corrective: preach the greater Mediator the book reaches for.
  3. Treating the tabernacle chapters (25–31; 35–40) as tedious detail to skip. Bites at: the whole Sinai block. Why wrong: the repetition (“as the LORD commanded Moses”) and the creation-shaped, glory-filled climax are the book’s destination — God dwelling with his people. Corrective: preach the tent as the answer to Eden’s lost presence and the anticipation of Immanuel.
  4. Divorcing law from grace (Sinai as merit). Bites at: 19–24. Why wrong: redemption precedes the law; the covenant is the response of a rescued people. Corrective: the imperative rides on the indicative — always.
  5. Sentimentalising the Red Sea / missing the internal patterns. Bites at: 14–15. Why wrong: the Sea inverts the Nile-as-grave of ch. 1; the Song is a janus looking back and forward. Corrective: preach the reversal and let the matched patterns (1↔︎7; 24↔︎32; 25–31↔︎35–40) shape a series.
  6. Reading the calf (32) as merely Israel’s lapse. Bites at: 32–34. Why wrong: it exposes that the deepest problem is the redeemed people’s own heart, which no exodus cures — the pressure that makes 34:6–7 and the new-heart promises necessary. Corrective: preach the stiff-necked heart and the God who stays.

Pastoral / interpretive landmines (flag). The hardening of Pharaoh (divine and human agency); the “bridegroom of blood” crux (4:24–26); the sense of 6:3; and the aspect of ʾehyeh ʾăšer ʾehyeh (3:14, “I AM” / “I WILL BE”). Give each careful solo work before the pulpit. [S: sweep open questions]

A fifteen-part series maps cleanly; several units are best preached as matched pairs (1↔︎7 the Nile/Sea; 25–31↔︎35–40 instruction/construction). The tabernacle blocks can be compressed to one or two sermons if series length is tight, but do not cut the glory-filling (ch. 40) — it is the destination.
