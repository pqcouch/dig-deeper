Dig Deeper: Exodus 15:22–17:16

Translation: ESV Pulpit translation: ESV (Anglicised) — same text as the report default Date: 6 August 2026 Book-overview context: book-overview-exodus.md v.1.0.3 (finalised) — active layer Series context: Exodus preaching sweep, unit 8 of 15. Fourteenth solo dig. One Standard-weight unit remains after this: 18:1–27.

The Passage

Sixty-one verses across four episodes. The full text of 15:22–27, chapter 16 and chapter 17 was verified against a public-domain text (KJV) before the tools were worked, including a count of the grumbling and testing vocabulary. Quoted ESV wording below is held at working-memory confidence; the load-bearing verses are given, the rest summarised by range.

15:22–27 — Marah and Elim. Three days in the wilderness of Shur without water; the water at Marah is bitter; the people grumble; the LORD shows Moses a log, and the water is made sweet. Then: “There he made for them a statute and a rule, and there he tested them, saying, ‘If you will diligently listen to the voice of the LORD your God, and do that which is right in his eyes, and give ear to his commandments and keep all his statutes, I will put none of the diseases on you that I put on the Egyptians, for I am the LORD, your healer’” (vv. 25b–26). They come to Elim — twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees.

16:1–36 — Bread from heaven. The wilderness of Sin, the fifteenth day of the second month. The whole congregation grumbles: “Would that we had died by the hand of the LORD in the land of Egypt, when we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full, for you have brought us out into this wilderness to kill this whole assembly with hunger” (v. 3). The LORD answers: “Behold, I am about to rain bread from heaven for you, and the people shall go out and gather a day’s portion every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not” (v. 4). Moses to the people: “Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD” (v. 8). The glory appears in the cloud (v. 10). Quail at evening, and in the morning a fine flake-like thing: “When the people of Israel saw it, they said to one another, ‘What is it?’ For they did not know what it was” (v. 15). An omer each; no leftovers (vv. 16–21); double on the sixth day; “Tomorrow is a day of solemn rest, a holy Sabbath to the LORD” (v. 23); some go out on the seventh and find none; “How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?” (v. 28). An omer of manna is kept in a jar “before the Testimony” (v. 34). Closing narrator’s notes: forty years of manna, and “an omer is the tenth part of an ephah” (vv. 35–36).

17:1–7 — Water from the rock. Rephidim; no water; the people quarrel with Moses, who answers, “Why do you quarrel with me? Why do you test the LORD?” (v. 2). Moses cries out: “They are almost ready to stone me” (v. 4). The LORD: “Take in your hand the staff with which you struck the Nile, and go… Behold, I will stand before you there on the rock at Horeb, and you shall strike the rock, and water shall come out of it” (vv. 5–6). “And he called the name of the place Massah and Meribah, because of the quarrelling of the people of Israel, and because they tested the LORD by saying, ‘Is the LORD among us or not?’” (v. 7).

17:8–16 — War with Amalek. Amalek comes and fights Israel at Rephidim. Joshua leads the men out; Moses stands on the hilltop with the staff of God; when his hand is up Israel prevails, when it drops Amalek prevails; Aaron and Hur hold his hands steady until sunset. Joshua overwhelms Amalek. “Write this as a memorial in a book and recite it in the ears of Joshua, that I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven” (v. 14). Moses builds an altar and calls it The LORD Is My Banner, saying, “A hand upon the throne of the LORD! The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation” (vv. 15–16).

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

  1. Israel asks the book’s second great question at 17:7 — “Is the LORD among us or not?” — and it is the redeemed insider’s counterpart to Pharaoh’s “Who is the LORD?” at 5:2. Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Vocabulary, Narrator’s Comment, Internal echo (Move 4), Biblical Theology. Pharaoh’s question is answered by the plagues (“that you may know that I am the LORD”); Israel’s is answered by the tabernacle (“that I may dwell in their midst”, 25:8). 29:46 answers both in a single sentence — they shall know that I am the LORD… that I might dwell among them. The two halves of Exodus each answer one question. [T] for both questions; [I] for the pairing. High confidence.

  2. The block runs on a bidirectional testing thread, and the balance is exact: God tests Israel twice, Israel tests God twice. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Vocabulary, Original Languages, Narrator’s Comment. Nissâ at 15:25 (“there he tested them”) and 16:4 (“that I may test them”) — God the subject; at 17:2 and 17:7 — Israel the subject, God the object. The place is then named Massah, “Testing”, from that root. The wilderness is a test-bench, and both parties are on it — but only one of them is entitled to be. [T] High confidence.

  3. Law, tôrâ, and Sabbath are all given — and all broken — before Sinai. Surfaced by: Structure, Positional Necessity, Narrator’s Comment, Linking Words, Schnittjer Pass. A “statute and a rule” at Marah (15:25); “whether they will walk in my law (tôrâ)” at 16:4; the Sabbath given, kept, and violated at 16:23–30; and God’s own verdict, “How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?” (16:28) — all before chapter 19. Sinai does not introduce law to Israel; it covenants and codifies a law Israel already has and has already failed. [T] High confidence.

  4. Grumbling is the block’s leading word — and the word is a wilderness word found almost nowhere else in the OT. Surfaced by: Repetition, Tone, Narrator’s Comment, Linking Words, Author’s Purpose. The verb lûn occurs five times (15:24; 16:2, 7, 8; 17:3), with the cognate noun tĕlunnôt accounting for the rest of the ten English renderings — eight of the ten fall in chapter 16, six of those in Moses’ short speech at vv. 7–9. 15:24 is the verb’s first appearance in the Old Testament, and its OT usage is confined to these wilderness narratives and the Numbers parallels. Moses supplies the interpretation the episode needs: “Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD.” The complaint is horizontal in form and vertical in fact — and the commentators are clear it is open rebellion, not mere disgruntlement. [T] for the distribution; [S: logos-research] for the lexical restriction. High confidence. [revised: logos-research]

  5. The staff that struck the Nile is the staff that strikes the rock — and the text makes the link itself. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Internal echo, Christological Reading, Translations. “Take in your hand the staff with which you struck the Nile” (17:5) is an explicit narrator-level cross-reference to 7:20. The instrument that made water undrinkable now makes rock give drink. And the LORD adds something easy to miss: “I will stand before you there on the rock” — he positions himself on the thing that is about to be struck. [T] for the staff-link, which the text states; [I] for pressing the standing-on-the-rock detail. High confidence on the echo; moderate on the pressure.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level — taken from the overview (v.1.0.3) rather than re-derived: to reveal the LORD as the covenant-keeping Redeemer who defeats the powers, binds his people to himself at Sinai, and comes down to dwell; the book’s own thesis sentence is 29:46. [S: book-overview]

Passage level — this block exists to show what the redeemed people are actually like, and what their God is actually like, in the gap between rescue and covenant. Three things it contributes that no other unit does:

  • It supplies the human material Sinai will be offered to. The people who say “All that the LORD has spoken we will do” (19:8) are the people the reader has just watched grumble at every stage of a six-week journey. The block is not a detour before the mountain; it is the character reference the mountain scene requires.
  • It establishes daily dependence as the shape of the redeemed life. A day’s portion every day; no stockpiling; what is hoarded rots. This is not incidental colour — 16:4 makes it the content of a test, and Deuteronomy 8:3 will make it the lesson of the whole wilderness.
  • It plants the question the second half of the book answers. 17:7 is not a throwaway complaint; the narrator preserves it as the reason two places got their names.

A note on what the block is not doing. It is not primarily teaching about God’s provision in our difficulties, though it does that. Its own repeated interpretive word is test — the passage tells the reader four times what is going on, and “test” is the word it uses. [T]

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. The Song of the Sea (15:1–21) ends by looking past the sea to arrival: “You will bring them in and plant them on your own mountain, the place, O LORD, which you have made for your abode, the sanctuary” (15:17), closing with “The LORD will reign for ever and ever” (15:18). Immediately before that, 14:31: “the people feared the LORD, and they believed in the LORD and in his servant Moses.” And behind both stands 3:12, the sign given at the bush: “you shall serve God on this mountain.”

Necessity answer — why here, and not elsewhere. Four reasons, and they compound:

  1. It is the only place a journey can go. The Song has promised arrival; the arrival is at 19:1–2. This block is the distance between them, and the text measures it in itineraries and dates (15:22, 27; 16:1; 17:1) — the only stretch of Exodus that moves.
  2. It must precede the covenant proposal, because it establishes the people’s actual condition. 19:4–6 is addressed to a people whose behaviour the reader has now seen. Preach 19:8’s vow without this block and it sounds admirable; preach it after, and it sounds like a promise made by people who have grumbled four times in six weeks. The block is what makes chapter 32 unsurprising rather than shocking.
  3. It must precede Sinai because it puts law before Sinai. 15:25’s statute, 16:4’s tôrâ, and 16:28’s “my commandments and my laws” mean that when the Decalogue arrives, Israel is not receiving law for the first time — they are receiving it as covenant, having already demonstrated they cannot keep it. This is a structural argument about the relationship of law and grace built into the itinerary.
  4. It must precede the tabernacle block because it asks the question the tabernacle answers. “Is the LORD among us or not?” (17:7) is answered at 25:8 (“that I may dwell in their midst”), threatened at 33:3 (“I will not go up among you”), pleaded at 34:9, and settled at 40:34. Chapters 25–40 are, among other things, a sixteen-chapter answer to a question asked at a dry well in chapter 17. [I] on the framing; [T] on each verse. High confidence.

Implication for this passage. The block’s position converts it from a string of miracle-stories into an argument. It reads as: this is who they are; this is what they ask; now watch what God offers them at the mountain, and what he builds in their midst.

Revisited after Tool 11. Move 4 sharpened item 4 and added a fifth: the Sabbath legislation of 31:12–17 and 35:1–3 presupposes a Sabbath already given and already broken (16:27–30). The Sinai command is not the institution of the Sabbath but the covenanting of it.

General context

Immediately before: the Song of the Sea and Miriam’s refrain (15:1–21) — the high-water mark of Israel’s confidence in the book. Immediately after: Jethro’s arrival, confession and counsel (18:1–27), then Sinai (19:1). The section: the overview’s second movement, “Through the wilderness — the journey to the mountain” (15:22–18:27), of which this is the larger part.

Widely read out of context? Yes, in three places, and all three are flagged under Pitfalls: 15:26 (“I am the LORD, your healer”), 17:11–12 (Moses’ raised hands as a prayer-meeting text), and 16:18 (the manna’s equalisation, which Paul does apply — to money).

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
15:22–27 Bitter water made sweet — Shur, Marah, Elim Test 1 (God tests Israel). A statute and a rule; the healer-name; then twelve springs and seventy palms
16:1–36 Bread from heaven — the wilderness of Sin Test 2 (God tests Israel). Daily portion, the glory in the cloud, the Sabbath given and broken, the jar kept before the Testimony
17:1–7 Water from the rock — Rephidim Test 3 (Israel tests God). Massah and Meribah; “Is the LORD among us or not?”

Device — A–B–A′ with a coda that breaks the pattern. Water (A), bread (B), water (A′) is a clean frame; the food account sits inside a water bracket. The war is a fourth panel that does not belong to the sequence, and its non-belonging is the point.

The pattern, and the break. The first three episodes run an identical six-beat sequence:

  1. itinerary notice → (ii) a lack → (iii) the people grumble → (iv) Moses cries out / the LORD speaks → (v) provision → (vi) a naming or a statute.

The fourth episode keeps the itinerary (17:8, “Then Amalek came”) and then breaks three of the remaining beats: the threat is an enemy, not a lack; there is no grumbling at all; and the closing is not a name for a place but an altar, a book and an oath. The break is admissible precisely because the pattern is citable — three instances, six beats each. What it says: the wilderness has two kinds of trouble, and Israel only complains about one of them. [T]

A qualification from the checking stage, and it narrows the claim. Dozeman reads the Amalek episode as following the general form of the preceding stories and reusing their key words (Eerdmans Critical Commentary, 397) — i.e. as continuous with them rather than as a rupture. He is right about the form and I overstated the break in my first draft. The accurate statement is narrower: the fourth panel keeps the shape and the vocabulary, and the one beat it drops is the grumbling. That single omission is the finding, and it is stronger for being smaller — the only episode in the block where Israel faces a threat and does not complain is the one where the threat is an enemy rather than an absence. [revised: logos-research] High confidence on the omission; the wider “breaks every beat” framing withdrawn.

Proportion. 6 / 36 / 7 / 9 verses. Chapter 16 is longer than the other three episodes combined, and the narrator brakes hardest on the gathering regulations and the Sabbath. The emphasis is not the miracle of the bread but the discipline of the bread — which is exactly what 16:4 said the test would be about.

A smaller frame inside 17. Both halves of chapter 17 happen at Rephidim (17:1, 8). The question of v. 7 and the answer of vv. 8–16 share a location. [T]

4. Linking Words

Narrative, so connectors are sparse — but four carry real weight.

  • 15:26, the conditional. “If you will diligently listen to the voice of the LORD your God… I will put none of the diseases on you.” This is the first conditional covenant sentence in Exodus, and its construction is repeated almost exactly at 19:5 (“if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my covenant, you shall be my treasured possession”). Conditional, not evidential — the blessing is contingent. The covenant “if” is rehearsed at a bitter well four chapters before the mountain. [T] High confidence.
  • 16:8, the ←for←. “…for the LORD has heard your grumbling that you grumble against him. What are we? Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD.” The “for” grounds Moses’ whole disclaimer: the reason he can deflect the complaint is that its true addressee has already heard it.
  • 16:29, cause and consequence in one breath. “See! For the LORD has given you the Sabbath; therefore on the sixth day he gives you bread for two days.” The Sabbath is stated as a gift (←for←) before the double portion is explained as its consequence (→therefore→). Order matters: gift, then arrangement, then command. [T]
  • 17:16, the ←for←. “For he said, ‘A hand upon the throne of the LORD!…’” — the narrator grounds the altar’s name in Moses’ oath-saying. (The clause itself is a crux; see Tool 8 and Textual Variants.)

One near-connector worth noting: 17:8’s “Then Amalek came” is bare parataxis in the Hebrew — no logical connector at all. English “then” is sequence, not cause. Resist reading it as “and so, because they doubted, God sent Amalek.” The text does not say that. [T]

5. Parallels

Prose, so mostly N/A — but two constructions are worth the space.

15:26 — a fourfold piling-up. Four near-synonymous clauses stacked:

if you will diligently listen to the voice of the LORD your God / and do what is right in his eyes / and give ear to his commandments / and keep all his statutes

Synonymous accumulation, not four separate conditions — the effect is comprehensiveness, and it prefigures the way Deuteronomy will talk. [T] Moderate–high.

16:8 — a chiastic disclaimer. “…your grumbling which you grumble against him — and what are we? Your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD.” The “us” is enclosed by the LORD on both sides, which is the sentence’s whole argument. Moderate — the effect is clearer in Hebrew word order than in English; flagged for verification.

N/A for the rest: this is narrative prose with no sustained poetic structure. (Contrast the immediately preceding unit, which is the Song of the Sea — the shift from poetry to prose at 15:22 is itself a structural marker.)

6. Narrator’s Comment

Unusually rich — and three of the comments are written from far outside the story.

Verse Comment What it does
15:23b “Therefore it was named Marah” Etiology; glosses the name as “bitter”
15:25b “There he made for them a statute and a rule, and there he tested them” The interpretive key to the whole episode. Without it, Marah is a miracle story. With it, it is a test — and a legislative moment
16:31 “…called its name manna. It was like coriander seed, white, and the taste of it was like wafers made with honey” Descriptive gloss for readers who have never seen it
16:34 “…so Aaron placed it before the Testimony to be kept” The Testimony does not exist yet in narrative time — the ark is commanded at 25:16 and built at 37:1. A proleptic notice
16:35 “The people of Israel ate the manna forty years… till they came to the border of the land of Canaan” Written from beyond the wilderness. The narrator knows how the forty years ended
16:36 “An omer is the tenth part of an ephah” A gloss for readers who no longer use the measure — the clearest signal in the block of a readership at some distance from the events
17:7 “…because of the quarrelling… and because they tested the LORD, saying, ‘Is the LORD among us or not?’” Supplies both the reason for the names and the quotation — the narrator preserves the sentence itself

Two observations the preacher should have. First, 15:25b and 17:7 are the two comments that carry the block’s theology, and both use the testing vocabulary — the narrator is telling the reader what genre of event these are. Second, the cluster at 16:34–36 places the narrator at a considerable remove: after the ark exists, after the forty years, and after the omer has fallen out of use. [T] for the observations; [I] for what they imply about composition, and nothing here is [T] about authorship or date — the notices show distance, not a name or a decade.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory (run before triage). Places: wilderness of Shur, Marah, Elim, wilderness of Sin, Sinai, Rephidim, Massah, Meribah, Horeb, Egypt, Canaan. Persons/peoples: Israel, Moses, Aaron, Joshua, Hur, Amalek, the Egyptians. Numbers: three days, twelve springs, seventy palm trees, the fifteenth day of the second month, sixth/seventh day, forty years, an omer, a tenth of an ephah.

Routine scenery: Shur, Sin, Rephidim, Egypt, Canaan, Moses, Aaron, Israel — no canonical freight beyond their ordinary reference in this book. Four candidates carry freight and get the full check: Amalek, Joshua, Horeb, and the Elim numbers. Marah, Massah and Meribah are name-wordplays glossed by the text itself.

Amalek — the load-bearing hyperlink, with an outstanding word attached.

Load-the-story (source narrative re-read, not recalled): Genesis 36:12 — “Timna was a concubine of Eliphaz, Esau’s son; she bore Amalek to Eliphaz.” Amalek is Esau’s grandson. The genealogy sits inside the tôledôt of Esau, and it follows Isaac’s word over Esau at Gen 27:40: “by your sword you shall live.” Israel’s first war as a nation is against Jacob’s brother’s line, fought by men who live by the sword because their ancestor was told he would. [T] for the genealogy; [I] for pressing the Jacob–Esau frame. Moderate–high.

Outstanding-word check — this is the strongest form of the device and it fires here. 17:14 and 17:16 are an unresolved divine oath: “I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek from under heaven” and “The LORD will have war with Amalek from generation to generation.” The word is left hanging, and the canon detonates it in stages:

  • Deuteronomy 25:17–19 — the command re-issued to the next generation: “Remember what Amalek did to you… you shall blot out the memory of Amalek.” Note the irony the Hebrew sets up: Israel must remember in order to blot out the memory.
  • 1 Samuel 15 — Saul is sent to execute the oath, spares Agag, and is rejected as king for it. The unfinished word of Exodus 17 is the thing Saul’s kingship founders on.
  • 1 Samuel 30; 2 Samuel 1 — Amalekites raid Ziklag; an Amalekite claims to have finished Saul off.
  • Esther 3:1 — Haman the Agagite. The genocide-plot against the Jews in the diaspora is launched by a descendant of the king Saul spared.

An oath sworn at Rephidim drives a plot line across four books and is still unresolved when Esther opens. This is a high-significance citation and it receives Move 1 treatment under Tool 11. [T] for each link; [I] for the chain as a single argument. High confidence.

Joshua — first appearance in the canon. He arrives at 17:9 with no patronymic, no introduction, and no explanation, as though the reader already knows him — and is immediately given two roles: he fights, and he is the one in whose ears the Amalek memorial must be recited (17:14). Name: Yĕhôšuaʿ, “the LORD saves”; the Greek carrier is Iēsous, the same form as “Jesus” (cf. Heb 4:8, where the ambiguity is exegetically live). Note that Numbers 13:16 records Moses renaming Hoshea to Joshua, so the name here is used proleptically. [T] for the appearance and the two roles; high confidence on the Greek carrier.

Hur. First appearance; recurs at 24:14 (left with Aaron in charge while Moses ascends) and — if the same man — at 31:2 and 35:30 as Bezalel’s grandfather. A minor figure with a surprising downstream: the man who held up Moses’ hand is grandfather to the man who builds the tabernacle. Moderate — the identification is standard but not stated in the text.

Horeb (17:6). They are at Rephidim, but the rock is “at Horeb” — the mountain of God (3:1), where the bush burned and where they are heading. Water comes out of the mountain before Israel arrives at the mountain. [T] High confidence, and easily missed.

Elim’s twelve and seventy (15:27). Twelve springs and seventy palms, in a book that opened by naming twelve sons and counting seventy persons (1:1–5) — and which will shortly seat seventy elders before God (24:1, 9). The text does not comment, which is why this stays moderate: it is the kind of number-pairing the Torah does deliberately, but here it is unglossed. Report it; don’t build on it.

Paronomasia check (words repeated three or more times). Lûn recurs ten times and shows no root-play with the passage’s theological register. But nāsâ (test, N-S-H) recurs four times and the block ends on nēs (banner, N-S-S) at 17:15 — different roots, genuinely similar sound, in a passage that has just named a place “Testing”. The assonance is real; the etymology is not. Flagged moderate at best under the hard rule and routed to verification — this is exactly the claim that gets over-preached.

One pulpit note. “The wilderness of Sin” (16:1) has nothing to do with the English word “sin”; it is a place name, probably related to Sinai. A congregation following in English will hear the pun, and it is not there. Say so in half a sentence.

8. Translations

16:15 — the most consequential divergence in the block.

  • ESV / NASB: “What is it?”
  • KJV: “It is manna.”

The Hebrew is mān hûʾ. The narrator’s very next clause settles it: “for they did not know what it was.” That clause only makes sense if the people are asking a question, not supplying a name — and v. 31 then reports that “the house of Israel called its name manna”, i.e. the question became the name. ESV and NASB are clearly preferable; the KJV flattens a wordplay the narrator has built the verse around. [T] High confidence.

The payoff is worth a sentence in the sermon: the name of God’s provision is a question. Israel eats “what-is-it?” for forty years, and at the other end of the block asks “Is the LORD among us or not?” The bread and the doubt have the same grammar.

17:2 — “test” vs “tempt”. KJV “wherefore do ye tempt the LORD?”; ESV/NASB “test”. Modern English “tempt” implies enticement to evil (cf. James 1:13) and is now misleading. ESV preferred.

17:16 — a genuine crux.

  • ESV: “A hand upon the throne of the LORD!”
  • KJV: “Because the LORD hath sworn”
  • NASB: “The LORD has sworn”

The Hebrew is kî-yād ʿal-kēs yāh. Kēs is an unusual form (the ordinary word for throne is kissēʾ), and this is one of the most notoriously difficult phrases in the Old Testament — the versions split between rendering it literally as a gesture (ESV) and idiomatically as its meaning (KJV/NASB, “the LORD has sworn”).

A correction to my own first draft. I initially wrote that the ESV’s hand-on-the-throne should be preached with the alternative merely noted, and dismissed the nēs (“banner”) emendation as “attractive because it is tidy, which is a reason to distrust it”. The checking stage shows both judgements were too confident. There is no consensus preference among commentators, and at least four live construals are in play:

  1. kēs = “throne”, the hand raised to it in intercession or as a sign of divine power (NET notes).
  2. kēs = kissēʾ with the preposition read as oppositional — “a hand against the throne of Yahweh”, i.e. Amalek has assaulted God’s sovereignty (Currid, EP Study Commentary, 369). This is a materially different sermon: the verse becomes a statement about what Amalek did, not about an oath Moses or God swore.
  3. An oath-taking expression, the raised hand testifying to God’s sworn commitment (Ross & Oswalt, Cornerstone).
  4. Emendation to nēs, “banner”, harmonising with v. 15. It lacks manuscript support (Currid) — but it was proposed in the seventeenth century and has gained considerable scholarly traction (Childs, OTL, 311). I understated its standing.

Preaching implication, revised: the sense is genuinely contested, not just the imagery. Preach the ESV’s rendering, and give one sentence to the fact that the clause is obscure and that some read it as Amalek’s hand raised against God’s throne. Do not present any construal as settled. Moderate — and the contest is the finding. [revised: logos-research]

15:25 — “a log” (ESV) / “a tree” (KJV). Hebrew ʿēṣ covers both. The ESV is right to avoid “tree”, which invites an allegory of the cross the text does not offer. (The allegory has a long history; it is not warranted by the word.)

Ancient versions check — classified under the Three-Way Triage

The moderns substantively agree on the block’s load-bearing renderings, so the check is triggered. Three findings, one in each category:

Category 1 — translation loss (not a variant). The mān hûʾ / mān wordplay of 16:15 and 16:31 cannot survive into Greek: the LXX renders the question as τί ἐστιν τοῦτο; and then transliterates the name as μαν, so the Greek reader gets the name and the question but not the pun that binds them. This is what translation does; it is not evidence about the LXX. The English versions divide on the same problem — the ESV keeps the question and loses the name-link, the KJV keeps the name and loses the question. Moderate–high; LXX wording flagged for verification.

Category 2 — substantive variant. Confirmed at the checking stage. At 17:16 the LXX combines the Hebrew words into one and reads the consonants ksyh as “hidden”, producing “in the concealed hand the LORD fights against Amalek from generation to generation” (Dozeman, Eerdmans Critical Commentary, 392). This is not a stylistic smoothing but a different construal of the consonantal text, and it is the only place in the block where the traditions part on substance. No default is applied in either direction: the Hebrew is the working text because the ESV translates it, and the Greek is recorded as a genuinely different reading of the same consonants — which is a translator’s decision about an obscure text, not evidence that either tradition is corrupt. [revised: logos-research] Moderate–high, now sourced.

Category 3 — the Greek as the NT’s own text. Psalm 95:8 in the LXX appears to translate the two place-names rather than transliterating them — Meribah becoming παραπικρασμός (“rebellion”, “embitterment”) and Massah πειρασμός (“testing”) — so that the readers of Hebrews 3:8, 15 and 4:7 receive not two obscure wilderness place-names but two nouns of accusation, and the warning of Hebrews 3:7–4:13 is built on that decision. This would stand whichever text is prior, and it is a Tool 11 finding, not a translation error.

9. Tone and Feel

Dominant register: attrition. Not drama — grind. The block’s emotional signature is the wearing-down of everyone in it.

The escalation is real and traceable. At 15:24 the people “grumble” — a murmur. At 16:2 “the whole congregation” grumbles, and 16:3 turns it into a death-wish with an accusation attached. At 17:2 they “quarrel” (rîb) — the verb moves from muttering to legal contention. And at 17:4 Moses says “They are almost ready to stone me.” In three episodes the people move from complaining about the water to being ready to kill the mediator. [T] High confidence.

The nostalgia is a lie, and it is the block’s most human moment. “When we sat by the meat pots and ate bread to the full” (16:3). Chapter 1 has told the reader what Egypt was: forced labour, ruthless taskmasters, and a decree to drown their sons. There were no full bread pots. Hunger has rewritten the past into something worth going back to — which is not a stupid thing to do, it is what hunger does. This is worth naming gently from the pulpit rather than scoring points off. [T] for the contradiction with ch. 1; high confidence.

Devices carrying the tone. Sensory specificity in the bread account (coriander seed, white, wafers with honey; and the counter-image — “it bred worms and stank”); the relentless dailiness of the gathering vocabulary; Moses’ two exasperated rhetorical questions (“What are we?”, 16:7–8) and his one desperate one (“What shall I do with this people?”, 17:4).

The one slow-motion sequence. 17:12 brakes hard: the heavy hands, the stone brought and placed, the sitting down, one man on each side, “and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun.” After a chapter of complaint, the narrator lingers on three men on a hill holding still. The change of pace is the passage’s only tenderness.

What is absent, and it matters: God is never angry here. Across sixty-one verses and four grumblings there is no divine anger, no plague, no judgement — the sharpest note is a question, “How long will you refuse…?” (16:28). Set that beside Numbers 11, where a later grumbling for meat is met with fire and a plague, and the difference is stark. Two cautions: these are different episodes, not one told twice; and the absence is admissible as an observation about this text, not as a claim about a pattern I cannot cite. But for the preacher it is the block’s quiet astonishment — the wilderness generation’s first four complaints are answered entirely with food, water and victory. [T] for the absence in this block; [I] for the contrast’s force. Moderate–high.

Soundtrack. A repeating four-bar figure that will not resolve, scored for low strings — and then, at 17:8, brass.

10. Repetition

Word-level.

  • grumble (lûn / tĕlunnôt) — ten English renderings across seven verses: 15:24; 16:2, 7 (×2), 8 (×3), 9, 12; 17:3. Eight are in chapter 16, and six of those fall in Moses’ three-verse speech (vv. 7–9). The concentration is not decorative: Moses says the word over and over until he can name its real object. The verb accounts for five of them — 15:24; 16:2, 7, 8; 17:3 — and the cognate noun tĕlunnôt for the remainder (16:7, 8, 9, 12), which is why a verb-only search returns five and an English search returns ten. High confidence. [revised: logos-research]
  • test (nāsâ) — four times, two in each direction (15:25; 16:4; 17:2, 7).
  • water / drink — the block’s most frequent concrete noun-and-verb pairing: 15:22, 23 (×3), 24, 25, 27 (×2); 17:1, 2 (×2), 3, 6 (×2). The block opens with no water and closes with a war; water is what the frame is made of.
  • bread — 16:3, 4, 8, 12, 15, 22, 29, 32.
  • morning — a drumbeat through ch. 16 (vv. 7, 8, 12, 13, 19, 20, 21, 23, 24). The manna account is structured by dailiness at the level of its vocabulary.
  • gather (lāqaṭ) — recurs through 16:16–27, with the sixth/seventh-day variation.
  • the whole congregation — 16:1, 2, 9, 10; 17:1. The grumbling is corporate, and the narrator keeps saying so.

Idea-level.

  • “You have brought us out to kill us.” The same accusation, four times across the book, in four different mouths: the foremen at 5:21; the people at the sea, 14:11–12; the congregation at 16:3; the thirsty at 17:3. Israel accuses its deliverer of homicidal intent at every crisis. This is a genuine book-level thread and it is not in the overview. [T] High confidence.
  • God hears. 16:7, 8, 9, 12 — four times in six verses, “the LORD has heard your grumbling.” Set against 2:24 (“God heard their groaning”) and 6:5, the same verb now takes complaint rather than suffering as its object — and answers it anyway. [T] Moderate–high.
  • Knowing. 16:6 (“you shall know that it was the LORD who brought you out”) and 16:12 (“you shall know that I am the LORD your God”). The plague-cycle’s refrain, turned round on Israel. In chapters 7–11 the formula’s object is Egypt and Pharaoh; here it is the redeemed. [T] High confidence.

12. Genre

Hebrew historical narrative with two embedded non-narrative forms: legislation (15:25b–26; 16:16–30) and etiological naming (Marah, 15:23; Massah and Meribah, 17:7; The LORD Is My Banner, 17:15). The naming-formula is the block’s structural signature — three of the four episodes end by attaching a name to what happened.

Reading rules: it happened, and it is theologically loaded. Use Narrator’s Comment (which is unusually productive here) and Copycat (where the NT has already ruled — see Tool 13).

One genre note with teeth. 16:35–36 shifts register from narrative to retrospective annotation. A preacher reading straight through will move from “the people of Israel ate the manna forty years” to “an omer is the tenth part of an ephah” without noticing that both sentences are spoken from outside the story. Mark the shift when you read it aloud.

13. Copycat

The NT has already ruled on this passage, which is rare and decisive. 1 Corinthians 10:6 — “these things took place as examples for us, that we might not desire evil as they did” — and 10:11, “these things happened to them as an example, but they were written down for our instruction.” Verse 10 names the specific behaviour: “nor grumble, as some of them did.” Israel in this block is a negative example on apostolic authority, not by our inference.

Where the block is commonly read as prescriptive and shouldn’t be: the Aaron-and-Hur “hold up your leader’s arms” application, and the manna’s daily gathering read as a devotional-quiet-time technique. Where it genuinely is prescriptive: the Sabbath (16:23–30) — but as covenant law given to Israel, to be read through the Bible Timeline, not lifted directly.

One place the NT prescribes what looks descriptive. 16:18 (“whoever gathered much had nothing left over, and whoever gathered little had no lack”) is quoted by Paul at 2 Corinthians 8:15 and applied to the Corinthian collection — a narrative detail turned into a principle of Christian giving by apostolic authority. If the sermon touches the gathering regulations, this is the warranted application and it is not obvious.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits: after Passover and the Sea, before Sinai. Israel is redeemed but not yet covenanted; delivered from Egypt but not yet given the land; fed but not yet at rest.

Where the reader sits: this side of the cross and the resurrection, indwelt by the Spirit, awaiting the consummation.

What has happened in between that changes the reading. This is the toolkit’s own paradigm case of the “Russian dolls” timeline — the wilderness generation’s position maps onto ours, and the NT says so twice over: 1 Corinthians 10:1–13 (they were baptised into Moses, ate spiritual food, drank from the Rock, and fell — “now these things happened as an example”) and Hebrews 3:7–4:13 (Ps 95’s Massah/Meribah warning applied directly to the church: “Take care, brothers, lest there be in any of you an evil, unbelieving heart”). We are between the deliverance and the rest, exactly as they were. That is not an analogy we are constructing; it is the NT’s own use of this block.

Timeline errors to avoid. (a) Reading 15:26’s health promise as a standing guarantee to Christians — it is a conditional clause in a pre-Sinai covenant addressed to a nation, and Hebrews 11:35–38 is the canonical corrective. (b) Reading the manna as a template for expecting daily material supply in the same form; Christ makes it a sign pointing to himself (John 6:32–35). (c) Reading the Amalek oath as a warrant for anything at all in the present — see Difficult Verses.

15. Who Am I?

The toolkit names this passage as its own example, and the answer is unusually clear: we are the grumblers.

  • Not Moses. He is the mediator whose hands must be held up and who is nearly stoned — the office is Christ’s, not ours (1 Tim 2:5; Heb 3:1–6, which sets Moses and Christ side by side using this stretch of the wilderness as its evidence).
  • Not Joshua, and not Aaron and Hur.
  • Us — as warning. 1 Cor 10:6, 10; Heb 3:7–4:13. The identification is explicit, repeated, and directed at the church.

But note the shape of the warning, because it is easy to preach it as bare threat. Hebrews’ application of Massah is “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts” — an invitation with a deadline, not a condemnation. And 1 Corinthians 10 ends at v. 13 with “God is faithful, and he will not let you be tempted beyond your ability.” Both NT uses of this block end in mercy. A sermon that identifies the congregation with the grumblers and stops there has stopped one verse early in both places.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. That the reader, standing where Israel stood, would hear the question “Is the LORD among us or not?” as their own, and would let the book answer it — not with an argument but with bread, water, a victory, and finally a tent in the middle of the camp.

Worldview before behaviour. Three things the block asks the reader to think:

  • The wilderness is a test-bench, not an accident. Four times the text says so. The hunger is not a gap in God’s care; 16:4 makes it the instrument of the care.
  • Provision is daily by design. The manna cannot be stockpiled, and the attempt to stockpile it is the block’s one instance of disobedience punished by the thing itself rotting. God’s people are meant to need him tomorrow.
  • Grumbling is not a small sin, because it is not really horizontal. 16:8 is the sentence that reframes every complaint the congregation has made this week.

Behaviour — stop and start. Stop: rewriting the past as better than it was in order to justify present discontent (16:3); treating complaint as though its object were the circumstances, or the leadership, rather than God. Start: the daily-portion discipline — receiving today’s provision today; keeping the Sabbath as the gift 16:29 calls it before it is the command 20:8 makes it.

Motivation, so this doesn’t become moralism. The motive the text supplies is not “try harder not to grumble”. It is that the God being grumbled at responds with food. Every complaint in this block is answered with provision, and not one with judgement. The imperative rides on that indicative or it is not this passage’s imperative.

Four audiences. - For me: which of my complaints, said aloud this week, was actually addressed to God? - For a Christian friend: the one who is in the gap — rescued, but not arrived, and finding the gap longer than expected. This block is written for exactly that person. - For the church together: the grumbling here is corporate — “the whole congregation”, five times. Congregational discontent is a category Scripture takes seriously. - For the unbeliever: a God who is asked point-blank whether he is even there, and who answers by feeding the people who asked.

Prayer. Confession for complaint dressed as observation; thanks for daily provision that has been received without being noticed; petition for the grace to need God tomorrow.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • The conditional at 15:26 uses the infinitive absolute with the finite verb — “if you will diligently listen” renders a doubled-up construction (šāmôaʿ tišmaʿ) that English can only carry with an adverb. The emphasis is real and the ESV’s “diligently” is doing the work of a Hebrew intensifier. High confidence.
  • The wayyiqtol chain carries the itineraries and provisions; the notable disjunctive breaks are at 16:31, 16:35 and 16:36, which is precisely where the narrator steps outside the story. The grammar marks the narrator’s asides. Moderate–high.
  • 17:16’s clause is syntactically incomplete as it stands — a noun phrase (“a hand upon the throne of Yah”) with no verb — which is why the versions supply one (“has sworn”). The abruptness is likely the point: it reads as a quoted oath-formula rather than a sentence. Moderate.

Wordplay and sound-features

  • Mān hûʾ → mān (16:15, 31): the name of the bread is the question the people asked. The strongest and most certain wordplay in the block, and the one an English congregation will miss entirely unless it is pointed out. High confidence.
  • Marah / bitter (15:23) — glossed by the narrator; high.
  • Massah / nāsâ and Meribah / rîb (17:7) — both glossed; high.
  • Nāsâ (N-S-H) and nēs (N-S-S) — resolved at the checking stage, and the resolution improves the finding. The roots are etymologically distinct, so there is no morphological wordplay and YHWH nissî does not mean “the LORD is my test”. But the connection is not therefore nothing. Dozeman reads chapter 17 as building deliberate structural echoes through parallel etiologies — the test (nāsâ) named at Massah and the banner (nēs) named at the altar — with the Amalek episode following the form of the preceding stories and reusing their key words (Eerdmans Critical Commentary, 397). So the link is architectural, not lexical: the chapter names a place for Israel’s testing and then names an altar for God’s ensign, and the two namings answer each other. That is a better and safer sermon point than the etymology would have been. [revised: logos-research] Moderate–high as structure; the etymological claim is closed off.

Recommended verification: the Hebrew verb/noun distribution of lûn across the block (the count matters for a headline finding); the LXX of 17:16; whether šabbāt as a noun genuinely first occurs at 16:23 in Exodus; and the standing of the nāsâ/nēs sound-link.

Textual Variants

Category 2 divergence at 17:16 — the only one in the block.

  • The issue: the Hebrew yād ʿal-kēs yāh. Kēs is not the ordinary word for throne (kissēʾ), and the clause has no verb.
  • The readings: (a) kēs as a shortened or variant form of kissēʾ, “throne” — a hand raised to the LORD’s throne in an oath-gesture (ESV, and most modern commentators); (b) the clause treated as an idiom for oath-taking and rendered by its sense, “the LORD has sworn” (KJV, NASB); (c) the LXX apparently reads the consonants from a root meaning “to hide”, giving something close to “with a hidden hand the LORD makes war on Amalek”; (d) a minority suggestion that kēs is a corruption of nēs, “banner”, harmonising v. 16 with the altar-name in v. 15.
  • Most likely original: the MT’s kēs is the harder reading and is likely original; reading (a) is the best sense of it. Reading (d) is attractive precisely because it is tidy, which is a reason to distrust it.
  • Preaching implication: minimal for doctrine, real for imagery. Preach the ESV’s hand-on-the-throne and note in a clause that the versions differ. Do not build on the LXX reading without checking it.
  • Confidence: moderate on the MT construal; my recall of the LXX is uncertain and is listed under Open Questions.

Everything else in the block is textually stable. The catalogue’s OT entries do not touch 15:22–17:16.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period: the first two months after the exodus — the text dates the manna episode precisely to “the fifteenth day of the second month” (16:1), one month to the day after the departure (cf. 12:6, 51). [T]
  • Location: the Sinai peninsula’s western wilderness — Shur, Marah, Elim, the wilderness of Sin, Rephidim. The specific identifications are all contested and none of the exegesis depends on them; the text’s own interest is in the sequence and the lack, not the map. [S] Uncertain on identifications — flagged.
  • Political context: none. This is the only stretch of Exodus with no king, no court, and no adversary until 17:8. The absence is part of the wilderness’s meaning.

What the original audience knew

  • Water is the whole problem. In this terrain a three-day march without water (15:22) is not inconvenience but a survival crisis, and brackish springs that cannot be drunk are a known and bitter disappointment. The first hearers needed no explanation of why Marah was a catastrophe. [I]
  • Quail migrate. Large flocks cross the Sinai in spring, exhausted enough to be taken by hand — which is when the text places the episode (the second month). The narrative does not present this as remarkable; it gives the quail one verse (16:13) and the bread thirty. [S] Moderate.
  • The manna is presented as unexplained. A tamarisk-secretion explanation is often offered, and the text’s description (small, flake-like, sweet, melting in the sun) is not inconsistent with it — but the narrative’s own features cut against a natural reading: it falls six days and not the seventh, rots overnight except before the Sabbath, and lasts forty years. Whatever a preacher’s view, the text is not interested in the mechanism, and neither should the sermon be. [S] Flagged — the identification is a modern hypothesis, not a datum.
  • An omer means nothing to a later reader. 16:36 exists because the measure had fallen out of use. The first readers of the finished book needed the gloss; the wilderness generation did not. [T]/[I]
  • Amalek were nomadic raiders of the Negev and Sinai. Deuteronomy 25:18 supplies what Exodus 17 withholds: “he attacked you… all who lagged behind you, when you were faint and weary.” The attack was on stragglers. That detail is not in Exodus and should be attributed to Deuteronomy when used. [T: Deut]
  • “Write this in a book” (17:14) is a known ANE practice — the making of a permanent memorial record of a victory and an oath. Within Exodus it is one of only a handful of writing-notices (17:14; 24:4, 7; 34:27–28), and it is the first. [T] for the notice; [S] for the ANE parallel, moderate.

What this changes about how we read

The dating at 16:1 means the whole block covers roughly six weeks. Israel’s four grumblings are not the erosion of decades; they are the first month and a half. And the writing-notice at 17:14 means the book contains, near its centre, an instruction to write down part of itself — which is the closest thing in Exodus to a comment on its own making.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: Torah. The canonical reader of a Torah passage knows creation, fall, flood, the patriarchs, and everything of Exodus to this point.
  • What the canonical reader knows — and this is decisive here: per the extension’s Torah rule, the compositional and canonical audiences coincide: wilderness Israel with the tabernacle standing in the camp. That is not background archaeology; it is the reading lens built into the text. Three consequences for this block:
    1. 16:34’s “before the Testimony” is not a puzzle to them — it is an address. They know where the jar is. The reader who first heard this chapter could, in principle, walk to the tent and stand near the pot of manna that proves the story.
    2. The Sabbath of 16:23 is a practice they are already keeping when they hear it narrated as newly given.
    3. They are eating the manna while hearing about the manna. 16:35’s “forty years” is spoken to people somewhere inside those forty years.
  • Canonical register: Genesis is the live neighbour, and it fires here through Amalek’s genealogy (Gen 36:12) and the Elim numbers.

The first hearers

  • Who they were: the redeemed but unsettled — out of Egypt, not into the land, dependent on a daily miracle for food and on an itinerant God for water.
  • Their situation: materially precarious and theologically uncertain, hearing their own parents’ complaints read back to them.
  • What they brought: the memory of Egypt (and its distortions), the experience of the pillar and the cloud, and a live question about whether this God stays.

Where the passage fits their world

  • The block names their own recurring question out loud — “Is the LORD among us or not?” — and preserves it in two place-names rather than suppressing it. Israel’s canonical memory includes a map with its own doubt written on it.
  • The gathering regulations describe a life they are living. For them this is not a story about bread; it is the rule for tomorrow morning.
  • The Amalek oath is unfinished business handed to them. 17:14 says the memorial is to be recited in Joshua’s ears — and Joshua is the man who will lead them into the land. The oath is given to the next generation as a task.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: that the people wish they had died in Egypt (16:3) — and that God answers with bread rather than judgement.
  • Surprising: that the covenant “if” (15:26) and the Sabbath (16:23) arrive before the mountain; and that the first battle is against relatives.
  • Comforting: “I am the LORD, your healer” (15:26); the manna’s exact sufficiency, “whoever gathered little had no lack” (16:18); and that the glory appears in the middle of a complaint (16:10).
  • Disturbing: “How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?” (16:28) — a divine question with no answer recorded; and the oath of perpetual war (17:16).

What we bring that they didn’t

Individualism (the grumbling here is corporate five times over, and the “you” of the gathering rules is plural); a food supply that is not daily, which makes the manna’s discipline nearly unintelligible to us as anything but metaphor; a therapeutic instinct that hears “grumbling” as processing rather than as accusation; and a strong distaste for texts about warfare that will make 17:14–16 land badly if it is not handled.

Implication for the sermon

Recovering the reception moves the sermon’s centre off “God provides” and onto “Is he here?” — which is the question the text actually preserves, and the one the congregation is more likely to be asking.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. Wilderness testing (exodus / new exodus) — high confidence

  • Where this passage sits: the paradigm instance. Redemption is behind, inheritance ahead, and the between-time is explicitly a test (15:25; 16:4).
  • Before: the flood’s forty days (Gen 7:4) and Abraham’s testing (Gen 22:1, the same verb nāsâ) establish the vocabulary; the Sea has just been crossed.
  • After: Deuteronomy 8:2–3 interprets the whole wilderness in this block’s terms — “that he might humble you, testing you… and he humbled you and let you hunger and fed you with manna… that he might make you know that man does not live by bread alone.” Then Psalm 95:8–11 turns Massah and Meribah into the standing warning, and Hebrews 3:7–4:13 applies that warning to the church. And Jesus, forty days in the wilderness and hungry, answers the tempter by quoting Deuteronomy 8:3 (Matt 4:4; Luke 4:4) — the verse that interprets this passage. Israel was tested by hunger and grumbled; Christ was tested by hunger and trusted.
  • Payoff: the trajectory is not “we should be more like Israel or less”; it is that the wilderness test has been taken again and passed, by someone quoting the manna’s own lesson.

2. Sabbath and rest — high confidence

  • Where this passage sits: the Sabbath’s first appearance as an institution — given as a gift (16:29), practised (16:30), and violated (16:27) before it is ever commanded from a mountain.
  • Before: Genesis 2:2–3, God’s own cessation.
  • After: Exodus 20:8–11 grounds the command in creation; 31:12–17 makes it the covenant sign; and Hebrews 3:7–4:11 fuses this block’s two halves — the Massah warning and the Sabbath rest — into a single argument that “there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God.”
  • Payoff: Hebrews reads Exodus 16 and Exodus 17 together, which is a strong hint that they should be preached together. The rest was offered in the wilderness in the form of a day; it was refused in the form of unbelief; it remains.

Confluence — and it is climactic, not parallel. These two themes do not merely run alongside each other. Hebrews 3–4 argues that the rest Israel forfeited at Massah is the rest the Sabbath was pointing at, and that both are entered by faith in Christ. The testing thread and the rest thread meet in one reality the NT names. That convergence is where the preaching gold is, and it is the reason not to split this block into a “manna sermon” and a “water sermon”.

3. Presence — moderate–high, and it belongs to the overview’s spine

17:7’s question is the presence-spine’s low point, and the tabernacle is its answer. Recorded here rather than developed, since the overview carries it.

Schnittjer Pass

Skeleton. Exodus · second division, through the wilderness (15:22–18:27) · serving the book’s leading idea, the progressive proximity of God — this is the division where proximity is doubted rather than displayed.

Findings

  • (Step 3 — shape.) The block is a janus. It faces back to the Sea (the same God who drowned an army now cannot be trusted for water) and forward to the mountain (the covenant “if” at 15:26, the tôrâ at 16:4, the Sabbath at 16:23, and the arrival at Horeb’s rock in 17:6). It is the hinge on which Exodus turns from deliverance to covenant. High confidence.
  • (Step 1 — selectivity and braking.) The narrator brakes on the gathering rules and accelerates through the miracles. The bread falls in one verse (16:14); how to collect it takes fifteen. The quail get a clause. Where the narrator slows is where the weight is, and the weight is on obedience in the ordinary. High confidence.
  • (Step 2 — narrative space.) The movement is toward the mountain, and the mountain reaches back. The rock is “at Horeb” (17:6) while the camp is at Rephidim; Moses climbs “the top of the hill” for the battle (17:9–10) — a rehearsal in miniature of the ascent that dominates chapters 19–34. Height and provision are already linked before Sinai. Moderate–high.
  • (Step 4 — leading word.) Lûn is the unit’s leading word, and it is displaced at the fourth episode by silence — the pattern-break identified under Tool 3. High confidence.
  • (Step 5 — first vs second reading.) Three notices only work on the second reading: “before the Testimony” (16:34), “forty years” (16:35), and “an omer is the tenth of an ephah” (16:36). A first-time reader does not yet know what the Testimony is; a re-reader knows the jar sits in the ark. The chapter is written to be read twice. High confidence — this is the Schnittjer move that most repays the preacher here.
  • (Step 6 — polyacoustic intertextuality.) The rock-and-water scene builds an echo rather than delivering a type. Struck rock → water in the wilderness (17:6) is picked up at Num 20:8–11 (where striking becomes disobedience), Ps 78:15–16, Ps 105:41, Isa 48:21, and finally 1 Cor 10:4. Heard polyacoustically, the sequence is about a God who gives drink from unpromising places to people who do not deserve it — which is what Paul says. Do not flatten it to “the rock = Jesus, struck once for us” and stop; the canon builds this note over five texts. Moderate–high.

Echoes / signals: leading word lûn; structural device A–B–A′ plus pattern-breaking coda; intertextual links to Gen 36:12 (Amalek) and forward to Deut 8 / Ps 95.

Nil returns: no chiasm detected across the block (the A–B–A′ water frame is not a chiasm — there is no centred pivot, and the fourth panel breaks any symmetry); no type-scene in the technical sense.

Irreducibility. The block resists being reduced to “God provides for his people”. Its own summary sentence would have to include the test, the question, the law before the mountain, and the oath — and a sermon that keeps only the provision has kept the least interesting quarter of it.

Christological Reading

Type of Christological connection: typology (the manna and the rock, both NT-authorised), contrast (the wilderness test, taken again and passed), and trajectory (rest).

How the passage points to Christ

1. The bread — typology, passing all four tests. Category: provision/covenant sustenance, an established theological category, not an incidental detail. NT precedent: explicit and extended — John 6:31–58, where Jesus takes up “he gave them bread from heaven to eat” and answers “I am the bread of life… the bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” Escalation: stated by Christ himself in the strongest possible form — “Your fathers ate the manna in the wilderness, and they died… whoever eats this bread will live for ever” (6:49, 58). Authorial pattern: Exodus itself marks the manna as a thing to be kept and shown to later generations (16:32–33), i.e. the text signals its own forward reference. Passes all four → high confidence.

2. The rock — typology, NT-authorised and to be handled with care. 1 Corinthians 10:4: “they drank from the spiritual Rock that followed them, and the Rock was Christ.” Two textual details in Exodus 17 give this more purchase than is usually noticed: the staff is identified as the one that struck the Nile (17:5), so the instrument of judgement becomes the instrument of supply; and the LORD says “I will stand before you there on the rock” (17:6) — he places himself on the thing that is about to be struck. [T] for both details; [I] for pressing the second, and it should be offered as an observation rather than asserted as the text’s argument. Moderate–high.

3. The wilderness test — contrast, and it is the block’s sharpest Christological point. Deuteronomy 8:3 interprets the manna: “man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD.” Jesus quotes that verse, hungry, in a wilderness, after forty days (Matt 4:4; Luke 4:4). The correspondence is not ours to construct — the NT makes it by citation. Israel was tested by hunger and demanded bread; Christ was tested by hunger and refused to demand it, quoting the lesson Israel failed to learn. High confidence.

4. Rest — trajectory. The Sabbath given in the wilderness (16:23) and the rest forfeited at Massah (Ps 95) are fused at Hebrews 4:9: “there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God.”

5. Joshua — a light touch, correctly weighted. Yĕhôšuaʿ / Iēsous, appearing here for the first time, fighting Israel’s first battle and charged to remember. Hebrews 4:8 makes the name-play explicitly (“if Joshua had given them rest…”). This is a trajectory reading, not a typology — flag it as such and do not lean on it. Moderate.

Moralism check

  • The “be like X” temptations here, and there are three: be like Moses, persevering with difficult people; be like Aaron and Hur, supporting your leaders; and the negative form, don’t be like the Israelites, be more grateful. All three are available, and all three are the sermon this passage does not need.
  • The gospel grounding: every provision in the block has God as its subject, and not one of the four grumblings is met with judgement. The passage’s own logic is grace-before-demand: the water is sweetened before the statute is given (15:25); the Sabbath is called a gift before it is a rule (16:29).
  • Christ as hero, not example: the manna he is; the rock he is; the test he took and passed; the rest he gives. The one figure in this block we are told to identify with is the crowd — and the one we are told to trust is not in it.

Confidence: high on the manna and the wilderness-test contrast; moderate–high on the rock; moderate on Joshua.

Difficult / Contested Verses

1. Exodus 17:14–16 — “I will utterly blot out the memory of Amalek”

  • Category: ethical / apologetic — the block’s hardest verse by a distance.
  • The difficulty: a divine oath of perpetual war against an ethnic group, executed at 1 Samuel 15 as a command to kill non-combatants. This sits in the same family as the ḥērem texts, and a thoughtful hearer will connect them.
  • Common mishandlings: passing over it in the reading; treating it as a spiritual metaphor for “besetting sins” (a very common move and an evasion — the text is about a people); or defending it briskly with “they attacked first”, which is true (Deut 25:18 adds that they attacked the stragglers) but is not proportionate to a sworn war “from generation to generation”.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the answer to 17:7. The people asked whether the LORD was among them; the reply is a victory, an altar named for him, and a commitment that outlasts the generation asking. Its function in the passage is assurance, not ethics — which constrains how much weight the ethical question should be given in this sermon.
  • The main interpretive options: (a) a unique, unrepeatable judicial act against a specific people at a specific point in redemptive history, on a par with the flood and the conquest; (b) a covenantal-protection oath, whose object is the preservation of the promise-line rather than the destruction of a nation as such; (c) read forward canonically, where the enmity resurfaces at 1 Sam 15 and Esther 3 and is finally answered not by extermination but by the cross’s disarming of the powers (Col 2:15).
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: all three have textual purchase, and they are not mutually exclusive. What the text will not bear is any present-day application to any present-day people. The oath is God’s, sworn by God, and executed under a covenant order that has ended.
  • Suggested handling: address it briefly and directly — two or three minutes, naming the difficulty rather than waiting to be asked. Note the Deuteronomy 25:18 detail (they picked off the exhausted at the back), note that the oath’s function here is assurance, and note where the canon takes it.

2. Exodus 15:26 — “I am the LORD, your healer”

  • Category: weaponised / pastoral. The most likely verse in this block to have already hurt someone in the room.
  • The difficulty: the verse is a mainstay of healing ministries and prosperity teaching, read as a standing guarantee of physical health for believers.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the motive clause of a conditional covenant sentence addressed to a nation at a specific moment (“if you will diligently listen… I will put none of the diseases on you that I put on the Egyptians”). The comparison is to the plagues, not to illness in general: the promise is that Israel will not be treated as Egypt was.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the clause is conditional, corporate, national, and defined by its Egyptian comparison. It is not a warrant for individual health claims. And the name itself is unconditional and permanent — “I am the LORD, your healer” is a self-declaration, not a transaction. The preacher can and should preach the name warmly while declining the misuse.
  • Suggested handling: address briefly, and pastorally rather than polemically. Someone has been told their illness persists because their faith failed. Hebrews 11:35–38 is the canonical corrective and it should be quoted.

3. Exodus 16:20 and 16:28 — Moses’ anger and God’s exasperation

  • Category: doctrinal, mild.
  • The difficulty: “and Moses was angry with them” (16:20) sits without comment, and “How long will you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?” (16:28) is a divine question that receives no answer.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the narrator neither approves nor condemns Moses’ anger — it is reported. 16:28 is the block’s one sharp note, and its rhetorical function is to mark that the manna genuinely was a test and that Israel has genuinely failed it. Do not soften it; the block’s grace is more striking with it left in.
  • Suggested handling: no need to make a point of either; be ready if asked.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: The Aaron-and-Hur leadership sermon

  • What it looks like: “Every Moses needs an Aaron and a Hur — who is holding up your pastor’s arms?”
  • Why it’s wrong: the text draws no lesson from it, does not call it prayer, and does not commend Aaron and Hur for anything. What is in Moses’ hand is the staff of God (17:9) — the same staff that struck the Nile and the sea — so the raised hand is far more likely the book’s standing sign of God acting than a posture of intercession. The sermon converts a descriptive detail into a prescription, which is precisely the Copycat error.
  • The corrective: preach the staff, not the arms. If the support of others is worth a sentence, give it a sentence and label it as inference.

Pitfall 2: Making the block a sermon about God’s provision, and stopping there

  • What it looks like: “God provides — bitter water made sweet, bread from heaven, water from the rock. Trust him with your needs.”
  • Why it’s wrong: true, and a quarter of the passage. The text says four times that these are tests, preserves the people’s question as two place-names, gives law before Sinai, and ends on a war. A provision sermon has to discard the block’s own interpretive vocabulary to stay a provision sermon.
  • The corrective: let 17:7 be the sermon’s centre of gravity and the provisions be the answer to it.

Pitfall 3: Scolding the congregation for grumbling

  • What it looks like: a sermon whose application is “stop complaining and be grateful.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it inverts the passage’s own proportions. Not one of the four grumblings is met with judgement — they are met with sweet water, bread, meat, water and a victory. A sermon that is harsher on the congregation than God is on Israel has misread the tone.
  • The corrective: preach 16:8 — “your grumbling is not against us but against the LORD” — as diagnosis, and then preach what the LORD does about it, which is feed them. The rebuke lands harder when the grace is left in.
  • But do not soften the word itself in the other direction. The commentators are clear that lûn describes open rebellion, not disgruntlement (Currid, 327, 329) — this is not moaning about the catering. The sermon’s restraint should be about the response the passage records, not about the seriousness of the sin it names. [revised: logos-research]

Pitfall 4: Preaching 15:26 as a health promise

  • What it looks like: “God is Jehovah-Rapha; claim your healing.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the clause is conditional, corporate, national, and its comparison is to the plagues on Egypt, not to illness generally. See Difficult Verses.
  • The corrective: preach the name — which is unconditional — and decline the transaction. Do it gently; someone in the room has been damaged by the misuse.

Pitfall 5: Skipping or spiritualising the Amalek oath

  • What it looks like: ending the reading at 17:13; or “Amalek represents the flesh, the sin that attacks us when we are weary.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the allegory has a long and respectable pedigree and it is still an evasion — the text is about a people, an oath, and a book. And ending at v. 13 removes the altar, the name, and the answer to v. 7.
  • The corrective: read to v. 16, address the difficulty in three honest minutes, and preach the altar-name as the reply to “Is the LORD among us or not?”

Pitfall 6: Splitting the block into separate sermons

  • What it looks like: a manna sermon and a rock sermon on consecutive weeks.
  • Why it’s wrong: the four episodes share a six-beat pattern whose break at 17:8 is a finding; the testing thread only balances when all four are in view; and Hebrews 3–4 reads chapters 16 and 17 as a single argument. Split it and you lose the pattern, the balance, and the NT’s own reading.
  • The corrective: one sermon on the whole block. If the series has room for two, divide at 17:1 and make the second sermon “Is the LORD among us?” — but say in the first that the question is coming.
