# Three Parts of the Law: An Assessment of Dillehay

**Article assessed:** Justin Dillehay, "3 Parts of the Law: A Case for Continuity of the Moral Law", The Gospel Coalition website (undated in the copy supplied). Written as a defence of the threefold division, building on Philip S. Ross, *From the Finger of God* (2010).

**Companion document:** *One Law or Three? An Assessment of Wellum and of Green & Caneday* (8 October 2026). Section references in the form "Companion §6" point to it.

**Texts used:** WLC Hebrew, Swete LXX and SBLGNT from the `_texts/` corpus. English is for reference only. Every Hebrew and Greek reading quoted below was checked in the corpus on 8 October 2026.

**Date:** 8 October 2026

## 1. Summary

Dillehay's article is the strongest of the three pieces on a textual level, and it is the one that answers the objection the other two leave hanging. He sees what the critics' papers did not: the Torah *itself* treats some laws as binding beyond Israel and others as Israel's alone. His evidence is Deuteronomy 14:21 and Leviticus 18:26–28, the same texts the companion document used for its §6 synthesis. That synthesis turns out to be essentially the Ross–Dillehay line, and credit belongs here. He also does what Green and Caneday did not do: he names the Fourth Commandment as the real point of dispute.

The article has three weaknesses.

**First, its evidence supports a twofold distinction, not a threefold one.** Natural law against positive law, or moral against cultic: that is what the Torah evidence, the prophets and the Fathers he cites actually show. None of his four lines of evidence establishes the *judicial* law as a separate third category.

**Second, his Sabbath section undercuts his foundation.** His case rests on "the Decalogue = codified natural law", and the test he offers is "one had always been wrong; the other hadn't". The Sabbath fails that test on the textual evidence:
- the noun שַׁבָּת occurs nowhere in Genesis;
- the Sabbath is "given" to Israel at Exodus 16:29 and made a "sign" at 31:13 and Ezekiel 20:12;
- Isaiah 1:13, which he cites, puts it in the cult column;
- Jesus' second use of Hosea 6:6, which he also cites, comes in a Sabbath dispute (Matt 12:7).

He conceals none of this; he concedes that the Fourth Commandment mixes natural and positive law. But that concession means the Decalogue's boundaries and the moral law's boundaries do not coincide. That is the critics' central point.

**Third, the texts most often used against him are not engaged.** He names Colossians 2:16, Galatians 4:10 and Romans 14 but does not answer them. He passes over Hebrews 7:11–12, the critics' main text, and 2 Corinthians 3:7–11, where the stone-engraved ministry is "being set aside". His patristic witnesses, Irenaeus and Tertullian, are anti-Sabbatarian in the very works he quotes.

On rigour, this is a well-sourced popular article. It names its interlocutors (Ross, Jewett, VanDrunen, Frame), argues rather than asserts, and is honest about the limits of its case. Its exegesis is selective at the points that matter most.

## 2. The article's structure

After a definition, Dillehay offers a careful clarification: the view "doesn't deny that Christ fulfilled the whole law or that all three kinds of law were mixed in the same old covenant". It claims only that differences in New Testament treatment reflect "a difference in nature that already existed during the Old Testament and could have been discerned even then". Five lines of argument follow:

1. the moral order of the universe (natural law; Irenaeus, Tertullian, VanDrunen, Cooper);
2. the Pentateuch (Deut 14:21; Lev 18:26–28);
3. the Prophets and Writings ("the primacy of morality over the cult");
4. the Gospels (Jesus' treatment of law; the antitheses; Ross's three modes of fulfilment);
5. Paul (1 Cor 7:19; Gal 4; Eph 2, 4–6; Rom 13; 1 Tim 1).

A section on the Fourth Commandment and a conclusion by analogy with the Trinity close the article.

The thesis is **more precise than the critics allow for**. Wellum and Green & Caneday attack the view that the law came in three separable parts. Dillehay explicitly does not hold that view. His claim is about *discernible differences in nature*. That is close to Green and Caneday's "aspects" and to Wellum's admitted "distinctions". All three papers agree more than their framing suggests (§6).

## 3. Strengths

### 3.1 The Pentateuch evidence is genuine and correctly read

**Deuteronomy 14:21.** Verified: לֹא תֹאכְלוּ כָל־נְבֵלָה לַגֵּר אֲשֶׁר־בִּשְׁעָרֶיךָ תִּתְּנֶנָּה וַאֲכָלָהּ אוֹ מָכֹר לְנָכְרִי כִּי עַם קָדוֹשׁ אַתָּה לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ. "You shall not eat any carcass; you may give it to the sojourner in your gates and he may eat it, or sell it to a foreigner, *for you are a holy people to the LORD your God*." The prohibition is grounded in Israel's status and explicitly does not apply to the outsider. Dillehay's conclusion, "obviously not considered a universal moral norm", is right.

*A nuance he does not mention.* Leviticus 17:15 treats the same food differently. Whoever eats נְבֵלָה, "whether native or sojourner" (בָּאֶזְרָח וּבַגֵּר), must wash and is unclean until evening. In Leviticus the sojourner is inside the purity system; in Deuteronomy he may simply take the carcass as food. The *nokrî* (foreigner) is outside both. So "sojourner" is not one fixed category across the Torah. This does not weaken his point, since neither book makes the law universal. It does mean the line runs differently in the two books.

**Leviticus 18:26–28.** Verified (Companion §6.1). The sexual prohibitions bind "the native and the sojourner" (18:26: הָאֶזְרָח וְהַגֵּר). The land vomited out the earlier nations for these acts (18:27–28), though those nations were never under Sinai. Dillehay draws the right inference: "some sins are a reproach to any people". Proverbs 14:34 is apt (צְדָקָה תְּרוֹמֵם־גּוֹי, "righteousness exalts a nation").

Together these two texts are the best reply to Green and Caneday's claim that "no faithful Israelite" could have told the difference. On Dillehay's reading the Torah tells him. It is also a fair and well-aimed rebuttal of Jewett's claim that the distinction "rests upon the teaching of the New Testament, not upon location in the Old Testament".

### 3.2 The "primacy of morality over the cult" is real, and he reads it carefully

**Hosea 6:6.** Dillehay says the verse is "not a call for the immediate abolition of the sacrificial system" but "a statement of priority". The Hebrew supports him. The first line is absolute in form, חֶסֶד חָפַצְתִּי וְלֹא־זָבַח, "steadfast love I desire, *and not* sacrifice". The parallel line is comparative, וְדַעַת אֱלֹהִים מֵעֹלוֹת, "and knowledge of God *more than* burnt offerings". The parallel tells you how to read the first line. The Septuagint makes the comparison explicit in both lines (ἔλεος θέλω ἢ θυσίαν… ἢ ὁλοκαυτώματα, "rather than"). Matthew 9:13 and 12:7 follow the Hebrew form (καὶ οὐ θυσίαν). His reading is the right one.

**Psalm 51** confirms it, though he does not use this. The psalm that says "you do not delight in sacrifice" (Hebrew 51:18) ends with "*then* you will delight in right sacrifices… then bulls will be offered on your altar" (51:21). Priority, not abolition, is the psalm's own framing. **Jeremiah 7:22–23** is the strongest text of this kind and is not cited: "I did not speak to your fathers or command them, in the day I brought them out of Egypt, concerning burnt offering and sacrifice; but this thing I commanded them: obey my voice."

### 3.3 He names the real issue

"Were it not for the fourth commandment's inclusion, I suspect no one would object to seeing the Ten Commandments as a codification of permanent moral law." This is candid and, on the evidence of all three papers, accurate. The companion document reached the same conclusion independently (Companion §2, §6.4).

### 3.4 The 1 Corinthians 7:19 argument is his best New Testament argument

Circumcision was "a commandment of God", yet Paul says it "counts for nothing" beside "keeping the commandments of God". Dillehay asks *why this one*. His answer is that it was never binding before Abraham and always pointed beyond itself (Deut 10:16; Jer 4:4). The Hebrew adds something to this. Circumcision is called a בְּרִית עוֹלָם, "an everlasting covenant" (Gen 17:13), and Paul still sets it aside. This fits the pattern found in the Torah's "for ever" vocabulary: it attaches to positive and cultic institutions, not to the Decalogue (Companion §6.3). So "everlasting" in the Torah does not mark the moral law, and Paul treats such institutions as provisional. That supports a natural/positive distinction exactly as Dillehay frames it.

### 3.5 1 Timothy 1:8–10 follows the order of the Decalogue

He cites it without exploiting it. Verified: after the general terms (ἀνόμοις… ἀσεβέσι… ἀνοσίοις καὶ βεβήλοις, roughly the first table), the list runs πατρολῴαις καὶ μητρολῴαις (fifth), ἀνδροφόνοις (sixth), πόρνοις, ἀρσενοκοίταις (seventh), ἀνδραποδισταῖς (eighth, read as man-stealing, cf. Exod 21:16), ψεύσταις, ἐπιόρκοις (ninth). Paul is still using the Decalogue's sequence to organise a vice list *after* the cross. This is better evidence for the Decalogue's enduring structural role than anything in the article's main text.

### 3.6 Fair characterisation and real interlocutors

He states the opposing view fairly ("well-intended, perhaps") and names who holds it. He grounds his own view in the confessions (WCF, Savoy, 2LC 19) and in a monograph-length defence (Ross). He separates the threefold division from Sabbatarianism ("they don't necessarily have to" go together) and notes that Calvin, a non-Sabbatarian, held the division. That is an honest concession that takes the heat out of the issue.

## 4. Weaknesses

### 4.1 The evidence establishes two categories, not three

Everything he offers from the Old Testament separates *universal or moral* law from *Israel-specific or cultic* law:
- Deuteronomy 14:21 against Leviticus 18;
- the prophets' sacrifice against steadfast love;
- the Fathers' natural precepts against later precepts.

Nothing separates the **judicial** law as a third kind. The Torah texts in fact fuse moral and judicial law. Leviticus 20:10 attaches the death penalty (מוֹת־יוּמַת) to the adultery Leviticus 18 forbids. Is "adultery shall be put to death" moral or judicial? The penalty belongs to Israel's polity, and the prohibition is natural law. The judicial category does not divide the laws; it divides *one* law into its rule and its sanction. Dillehay's definition, that judicial law binds "only insofar as its general equity reflects the natural law", shows this. On his own account the judicial law has no independent force; it is natural law plus Israel's sanctions. His actual position is a **twofold distinction with a theory of application**. That may be correct, but it is not what the title defends.

The patristic witnesses confirm it. Irenaeus's scheme in *Against Heresies* 4.15–16 is twofold: natural precepts (the Decalogue) against precepts of bondage given after the calf. The threefold form is medieval (Aquinas, *ST* I–II q. 99). Ross's claim, as Dillehay reports it, that this "was the view… of many early church fathers" is true of the *moral/positive* distinction and not of the *threefold* one. (Patristic references not checked in Logos for this assessment; see §8.)

### 4.2 The Sabbath fails his own test

His criterion for moral law is natural law: "One had always been wrong; the other hadn't." He supports pre-Sinai Sabbath obligation with Genesis 2:1–4 and Exodus 16:23. Against this:

- **The word is absent from Genesis.** A lemma search finds שַׁבָּת (H7676) **0 times in Genesis**. Its first occurrence is Exodus 16:23, and it occurs 111 times in 89 verses of the Hebrew Bible. Genesis 2:2–3 says God *ceased* (šābat, the verb), blessed and sanctified the day. No human is commanded, and no patriarch keeps it. The Genesis 1:1–2:3 dig also noted that Exodus 20:11 changes the verb to נוּחַ.
- **Exodus 16 presents it as a gift to Israel.** רְאוּ כִּי־יְהוָה נָתַן לָכֶם הַשַּׁבָּת, "See, the LORD has *given you* the Sabbath" (16:29). Nehemiah 9:14 says God "made known (הוֹדַעְתָּ) your holy Sabbath" to them at Sinai. Ezekiel 20:12: "I *gave* them my Sabbaths to be a *sign* between me and them". Exodus 31:13, 17: "a sign between me and the sons of Israel". A sign given to one people is the opposite of a law "written on the human heart".
- **The prophets he cites put it in the cult column.** Isaiah 1:13, inside the passage he lists (1:11–17): חֹדֶשׁ וְשַׁבָּת קְרֹא מִקְרָא לֹא־אוּכַל אָוֶן וַעֲצָרָה, "new moon and Sabbath, the calling of assemblies — I cannot endure iniquity with solemn assembly". The Sabbath stands alongside offerings, incense and new moons.
- **Jesus' use of Hosea 6:6 in Matthew 12:7 is a Sabbath ruling.** Dillehay cites both uses of Hosea 6:6 (Matt 9:13; 12:7) as the primacy of morality over ritual. The second is spoken in defence of disciples accused of breaking the Sabbath (12:1–8). Jesus puts strict Sabbath observance on the "sacrifice" side of the scale.
- **The anthropological evidence he cites does not include it.** Cooper's "universal moral code" agrees with the Decalogue's *second table*. No known non-Israelite moral code requires one day's rest in seven. Romans 2:14–15, which he cites, speaks of Gentiles doing "by nature" the things of the law. Nobody argues that Gentiles kept the seventh day by nature.

Dillehay sees the difficulty and handles it with WCF 21.7: the Fourth Commandment mixes natural and positive law. But **WCF 21.7 calls the one-day-in-seven requirement "a positive, moral, and perpetual commandment"**, which means *moral* and *natural* are not the same thing. If the moral law can contain positive commands that nature does not teach, natural law cannot be the criterion for what is moral. Then the only way to identify the moral law is its inclusion in the Decalogue, which is the thing the natural-law argument was meant to establish independently. The argument becomes circular at exactly the point where it is needed.

### 4.3 His Fathers are anti-Sabbatarian

The two quotations are accurate as far as they go. In the works quoted, however:
- **Irenaeus** (*Adv. Haer.* 4.16.1–2) treats the Sabbath, with circumcision, as a *sign* (quoting Ezek 20:12). He argues that the righteous before Moses were justified "without circumcision and without observance of Sabbaths".
- **Tertullian's** "law unwritten… before the Law of Moses" comes from *Against the Jews* (ch. 2). The same work argues (chs. 2–4) that Adam, Abel, Noah, Enoch and Melchizedek were righteous without keeping the Sabbath, and that the Sabbath was temporary.

Both Fathers support the natural-law Decalogue *minus the Sabbath*. Brought in as witnesses to a Sabbatarian reading of the Ten, they testify against it. (Check these references in Logos before citing; see §8.)

### 4.4 The Gospels argument is selective

**Matthew 5:18–20 cannot be limited to moral law.** Dillehay applies it to the moral law as "an abiding reflection of God's will". But Jesus speaks of ἰῶτα ἓν ἢ μία κεραία, "one iota or one stroke", and of "one of the *least* of these commandments" (5:19). That is the language of the smallest details, which by any reckoning include ceremonial ones. The verse either covers the whole law, fulfilled in different ways (Ross's three modes, which Dillehay also cites), or it does not discriminate at all. Using it for the moral law's abiding force alone imports the tripartite scheme into the text, which is what the critics say happens.

**The antitheses he omits are the hard ones.** He shows that the anger, lust and enemy-love antitheses have Old Testament roots. Exodus 23:4–5, Proverbs 25:21–22, Job 31:1 and 2 Kings 6:22 are well chosen and verified. Proverbs 22:24–25 is weak, since it counsels against *associating* with an angry man and does not forbid anger. He passes over the two antitheses where "not raising the bar" is hardest to maintain, and which touch *judicial* law:
- 5:33–37: the Torah regulates oaths and vows (cf. Deut 23:22); Jesus says μὴ ὀμόσαι ὅλως, "do not swear at all";
- 5:38–42: ὀφθαλμὸν ἀντὶ ὀφθαλμοῦ is the court's rule of proportion (Exod 21:24), and Jesus applies it to personal retaliation.

Those two texts are where his account of the judicial law ought to be tested, and they are absent.

**Micah 6:8 behind Matthew 23:23 is "likely" only on a generous reading.** Micah's triad is מִשְׁפָּט, חֶסֶד and "walk humbly". Matthew's is κρίσις, ἔλεος, πίστις. Zechariah 7:9 (מִשְׁפַּט אֱמֶת… וְחֶסֶד וְרַחֲמִים) has all three Hebrew roots, *mišpāṭ*, *ʾĕmet* (≈ πίστις) and *ḥesed*, and is at least as good a candidate. The general point, that Jesus is drawing on the prophets, stands. The specific claim is overconfident.

### 4.5 The critics' main texts are not engaged

- **Hebrews 7:11–12.** "The people received the law on the basis of [the priesthood]… when the priesthood is changed, of necessity there is a change of law" (Companion §3.2). This is Wellum's strongest text and it is absent here.
- **2 Corinthians 3:7–11.** No paper in this set addresses it, though it bears directly on the Decalogue. ἡ διακονία τοῦ θανάτου ἐν γράμμασιν ἐντετυπωμένη λίθοις, "the ministry of death, engraved in letters on stones" (3:7), can only be the tablets. It is τὸ καταργούμενον, "what is being set aside" (3:11), the same verb as Ephesians 2:15. A tripartite reply exists: what is set aside is the *ministry* or *covenant* (3:14 τῆς παλαιᾶς διαθήκης), not the moral content, and 3:3 contrasts *where* the law is written (stone against hearts), not *whether*. But it has to be made.
- **Colossians 2:16–17, Galatians 4:10 and Romans 14:5.** These are named as "seemingly anti-Sabbath" and then left unanswered. Colossians puts σαββάτων among the things that are σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων. Romans says ὃς μὲν κρίνει ἡμέραν παρ᾽ ἡμέραν, ὃς δὲ κρίνει πᾶσαν ἡμέραν, "one judges one day above another, another judges every day alike", and leaves it to conscience. Moving the discussion to intra-Reformed variety (Calvin, Heidelberg, Frame) does not answer the texts.

### 4.6 Smaller points

- **The history-of-ideas claim** that the threefold division declined as natural law fell out of favour in the twentieth century is suggestive. "Probably not an accident" signals that it is not demonstrated, and it is not.
- **The anthropological appeal** rests on one dated authority, John M. Cooper, a Catholic priest-anthropologist of the early twentieth century. It would need current comparative work to carry weight, and on its own terms it supports only the second table.
- **The Trinity analogy fails at its key point.** The persons of the Trinity are distinct, co-eternal and inseparable. On Dillehay's own view two of the law's three "parts" have expired. The analogy imports permanence and equality that his position denies.
- **"General equity" is glossed as natural law.** That is a common Reformed reading of WCF 19.4 but not the confession's own wording. Theonomists dispute it, and it should be flagged as an interpretation.
- **Ephesians 2:14–15** is read as abolishing only the ceremonial "dividing wall" without engaging the phrase τὸν νόμον τῶν ἐντολῶν ἐν δόγμασιν, which on its face refers to *the law* as such.

## 5. Verdict

Dillehay's case is better grounded in the Old Testament than either critique. On the central question, whether the New Testament's different treatment of different laws "reflects a difference in nature that already existed", he is right, and Deuteronomy 14:21, Leviticus 18:26–28 and 1 Corinthians 7:19 show it.

What he has demonstrated is a **natural/positive (moral/cultic) distinction**, not the threefold division as such. And the Decalogue, the very document the tripartite tradition calls the summary of the moral law, contains a command that by his own criterion is at least partly positive.

As a popular article it is honest, well-sourced and fair to its opponents. As an argument it is strongest where it is textual and weakest where it is confessional.

## 6. How this changes the picture from the first two papers

**1. All three papers agree on the ground of enduring obligation.** Dillehay says morality is "grounded in the character of God… before Moses". Green and Caneday say "God's character, not the Decalogue's tenure as covenant document". Wellum says the law's purpose was "to reveal God's character", and Moo, whom he quotes, speaks of "God's eternal moral norms". No one in this debate holds that the Decalogue binds Christians *because* it was given at Sinai.

**2. All three agree that the law contains discernible differences.** Wellum names them, Green and Caneday call them "aspects", and Dillehay calls them "differences in nature". The disagreement is over whether the *Decalogue as a whole* is the definitive summary of the enduring part, and over hermeneutical procedure: read Moses directly (Dillehay) or only through Christ and the apostles (Wellum).

**3. The Sabbath is the hinge, and only Dillehay says so.** The textual evidence on it (§4.2; Companion §6.4) is the hardest for the tripartite side.

**4. Shared gaps.** None of the three engages 2 Corinthians 3:7–11 or Acts 15 (Companion §6.2), or faces the Torah's own "for ever" vocabulary (Companion §6.3). These are the next texts to work through if you want a position that goes beyond all three.

**5. A correction to the companion document.** Its §6.1 presents the Leviticus 18 / Leviticus 20 / Deuteronomy-type distinction as "my synthesis". Dillehay, following Ross, already argues it from Deuteronomy 14:21 and Leviticus 18:26–28. The synthesis should be credited to that line of argument, with Leviticus 20:24–26, Genesis 9:3–4, Leviticus 17 → Acts 15 and the *ʿôlām* count as additions to it.

## 7. For conversations

**"Isn't the threefold division just a later invention?"** Its *twofold* core (natural or moral against positive or ritual) is ancient: Irenaeus, Tertullian, and the rabbis' *mišpāṭîm* and *ḥuqqîm*. It is arguably in the Torah itself (Deut 14:21; Lev 18:26–28). The *threefold* form, with judicial law as a separate category, is medieval.

**"Can you tell from the Old Testament which laws are moral?"** Often, yes. The Torah marks some laws as Israel's alone ("for you are a holy people", Deut 14:21) and holds the nations accountable for others (Lev 18:27–28). The prophets rank love above sacrifice (Hos 6:6; Jer 7:22–23).

**"So is the Sabbath moral law?"** On textual grounds this is the hardest case for the traditional view. The word is absent from Genesis, the Sabbath is "given" to Israel as a sign (Exod 16:29; 31:13; Ezek 20:12), it is grouped with the cult (Isa 1:13) and called a shadow (Col 2:16–17), and Jesus applies "mercy, not sacrifice" to it (Matt 12:7). Against this stand the creation grounding (Gen 2:2–3; Exod 20:11), its place in the Ten, and "a sabbath rest remains" (Heb 4:9, σαββατισμός). Even the Westminster Confession calls it "positive" as well as "moral" (21.7).

**"Didn't Paul throw out the stone tablets?"** 2 Corinthians 3:7–11 says the *ministry* engraved on stone is "being set aside". Whether that means the covenant administration or its content is the point both sides must argue. Note that 3:3 contrasts stone with *hearts*, which echoes Jeremiah 31:33's "my torah… on their heart".
