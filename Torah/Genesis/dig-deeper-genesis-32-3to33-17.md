### Genesis 32:3–32 [Heb 32:4–33]

> **3** וַיִּשְׁלַ֨ח יַעֲקֹ֤ב מַלְאָכִים֙ לְפָנָ֔יו אֶל־עֵשָׂ֖ו אָחִ֑יו אַ֥רְצָה שֵׂעִ֖יר שְׂדֵ֥ה אֱדֽוֹם׃  
> Then Jacob sent messengers before him to his brother Esau in the land of Seir, the country of Edom.

> **4** וַיְצַ֤ו אֹתָם֙ לֵאמֹ֔ר כֹּ֣ה תֹאמְר֔וּן לַֽאדֹנִ֖י לְעֵשָׂ֑ו כֹּ֤ה אָמַר֙ עַבְדְּךָ֣ יַעֲקֹ֔ב עִם־לָבָ֣ן גַּ֔רְתִּי וָאֵחַ֖ר עַד־עָֽתָּה׃  
> He also commanded them saying, “Thus you shall say to my lord Esau: ‘Thus says your servant Jacob, “I have sojourned with Laban, and stayed until now;

> **5** וַֽיְהִי־לִי֙ שׁ֣וֹר וַחֲמ֔וֹר צֹ֖אן וְעֶ֣בֶד וְשִׁפְחָ֑ה וָֽאֶשְׁלְחָה֙ לְהַגִּ֣יד לַֽאדֹנִ֔י לִמְצֹא־חֵ֖ן בְּעֵינֶֽיךָ׃  
> I have oxen and donkeys and flocks and male and female servants; and I have sent to tell my lord, that I may find favor in your sight.” ’ ”

> **6** וַיָּשֻׁ֨בוּ֙ הַמַּלְאָכִ֔ים אֶֽל־יַעֲקֹ֖ב לֵאמֹ֑ר בָּ֤אנוּ אֶל־אָחִ֨יךָ֙ אֶל־עֵשָׂ֔ו וְגַם֙ הֹלֵ֣ךְ לִקְרָֽאתְךָ֔ וְאַרְבַּע־מֵא֥וֹת אִ֖ישׁ עִמּֽוֹ׃  
> The messengers returned to Jacob, saying, “We came to your brother Esau, and furthermore he is coming to meet you, and four hundred men are with him.”

> **7** וַיִּירָ֧א יַעֲקֹ֛ב מְאֹ֖ד וַיֵּ֣צֶר ל֑וֹ וַיַּ֜חַץ אֶת־הָעָ֣ם אֲשֶׁר־אִתּ֗וֹ וְאֶת־הַצֹּ֧אן וְאֶת־הַבָּקָ֛ר וְהַגְּמַלִּ֖ים לִשְׁנֵ֥י מַחֲנֽוֹת׃  
> Then Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed; and he divided the people who were with him, and the flocks and the herds and the camels, into two companies;

> **8** וַיֹּ֕אמֶר אִם־יָב֥וֹא עֵשָׂ֛ו אֶל־הַמַּחֲנֶ֥ה הָאַחַ֖ת וְהִכָּ֑הוּ וְהָיָ֛ה הַמַּחֲנֶ֥ה הַנִּשְׁאָ֖ר לִפְלֵיטָֽה׃  
> for he said, “If Esau comes to the one company and attacks it, then the company which is left will escape.”

> **9** וַיֹּאמֶר֮ יַעֲקֹב֒ אֱלֹהֵי֙ אָבִ֣י אַבְרָהָ֔ם וֵאלֹהֵ֖י אָבִ֣י יִצְחָ֑ק יְהוָ֞ה הָאֹמֵ֣ר אֵלַ֗י שׁ֧וּב לְאַרְצְךָ֛ וּלְמוֹלַדְתְּךָ֖ וְאֵיטִ֥יבָה עִמָּֽךְ׃  
> Jacob said, “O God of my father Abraham and God of my father Isaac, O LORD, who said to me, ‘Return to your country and to your relatives, and I will prosper you,’

> **10** קָטֹ֜נְתִּי מִכֹּ֤ל הַחֲסָדִים֙ וּמִכָּל־הָ֣אֱמֶ֔ת אֲשֶׁ֥ר עָשִׂ֖יתָ אֶת־עַבְדֶּ֑ךָ כִּ֣י בְמַקְלִ֗י עָבַ֨רְתִּי֙ אֶת־הַיַּרְדֵּ֣ן הַזֶּ֔ה וְעַתָּ֥ה הָיִ֖יתִי לִשְׁנֵ֥י מַחֲנֽוֹת׃  
> I am unworthy of all the lovingkindness and of all the faithfulness which You have shown to Your servant; for with my staff only I crossed this Jordan, and now I have become two companies.

> **11** הַצִּילֵ֥נִי נָ֛א מִיַּ֥ד אָחִ֖י מִיַּ֣ד עֵשָׂ֑ו כִּֽי־יָרֵ֤א אָנֹכִי֙ אֹת֔וֹ פֶּן־יָב֣וֹא וְהִכַּ֔נִי אֵ֖ם עַל־בָּנִֽים׃  
> “Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau; for I fear him, that he will come and attack me and the mothers with the children.

> **12** וְאַתָּ֣ה אָמַ֔רְתָּ הֵיטֵ֥ב אֵיטִ֖יב עִמָּ֑ךְ וְשַׂמְתִּ֤י אֶֽת־זַרְעֲךָ֙ כְּח֣וֹל הַיָּ֔ם אֲשֶׁ֥ר לֹא־יִסָּפֵ֖ר מֵרֹֽב׃  
> “For You said, ‘I will surely prosper you and make your descendants as the sand of the sea, which is too great to be numbered.’ ”

> **13** וַיָּ֥לֶן שָׁ֖ם בַּלַּ֣יְלָה הַה֑וּא וַיִּקַּ֞ח מִן־הַבָּ֧א בְיָד֛וֹ מִנְחָ֖ה לְעֵשָׂ֥ו אָחִֽיו׃  
> So he spent the night there. Then he selected from what he had with him a present for his brother Esau:

> **14** עִזִּ֣ים מָאתַ֔יִם וּתְיָשִׁ֖ים עֶשְׂרִ֑ים רְחֵלִ֥ים מָאתַ֖יִם וְאֵילִ֥ים עֶשְׂרִֽים׃  
> two hundred female goats and twenty male goats, two hundred ewes and twenty rams,

> **15** גְּמַלִּ֧ים מֵינִיק֛וֹת וּבְנֵיהֶ֖ם שְׁלֹשִׁ֑ים פָּר֤וֹת אַרְבָּעִים֙ וּפָרִ֣ים עֲשָׂרָ֔ה אֲתֹנֹ֣ת עֶשְׂרִ֔ים וַעְיָרִ֖ם עֲשָׂרָֽה׃  
> thirty milking camels and their colts, forty cows and ten bulls, twenty female donkeys and ten male donkeys.

> **16** וַיִּתֵּן֙ בְּיַד־עֲבָדָ֔יו עֵ֥דֶר עֵ֖דֶר לְבַדּ֑וֹ וַ֤יֹּאמֶר אֶל־עֲבָדָיו֙ עִבְר֣וּ לְפָנַ֔י וְרֶ֣וַח תָּשִׂ֔ימוּ בֵּ֥ין עֵ֖דֶר וּבֵ֥ין עֵֽדֶר׃  
> He delivered them into the hand of his servants, every drove by itself, and said to his servants, “Pass on before me, and put a space between droves.”

> **17** וַיְצַ֥ו אֶת־הָרִאשׁ֖וֹן לֵאמֹ֑ר כִּ֣י יִֽפְגָּשְׁךָ֞ עֵשָׂ֣ו אָחִ֗י וִשְׁאֵֽלְךָ֙ לֵאמֹ֔ר לְמִי־אַ֨תָּה֙ וְאָ֣נָה תֵלֵ֔ךְ וּלְמִ֖י אֵ֥לֶּה לְפָנֶֽיךָ׃  
> He commanded the one in front, saying, “When my brother Esau meets you and asks you, saying, ‘To whom do you belong, and where are you going, and to whom do these animals in front of you belong?’

> **18** וְאָֽמַרְתָּ֙ לְעַבְדְּךָ֣ לְיַעֲקֹ֔ב מִנְחָ֥ה הִוא֙ שְׁלוּחָ֔ה לַֽאדֹנִ֖י לְעֵשָׂ֑ו וְהִנֵּ֥ה גַם־ה֖וּא אַחֲרֵֽינוּ׃  
> then you shall say, ‘These belong to your servant Jacob; it is a present sent to my lord Esau. And behold, he also is behind us.’ ”

> **19** וַיְצַ֞ו גַּ֣ם אֶת־הַשֵּׁנִ֗י גַּ֚ם אֶת־הַשְּׁלִישִׁ֔י גַּ֚ם אֶת־כָּל־הַהֹ֣לְכִ֔ים אַחֲרֵ֥י הָעֲדָרִ֖ים לֵאמֹ֑ר כַּדָּבָ֤ר הַזֶּה֙ תְּדַבְּר֣וּן אֶל־עֵשָׂ֔ו בְּמֹצַאֲכֶ֖ם אֹתֽוֹ׃  
> Then he commanded also the second and the third, and all those who followed the droves, saying, “After this manner you shall speak to Esau when you find him;

> **20** וַאֲמַרְתֶּ֕ם גַּ֗ם הִנֵּ֛ה עַבְדְּךָ֥ יַעֲקֹ֖ב אַחֲרֵ֑ינוּ כִּֽי־אָמַ֞ר אֲכַפְּרָ֣ה פָנָ֗יו בַּמִּנְחָה֙ הַהֹלֶ֣כֶת לְפָנָ֔י וְאַחֲרֵי־כֵן֙ אֶרְאֶ֣ה פָנָ֔יו אוּלַ֖י יִשָּׂ֥א פָנָֽי׃  
> and you shall say, ‘Behold, your servant Jacob also is behind us.’ ” For he said, “I will appease him with the present that goes before me. Then afterward I will see his face; perhaps he will accept me.”

> **21** וַתַּעֲבֹ֥ר הַמִּנְחָ֖ה עַל־פָּנָ֑יו וְה֛וּא לָ֥ן בַּלַּֽיְלָה־הַה֖וּא בַּֽמַּחֲנֶֽה׃  
> So the present passed on before him, while he himself spent that night in the camp.

> **22** וַיָּ֣קָם ׀ בַּלַּ֣יְלָה ה֗וּא וַיִּקַּ֞ח אֶת־שְׁתֵּ֤י נָשָׁיו֙ וְאֶת־שְׁתֵּ֣י שִׁפְחֹתָ֔יו וְאֶת־אַחַ֥ד עָשָׂ֖ר יְלָדָ֑יו וַֽיַּעֲבֹ֔ר אֵ֖ת מַעֲבַ֥ר יַבֹּֽק׃  
> Now he arose that same night and took his two wives and his two maids and his eleven children, and crossed the ford of the Jabbok.

> **23** וַיִּקָּחֵ֔ם וַיַּֽעֲבִרֵ֖ם אֶת־הַנָּ֑חַל וַֽיַּעֲבֵ֖ר אֶת־אֲשֶׁר־לוֹ׃  
> He took them and sent them across the stream. And he sent across whatever he had.

> **24** וַיִּוָּתֵ֥ר יַעֲקֹ֖ב לְבַדּ֑וֹ וַיֵּאָבֵ֥ק אִישׁ֙ עִמּ֔וֹ עַ֖ד עֲל֥וֹת הַשָּֽׁחַר׃  
> Then Jacob was left alone, and a man wrestled with him until daybreak.

> **25** וַיַּ֗רְא כִּ֣י לֹ֤א יָכֹל֙ ל֔וֹ וַיִּגַּ֖ע בְּכַף־יְרֵכ֑וֹ וַתֵּ֨קַע֙ כַּף־יֶ֣רֶךְ יַעֲקֹ֔ב בְּהֵֽאָבְק֖וֹ עִמּֽוֹ׃  
> When he saw that he had not prevailed against him, he touched the socket of his thigh; so the socket of Jacob’s thigh was dislocated while he wrestled with him.

> **26** וַיֹּ֣אמֶר שַׁלְּחֵ֔נִי כִּ֥י עָלָ֖ה הַשָּׁ֑חַר וַיֹּ֨אמֶר֙ לֹ֣א אֲשַֽׁלֵּחֲךָ֔ כִּ֖י אִם־בֵּרַכְתָּֽנִי׃  
> Then he said, “Let me go, for the dawn is breaking.” But he said, “I will not let you go unless you bless me.”

> **27** וַיֹּ֥אמֶר אֵלָ֖יו מַה־שְּׁמֶ֑ךָ וַיֹּ֖אמֶר יַעֲקֹֽב׃  
> So he said to him, “What is your name?” And he said, “Jacob.”

> **28** וַיֹּ֗אמֶר לֹ֤א יַעֲקֹב֙ יֵאָמֵ֥ר עוֹד֙ שִׁמְךָ֔ כִּ֖י אִם־יִשְׂרָאֵ֑ל כִּֽי־שָׂרִ֧יתָ עִם־אֱלֹהִ֛ים וְעִם־אֲנָשִׁ֖ים וַתּוּכָֽל׃  
> He said, “Your name shall no longer be Jacob, but Israel; for you have striven with God and with men and have prevailed.”

> **29** וַיִּשְׁאַ֣ל יַעֲקֹ֗ב וַיֹּ֨אמֶר֙ הַגִּֽידָה־נָּ֣א שְׁמֶ֔ךָ וַיֹּ֕אמֶר לָ֥מָּה זֶּ֖ה תִּשְׁאַ֣ל לִשְׁמִ֑י וַיְבָ֥רֶךְ אֹת֖וֹ שָֽׁם׃  
> Then Jacob asked him and said, “Please tell me your name.” But he said, “Why is it that you ask my name?” And he blessed him there.

> **30** וַיִּקְרָ֧א יַעֲקֹ֛ב שֵׁ֥ם הַמָּק֖וֹם פְּנִיאֵ֑ל כִּֽי־רָאִ֤יתִי אֱלֹהִים֙ פָּנִ֣ים אֶל־פָּנִ֔ים וַתִּנָּצֵ֖ל נַפְשִֽׁי׃  
> So Jacob named the place Peniel, for he said, “I have seen God face to face, yet my life has been preserved.”

> **31** וַיִּֽזְרַֽח־ל֣וֹ הַשֶּׁ֔מֶשׁ כַּאֲשֶׁ֥ר עָבַ֖ר אֶת־פְּנוּאֵ֑ל וְה֥וּא צֹלֵ֖עַ עַל־יְרֵכֽוֹ׃  
> Now the sun rose upon him just as he crossed over Penuel, and he was limping on his thigh.

> **32** עַל־כֵּ֡ן לֹֽא־יֹאכְל֨וּ בְנֵֽי־יִשְׂרָאֵ֜ל אֶת־גִּ֣יד הַנָּשֶׁ֗ה אֲשֶׁר֙ עַל־כַּ֣ף הַיָּרֵ֔ךְ עַ֖ד הַיּ֣וֹם הַזֶּ֑ה כִּ֤י נָגַע֙ בְּכַף־יֶ֣רֶךְ יַעֲקֹ֔ב בְּגִ֖יד הַנָּשֶֽׁה׃

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools, **as revised by claim audit 3**. Five, the old third having been discarded.

1. **The unit's boundaries are the manuscript's own — and that is the single ground the extent rests on.** `[T]` `[S: audit 3]` In Leningrad, as the WLC prints it, a *petuchah* closes Heb 32:3 and a *setumah* closes 33:17, with no division of any kind between — verified twice, in the reading text and independently in the OSIS source. **Both chapter systems disagree with it**, the English break falling two verses earlier and the Hebrew three, so the Masoretic layout assigns the Mahanaim angels to the Laban unit; that disagreement is itself the argument for beginning at 32:3 rather than at 32:1. Surfaced by: the apparatus check, Structure. *High confidence on the boundaries.* **Two cautions the audit imposed.** The silence between them is **not** evidence: the preceding Masoretic block runs 148 verses, the longest in Genesis, against a median of 8 — the Masoretes are paragraphing coarsely here, and it is the two markers that carry the weight, not the gap. And the lexical "frame" — פָּגַשׁ at 32:17 and 33:8, its only two Genesis verses, and חָצָה at 32:7 and 33:1, likewise — brackets roughly **32:6–33:1**, so it is evidence of **cohesion around the Jabbok**, not of this extent; on a baseline of every 47-verse Genesis window, 180 windows score as well or better.

2. **פָּנִים ("face") is the leading word, and 32:20 is one of only four verses in the Hebrew Bible to carry it four times. Both English versions render one of its four faces as "face"; the Greek renders three.** `[T]` `[S: audit 3]` פָּנִים stands **15 times in 9 verses** of this unit (WLC) — 32:3, 32:16, 32:17, 32:20 ×4, 32:21, 32:30 ×2, 33:3, 33:10 ×2, 33:14 ×2. Verses with four or more in the whole Hebrew Bible: Ezek 10:14 (×7) and Ezek 1:10 (×5), both of the cherubim's faces; Isa 21:15 (×4), all of them the preposition מִפְּנֵי; and Gen 32:20. **The counting convention, which the audit requires be declared:** these are *lemma* counts, and on them Gen 32:20 is also the only narrative verse among the four. **Count only the substantive "face" and 32:20 has three, tying with Exod 34:35 — which is also narrative prose.** Say which count is in use before calling it unique. Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Translations, Original Languages. *High confidence on the figures.*

3. **Jacob prays by quoting God, and across three verses the quotation grows.** `[T]` `[S: audit 3]` At 32:9 he has God say "Return to your country and to your relatives, **and I will do you good**" — where at 31:3 God said "and I will **be with you**", a near-homophone. By 32:12 that has become the infinitive absolute, "I will **surely** do you good" — a form which stands in **one verse of the Hebrew Bible**. The escalation is local and sequential, inside one prayer, and that is the primary evidence. The cross-references corroborate it: the opening verb וְשַׂמְתִּי אֶת־זַרְעֲךָ stands in two verses only, Gen 13:16 (to **Abram**, a *dust* promise) and here; "which cannot be numbered for multitude" stands in three, its Genesis partner being **the angel's word to Hagar about Ishmael** (16:10); and Jacob was promised **dust** at 28:14, never sand. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Tool 11 Move 4, Original Languages. *High confidence on the lexical data.* **Three corrections from the audit.** God *does* speak יָטַב in Genesis — at 4:7, to Cain, twice — so the claim is that he never speaks it **as a promise**. Jacob's "sand of the sea" is a **compression** of Gen 22:17's "sand which is on the seashore", not a quotation of it. And the composite technique is **not peculiar to this passage**: Eve at 3:2–3, Abraham at 24:7, the servant at 24:37–38, Jacob again at 48:3–4 and Joseph at 50:24 all quote divine speech inexactly. This is the densest and most self-serving instance, not a singular move. Commentators split on motive — the one in the library reads it as paraphrase, two others as manipulation.

4. **33:4 is the first instance of a reconciliation formula the book uses three times and the rest of the canon never.** `[T]` `[S: audit 3]` **נָפַל עַל־צַוָּאר — "fell on his neck" — stands in exactly three verses of the whole Hebrew Bible, all in Genesis: 33:4, 45:14, 46:29 — and all three weep.** Esau and Jacob, then Joseph and Benjamin, then Joseph and Jacob: the gesture is the book's shorthand for estranged kin restored, and this is where it enters. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Tool 11 Move 4, Christological Reading. *High confidence.* **This replaces a claim the audit discarded** — that the three Jacob–Esau "neck" verses (27:16, 27:40, 33:4) form a designed arc. They do not: 27:16 is a *clothing* verse and pairs with 41:42, the chain on Joseph's neck; 27:40 is the yoke idiom, which accounts for 18 of the noun's 38 canon verses; only the bare noun joined them. **And the downstream link is weaker than first stated:** Gen 33:4 and Luke 15:20 remain the only two verses in the Greek canon with running, falling on the neck and kissing together, but they **share no word form**, the verbatim string matches Gen 46:29 and Tobit 11:9, and Acts 20:37 — same author as Luke — is nearer on the words. *Possible, not moderate–high.*

5. **The blessing is handed back, and the oracle is performed in reverse.** `[T]` בְּרָכָה stands once in the unit, at 33:11 — "take my blessing" — and its partner is Esau's accusation at 27:36, "he has taken away my blessing". Isaac's blessing had said "let your mother's sons bow down to you" (27:29, שָׁחָה); here Jacob bows seven times to Esau (33:3) and his wives and children bow as well (33:6, 7). **The ESV and the LXX keep the word "blessing" at 33:11; the NASB95 levels it to "gift"** and the return is invisible to anyone reading the study text alone. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Translations, Tool 11 Move 4, Christological Reading. *High confidence.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview states the book's pressure as a measurable one — *bārak* 73× against *ʾārar* 9×, the ratio inverting at the call of Abram — and the question as: can the blessing outrun the curse, and through whom? `[S: overview]` This unit is where that question is asked of the second generation's quarrel, and it is asked in the vocabulary of blessing: בָּרַךְ twice (32:26, 32:29) and בְּרָכָה once (33:11), all three in scenes where a blessing is being extracted, withheld, or given back.

**Passage level.** The narrator's concern here is not whether Jacob survives Esau — he tells us at 33:4 that he does, and quickly. It is **what Jacob is left holding.** Three things are taken from him in the course of the unit and one thing is given: he loses the posture of the firstborn (he bows, 33:3), the name he was born with (32:28), and the gift he had assembled as a hedge (33:11); he gains a limp (32:31) and a blessing he asked for rather than stole (32:26). The passage exists to put the man who got the blessing by grasping into the position of asking for one, twice in one night — once from God and once from his brother. `[I]`

The narrator does not editorialise, which is the book's habit and the third of the overview's preaching traps. `[S: overview]` He does, however, arrange: Jacob's own words carry the self-assessment the narrator declines to supply (32:10, "I have become small").

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Two things have just been set in place, one at each level.

*Immediately.* At 32:1–2 (Heb 32:2–3) the angels of God meet Jacob and he names the place **Mahanaim**, "two camps" — a dual, given to a place where he has just seen *one* camp ("this is God's camp", מַחֲנֵה אֱלֹהִים). `[T]` The name is a dual with nothing yet to be dual about.

*At the book level.* Three outstanding words hang over Jacob from chapters 25–31: the oracle that "the older shall serve the younger" (25:23); Isaac's blessing, which made his brothers bow to him and cursed those who cursed him (27:29); and Esau's vow to kill him (27:41), which is the reason he left and the reason returning is dangerous. And one promise is outstanding in the other direction: God's own "return to the land of your fathers, and I will be with you" (31:3).

**Necessity answer.** The passage exists *here* because the dual name has been given and has not yet been filled. The first thing Jacob does on hearing that Esau is coming is **divide his people into two camps** — לִשְׁנֵי מַחֲנוֹת, 32:7 — and the first thing he says in prayer is "now I have become **two camps**" (32:10). `[T]` The place-name God's angels prompted is immediately occupied by Jacob's fear. The angelic camp named Two Camps is answered, within five verses, by a frightened man's two camps; and the division is made with חָצָה, a verb that **stands in Genesis only at 32:7 and 33:1** — he divides in fear before Esau comes, and divides again when he sees him coming. `[T]`

That is why the Leningrad *petuchah* after Heb 32:3 is not a wall. The paragraph break separates the naming from the enactment, and the word carries across it. The unit is the answer to the verse before it, which is exactly the relation a paragraph division marks.

**Implication for this passage.** Mahanaim is not a pious aside. Read positionally, the unit is about what Jacob does with a word of reassurance: he hears "God's camp" and makes two camps of his own. The prayer at 32:9–12 sits between the dividing (32:7–8) and the present (32:13–21), and it is the only thing Jacob does in the unit that is not a contingency. `[I]`

#### General context

**Immediately before (32:1–2).** The Mahanaim encounter, which closes a paragraph in Leningrad and which the vocabulary ties forward. Note that פָּגַע ("met") at 32:1 and פָּגַע at 28:11 ("he came upon the place") bracket the twenty years away — an observation that belongs to the preceding unit, not this one, but which explains why the "meeting" verb changes when Esau becomes the one met.

**Immediately after (33:18–20).** A three-verse paragraph of its own in Leningrad (setumah after 33:17 and after 33:20): Jacob arrives at Shechem, buys a field, and builds an altar he names El-Elohe-Israel — the first use of his new name in a name he gives. The unit's close at 33:17 therefore separates the *dwelling* from the *worship*, and separates both from chapter 34, whose trouble begins at the city 33:18 names.

**Section and book.** The unit sits inside the *toledot* of Isaac (25:19–35:29), which is the structural spine the overview names and which Patrick's own reading of DeRouchie confirms. It is the last extended narrative before the Dinah chapter, and the last scene in which Esau appears alive.

**Widely read out of context?** Yes, in one specific way. Genesis 32:22–32 is routinely preached as a free-standing sermon on prayer and perseverance — "I will not let you go unless you bless me" — and the overview's own unit title ("Peniel: I will not let you go") shows how strong the pull is. `[S: overview]` Detached from 32:3–21 and 33:1–17, the wrestling becomes a lesson in persistence. Kept inside the paragraph, it is one of two encounters in a single night-and-morning, and the second one is with a brother whose face Jacob will call God's face (33:10).

### 3. Structure

The manuscript's own division is the outer frame; within it the scene changes are clean.

| Verses (Eng) | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 32:3–6 | The embassy | Jacob sends *malʾākîm* to Esau; the report comes back: four hundred men |
| 32:7–8 | The division | Fear → two camps; the dual name of 32:2 enacted |
| 32:9–12 | The prayer | The unit's only address to God, and its only occurrence of the covenant name |
| 32:13–21 | The present | Five droves, three briefings, the plan to cover a face |
| 32:22–32 | The ford | Alone; the wrestling; the name; the limp; the food law |
| 33:1–11 | The meeting | Esau runs; the two gifts; "take my blessing" |
| 33:12–17 | The parting | Two offers declined; Esau to Seir, Jacob to Succoth |

**The device is a frame with a hinge.** The outer members are two embassies in opposite directions: Jacob's messengers go ahead to Esau (32:3–6) and Esau's escort is declined on the way back (33:12–17). Inside them, two gift-transactions: the *minḥah* sent ahead (32:13–21) and the *minḥah* pressed on a reluctant recipient (33:8–11). At the centre, alone, the ford.

Four observations make the frame more than decorative, and each is checkable:

- **"Four hundred men" stands at 32:6 and at 33:1** — the report and the sight. Between them lies everything Jacob does about it. `[T]`
- **חָצָה ("divide") stands at 32:7 and 33:1**, its only two occurrences in Genesis — the same act, once in the dark and once in daylight. `[T]`
- **The present crosses before his face (32:21, עַל־פָּנָיו) and then Jacob crosses before his family's face (33:3, לִפְנֵיהֶם).** He puts his goods in front of him, then puts himself in front of them. `[T]`
- **The unit opens and closes with a journey resumed:** 32:3 "Jacob sent messengers before him" and 33:16–17 "Esau returned on his way to Seir; Jacob journeyed to Succoth". Two men, two destinations, one of which is the land of promise.

I decline a chiasm here. The elements pair but they do not nest, and the centre (32:22–32) is not the middle term of a symmetry so much as the thing the two halves are arranged *around*. "Frame with a hinge" says what the text does without claiming more.

### 4. Linking Words

Hebrew narrative runs on the *wayyiqtol* chain, and tracing every "and" here would be noise. Four connectives carry weight.

**כִּי at 32:8** (→*for*→, giving the reason): "**for** he said, If Esau comes to the one company and attacks it, then the company which is left will escape." The כִּי grounds the division in a calculation, and the calculation is stated as direct speech — the narrator lets us hear Jacob doing arithmetic with his family. `[T]`

**כִּי at 32:11** — "deliver me … **for** I fear him". The prayer's one stated ground is fear, not covenant. The covenant appears in the prayer as a quotation (32:9, 32:12), not as the reason for asking.

**כִּי אִם at 32:26** — "I will not let you go **unless** you bless me." The construction is exceptive, and it is the only conditional in the unit. Jacob, who has twice obtained what he wanted by arrangement, states a condition to a man he cannot throw.

**עַל־כֵּן at 32:32 and 33:17** (→*therefore*→): "**therefore** the sons of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hip" and "**therefore** the place is named Succoth". Two "therefore"s, both narratorial, both reaching out of the story into the reader's present — one into a food custom, one into a place-name. They are the unit's two doors into the reader's world, and they are discussed under Tool 6.

Worth noting what is *absent*: there is no connective between the wrestling and the meeting. 32:32 ends with the limp; 33:1 begins "Jacob lifted his eyes and looked". The two scenes are juxtaposed without a word of explanation, and the reader is left to make the join. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

**Largely N/A** — this is narrative prose, not poetry, and there is no parallelistic structure to read. Two near-parallel constructions earn a mention because they are doing structural work rather than poetic work:

- **32:20's four clauses on *panim*** are arranged as a chain rather than a parallel: "I will cover his face with the present going before my face, and afterward I will see his face; perhaps he will lift my face." Four faces, four different relations — propitiation, precedence, sight, acceptance. The accumulation is the point and it is closer to a Repetition finding (Tool 10) than a Parallels one.
- **33:3 and 33:6–7** stage the same act three times with ascending subjects: Jacob bows (33:3), the maids and their children bow (33:6), Leah and her children bow, then Joseph and Rachel bow (33:7). Four prostrations in five verses, שָׁחָה four times. `[T]` A narrative, not poetic, parallelism.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

The narrator intrudes four times, and two of the intrusions are the most consequential sentences in the unit.

**32:32 — the food law.** "**Therefore** to this day the sons of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hip." This is the authorial aside at its most explicit: the narrator steps out of the ninth-century-BC night at the Jabbok and addresses a reader who keeps a custom. `[T]` It is one of the overview's six verified "to this day" asides (19:37, 38; 26:33; 32:32 [Heb 32:33]; 35:20; 47:26). `[S: overview]` Two things follow. First, the narrator's own interpretation of the wrestling is **not** that Jacob won; it is that Israel limps, and remembers it at every meal. Second, the aside names the actors "**the sons of Israel**" — using the name given eight verses earlier, in its national sense, for the first time in the book. The name's career begins here as a man's and is immediately used as a people's.

**33:17 — the place-name.** "**Therefore** the place is named Succoth." The second aside, and the second reach into the reader's present. See Tool 7 for what the name carries.

**32:22 "Now he arose that same night"** is a quieter intrusion — the narrator supplying the time, which the preceding scene did not require.

**32:25's "When he saw that he had not prevailed against him"** is the one place the narrator gives us the inside of the wrestler rather than of Jacob — and he does it without naming him. We are told what the man perceived before we are told what he is. The narrator's refusal to name him is sustained to the end of the scene: he is אִישׁ ("a man") at 32:24 and nothing else, and the only identification in the chapter is Jacob's own, after the fact ("I have seen God face to face", 32:30). **The narrator never confirms it.** `[T]` That restraint is a datum, and it constrains what a sermon may assert.

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory.** Esau, Jacob, Israel; Seir, Edom, Mahanaim (named in 32:2, just outside), Jabbok, Peniel, Penuel, Succoth; Leah, Rachel, Joseph; the two maids (unnamed here); "four hundred men". Routine scenery: Leah, Rachel, Joseph, the maids. Candidates with canonical freight, all four checked below: **Seir/Edom, Jabbok, Peniel/Penuel, Succoth.** `[T]`

#### The leading word — פָּנִים ("face")

**15 occurrences in 9 verses** of the unit (WLC, lemma-checked): 32:3 לְפָנָיו, 32:16 לְפָנַי, 32:17 לְפָנֶיךָ, 32:20 פָנָיו · לְפָנָי · פָנָיו · פָנָי, 32:21 פָּנָיו, 32:30 פָּנִים ×2, 33:3 לִפְנֵיהֶם, 33:10 פָנֶיךָ · פְּנֵי, 33:14 לִפְנֵי · לְפָנַי. Eight are the substantive "face", seven the preposition "before". Across Heb 32:2–35:29 — the extent of the earlier dig — the figure is 20 occurrences in 14 verses, so **three-quarters of the block's faces fall in this paragraph's 47 verses.** `[T]`

The chain is not decorative; it is the unit's argument in one word. Jacob sends gifts *before his face* to cover a *face* so that he may *see the face* and have his own *face lifted* (32:20); he names the ford "Face of God" because he has seen God *face to face* (32:30); and at the meeting he says to his brother "I have seen *your face* as one sees the *face of God*" (33:10). **The sentence Jacob says to Esau is the sentence he said about the wrestler**, and the word that joins them is the word the English versions mostly do not print.

*High confidence.* The count is lemma-verified; the reading of the chain is `[I]` and well supported by the distribution.

#### Two phrases Jacob coins, and where they go

**אוּלַי and אֲכַפְּרָה in one verse (32:20).** The two words stand together in **two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 32:20 and Exod 32:30.** `[T]` **But they are not the same construction, and a draft of this report said they were.** `[S: audit 3]` At Exod 32:30 Moses says "**perhaps** I can **make atonement** for your sin" — the one governs the other. **At Gen 32:20 the אוּלַי governs the last clause of the verse, "perhaps he will lift up my face", not the atonement verb**, which stands earlier under "for he said". The NASB95 renders the syntax correctly, and so does the Greek, which has no "perhaps" at Exod 32:30 at all. **This is a co-occurrence of two words in a verse, never a shared idiom** — and the correction also applies to the standing claim-audit queue item that records it.

**What to keep instead, which is stronger:** **Gen 32:20 is the only place in the canon where כִּפֶּר ("cover, atone") takes a person's face as its direct object.** `[T]` `[S: audit 3]`

**פָּנִים אֶל־פָּנִים — "face to face" (32:30).** **Five verses in the Hebrew Bible: Gen 32:30, Exod 33:11, Deut 34:10, Judg 6:22, Ezek 20:35** — verified by ordered phrase on both the plain and the skeletal pass, which agree. `[T]` Jacob coins it; its next two users are **Moses** ("the LORD used to speak to Moses face to face", Exod 33:11; "no prophet has risen like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face", Deut 34:10), and then Gideon, who says it in terror ("Alas, O Lord GOD! For now I have seen the angel of the LORD face to face", Judg 6:22) — the same relief Jacob expresses, that he is still alive.

**A draft of this report proposed that the two phrases converge on Exodus 32–34 by design. Claim audit 3 discarded it.** `[S: audit 3]` On a baseline run at six settings, Exod 32–34 ranks **39th to 159th of 813 chapters** as a partner to this passage, and between five and twenty-one other Genesis blocks share more with it; the Greek pulls the two further apart, since Exod 33:11 reads ἐνώπιος ἐνωπίῳ rather than πρόσωπον πρὸς πρόσωπον. **The two distributions stand as distributions; the convergence does not stand as a design.** And of the five "face to face" verses, the one that shares a second rare idiom with this passage is not Exod 33:11 but **Judg 6:22** — see the reverse table.

#### The present — מִנְחָה (32:13, 18, 20, 21; 33:10)

**Five occurrences in the unit; twelve in all of Genesis — and the twelve fall in three places only.** `[T]` 4:3, 4, 5 (Cain's and Abel's offerings); 32:13, 18, 20, 21 and 33:10 (Jacob's to Esau); 43:11, 15, 25, 26 (the brothers' to Joseph). **Three brother-conflicts, and the word occurs nowhere else in the book.** The distribution is verified; what it *means* is capped — see Phase 5.5, where the claim audit's verdict on a Cain-and-Abel reading of this unit is recorded. *High on the distribution; moderate at most on any design, and a reversal-of-Cain reading is not available.* The word's later career is overwhelmingly cultic — the grain offering of Leviticus — but here it is diplomatic tribute, and the Greek reads it so (δῶρα, "gifts", at every occurrence). The question of whether the cultic sense is active is left to Tool 11 and the biblical-theology extension rather than decided here; what is checkable is that **Jacob calls it מִנְחָה four times and then, at 33:11, calls it בְּרָכָה.** The thing changes name in his mouth at the moment Esau takes it.

#### The camp — מַחֲנֶה (32:7, 8 ×2, 10, 21; 33:8)

**Six occurrences in the unit; eight in all of Genesis.** `[T]` Mahanaim itself (32:2) is a separate lemma, a proper noun, and is not counted among them. The arc is worth laying out, because the word's *number* changes as the unit proceeds:

| Verse | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| 32:2 | מַחֲנֵה אֱלֹהִים / מַחֲנָיִם | one camp, seen; a dual name, given |
| 32:7 | לִשְׁנֵי מַחֲנוֹת | two camps, made in fear |
| 32:8 | הַמַּחֲנֶה הָאַחַת … הַמַּחֲנֶה הַנִּשְׁאָר | the one and the remainder |
| 32:10 | הָיִיתִי לִשְׁנֵי מַחֲנוֹת | "I have become two camps" |
| 32:21 | בַּמַּחֲנֶה | the camp he lodges in |
| 33:8 | כָּל־הַמַּחֲנֶה הַזֶּה | "all this camp" — Esau's question, singular again |

Esau's question at 33:8 collapses the division Jacob made. The man who split his household in two to survive the meeting is asked, at the meeting, about **one** camp. `[I]`

#### Two verbs that occur nowhere else

- **אָבַק ("to wrestle"), 32:24, 25 — two occurrences in the whole Hebrew Bible, both in this unit.** `[T]` There is no other wrestling in Scripture. The root is unattested outside these two verses, which is as rare as a verb gets, and it means the narrator has reached for a word he uses once.
- **שָׂרָה ("to strive"), 32:28 — two occurrences in the whole Hebrew Bible: here, and Hosea 12:3 [Heb 12:4].** `[T]` The verb that explains the name *Israel* occurs exactly once more in the canon, in a prophet's retelling of this night. The etymology the text offers for the nation's name rests on a word used twice.

#### קָטֹנְתִּי — "I have become small" (32:10)

**The verb קָטֹן ("be small") stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible — Gen 32:10, 2 Sam 7:19, Amos 8:5, 1 Chr 17:17 — and Gen 32:10 is its only first-person form.** `[T]` Nobody else in Scripture says "I have become small."

It matters because of who is saying it. Jacob is **הַקָּטָן**, "the younger", in the mouths of the narrator and of Rebekah (27:15, 27:42) — the same root. The man whose whole story turns on being the younger son says, in that root, *I have become small*. Both English versions lose it: NASB95 "I am unworthy", ESV "I am not worthy of the least". *High confidence on the distribution; moderate–high that the root-play is deliberate, since Genesis plays on names and roots constantly and the alternative — that קָטֹנְתִּי is simply idiom for unworthiness — is also available.*

#### The names, checked

- **Seir / Edom (32:3; 33:14, 16).** The narrator supplies "the country of Edom" at 32:3, which the reader has met at 25:30 where Esau is named Edom over a bowl of red stew. The gloss is the narrator telling the reader that this brother is a nation. `[T]` Esau returns to Seir at 33:16; Jacob promises at 33:14 to come to him at Seir and the book never records that he did. `[T]`
- **Jabbok (32:22).** **Seven occurrences in the Hebrew Bible: Gen 32:22, then Num 21:24; Deut 2:37; 3:16; Josh 12:2; Judg 11:13, 22 — every one of the other six a boundary.** `[T]` After Genesis, the Jabbok is nothing in Scripture but a border. Jacob wrestles at what becomes the edge of the land.
- **Peniel / Penuel (32:30, 31).** **Eight verses: Gen 32:30, 31; Judg 8:8, 9, 17; 1 Kgs 12:25; 1 Chr 4:4; 8:25.** `[T]` Gideon breaks down its tower and kills its men (Judg 8:17); Jeroboam rebuilds it (1 Kgs 12:25). The place where a patriarch saw God's face becomes a fortress razed by a judge and refortified by the king who made the calves. *The canonical history is checked; what Genesis intends by it is nothing — Genesis plants, the later books use.* `[T]` for the distribution, `[S]` for any reading of it.
- **Succoth (33:17).** **Sixteen verses, and Gen 33:17 is the first in the canon.** `[T]` The next two are **Exod 12:37 and 13:20 — Israel's first station on leaving Egypt** — followed by Num 33:5, 6, then Josh 13:27; Judg 8:5–16; 1 Kgs 7:46; Ps 60:6; 108:7; 2 Chr 4:17. **The honest limit: the Transjordanian Succoth of Gen 33:17 and the Egyptian Succoth of Exod 12:37 are almost certainly different places.** The link is the *name*, not the site, and the name means "booths". So: the first thing Jacob builds on re-entering the land gives its name to the place Israel first camps on leaving Egypt, and to the feast at which Israel lives in booths to remember it. **Claim audit 3 reduced this to a canonical echo only.** `[S: audit 3]` Eleven of the sixteen occurrences put Succoth in Transjordan or the Jordan valley and four put it in Egypt, so the texts do not allow the sites to be one place; and **סֻכּוֹת is a common noun**, "booths", for which **33:17 supplies its own etymology — the only verse in Genesis to use the common noun at all.** *What may be said: the name Israel's first camp out of Egypt bears is the name Israel's ancestor gave to his first camp back in the land. What may not: that they are the same place, or that either is named for the other. Words weak; echo possible.*

Both of the unit's two named places — **Penuel and Succoth** — are destroyed by Gideon in a single campaign (Judg 8:8–17; 8:5–16). `[T]` Noted as a distributional fact, not a design.

The study text and his own church's text diverge at four points that bear on findings. The LXX is set beside them because at two of the four it is the better witness.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 32:9 | "I will prosper you" | "that I may do you good" | וְאֵיטִיבָה (יָטַב) | Both consistent internally with 32:12; the ESV's "do you good" is closer to the root |
| 32:10 | "I am unworthy of all" | "I am not worthy of the least of all" | קָטֹנְתִּי מִכֹּל | **Both lose the root.** The ESV's "the least of" has no counterpart in the Hebrew; the NASB95 is shorter but equally rootless |
| 32:20 | "I will appease him … his face … accept me" | "I may appease him … his face … accept me" | פָנָיו · לְפָנָי · פָנָיו · פָנָי | **Four faces in the Hebrew, one printed in both versions.** The Greek prints three |
| 33:11 | "my gift" | "my blessing" | בִּרְכָתִי | **The ESV and the LXX agree against the NASB95.** "Gift" severs the 27:36 link entirely |

**Translation loss, not variance (category 1 of the triage).** The 32:20 and 32:10 entries are features of the Hebrew that no translation carries: a fourfold *panim* cannot be printed four times in English without producing nonsense ("I will cover his face with the present going before my face"), and a root-play between *the younger* and *I have become small* has no English equivalent. These are **not** translation-tradition splits and must not be reported as faults in the versions. What they mean for the preacher is that **two of this unit's strongest findings are invisible from any English pulpit Bible**, and must be given to the congregation in words rather than pointed to on the page.

**The one that is a genuine pulpit note (33:11).** Here the versions make different interpretive decisions and one of them closes a door. The Hebrew בִּרְכָתִי is the noun of 27:36 — "he has taken away my **blessing**" — and 33:11 is its only occurrence in this unit. The NASB95's "gift" is defensible as sense (it is tribute, and the same object was called מִנְחָה four times) and it is **fatal to the chain**: a congregation hearing "take my gift" cannot hear the blessing being returned. The ESV keeps it, and so does the Greek (λάβε τὰς εὐλογίας μου, "take my blessings", plural). **Where his own church's text is in use, the pew Bible is the better witness at the unit's climax** — and the preacher reading the NASB95 at his desk is the one at risk of missing it.

**Ancient versions check.** Run, and it returns two results worth keeping and one correction.

1. **The Greek keeps the face-chain where the English does not.** At 32:20 Swete has ἐξιλάσομαι τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ … ὄψομαι τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ· ἴσως γὰρ προσδέξεται τὸ πρόσωπόν μου — three of the four. At 32:30 πρόσωπον πρὸς πρόσωπον; at 33:10 τὸ πρόσωπόν σου ὡς ἄν τις ἴδοι πρόσωπον θεοῦ. The LXX of this unit is a more transparent witness to its leading word than either English version.
2. **The Greek translates the place-names rather than transliterating them.** Mahanaim becomes Παρεμβολαί ("Camps", 32:2) and Peniel/Penuel becomes Εἶδος θεοῦ ("Form of God", 32:30, 31). A Greek reader is told what the names mean and loses that they are names. *High confidence — checked in Swete.*
3. **ἐξιλάσκομαι at 32:20 is the Levitical atonement verb**, which the Greek has chosen for Jacob's אֲכַפְּרָה. See Tool 11 and the biblical-theology extension; this is category 3 of the triage (a Greek rendering the later canon reasons from) and is not a translation error.

**Pulpit divergence note.** N/A — no pulpit text declared, no sermon in view. The 33:11 finding above is recorded against the ESV because that is his own church's text and the divergence is load-bearing; it should be re-run against whatever version is declared when a sermon is in view. In particular, **the NIV84 reading at 33:11 has not been checked** and should be before this unit is preached elsewhere.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Dread, then relief that arrives too fast to be trusted.**

The first half is built out of accumulating detail, and the accumulation is the fear. The livestock are counted out one line at a time — two hundred she-goats, twenty he-goats, two hundred ewes, twenty rams, thirty camels with their young, forty cows, ten bulls, twenty she-asses, ten foals (32:14–15). Nine items, 550 animals, in two verses. The inventory is not information; it is a man calculating in the dark, and the reader is made to sit through the arithmetic. `[I]`

Then: "**Then Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed**" (32:7) — the narrator's only direct statement of Jacob's emotion, placed before the arithmetic rather than after it, so that everything that follows is read as fear's product.

The night at the ford is spare to the point of strangeness. Short clauses, no scenery, nobody named: *a man wrestled with him until daybreak.* The dialogue is four exchanges and two of them are questions about names. The contrast with the itemised livestock is total, and it is the unit's sharpest tonal move: the thing Jacob prepared for at enormous length is not the thing that happens to him.

The meeting is a reversal of register. Esau **runs** — the only running in the unit — and embraces, falls on the neck, kisses, and weeps, four verbs in one verse (33:4), while Jacob is still bowing to the ground seven times. The emotional asymmetry is the point: one brother is undone and the other is still negotiating. Jacob's next speech is a diplomatic formula ("if now I have found favour in your sight") and then two refusals of company.

**Soundtrack.** For 32:3–21, a clock. For the ford, a single cello and a lot of silence. For 33:1–11, strings coming in warm and the one brother who is not playing along.

**For the preacher:** the unit does not end in resolution and should not be preached as though it does. Esau goes to Seir, Jacob goes the other way, and the two brothers in Genesis who have just wept on each other's necks never speak again in the book. `[T]`

**The servant-and-lord drumbeat is the finding most easily missed, because the English carries it perfectly and nobody counts.** The oracle said the older would serve the younger (25:23) and Isaac's blessing said "be lord over your brothers" (27:29, גְּבִיר). In these 47 verses Jacob says the opposite **thirteen times**: אָדוֹן eight times, every one "my lord" of Esau, and עֶבֶד five times of himself to Esau. `[T]`

> **A sense-check correction, recorded because it is the kind that matters.** The raw lemma count for עֶבֶד in the unit is nine, and a first draft of this report reported nine and called them all Jacob's self-designation. **Four of the nine are something else:** "male and female servants" in the property list (32:5), Jacob's own servants driving the droves (32:16 ×2), and "your servant" addressed to **God** rather than to Esau (32:10). The figure for the inversion is five, not nine. A lemma count is not a finding until the matched words have been read, which is what gate item (e3) requires and what this draft initially failed to do.

**The covenant name occurs exactly once.** יְהוָה stands in these 47 verses at **32:9 and nowhere else** — one occurrence against 163 in Genesis. `[T]` It falls in the prayer, and specifically in the clause where Jacob quotes God's command to return. God is otherwise named אֱלֹהִים — **7 occurrences in 6 verses** of the unit (32:9 ×2, 32:28, 32:30, 33:5, 33:10, 33:11). The unit's only use of the covenant name is in a quotation of covenant speech — and it is the quotation Move 4(e) shows to be edited. Surfaced here, developed at Tool 11.

**Near-repetition worth noting.** Jacob's instruction to the first drove (32:17–18) is reported, then re-issued to the second, the third, and all of them (32:19) in summary rather than in full — "after this manner you shall speak". The narrator gives the formula once and then tells us it was repeated, which is a different device from giving it four times, and it keeps the pace up while making the plan feel exhaustive. `[I]`

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Source inventory — and why this section is shaped as it is.** The overview's finding governs: **Genesis quotes nothing and alludes to nothing, because there is no earlier scriptural source.** `[S: overview]` Moves 1–3 therefore have no external citation to work on in this unit, and **Move 4 carries the whole load** — which is the overview's explicit instruction to the digs. A reverse check (where this unit is used downstream) is given after Move 4, since that is the honest substitute.

**Moves 1–3: nil return, with its reason.** No external OT citation or allusion in Gen 32:3–33:17. There is no earlier Scripture for Genesis to cite. Move 1 has no source passage, Move 2 has nothing to track in the normal direction, Move 3 has no pairing. This is a structural nil, not an unsearched one.

#### Move 4 — Internal echo check

Six findings, all with the opening material of the Jacob cycle held open, and all lemma-verified.

**(a) 27:16 and 27:40 → 33:4 — the neck.** *(answers §earlier; high confidence)* `[T]`
צַוָּאר ("neck") stands in six verses of Genesis: 27:16, 27:40, 33:4, 41:42, 45:14, 46:29. Three are Jacob and Esau and they run in sequence. At 27:16 Rebekah puts the goatskins "on his hands and on the smooth part of his **neck**" — the neck is the instrument of the deception, the part of Jacob that had to be disguised to feel like Esau. At 27:40 Isaac tells Esau "you shall break his yoke from your **neck**". At 33:4 Esau "fell on his **neck** and kissed him". *What it adds:* the brother who was deceived by a dressed-up neck puts his arms round the real one. The chain is not available in English by any verbal signal — all three verses say "neck" — which is why it needs counting rather than reading.

**(b) 27:29 → 33:3, 6, 7 — the bowing.** *(reverses §earlier; high confidence)* `[T]`
Isaac's blessing: "let peoples serve you … and let your mother's sons **bow down** (שָׁחָה) to you; be **lord** (גְּבִיר) over your brothers" (27:29). Here Jacob bows to the ground seven times (33:3) and his household bow twice more (33:6, 7), while calling Esau **my lord** eight times and himself **your servant** nine. *What it adds:* the blessing is not revoked and it is not performed. The man who holds it behaves throughout as though he does not, and the text neither explains nor condemns the discrepancy. A sermon has to decide what to make of that; the exegesis's job is to show that the discrepancy is deliberate and measured.

*An apparatus note belongs here.* **27:29's first "bow down" is a ketiv** — the WLC prints וישתחו unpointed, with the qere left to the Masorah — which is precisely the trap the corpus README documents, and the reason this chain was confirmed by lemma rather than by surface search. `[T]` as a datum about Leningrad; the division of ketiv and qere is reception, `[S]`.

**(c) 27:36 → 33:11 — the blessing returned.** *(answers §earlier; high confidence)* `[T]`
בְּרָכָה occurs once in the unit (33:11) and Esau's accusation is its partner: "he has taken away my **blessing**" (27:36). Jacob's words at 33:11 are קַח־נָא אֶת־בִּרְכָתִי — "take, please, my blessing". *What it adds:* the only thing in the unit Jacob gives away by name is the thing he is accused of having stolen. Whether this is restitution, or a man buying off a grievance with the word that names it, the text does not say — and the gift's other name, מִנְחָה (four times), keeps both readings live. See Tool 8: the NASB95 removes the reader's access to this finding.

**(d) 25:23 → the whole unit — the oracle inverted.** *(reverses §earlier; moderate–high confidence)* `[T]` for the data, `[I]` for the inversion
"The older shall serve (עָבַד) the younger" (25:23). In this unit אָדוֹן ("lord") stands eight times, every one of them "my lord" of Esau, and Jacob calls himself Esau's **servant** five times (32:4, 18, 20; 33:5, 14) — thirteen self-abasements in 47 verses, every one inverting the oracle. (The lemma עֶבֶד occurs nine times; four are household servants and one is addressed to God. See the sense-check note under Tool 10.) *What it adds:* the unit performs the oracle backwards for 47 verses and does not comment. *Moderate–high rather than high because the inversion is a pattern across many verses rather than a single verbal echo, and a reader could take Jacob's deference as diplomatic convention rather than as a reversal of 25:23.* Both readings are available; the count is not in question.

**(e) 31:3 and 28:14 → 32:9, 12 — the promise as Jacob quotes it.** *(answers §earlier; high confidence on the data)* `[T]`
This is the finding that most repays the lemma work, and it has three parts.

- **The verb.** At 32:9 Jacob addresses "the LORD who said to me, 'Return to your country and to your relatives, **and I will prosper you**'" (וְאֵיטִיבָה, יָטַב), and at 32:12 "**You** said, 'I will surely prosper you'" (הֵיטֵב אֵיטִיב, infinitive absolute). God's words at 31:3 are: "Return to the land of your fathers and to your relatives, **and I will be with you**" (וְאֶהְיֶה עִמָּךְ). **יָטַב stands in nine verses of Genesis — 4:7; 12:13, 16; 32:9, 12; 34:18; 40:14; 41:37; 45:16 — and God speaks it in none of them.** The only two occurrences in which it is attributed to God are the two in which Jacob quotes him.
- **The simile.** "As the **sand** of the sea" (32:12). חוֹל ("sand") stands in three verses of Genesis: 22:17, 32:12, 41:49. **22:17 is to Abraham.** What was said to Jacob at 28:14 was כַּעֲפַר הָאָרֶץ, "as the **dust** of the earth" — and to Abraham at 13:16 likewise. The sand belongs to the Akedah oath; Jacob's own promise was dust.
- **The clause.** "Which cannot be numbered for multitude" (אֲשֶׁר לֹא־יִסָּפֵר מֵרֹב). **Three verses in the Hebrew Bible carry this clause: Gen 16:10, Gen 32:12, 1 Kgs 3:8.** Gen 16:10 is **the angel of the LORD to Hagar**, of Ishmael. *The plain-pass phrase search found only 32:12; the skeletal pass recovered 16:10 and 1 Kgs 3:8, the spelling differing — the gate working exactly as it is meant to.*

*What it adds:* Jacob prays by quoting the covenant, and the quotation is a composite of a verb God does not use, a simile given to his grandfather and a clause given to Hagar. **Two readings are honestly available and the text decides neither.** (i) The narrator is showing a man who improves the promise in the retelling — the grasper grasping in prayer, which is of a piece with a unit in which he hedges, divides and calculates. (ii) Free quotation is ordinary narrative convention in Hebrew, "sand" and "dust" are interchangeable hyperboles, and *nothing* is being signalled. What is *not* available is a sermon that treats 32:12 as a straight citation of something God said to Jacob — because he did not. `[I]` on the reading, `[T]` on all three lexical data.

**(f) 28:20–22 → planted, not resolved here.** *(planted — watch for resolution; high confidence)* `[T]`
Jacob's vow at Bethel asked for bread, clothing, and a return "in **peace** (שָׁלוֹם) to my father's house", and promised a tithe. **None of it is discharged in this unit.** It is discharged at 35:1–7, outside the paragraph, and the next verse after this unit (33:18) says Jacob came שָׁלֵם to Shechem — the vow's own word, one consonant different, in a verse the manuscript puts in the next paragraph. `[T]` Flagged forward: **the vow is answered just across the seam**, which is a fact a sermon on this unit should know and a reason the three-unit re-cut needs its third member.

**Nil checks run and empty:** no echo of the flood, Babel or the Abrahamic land-grant in this unit; no use here of the *nākar* ("recognise") chain that runs 27:23 → 37:32 → 38:25 → 42:7; no *saw/took* seizure formula.

**Hosea 12:3–4 [Heb 12:4–5] — the canon reading this unit.** *(high confidence)* `[T]`
"In the womb he grasped his brother's heel (עָקַב), and in his manhood he **strove with God** (שָׂרָה אֶת־אֱלֹהִים); he strove with **the angel** (מַלְאָךְ) and **prevailed** (וַיֻּכָל); **he wept** (בָּכָה) and sought his favour (וַיִּתְחַנֶּן־לוֹ)."

Four things make this the most valuable downstream witness available.

1. **שָׂרָה is the two-occurrence verb** — Gen 32:28 and Hos 12:3, and nowhere else. Hosea is quoting the etymology, not merely the story.
2. **יָכֹל** picks up Gen 32:28's וַתּוּכָל, which in Genesis is the wrestler's word about Jacob (and whose other occurrence in the unit, 32:25, is the narrator's "he could not" about the wrestler). Hosea keeps it.
3. **Hosea identifies the wrestler as a מַלְאָךְ** where Genesis says only אִישׁ and lets Jacob say "God". That is the canon making a judgement Genesis withholds — and it is `[S]` for Genesis, not `[T]`.
4. **"He wept" is not at the Jabbok in Genesis; it is at the meeting (33:4).** A draft of this report took that to mean Hosea read 32:22–32 and 33:1–11 as one episode, and called it the best argument for the extent. **Claim audit 3 withdrew it.** `[S: audit 3]` The weeping is a **formula**, not a fingerprint: נָפַל + צַוָּאר + בָּכָה stands in three verses, all Genesis, and at **45:14 the verb is the singular Hosea actually has**, where 33:4 reads "and they wept", plural. Hosea's Greek reads the verse corporately and plurally; Hos 12:3 moves from Gen 25/27 to Gen 32 inside one verse; v.4 locates the finding at **Bethel**; and Hos 12 ranks 548th of 813 chapters for lexical contact with this passage. **Hosea witnesses to the naming scene. It cannot delimit a unit.**

**Luke 15:20 ← Gen 33:4.** *(moderate–high confidence)* `[T]` for the distribution; `[I]` for intentional allusion
The claim can be put precisely. A siglum-stripped, accent-stripped ordered search of Swete's LXX and the SBLGNT together returns **exactly two verses in the whole Greek canon containing running, falling on the neck, and kissing: Gen 33:4 and Luke 15:20.** The wider idiom "fell on the neck" returns Gen 33:4, 45:14, 46:29; Tob 11:9, 11:13 (both Greek texts); Luke 15:20; Acts 20:37 — and, in the quite different "yoke on the neck" sense, Deut 28:48; Jer 35:14 [Eng 28:14]; Lam 1:14; 5:5; Acts 15:10; Est 5:2; Dan 5:7.

*Source context (Move 1, run on the Genesis side since this is the reverse direction):* the embrace at 33:4 comes after a night of fear and a morning of sevenfold prostration, from the brother who had sworn to kill him, and it is unexplained. Esau gives no reason and asks no apology.

*Honest competition.* **Tobit 11:9 is the closer verbal partner for one clause** — its καὶ ἔκλαυσαν ἀμφότεροι is verbatim with Gen 33:4's ending — and it has the running too, though not the kiss. Luke's own wording adds ἐσπλαγχνίσθη and intensifies the kiss to κατεφίλησεν, neither of which is in Genesis. So: Genesis supplies the unique three-verb set, Tobit supplies a nearer match for the weeping, and Luke has his own vocabulary on top. *The conclusion that holds is that Luke 15:20 is written in the idiom this unit originates and Tobit continues; a claim that Luke is specifically invoking Esau needs more than the distribution gives.* Capped at moderate–high and routed to Open Questions.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew narrative prose, in the patriarchal cycle. Reading rules: the events are presented as having happened; the theology is carried by arrangement, selection and repeated vocabulary rather than by comment; the narrator's asides (Tool 6) are the only authoritative interpretation offered inside the text.

Two embedded sub-genres worth naming:

- **An aetiology** at 32:32 — a narratorial explanation of a standing custom, in a form ("therefore … to this day") the book uses six times.
- **Two name-givings with stated etymologies** (32:28 Israel, 32:30 Peniel; and 33:17 Succoth without a formula). Genesis explains names constantly, and an explanation offered inside the text is a datum about what the narrator wants heard, not a claim about historical philology. `[I]`

Brief by design: the genre is not contested here.

### 13. Copycat

Narrative, so the tool applies, and it matters because this unit is preached as a model more often than almost any other in Genesis.

| Character / action | Category | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Jacob dividing the camp (32:7–8) | **Descriptive only** | Prudence born of fear. Neither commended nor condemned |
| Jacob's prayer (32:9–12) | **Descriptive, with a caution** | The posture (appealing to God's own promise) is exemplary and the NT commends such praying. But the *content* misquotes the promise (Move 4e). Preach the posture, not the wording |
| Jacob's gift (32:13–21) | **Descriptive only** | The text gives his motive in his own words ("I will cover his face"), and then lets Esau refuse the gift twice before taking it |
| "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (32:26) | **Unique event — and the most commonly mis-copied line in Genesis** | A one-off encounter at the ford of a man he cannot identify. It is not a method of prayer. See Preaching Pitfalls |
| Jacob's sevenfold prostration (33:3) | **Descriptive only** | Ancient court protocol, not humility to imitate or pride to condemn |
| Esau's embrace (33:4) | **Normative pattern, with the narrator's silence noted** | Of all the behaviour in the unit this is the one the text presents without qualification. The narrator adds no warning about Esau's motives — the dots over "and he kissed him" are the Masoretes' suspicion, not the author's (see Textual Variants) |
| Jacob's two refusals (33:12–15) | **Descriptive, possibly negative** | He gives a reason (the children and the flocks) and then goes the other way. The text neither endorses nor exposes him |

**The mechanism note.** The wrestling is the clearest *passive-receipt* act in the unit: Jacob does not win, he is injured, and the blessing comes after the injury and in response to a request. The posture the shape of the act implies is petition and dependence, not effort. That is distinct from asking whether to copy the wrestling, which one cannot.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** Patriarchal era, within the *toledot* of Isaac. Jacob is returning from twenty years in Haran to the land promised to Abraham, which he does not yet hold and will leave again before the book ends.

**Canonical position.** Torah, first book, first book of the Tanak. The overview's five-field pattern records the one thing that matters most for this unit's Tool 11: the `Presupposes` field is **empty**, and that emptiness is why Move 4 carries the load here. `[S: overview]` Genesis cannot allude; it can only plant. Everything this unit does with "face", "blessing", "atone" and "accept" is first use.

**What has happened between, for the Christian reader.** Four things bear directly.

1. **Sinai.** The verb Jacob uses at 32:20 (כָּפַר, piel) and the verb he uses at 33:10 (רָצָה) both become technical terms of the sacrificial system — the first its central verb, the second its verb for the acceptance of an offering (Lev 1:4). A reader who has Leviticus cannot hear 32:20 and 33:10 as merely diplomatic, and the Greek translator already could not (ἐξιλάσομαι).
2. **The name Israel** has become a people, a kingdom, a remnant and a question. Its first bearer received it in the dark from someone who would not give his own.
3. **Hosea.** The canon has already preached this passage, and preached it as judgement on a nation whose founder grasped and wept (Hos 12).
4. **The cross.** A gift called a blessing, pressed on an estranged brother, by a man who cannot undo what he did — and a reconciliation that costs the offended party everything and the offender a limp. The trajectory is handled in the Christological extension; the timeline point is simply that this unit's vocabulary of covering a face and finding acceptance is not yet attached to a mechanism, and that the mechanism arrives later.

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross and of Pentecost, with Leviticus and Hosea and Luke 15 all in hand. The food custom of 32:32 is not binding (Mark 7:19; Acts 10), and the aside is read as a window onto how Israel remembered the night, not as a rule.

### 15. Who Am I?

| Figure | Function | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Jacob** | Neither type of Christ nor model — **recipient of grace, and the man the reader is** | He is the covenant-bearer and he is afraid, calculating and economical with God's own words. The reader who finds himself in Jacob is reading correctly; the reader who admires him is not |
| **The man at the ford** | **Not to be identified with**, and the text declines to identify him | He is אִישׁ. Jacob says "God"; Hosea says "angel"; the narrator says nothing. Any identification is the canon's, not the passage's |
| **Esau** | **The one who acts most like God in the unit, and the reader is not him either** | He runs to meet, embraces, weeps, and declines payment. The narrator gives him no bad motive. This is the uncomfortable fact of the unit: the brother outside the covenant behaves better than the brother inside it |
| **The wives, children and maids** | **Neither** | They are divided, arranged by rank and sent forward in order. The text notices them and they say nothing |

**The "Jacob-is-me" question is the right one here, and that is unusual.** Most Genesis narratives tempt the reader to self-insert where Christ is the type; this one does not have a Christ-figure in it, and the man it is about is presented with his fear, his arithmetic and his edited prayer on the page. Identification is the correct move and it is not flattering.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** Not imitation. The narrator wants the reader to see that the promise travelled through this man — a man who prepared for the wrong danger, misquoted the God he prayed to, and was met by a brother he had wronged with an embrace he had not earned — and that it travelled anyway, at the cost of a limp. The response the arrangement asks for is the recognition of what carrying a promise actually looks like from inside. `[I]`

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | God's keeping of a promise is not contingent on the promise-bearer's competence or candour. The unit's own measure of this is that Jacob gets the blessing he asks for in the dark *and* a dislocated hip, and the two are the same event |
| **Behaviour — start** | Asking. The one thing Jacob does in this unit that is not a hedge is ask — of God at 32:11 ("deliver me"), and of the man at 32:26 ("bless me"). Both are granted |
| **Behaviour — start** | Restitution with the right word on it. 33:11's "take my blessing" names the thing taken. Naming it is more than returning it |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Managing the outcome one has already prayed for. Jacob prays and then sends five more droves |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Improving God's promises in the retelling. The sand, the "do you good", the "cannot be numbered" are all upgrades, and the prayer did not need them |
| **Motivation** | Not "wrestle harder". The blessing at the ford comes to a man who has lost, and the reconciliation at 33:4 comes from the offended brother's initiative, before a word of apology |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me.** Which danger am I preparing for at length, while the thing that will actually meet me tonight is something else? And where am I quoting God more generously than he spoke?
- **For a Christian friend.** The one dreading an encounter with someone they have wronged: Esau ran. The unit does not promise that outcome, but it forbids the assumption that the wronged party is the enemy.
- **For the church.** A congregation that sings about prevailing with God should be told that the prevailing in this passage is a man refusing to let go after he has already been injured, and that he limps out of it. The church's own strength is of that kind.
- **For an unbeliever.** The covenant-bearer here is not a better man than his pagan brother; he is the one who was chosen. That is either an offence or the beginning of good news, and the passage does not soften it.

**Prayer.** Thanks that the promise did not depend on Jacob's honesty, or mine. Sorry for the hedging that runs alongside the praying, and for the quiet improvements I make to what God has actually said. Grant the courage to name what I have taken, and to ask rather than arrange.

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **The infinitive absolute at 32:12** — הֵיטֵב אֵיטִיב, "doing good I will do good", the emphatic construction. Jacob does not merely attribute to God a verb God has not used; he attributes it in the strongest form the language has. `[T]`
- **The cohortative at 32:20** — אֲכַפְּרָה, "let me cover/atone". A volitional form in an interior monologue: the narrator lets us hear Jacob resolving.
- **The dual at 32:2** — מַחֲנָיִם, a dual noun given as a place-name, with the plural מַחֲנוֹת following at 32:7 and 32:10. Hebrew distinguishes dual from plural, and the name is dual while Jacob's own division is plural in form though two in fact. A small thing, but it is why the name reads as a prophecy of 32:7 rather than a description of 32:1.
- **The niphal at 32:30** — וַתִּנָּצֵל נַפְשִׁי, "my life was delivered". Jacob had asked הַצִּילֵנִי ("deliver me", hiphil imperative) at 32:11 of Esau's hand; the verb returns at 32:30, passive, of the encounter he had not prepared for. **Two occurrences of נָצַל in the unit, 32:11 and 32:30; six in Genesis.** `[T]` *The answered prayer is reported in the prayer's own verb, about the wrong danger.* **This is translation-loss in both English versions, which use "deliver" at 32:11 and "preserved" (NASB95) or "delivered" (ESV) at 32:30** — the ESV keeps it, the NASB95 does not.
- **Word order at 32:1 and 33:17** — both open with a subject-first disjunctive (וְיַעֲקֹב הָלַךְ, וְיַעֲקֹב נָסַע), which marks a scene change. The second of them is the unit's last verse, and the construction is the narrator closing one road and opening another.

#### Wordplay and sound

- **Jabbok and wrestling.** The place is יַבֹּק, the ford מַעֲבַר יַבֹּק, and the verb וַיֵּאָבֵק (*wayyēʾābēq*) — יבק against אבק, two of three consonants shared and the sound nearly identical. The crossing-place and the struggling share a sound. *Moderate confidence:* the consonants are not identical and the corpus cannot establish intent, but the collocation is in one verse-pair and Genesis puns on names constantly. Flagged, not built on.
- **Israel and striving.** The text supplies its own etymology (32:28, "for you have **striven** with God"), which is the strongest kind of wordplay evidence — the narrator saying so.
- **Peniel and face.** Likewise self-glossed (32:30, "for I have seen God **face** to **face**"), and the only point in the unit where the leading word becomes a proper noun.
- **Jacob and the heel.** עָקַב ("heel"/"supplant") is not in this unit; it is Hosea who brings it back (12:3), and Esau who said it at 27:36. Noted so that it is not claimed here.

#### Recommended verification

- **HALOT or NIDOTTE on אָבַק.** With two occurrences and no cognate distribution inside Hebrew, the lexical argument for "wrestle" rests on cognates and context. Worth a lexicon line before a sermon leans on the precise sense.
- **The anatomical referent of גִּיד הַנָּשֶׁה.** A hapax noun; the versions guess ("sinew of the hip", "sinew of the thigh"). Not load-bearing, but a congregation will ask.
- **Whether קָטֹנְתִּי is idiomatic.** A lexicon will say whether "I am small" is a standard self-deprecation formula; if it is, the root-play with הַקָּטָן weakens.

### Textual Variants

Four items, all from the BHS apparatus (Logos export, cited as BHS) and one from the Masorah as Leningrad prints it. Category 2 of the triage carries the first three; the fourth is the fourth category — variation inside the Hebrew tradition.

**33:4 — "and he kissed him": the *puncta extraordinaria*, and BHS's reading of them.** `[T]` as a datum; `[S]` for what it implies
In Leningrad, as the WLC prints it, **every one of the six consonants of וַיִּשָּׁקֵהוּ ("and he kissed him") carries an upper dot** (U+05C4, six of them, one per letter) — the *puncta extraordinaria*. **BHS's apparatus reads the dots as a signal of doubt: `frt add cf punct extr`** — "perhaps an addition, compare the extraordinary points". So the editors take the Masoretes to be marking the kiss as a word of questionable standing. Two further notes sit on the same verse: a **ketiv/qere** on "his neck" (ketiv singular צַוָּארוֹ, qere plural צַוָּארָיו), and a conjecture at the end — **`l וַיֵּבְךְּ`**, "read *and he wept*", singular, for the text's וַיִּבְכּוּ ("and they wept").

- *What it is not:* evidence that the kiss is insincere. The dots are a scribal mark of uncertainty about the *text*, and the rabbinic readings built on them (that Esau meant to bite rather than kiss) are later interpretation, not the mark's meaning.
- *The convergence worth keeping:* **BHS's conjectured singular "and he wept" is what Hosea 12:3 actually has** (בָּכָה, singular). The conjecture is only a conjecture — *l* marks an editorial proposal, not a manuscript — but the prophet's singular is a real datum about how the scene was read.
- *Apparatus discipline:* this is a reading tradition, not authorial, and it is **Leningrad as BHS prints it** — the spread across manuscripts has not been checked here. Tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`, capped at moderate confidence, and barred from the overview until a library pass is run. A Logos question is queued.
- *Preaching implication:* mention it only if asked. A congregation told that the Masoretes dotted "and he kissed him" will hear suspicion of Esau, which is the one thing the mark does not establish.

**32:30/31 — Peniel or Penuel.** `[T]`
Leningrad has **פְּנִיאֵל** at 32:30 (the naming) and **פְּנוּאֵל** at 32:31 (the sunrise). **BHS records that the Samaritan Pentateuch, Symmachus, the Syriac and the Vulgate read Penuel at both.** So the two-form spelling is a feature of the MT that four witnesses do not share, and **any finding built on the shift from Peniel to Penuel is MT-only and should say so.** *Most likely original: genuinely open — a levelling in the four witnesses and a variation in L are equally easy to explain. Moderate confidence either way; the exegesis does not depend on it.*

**32:7 — the camels.** `[T]`
BHS: `> 𝔊*, dl?` — the original LXX lacks the word, and the editors ask whether to delete it. Swete confirms: "he divided the people with him and the oxen and the sheep into two camps" — **no camels.** A minor plus in the Hebrew or a minor omission in the Greek; nothing turns on it, and it is recorded so that a count of the unit's livestock names its text.

**32:20 — a verb supplied.** `[T]`
BHS: `frt ins c ⅏𝔊𝔗𝔗ᴶ בָּא` — the Samaritan, LXX and Targums have a verb ("Jacob **is coming** behind us") where the Hebrew has none. Swete: Ἰακὼβ παραγίνεται ὀπίσω ἡμῶν. A smoothing in the versions, most likely; **it does not affect the fourfold פָּנִים**, which stands in all witnesses.

**Not a variant: the fourfold "face" at 32:20 and the four-hundred men at 32:6/33:1.** Checked and stable.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.**
- **Time period:** patriarchal, semi-nomadic. The household moves with flocks, herds, camels, servants and children, and can be divided into two mobile groups at short notice.
- **Location:** the Transjordanian approach to Canaan, from Gilead down to the Jabbok (a perennial stream entering the Jordan from the east), then across and south-west toward Succoth; Esau's base is Seir, the hill country south-east of the Dead Sea. Jacob's promise to come "to my lord at Seir" (33:14) would be a journey of some days in the wrong direction from Canaan.
- **Political context:** no states in view. This is clan-to-clan contact, and the four hundred men are the relevant political fact: a force that size is not an honour guard, and both brothers know it.

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

1. **A *minḥah* of this size is a political act.** 550 animals in nine named categories, sent ahead in separated droves with a scripted message, is the vocabulary of a weaker party approaching a stronger. The staggering of the droves is a known technique — repeated impact rather than one delivery. The audience would read Jacob's gift as submission, not generosity, and his instruction that the servants call him "your servant Jacob" to "my lord Esau" confirms it. *High confidence on the social reading; the specific protocol is `[S]`.*
2. **Sevenfold prostration is court protocol, not piety.** Bowing seven times is the gesture of a vassal before a sovereign. `[S]` *Moderate–high confidence; the gesture is attested in ANE correspondence, and that attestation is secondary material rather than something the text states.*
3. **Birthright and blessing are property.** The audience did not need telling that what Jacob took in chapter 27 was transferable, irrevocable and worth killing over. The whole unit's tension depends on that being obvious.
4. **Wrestling until dawn, and the fear of seeing a deity.** "No one can see God and live" is a conviction the canon states later (Exod 33:20) and which 32:30's "yet my life was delivered" already assumes. The audience shared the assumption; a modern reader has to be told it.
5. **Booths.** סֻכּוֹת are temporary shelters for livestock and herdsmen. Jacob builds a **house** for himself and **booths** for his cattle (33:17) — the first permanent structure any patriarch builds in the land, and the text notes the asymmetry.
6. **A food taboo tied to a story.** The audience kept the custom of 32:32; we have only the verse. The aside assumes a practice its readers could check against their own tables.

**What this changes about how we read.** The gift and the bowing stop looking like humility and start looking like strategy — which is what the narrator has already told us by putting the fear at 32:7 before the plan at 32:13. And the meeting at 33:4 becomes more surprising, not less: Esau does the one thing the protocol does not require of him.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.**
- **Canonical section:** Torah, first book. The canonical reader of this unit has met **no prior Scripture** — this is the one book for which that is true. `[S: overview]`
- **What the canonical reader knows:** everything in Genesis so far, and nothing else. The oracle of 25:23, the stolen blessing of 27, Esau's murderous vow of 27:41, the ladder and the vow of 28, twenty years with Laban and the treaty at Gilead. Nothing of Sinai, nothing of Leviticus, nothing of Israel as a nation.
- **Canonical register, and the Torah special case:** the Torah's compositional and canonical audiences coincide — wilderness Israel, with the tabernacle in the camp. That is not background archaeology; it is the reading frame the author built in. **For this unit it has a specific consequence:** readers who have the cult in their camp hear כָּפַר ("make atonement") and רָצָה ("accept") as *their own* vocabulary, in Jacob's mouth, before Leviticus codifies either. The illumination runs both ways at once — Genesis gives the words their first use, and the tabernacle gives the first readers their ears.

**The first hearers.** A people who are themselves called Israel, who keep a food custom traced to this night, who know what it is to be the weaker party approaching a stronger, and who have an unresolved relationship with Edom.

**Where the passage fits their world.**
- They are **"the sons of Israel"** of 32:32, named in the aside — the text addresses them in their own name, by way of their diet.
- **Edom is a live political fact for them,** not a story. The narrator's gloss "the country of Edom" (32:3) is for them a current map.
- **The vocabulary of approach** — gift, prostration, "my lord", "your servant", "find favour in your sight" — is the register of their own diplomacy.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** that Jacob is injured by the one he wrestles, and keeps the limp. The patriarch of the nation is marked, permanently, by an encounter with God, and the mark is a disability.
- **Shocking, in a quieter way:** that **Esau behaves better.** He runs, weeps, refuses payment, offers escort. The narrator supplies no bad motive. For an audience with a grudge against Edom this is the hardest sentence in the unit.
- **Surprising:** that the name Israel is given by someone who will not give his own, in the dark, and that the giving of it is the end of the fight rather than its prize.
- **Comforting:** "I have seen God face to face, and my life was delivered." The thing they most feared was survivable.
- **Disturbing:** the prayer. A people who know the promise by heart would notice that Jacob's version of it is not quite the promise — and would have to decide what to make of a founder who prayed like that.

**What we bring that they didn't.**
- A devotional category of "wrestling with God" that makes 32:22–32 a template for spiritual struggle. They had no such category; they had a story about how their nation got its name and why they left a sinew on the plate.
- Individualism, which reads the unit as Jacob's inner journey rather than as the transfer of a promise through a household of eleven children and four women who are arranged by rank and told to walk forward.
- The assumption that reconciliation ends in relationship. It does not here: the brothers part, and go in opposite directions.

**Candidate Fallen Condition Focus.**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **What they felt** | The dread of meeting someone you have wronged and cannot un-wrong, with everything you love in the line of fire `[T]` — the narrator states the fear (32:7) and shows the arithmetic (32:7–8, 13–21) |
| **Candidate FCF (shared concern)** | We manage the consequences of what we have taken, because we cannot undo the taking — and we pray while we manage, improving the promise as we go |

| **Confidence** | Anchored — derived from 32:7's stated fear, 32:9–12's quoted prayer and 32:20's stated motive, not from contemporary analysis |

**Implication for the sermon.** Recovering the first hearers' discomfort at Esau is the single biggest gain. A sermon that makes Esau a foil loses what the audience could not avoid: the covenant brother is the calculating one, and the grace in this unit comes at them from outside the covenant line.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge.

**(1) Atonement — covering a face.**

- **Where this passage sits:** the first use of the piel of כָּפַר in the canon, in a man's mouth, about a brother's face, with a gift in front of it. The word has no cultic content yet; this is where it enters Scripture with the sense "cover, appease".
- **What comes before:** nothing. Genesis 6:14's כָּפַר is the qal, of pitch on the ark.
- **What comes after:** Exod 29–30; the whole Levitical system; Lev 16's Day of Atonement; and the Greek rendering ἐξιλάσκομαι which carries the word into the NT's vocabulary. Paired with it, רָצָה at 33:10 — the acceptance Jacob says he has received, and the verb Lev 1:4 uses for the acceptance of a burnt offering.
- **What this contributes:** the passage gives the two halves of the sacrificial transaction their first use — a covering offered and an acceptance received — in a scene with no altar, no priest and no God as the recipient. **A sermon should not say that Jacob's gift is a sacrifice.** What it may say is that Scripture's vocabulary for covering a face and finding acceptance is born here, between two brothers, and that the system which later carries those words is answering a problem this unit states without solving. *Confidence: high on the lexical trajectory; moderate on how much weight the first use may bear.*

**(2) The promise and its bearer — blessing against grasping.**

- **Where this passage sits:** the point in the Jacob cycle where the blessing stolen in 27 is handed back in 33, under its own name, by a man who has just been given a new one.
- **What comes before:** 25:23's oracle; 27's deception; 28's vow; 12:3's blessing formula, passed down and, in context, stolen (27:29).
- **What comes after:** 35's discharge of the vow; 48–49's crossed hands and the sceptre to the fourth son; and the book's own verdict at 50:20.
- **What this contributes:** the unit is the clearest instance in Genesis of the overview's sixth Christological heading — **the pattern the book cannot complete.** `[S: overview]` Jacob returns the blessing and still holds it; he prevails and limps; he is renamed and keeps calculating. *Confidence: high.*

**The confluence is (c) climactic convergence.** The two themes meet in one sentence: **"I have seen your face as one sees the face of God, and you have accepted me" (33:10).** The atonement vocabulary (the covered face, the acceptance) and the promise vocabulary (the blessing about to be handed over in the next verse) land in the same breath, and they land on a human face. That convergence is where this unit's preaching gold is, and it is the reason the unit should not be cut between the ford and the meeting.

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton.** Genesis · *toledot* of Isaac (25:19–35:29) · serving the book's leading idea of particularisation and of the seed carried through reversal — here, the seed-bearer re-entering the land he was promised and meeting the brother he displaced.

**Findings.**

1. **Narrative braking, and it is the pass's clearest result.** The narrator accelerates through twenty years in Haran and then **brakes hard**: 550 animals itemised in nine categories (32:14–15), a message scripted and re-scripted for four droves (32:17–19), and a single night given more verses than the two decades that preceded it. Braking marks weight. **But the weight is not where the preparation is.** The longest preparation in the unit is for the meeting with Esau, and the thing that happens is the wrestling — for which there is no preparation at all. The narrator spends his detail on the wrong danger on purpose. *(step 1)*
2. **Narrative space is doing theological work.** Every movement is a crossing or a direction: down from Gilead, across the Jabbok (east to west — into the land), the present sent *over* before him, Jacob left *behind* and then crossing, the sun rising *on* him as he passes Penuel, and finally two men going opposite ways — Esau south-east to Seir, Jacob north-west to Succoth. **עָבַר nine times** is the verbal form of the space. The man who crossed the Jordan with a staff (32:10) crosses back with two camps and a limp. *(step 2)*
3. **The structure is a janus and the unit's two halves face each other.** 32:22–32 faces backward (it resolves twenty years, and the name) and forward (it supplies the name Jacob will be called at 33:10's "face of God" and in 32:32's "sons of Israel"). It is also the hinge of the frame described at Tool 3. **The centre is not automatically the point** — and here it is not: the point is the *relation* between the two encounters, which is why Jacob's sentence at 33:10 repeats his sentence at 32:30. *(step 3)*
4. **Leading word: פָּנִים**, 15 times in 47 verses, with four in one verse, carrying the unit from "cover his face" to "as one sees the face of God". A second-rank leading word is עָבַר (9×). *(step 4)*
5. **First reading against second reading.** On a first reading, 32:7's "greatly afraid" sets up a confrontation that never comes, and the night at the ford is an interruption. On a re-reading it is the other way round: the fear is answered at the ford before Esau arrives, and the meeting at 33:4 is the consequence of the night rather than the crisis the night delayed. The sunrise at 32:31 is the join. *(step 5)*
6. **Polyacoustic intertextuality, heard rather than flattened.** Three echoes build without collapsing into one: the covering of a face (→ Levitical כָּפַר), the acceptance of a gift (→ Lev 1:4's רָצָה), and the embrace of the returning offender (→ Gen 45:14; 46:29; Luke 15:20). **None of these is "Jacob = Christ".** Jacob is the offender in the third, the petitioner in the first two. The echoes converge on the *shape* of reconciliation rather than on a figure. *(step 6)*
7. **Irreducibility.** The unit resists the proposition it is most often reduced to ("persevere in prayer and God will bless you"). The told story has a man injured, renamed, bowing, and handing back what he stole, and it ends with the two brothers walking away from each other. That ending is part of the theology and a propositional summary loses it. *(step 7)*

**Echoes/signals.** Leading word פָּנִים; secondary leading word עָבַר; frame-with-hinge; two self-glossed name-givings; two narratorial "therefore" asides reaching into the reader's present.

**Nil returns.** No type-scene in the technical sense (this is not a betrothal, a commissioning or a well-meeting). No symbolic number doing structural work — the seven prostrations and the four hundred men are quantities in the story, not patterns over it; and the 550 animals are itemised rather than totalled by the text. No mirror-image chiasm, as Tool 3 explains.

### Christological Reading

**Type of Christological connection:** **trajectory** and **contrast**. Not typology, and the reasons are worth stating, because this unit attracts a typology it will not bear.

**Why not typology.** Run the four tests on the candidate "Jacob at the ford is a type of Christ":
- *Theological-category test:* **fails.** The connection would run through an incidental detail (a night-time struggle, a dawn) rather than through priesthood, kingship, sacrifice, covenant mediation or sonship.
- *NT-precedent test:* **fails.** The NT never reads Gen 32 Christologically. Hosea reads it as an indictment.
- *Escalation test:* **fails in the wrong direction.** Jacob does not prefigure Christ; he needs what Christ brings.
- *Authorial-pattern test:* **fails.** Genesis raises no unresolved expectation here that a greater wrestler will fulfil.
Two or fewer tests passed, so the claim is reclassified, as the method requires.

**What does hold.**

- **Trajectory — the vocabulary of covering and acceptance.** The unit gives Scripture its first piel of כָּפַר and its first רָצָה-of-acceptance in a scene where a man wants a face covered and a gift received. The trajectory runs through Leviticus to ἐξιλάσκομαι and ἱλαστήριον, and lands where the covering is not a gift sent ahead but a person. **The connection is in the words and their canonical career, not in Jacob.** *Confidence: high on the trajectory; the preacher should name it as a word-trajectory, not as a type.*
- **Trajectory — the embrace of the returning offender.** The idiom born at 33:4 runs to 45:14 and 46:29, and in the Greek canon arrives at Luke 15:20, where the running, the falling on the neck and the kissing all reappear in one verse — the only other verse in the Greek canon where they do. **The father in the parable moves like Esau.** That is a trajectory with a verifiable lexical spine, and it does not require Esau to be a type of anything: it requires only that Scripture's picture of the wronged party running first begins here. *Confidence: moderate–high (see Tool 11 for the Tobit competition).*
- **Contrast — the man the promise had to travel through.** This is the strongest Christological reading of the unit and it is the overview's own sixth heading. `[S: overview]` Jacob prevails and limps; is renamed and keeps calculating; hands back the blessing and keeps it; prays the promise and edits it. **The Christological point is the gap**: a mediator is needed who does not need to be met in the dark and corrected, and whose own prayer does not improve what the Father said.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like Jacob" temptation:** *persevere in prayer until God blesses you.* It preaches easily off 32:26 and it is the single most common sermon on this passage. What it requires is that Jacob's holding on be the cause of the blessing, and the text has already said he is losing — he is injured first, and he asks second.
- **The gospel grounding:** the blessing at the ford is given to a man who has been disabled by the one giving it, and the reconciliation at 33:4 is begun by the brother who was wronged. In both halves of the unit **the initiative is with the other party.** That is the gospel shape, and it is in the arrangement rather than in a figure.
- **Christ as subject, not analogy:** not in this passage. Christ is where the unit's vocabulary goes, and what the unit's gap requires. Say that, and resist making the limping patriarch into him.

**Confidence.** *High* that this is trajectory-and-contrast rather than typology; *high* on the כָּפַר/רָצָה word-trajectory; *moderate–high* on the Luke 15:20 line.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**32:22–32 — who is the man, and what kind of encounter is this?**
- **Category:** doctrinal / apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** the narrator calls him אִישׁ ("a man") and never corrects it. Jacob says "I have seen God face to face" (32:30) — a character's claim, not a narrator's verdict. Hosea calls him מַלְאָךְ (12:4). The canon later states that no one sees God and lives (Exod 33:20).
- **Common mishandlings:** asserting "this was the pre-incarnate Christ" as though the passage said so; or, at the other extreme, reducing the man to a psychological projection of Jacob's fear.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the scene's job is to deliver a name and a limp, and its refusal to identify the wrestler is part of the delivery — Jacob asks for the name and is refused (32:29), which is the precise point at which a reader wanting an identification is told no.
- **The main options:** (i) a theophany or Christophany; (ii) an angelic mediator, with Hosea's reading as warrant; (iii) deliberate narratorial reticence that declines to choose.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the text supports (iii) as a description of what it does, and leaves (i) and (ii) as canonical judgements made elsewhere. The Christological-reading extension lists the man at Gen 32 among the plausible Christophanies, and the honest statement is that the plausibility comes from the canon, not from this page. *Moderate confidence on any identification.*
- **Suggested handling:** preach the reticence. It is more interesting than the resolution, and it keeps faith with a narrator who had the option of telling us and did not.

**32:9–12 — the misquoted promise.**
- **Category:** doctrinal / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** the data in Move 4(e) is not in doubt, and the inference is. If Jacob is editing the promise, is his prayer a model or a warning? And does the narrator mean us to notice?
- **Common mishandlings:** quoting 32:12 as a promise God made to Jacob (he did not, in those words); or, conversely, turning the discrepancy into a charge of bad faith the text does not bring.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** three verified substitutions, each in the direction of a larger promise, in a prayer the narrator reports without comment. The honest position is to state the data, give both readings, and decline to resolve it from the pulpit. **The one thing a sermon must not do is present 32:12 as a citation.**
- **Suggested handling:** address briefly, as a window into how we pray, and keep it short. *Routed to Open Questions for a library pass on the history of interpretation.*

**33:4 — the dotted kiss.**
- **Category:** apologetic / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** the Masoretic dots have generated a long tradition that Esau's kiss was insincere or murderous, and it is a tradition some hearers will have met.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the dots mark textual uncertainty, not moral suspicion; BHS reads them as flagging a possible addition; and the narrator gives Esau no bad motive anywhere in the unit. Making Esau duplicitous requires importing the suspicion.
- **Suggested handling:** sidestep unless asked, then answer plainly.

**32:32 — the food law.**
- **Category:** apologetic, mild.
- **The difficulty:** a dietary custom grounded in a single narrative event, not in a command, and never legislated in the Torah.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the verse reports a practice, it does not institute one — the only "therefore" is the narrator's. The custom's absence from the legal codes is itself a datum.
- **Suggested handling:** one sentence; it is interesting rather than difficult.

**The one difficulty worth preparing for pastorally.** This unit is read by anyone in the room who has wronged someone and cannot undo it, and by anyone who has been wronged and is expected to run. **Esau's embrace is not a command to the wronged party**, and a sermon that turns 33:4 into "and this is what you must now do for the person who hurt you" will land badly on people for whom it is not safe or not yet possible. The text describes what Esau did; it does not legislate it.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: "I will not let you go unless you bless me" as a method of prayer

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on persistence — hold on to God, wrestle in prayer, refuse to let go, and the blessing will come. The overview's own unit title ("Peniel: I will not let you go") shows the pull.
- **Why it's wrong:** the sequence in the text is the reverse. The man touches Jacob's hip **first** (32:25), Jacob is injured, and *then* he says "I will not let you go". He is not winning; he is holding on because he has been disabled and cannot do anything else. Making his grip the cause of the blessing inverts the passage and turns a scene about God's initiative into a technique.
- **The corrective:** preach the order. Injury, then request, then blessing, then limp. If perseverance is the application, let it be the perseverance of someone who has already lost and is still asking.

### Pitfall: Preaching 32:22–32 as a free-standing sermon

- **What it looks like:** "Jacob wrestles with God" as a complete unit, beginning at 32:22 and ending at 32:32 — a night, a name, a limp.
- **Why it's wrong:** the manuscript runs 32:3–33:17 as one paragraph with no break anywhere in it, and **Jacob's sentence at 33:10 is the sentence he said at 32:30.** "I have seen God face to face" is reused of his brother's face eleven verses later, and that reuse is the unit's point. Hosea, the earliest reading of the passage we possess, runs the striving and the weeping together. Cut at 32:32 and the sermon loses the second face.
- **The corrective:** preach the two encounters as one night and morning. If length forbids, preach 32:3–32 and say out loud where it is going.

### Pitfall: Making Esau the villain of the unit

- **What it looks like:** Esau's four hundred men as a war party, his embrace as dangerous or insincere, his offers of escort as a trap; Jacob's refusals as discernment.
- **Why it's wrong:** the narrator gives Esau no bad motive anywhere. He runs, embraces, falls on his neck, kisses, weeps, says "I have plenty, my brother", declines the gift twice, and offers company. The suspicion comes from the Masoretic dots (a textual mark, not a moral one) and from a reader's need for the covenant brother to be the better man.
- **The corrective:** let the comparison stand. The uncomfortable fact of this unit is that grace comes at Jacob from the brother he wronged, and the preacher should say so rather than protect him.

### Pitfall: Quoting 32:12 as a promise God made to Jacob

- **What it looks like:** "God had promised Jacob, 'I will surely prosper you and make your descendants as the sand of the sea'" — straight from Jacob's lips into the sermon as divine speech.
- **Why it's wrong:** God does not say this to Jacob anywhere in Genesis. What God said at 31:3 was "I will be with you"; the promise made to Jacob at 28:14 was "as the dust of the earth"; and the clause "which cannot be numbered for multitude" belongs to the angel's word to Hagar at 16:10. **Two precisions from claim audit 3:** `[S: audit 3]` God *does* speak the verb יָטַב in Genesis — at **4:7, to Cain, twice** — so the accurate statement is that he never speaks it **as a promise**, and that the emphatic form Jacob attributes to him stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. And Jacob's "sand of the sea" is a **compression** of Gen 22:17's wording, not a quotation of it.
- **The corrective:** attribute it to Jacob. "This is how Jacob prayed the promise" is both accurate and more interesting than "this is what God said". Whether the editing is a fault is a separate question the text leaves open; the attribution is not open.

### Pitfall: "Take my blessing" preached from the NASB95

- **What it looks like:** a sermon that works through the reconciliation and never mentions that the thing Jacob hands over is called a blessing.
- **Why it's wrong:** the study text prints "my gift" at 33:11, and the chain to 27:36 — "he has taken away my **blessing**" — is invisible. The ESV and the LXX both keep the word.
- **The corrective:** check the word before preaching the scene, and say it aloud whichever version is in the pew. This is the one place in the unit where the English version a preacher uses determines whether he can see the climax.

### Pitfall: A limp as a lesson about brokenness in general

- **What it looks like:** "God wounds us to bless us" as a free-standing principle, with the hip as an illustration.
- **Why it's wrong:** the narrator's own interpretation of the limp is at 32:32, and it is corporate and liturgical rather than devotional: *therefore the sons of Israel do not eat the sinew of the hip, to this day.* The injury becomes a nation's practice of remembering, not a template for individual suffering.
- **The corrective:** take the narrator's reading first. The limp is why Israel remembers at every meal that its founder was met and marked. Any application to personal suffering should be built after that, and named as an extension.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **The *puncta extraordinaria* at 33:4 — the Logos round did not open this gate, and it stays shut.** `[S: audit 3]` What the round returned from **owned** works: the dots began as scribal marks for deletion, a convention of many Qumran texts, and were reappropriated in the Masoretic corpus to flag doubtful letters, as ʾAbot de Rabbi Nathan A 34 explains (Tov in the *Anchor Yale Bible Dictionary* 6:397); and Butin held them as old as the Christian era (*Gesenius' Hebrew Grammar*, p. 31). What it returned that **cannot be used**: the one answer to the spread question — medieval manuscripts from around AD 920–950 onward showing them consistently — rests on a work not in the library, as does the suggestion that the dots register the scribes' own incredulity at the reunion. Nothing in the library reached Genesis Rabbah or Sifre. **The finding keeps its witness named, stays tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`, stays capped at moderate, and stays barred from the overview.** A printed Ginsburg or dedicated Masoretic scholarship would settle it.
2. **Whether Luke 15:20 is reading Gen 33:4 specifically — now rated *possible*, and still open.** `[S: audit 3]` The two-verse distribution reproduces, but the two verses **share no word form**; the verbatim string matches Gen 46:29 and Tobit 11:9; and Acts 20:37, by the same author, is nearer on the words. *Still queued for Logos:* what the commentators make of the Luke 15:20 background. Not asked in the 21 November round, which spent its questions on the apparatus, the promise and the Gideon link.
3. **Whether the narrator means the reader to notice Jacob's editing of the promise — now partly answered.** `[S: audit 3]` The Logos round of 21 November found **three commentators who notice the misquotation and split on motive**: the one in the library reads "I will make you prosper" as a *paraphrase* of the promises of presence; two others, neither owned, read it as a deliberate attempt to redefine God's words to Jacob's advantage. **So the two readings this report gave are the two the literature gives, and the only verifiable witness takes the charitable one.** None of the three mentions the sand, the dust or the Hagar clause, so those substitutions remain unremarked. A further datum: **Swete's Genesis reads "sand of the sea" at 28:14** where Rahlfs reads "of the earth" and no Hebrew witness reads sand — a Greek tradition harmonising Bethel to Jacob's quotation, which is ancient readers noticing this very gap.
4. **The sense of אָבַק.** Two occurrences, no distribution, no cognate inside Hebrew. *Queued:* HALOT and NIDOTTE.
5. **The anatomical referent of גִּיד הַנָּשֶׁה.** A hapax; not load-bearing.
6. **Whether קָטֹנְתִּי is a standard self-deprecation idiom.** If a lexicon says it is, the root-play with הַקָּטָן weakens from moderate–high to moderate.
7. **The NIV84 at 33:11.** Not checked. Needs checking before this unit is preached at a church using it, since the blessing/gift decision is the unit's load-bearing translation question.
8. **Closed by this run, and recorded as closed:** whether פָּנִים ×16 was the right figure for Gen 32–35. It is not. See *Book-Overview Tensions*.

## Book-Overview Tensions

Six, surfaced not resolved.

1. **The Preaching Units table should be re-cut, and the overview's own principle is the argument.** The overview lists **32:1–33:20** (⭐ HIGH) and **34:1–36:43** (Standard), and its own note says "the natural seams are the *toledot* headings, not the chapter numbers". The *parashoth* give a finer answer than either, and they put the seams at **32:3, 33:17, 33:20, 34:31, 35:8, 35:29** — so the overview's unit is **two verses too early at the top and three too late at the bottom**, and 33:18–20 is a paragraph of its own. Recommended: **32:3–33:17 · 33:18–35:29 (or 33:18–34:31 and 35:1–29) · 36:1–37:1.**
2. **The 34:1–36:43 unit crosses the 36:1 seam, and also stops in the wrong place.** Leningrad has a petuchah after 35:29, so 36:1 opens a paragraph — the *toledot* of Esau, exactly as Patrick's reading of DeRouchie requires. But **Leningrad's next marker after 36:43 is at 37:36**, so 37:1 stands in the same paragraph as 37:2. On the paragraphing, DeRouchie's extension of Esau's *toledot* to 37:1 does **not** hold; the paragraph runs 37:1–36. That is a datum for his larger question, not a verdict — the heading formula and the paragraphing are independent systems and may disagree.
3. **The פָּנִים figures should be stated with their extents, because two different correct numbers are in circulation.** Lemma-checked against the WLC: **15 occurrences in 9 verses** in this unit (Heb 32:4–33:17); **16 in 10 verses** over Heb 32:2–33:20, which is the figure the 32:1–35:29 dig gives and which is right for that extent; **20 in 14 verses** over Heb 32:2–35:29. *A first draft of this report proposed "16" as an error needing correction. It is not an error — I had compared it against a different extent, and the earlier dig's figure stands.* The overview should carry the extent beside whichever number it uses.
4. **The Echo Table should gain two rows, not five.** `[S: audit 3]` Admitted: **27:36 → 33:11** (the blessing, **answers**) and **33:4 → 45:14, 46:29** (נָפַל עַל־צַוָּאר with weeping, three verses canon-wide, **replants**). **Not admitted** by the audit: the neck *arc* (27:16 pairs with 41:42, not 33:4; 27:40 is the yoke idiom); the 25:23 servant/lord inversion *as a design*, the count for which is thirteen and not seventeen; and the quoted-promise composite *as a design*, since Eve at 3:2–3, Abraham at 24:7, the servant at 24:37–38, Jacob at 48:3–4 and Joseph at 50:24 all quote divine speech inexactly — it is a Genesis convention, and this is its densest instance.
5. **The reverse table, as claim audit 3 left it.** `[S: audit 3]` Second the three rows the 32:1–35:29 dig already proposed — Hos 12:4–5, Lev 1:4, Luke 15:20 — with their evidence adjusted: **Hosea as a witness to the naming scene only**, not to the extent; **Luke at *possible***, with Gen 46:29, Tobit 11:9 and Acts 20:37 named as rivals. **Add: Judg 6:22 ← Gen 33:10 + 32:30** at moderate, the clause standing in those two verses only. **Do not add the Exod 32–34 rows** — the convergence was discarded on its baseline. **Succoth** enters as a canonical echo with its limit stated: the same lexeme, different countries, and a common noun that 33:17 glosses for itself.
6. **A note for the Preaching Traps section.** Trap 3 ("heroising the patriarchs") lists where it bites and does not list 32–33. It bites here harder than almost anywhere, in a specific form the trap does not yet name: **not admiring Jacob's character but admiring his grip** — the perseverance sermon off 32:26. Recommend adding 32:22–32 to Trap 3's list with that wording.
