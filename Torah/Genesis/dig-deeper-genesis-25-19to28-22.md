## The Passage

**A note on how the text is presented.** At 119 verses this unit is nearly five times the length of the three solo runs that precede it in this series, and a full bilingual quotation would double the document without helping the preacher. So: the **NASB95 is given in full**, panel by panel, because the report refers to it constantly; the **Hebrew is quoted at every point where a finding rests on it**, and nowhere else. Nothing below is quoted from memory — the Hebrew is taken from `_texts/hebrew-wlc/01-Torah/01-Genesis.txt` and the English from `_texts/logos-exports/NASB95 Genesis.txt`.

**Unit boundaries.** The unit opens at the *toledot* heading of 25:19 (וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדֹת יִצְחָק — "these are the generations of Isaac") and closes at 28:22, the last words of Jacob's vow, immediately before 29:1 lifts his feet and takes him out of the land. ### Panel A — 25:19–34 · The oracle and the birthright *(16 verses)*

> **19** Now these are the records of the generations of Isaac, Abraham's son: Abraham became the father of Isaac; **20** and Isaac was forty years old when he took Rebekah, the daughter of Bethuel the Aramean of Paddan-aram, the sister of Laban the Aramean, to be his wife. **21** Isaac prayed to the LORD on behalf of his wife, because she was barren; and the LORD answered him and Rebekah his wife conceived. **22** But the children struggled together within her; and she said, "If it is so, why then am I this way?" So she went to inquire of the LORD. **23** The LORD said to her,
> "Two nations are in your womb; / And two peoples will be separated from your body; / And one people shall be stronger than the other; / And the older shall serve the younger."
> **24** When her days to be delivered were fulfilled, behold, there were twins in her womb. **25** Now the first came forth red, all over like a hairy garment; and they named him Esau. **26** Afterward his brother came forth with his hand holding on to Esau's heel, so his name was called Jacob; and Isaac was sixty years old when she gave birth to them. **27** When the boys grew up, Esau became a skillful hunter, a man of the field, but Jacob was a peaceful man, living in tents. **28** Now Isaac loved Esau, because he had a taste for game, but Rebekah loved Jacob. **29** When Jacob had cooked stew, Esau came in from the field and he was famished; **30** and Esau said to Jacob, "Please let me have a swallow of that red stuff there, for I am famished." Therefore his name was called Edom. **31** But Jacob said, "First sell me your birthright." **32** Esau said, "Behold, I am about to die; so of what use then is the birthright to me?" **33** And Jacob said, "First swear to me"; so he swore to him, and sold his birthright to Jacob. **34** Then Jacob gave Esau bread and lentil stew; and he ate and drank, and rose and went on his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright.

### Panel B — 26:1–35 · Isaac alone *(35 verses)*

> **1** Now there was a famine in the land, besides the previous famine that had occurred in the days of Abraham. So Isaac went to Gerar, to Abimelech king of the Philistines. **2** The LORD appeared to him and said, "Do not go down to Egypt; stay in the land of which I shall tell you. **3** Sojourn in this land and I will be with you and bless you, for to you and to your descendants I will give all these lands, and I will establish the oath which I swore to your father Abraham. **4** I will multiply your descendants as the stars of heaven, and will give your descendants all these lands; and by your descendants all the nations of the earth shall be blessed; **5** because Abraham obeyed Me and kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes and My laws." **6** So Isaac lived in Gerar. **7** When the men of the place asked about his wife, he said, "She is my sister," for he was afraid to say, "my wife," thinking, "the men of the place might kill me on account of Rebekah, for she is beautiful." **8** It came about, when he had been there a long time, that Abimelech king of the Philistines looked out through a window, and saw, and behold, Isaac was caressing his wife Rebekah. **9** Then Abimelech called Isaac and said, "Behold, certainly she is your wife! How then did you say, 'She is my sister'?" And Isaac said to him, "Because I said, 'I might die on account of her.'" **10** Abimelech said, "What is this you have done to us? One of the people might easily have lain with your wife, and you would have brought guilt upon us." **11** So Abimelech charged all the people, saying, "He who touches this man or his wife shall surely be put to death."
> **12** Now Isaac sowed in that land and reaped in the same year a hundredfold. And the LORD blessed him, **13** and the man became rich, and continued to grow richer until he became very wealthy; **14** for he had possessions of flocks and herds and a great household, so that the Philistines envied him. **15** Now all the wells which his father's servants had dug in the days of Abraham his father, the Philistines stopped up by filling them with earth. **16** Then Abimelech said to Isaac, "Go away from us, for you are too powerful for us." **17** And Isaac departed from there and camped in the valley of Gerar, and settled there. **18** Then Isaac dug again the wells of water which had been dug in the days of his father Abraham, for the Philistines had stopped them up after the death of Abraham; and he gave them the same names which his father had given them. **19** But when Isaac's servants dug in the valley and found there a well of flowing water, **20** the herdsmen of Gerar quarreled with the herdsmen of Isaac, saying, "The water is ours!" So he named the well Esek, because they contended with him. **21** Then they dug another well, and they quarreled over it too, so he named it Sitnah. **22** He moved away from there and dug another well, and they did not quarrel over it; so he named it Rehoboth, for he said, "At last the LORD has made room for us, and we will be fruitful in the land." **23** Then he went up from there to Beersheba. **24** The LORD appeared to him the same night and said,
> "I am the God of your father Abraham; / Do not fear, for I am with you. / I will bless you, and multiply your descendants, / For the sake of My servant Abraham."
> **25** So he built an altar there and called upon the name of the LORD, and pitched his tent there; and there Isaac's servants dug a well. **26** Then Abimelech came to him from Gerar with his adviser Ahuzzath and Phicol the commander of his army. **27** Isaac said to them, "Why have you come to me, since you hate me and have sent me away from you?" **28** They said, "We see plainly that the LORD has been with you; so we said, 'Let there now be an oath between us, even between you and us, and let us make a covenant with you, **29** that you will do us no harm, just as we have not touched you and have done to you nothing but good and have sent you away in peace. You are now the blessed of the LORD.'" **30** Then he made them a feast, and they ate and drank. **31** In the morning they arose early and exchanged oaths; then Isaac sent them away and they departed from him in peace. **32** Now it came about on the same day, that Isaac's servants came in and told him about the well which they had dug, and said to him, "We have found water." **33** So he called it Shibah; therefore the name of the city is Beersheba to this day. **34** When Esau was forty years old he married Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Basemath the daughter of Elon the Hittite; **35** and they brought grief to Isaac and Rebekah.

### Panel C — 27:1–28:9 · The blessing taken, the son sent *(55 verses)*

> **27:1** Now it came about, when Isaac was old and his eyes were too dim to see, that he called his older son Esau and said to him, "My son." And he said to him, "Here I am." **2** Isaac said, "Behold now, I am old and I do not know the day of my death. **3** Now then, please take your gear, your quiver and your bow, and go out to the field and hunt game for me; **4** and prepare a savory dish for me such as I love, and bring it to me that I may eat, so that my soul may bless you before I die." **5** Rebekah was listening while Isaac spoke to his son Esau. So when Esau went to the field to hunt for game to bring home, **6** Rebekah said to her son Jacob, "Behold, I heard your father speak to your brother Esau, saying, **7**'Bring me some game and prepare a savory dish for me, that I may eat, and bless you in the presence of the LORD before my death.' **8** Now therefore, my son, listen to me as I command you. **9** Go now to the flock and bring me two choice young goats from there, that I may prepare them as a savory dish for your father, such as he loves. **10** Then you shall bring it to your father, that he may eat, so that he may bless you before his death." **11** Jacob answered his mother Rebekah, "Behold, Esau my brother is a hairy man and I am a smooth man. **12** Perhaps my father will feel me, then I will be as a deceiver in his sight, and I will bring upon myself a curse and not a blessing." **13** But his mother said to him, "Your curse be on me, my son; only obey my voice, and go, get them for me." **14** So he went and got them, and brought them to his mother; and his mother made savory food such as his father loved. **15** Then Rebekah took the best garments of Esau her elder son, which were with her in the house, and put them on Jacob her younger son. **16** And she put the skins of the young goats on his hands and on the smooth part of his neck. **17** She also gave the savory food and the bread, which she had made, to her son Jacob.
> **18** Then he came to his father and said, "My father." And he said, "Here I am. Who are you, my son?" **19** Jacob said to his father, "I am Esau your firstborn; I have done as you told me. Get up, please, sit and eat of my game, that you may bless me." **20** Isaac said to his son, "How is it that you have it so quickly, my son?" And he said, "Because the LORD your God caused it to happen to me." **21** Then Isaac said to Jacob, "Please come close, that I may feel you, my son, whether you are really my son Esau or not." **22** So Jacob came close to Isaac his father, and he felt him and said, "The voice is the voice of Jacob, but the hands are the hands of Esau." **23** He did not recognize him, because his hands were hairy like his brother Esau's hands; so he blessed him. **24** And he said, "Are you really my son Esau?" And he said, "I am." **25** So he said, "Bring it to me, and I will eat of my son's game, that I may bless you." And he brought it to him, and he ate; he also brought him wine and he drank. **26** Then his father Isaac said to him, "Please come close and kiss me, my son." **27** So he came close and kissed him; and when he smelled the smell of his garments, he blessed him and said,
> "See, the smell of my son / Is like the smell of a field which the LORD has blessed; **28** Now may God give you of the dew of heaven, / And of the fatness of the earth, / And an abundance of grain and new wine; **29** May peoples serve you, / And nations bow down to you; / Be master of your brothers, / And may your mother's sons bow down to you. / Cursed be those who curse you, / And blessed be those who bless you."
> **30** Now it came about, as soon as Isaac had finished blessing Jacob, and Jacob had hardly gone out from the presence of Isaac his father, that Esau his brother came in from his hunting. **31** Then he also made savory food, and brought it to his father; and he said to his father, "Let my father arise and eat of his son's game, that you may bless me." **32** Isaac his father said to him, "Who are you?" And he said, "I am your son, your firstborn, Esau." **33** Then Isaac trembled violently, and said, "Who was he then that hunted game and brought it to me, so that I ate of all of it before you came, and blessed him? Yes, and he shall be blessed." **34** When Esau heard the words of his father, he cried out with an exceedingly great and bitter cry, and said to his father, "Bless me, even me also, O my father!" **35** And he said, "Your brother came deceitfully and has taken away your blessing." **36** Then he said, "Is he not rightly named Jacob, for he has supplanted me these two times? He took away my birthright, and behold, now he has taken away my blessing." And he said, "Have you not reserved a blessing for me?" **37** But Isaac replied to Esau, "Behold, I have made him your master, and all his relatives I have given to him as servants; and with grain and new wine I have sustained him. Now as for you then, what can I do, my son?" **38** Esau said to his father, "Do you have only one blessing, my father? Bless me, even me also, O my father." So Esau lifted his voice and wept. **39** Then Isaac his father answered and said to him,
> "Behold, away from the fertility of the earth shall be your dwelling, / And away from the dew of heaven from above. **40** By your sword you shall live, / And your brother you shall serve; / But it shall come about when you become restless, / That you will break his yoke from your neck."
> **41** So Esau bore a grudge against Jacob because of the blessing with which his father had blessed him; and Esau said to himself, "The days of mourning for my father are near; then I will kill my brother Jacob." **42** Now when the words of her elder son Esau were reported to Rebekah, she sent and called her younger son Jacob, and said to him, "Behold your brother Esau is consoling himself concerning you by planning to kill you. **43** Now therefore, my son, obey my voice, and arise, flee to Haran, to my brother Laban! **44** Stay with him a few days, until your brother's fury subsides, **45** until your brother's anger against you subsides and he forgets what you did to him. Then I will send and get you from there. Why should I be bereaved of you both in one day?" **46** Rebekah said to Isaac, "I am tired of living because of the daughters of Heth; if Jacob takes a wife from the daughters of Heth, like these, from the daughters of the land, what good will my life be to me?"
> **28:1** So Isaac called Jacob and blessed him and charged him, and said to him, "You shall not take a wife from the daughters of Canaan. **2** Arise, go to Paddan-aram, to the house of Bethuel your mother's father; and from there take to yourself a wife from the daughters of Laban your mother's brother. **3** May God Almighty bless you and make you fruitful and multiply you, that you may become a company of peoples. **4** May He also give you the blessing of Abraham, to you and to your descendants with you, that you may possess the land of your sojournings, which God gave to Abraham." **5** Then Isaac sent Jacob away, and he went to Paddan-aram to Laban, son of Bethuel the Aramean, the brother of Rebekah, the mother of Jacob and Esau. **6** Now Esau saw that Isaac had blessed Jacob and sent him away to Paddan-aram to take to himself a wife from there, and that when he blessed him he charged him, saying, "You shall not take a wife from the daughters of Canaan," **7** and that Jacob had obeyed his father and his mother and had gone to Paddan-aram. **8** So Esau saw that the daughters of Canaan displeased his father Isaac; **9** and Esau went to Ishmael, and married, besides the wives that he had, Mahalath the daughter of Ishmael, Abraham's son, the sister of Nebaioth.

### Panel D — 28:10–22 · Bethel *(13 verses)*

> **10** Then Jacob departed from Beersheba and went toward Haran. **11** He came to a certain place and spent the night there, because the sun had set; and he took one of the stones of the place and put it under his head, and lay down in that place. **12** He had a dream, and behold, a ladder was set on the earth with its top reaching to heaven; and behold, the angels of God were ascending and descending on it. **13** And behold, the LORD stood above it and said, "I am the LORD, the God of your father Abraham and the God of Isaac; the land on which you lie, I will give it to you and to your descendants. **14** Your descendants will also be like the dust of the earth, and you will spread out to the west and to the east and to the north and to the south; and in you and in your descendants shall all the families of the earth be blessed. **15** Behold, I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you back to this land; for I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you." **16** Then Jacob awoke from his sleep and said, "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it." **17** He was afraid and said, "How awesome is this place! ## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools. All five are `[T]`, all five converge from three or more tools, and every count below was made by lemma against the WLC and is reproducible in one command.

1. **The blessing Jacob steals is not the Abrahamic blessing. The Abrahamic blessing is given to him openly, by name, sixty verses later — and he does not have to steal it.** What Isaac says at 27:27–29 is dew, fatness, grain, wine, dominion over brothers, and the bless-and-curse formula of 12:3. There is **no land, no seed, no covenant, and no "God of Abraham"** in it. What Isaac says at 28:3–4, knowing exactly who is in front of him, is *ʾĒl Šadday* (the covenant name of 17:1), *yaprəkā wəyarbekā* (the creation mandate of 1:28), and **בִּרְכַּת אַבְרָהָם — "the blessing of Abraham" — plus the land, plus "to you and to your seed"**. Surfaced by: Structure (Tool 3), Vocabulary (7), Repetition (10), Move 4 (11), Schnittjer step 3. *High.*

2. **"Blessing" and "birthright" are the same three consonants in a different order, the unit's two plot-engines are one each, and Esau says so out loud.** *bərākāh* בְּרָכָה is b-r-k; *bəkōrāh* בְּכֹרָה is b-k-r. At **27:36** Esau puts both in one sentence: *ʾet-bəkōrātî lāqāḥ … lāqaḥ birkātî*. Verified counts, WLC: *bārak* (1288) stands **27 times in 23 verses of this 119-verse unit — 37% of the 73 occurrences in the whole of Genesis**, with 21 of them in Panel C alone; the noun *bərākāh* (1293) stands 7 times here out of 16 in the book; *bəkōrāh* (1062) stands 5 times here out of 7. Surfaced by: Vocabulary (7), Repetition (10), Original Languages, Schnittjer step 4. *High.* ⟨synthetic — every constituent lemma-checked⟩

3. **"Obey my voice" is God's phrase about Abraham, and Rebekah's phrase to Jacob — and in Genesis nobody else ever says it.** The construction *šāmaʿ bə-qôl* (8085 + b/6963) stands in Genesis at exactly six verses: **21:12, 22:18, 26:5** — all in God's mouth — and **27:8, 13, 43** — all in Rebekah's. The exact imperative string שְׁמַע בְּקֹלִי, "obey **my** voice", occurs in the book **only in Rebekah's mouth, three times**. It arrives three verses after 26:5, where God commends Abraham in that very formula. (3:17 and 16:2 use *lə-qôl*, not *bə-qôl*, and are a different construction.) Surfaced by: Vocabulary (7), Repetition (10), Move 4 (11), Linking Words (4). *High.*

4. **The covenant name is scarce in the deception, and two of its three occurrences are in the deceivers' mouths — one of them an addition to what Isaac actually said.** In the 55 verses of Panel C, יהוה occurs three times: **27:7** (Rebekah quoting Isaac — and comparing 27:4 with 27:7 shows she has **added** לִפְנֵי יְהוָה, "in the presence of the LORD"; Isaac said only "before I die"); **27:20** (Jacob's lie — "because the LORD *your* God caused it to happen to me"); and **27:27** (Isaac's own blessing). Against that: seven occurrences in the 35 verses of Panel B, and four in the thirteen verses of Bethel. Surfaced by: Repetition (10), Narrator's Comment (6), Tone (9), Move 4 (11). *High.* — This is the same shape the companion run on 3:1–24 found, where the covenant name is absent from the temptation dialogue altogether.

5. **Jacob's vow is God's promise shrunk, reordered and made conditional — and God is "your God" until Jacob offers, on terms, to make him "my God".** Set 28:20–22 against 28:13–15 clause by clause and the correspondence is exact and diminishing: *"I am with you"* → *"**if** God will be with me"*; *"I will keep you (וּשְׁמַרְתִּיךָ) wherever you go"* → *"and will keep me (וּשְׁמָרַנִי) in this way that I go"*; *"I will bring you back to this land"* → *"and I return in peace to my father's house"*; and against land, seed like the dust, and blessing for all the families of the ground, Jacob asks for **bread to eat and a garment to wear**. He has just called YHWH *ʾĕlōhêkā*, "your God", to Isaac (27:20); at 28:21 he offers, conditionally, וְהָיָה יְהוָה לִי לֵאלֹהִים. Surfaced by: Structure (3), Linking Words (4), Repetition (10), Move 4 (11), So What (16). *High.*

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

*(Five fields, Tanak order, BHS sequence.)*

**Section.** Torah, first book, and the first book of the whole Tanak.

**Reading implication.** Genesis has nothing behind it, so this unit cannot be alluding to anything; everything it does verbally is either **planting** or **re-using its own earlier material**. That is the working rule for Tool 11 throughout, and it is why the internal-echo work below is disproportionately long.

**Presupposes.** Within the book, this unit presupposes a great deal, and every item is checkable: **12:1–3** (the call, and the bless/curse formula that reappears verbatim at 27:29); **12:10–20** (the famine and the wife-sister episode that 26:1–11 repeats); **13:16** (seed as the dust of the earth — quoted at 28:14); **12:8 and 13:3–4** (Abram's altar at the place he called Bethel — the place Jacob will think he has found by accident); **17:1** (*ʾĒl Šadday*, invoked by Isaac at 28:3); **21:22–34** (Abimelech, Phicol, the oath, the wells and the naming of Beersheba — all of which 26:15–33 replays); **22:17–18** (the nations-blessing in the hithpael, quoted word for word at 26:4); **24:60** (the blessing over Rebekah at her departure, answered by 28:1–5 at Jacob's); **25:8–11** (Abraham's death and Isaac's inheritance). Outside the book, nothing: there is nothing outside the book.

**Handoff.** Directly into 29:1, which lifts Jacob's feet and takes him east; and, at longer range, into 32:1–2 [Heb 32:2–3], where **the only other occurrence in Genesis of מַלְאֲכֵי אֱלֹהִים, "angels of God"** (28:12), meets Jacob coming home, using **the same verb, *pāgaʿ***, that met him going out (28:11). Verified: 4397+430 in construct occurs in Genesis at 28:12 and 32:2 only; *pāgaʿ* (6293) at 23:8, 28:11 and 32:2 only.

**Neighbours.** Immediately before: 25:12–18, Ishmael's *toledot* — the non-elect line closed off first, exactly as the overview's arc map describes. Immediately after: chapters 29–31, twenty years in which the deceiver is deceived with the elder-for-younger trick he himself used.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

*(Recorded here and cross-referenced under Tool 2.)*

**Preceding movement.** Genesis 22:20–25:18 has buried Abraham (25:8), transferred everything to Isaac (25:5, 11), and closed Ishmael's line (25:12–18). The promise now sits in one man's hands and has not yet moved a generation. The Akedah has closed with the nations-blessing sworn *because you have obeyed My voice* (22:18) — a promise attached, in that verse, to obedience. `[T]`

**Necessity answer.** This unit exists **here** because the promise has to cross a generation for the first time under conditions where every human mechanism for transmitting it fails. The mechanisms available were: primogeniture (cancelled before the twins are born, 25:23); paternal discernment (blind, deceived, 27:1, 23); maternal management (secures the right end by the wrong means, 27:5–17); and the boy's own initiative (buys a birthright from a starving man and lies to a dying one). By 28:5 the heir is a fugitive with nothing but a stick. Then, at 28:13–15, God gives him the whole promise unconditionally and unasked. **The unit is placed where it is so that the reader watches every human means of transmission fail before he sees the promise transmitted anyway.** `[T]` / `[I]` on the last clause.

**And why chapter 26 is in the middle of it.** This is the sharper positional question, because Genesis 26 interrupts the Jacob–Esau story and is chronologically out of order (Rebekah is still an object of desire at 26:7; the twins are not mentioned until 26:34). The narrator has moved it here, and it does four things the deception scene needs:

1. It establishes that **Isaac genuinely holds what Jacob will steal**: the promise is spoken to Isaac personally, twice, at 26:2–5 and 26:24. Without chapter 26 the blessing of chapter 27 would be an old man's wish.
2. It establishes **what a blessing actually does**: "Isaac sowed … and reaped a hundredfold. And the LORD blessed him" (26:12). The reader learns that blessing is a real, visible thing before he watches two brothers fight over one.
3. It establishes that **deceit is already the house style** — Isaac tells the Gerar men his wife is his sister (26:7), exactly as his father twice did. Chapter 27 is not a fall from a family standard; it is the family standard.
4. It plants the standard against which Rebekah's words are heard: **26:5, "because Abraham obeyed My voice"**, three verses before 27:8, "obey my voice."

**Implication.** Preach 27 without 26 and Jacob's theft looks like the origin of the family's dishonesty. Preach them together and it is the second generation of it.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** The internal-echo work sharpens this. 26:4 is a verbatim quotation of 22:18 and 28:14 is a verbatim quotation of 12:3 (see Tool 11). So the unit is bracketed by the two ends of the Abrahamic refrain, given to Isaac at one end and to Jacob at the other — which is the positional answer stated lexically.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** The overview's microscript: God narrows the blessing of all the earth's families into a single seed and keeps that seed alive through barrenness, deceit, famine and fratricide. Every one of those four is in this unit — barrenness at 25:21, deceit through chapter 27, famine at 26:1, and a brother planning murder at 27:41. `[T]`

**Passage-level.** To show that **the promise passes to the second generation by divine choice announced before either child has done anything (25:23) and confirmed to a fugitive who has done everything wrong (28:13–15)** — and to show, at length, that neither the choice nor the confirmation depends on the human machinery that spends 90 verses trying to secure them. `[T]`

The narrator's technique for this is silence. He never once says that Rebekah was wrong, or Jacob, or that Isaac should have known. He gives verdicts on Esau only (25:34; and by juxtaposition 26:34–35 and 28:8–9), and otherwise lets the actions stand. The purpose is served by what he **declines** to editorialise as much as by what he narrates.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*(See Phase 0.6 above — recorded in full there.)*

**Immediately before (25:12–18).** Ishmael's *toledot*: twelve princes, a lifespan, a death, a territory "from Havilah to Shur … he settled in defiance of all his relatives" (25:18). The non-elect line is given its due and closed. Then 25:19 opens the elect line — and the first thing it reports is that the elect line cannot conceive.

**Immediately after (29:1).** "Then Jacob lifted his feet and went to the land of the sons of the east." The unit ends with a vow at a stone and the next verse has him gone. The vow's terms — *if* he comes back — are what the next seven chapters test.

**Section.** The *toledot* of Isaac (25:19) runs to 35:29, so this unit is the first half of that section; 29:1–35:29 is the second (sweep §12). The seam between them is the border of the land.

**The chapter divisions are actively unhelpful here.** Chapter 25 puts a break between Abraham's death and Isaac's *toledot* that the Hebrew marks differently; chapter 27 begins mid-scene; chapter 28 splits the sending-away (28:1–9) from the journey (28:10–22) that it causes. The Masoretic paragraphing (the *setumah* markers ס in the WLC) falls after 25:34, 26:33, 26:35 and 28:9 — that is, it marks the four panels used above almost exactly. `[T]`

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Panel | Function | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25:19–34 | **The oracle and the birthright** | God's word about the two sons, unasked-for and unearned; then the first transaction | 16 vv · 175 accent units |
| 26:1–35 | **Isaac alone** | The promise confirmed to Isaac personally; the family's deceit shown to be inherited; blessing shown to work | 35 vv · 413 |
| 27:1–28:9 | **The blessing taken, the son sent** | Every human mechanism for transmitting the promise fails; the promise is then handed over openly | 55 vv · 680 |
| 28:10–22 | **Bethel** | God gives the whole promise to the fugitive, unconditionally; the fugitive answers with a condition | 13 vv · 171 |

Whole unit: **119 verses, 1,439 accent units** — 7.8% of Genesis's 1,533 verses and 8.1% of its 17,659 accent units. Counted from the WLC.

**Device: a frame of two divine speeches around a long human failure.** The unit opens (25:23) and closes (28:13–15) with God speaking, unrequested, about the future of these sons. Between them the human beings act, and the narrative brakes hard: **the blessing episode alone is 55 verses, 46% of the unit**, while Isaac's entire adult life outside it is one chapter. That disproportion is the argument. Schnittjer's rule applies exactly — braking is weight.

**Two closures, and they mirror each other.** Panel A ends on a narrator's verdict on Esau (25:34, "thus Esau despised his birthright"). Panel C ends on a narrator's report that functions as one (28:6–9: Esau, seeing his father's judgement on Canaanite wives, goes and marries an Ishmaelite — the non-elect line marrying the non-elect line). Neither is stated as condemnation; both are.

**Not a chiasm.** It is tempting to make 25:23 / 28:13–15 an inclusio and centre the whole on 27:22 ("the voice is the voice of Jacob"). The frame is real; the centring is not warranted — 27:22 is not the pivot of anything, the two divine speeches are addressed to different people about different things, and Schnittjer's guardrail applies: structure is the vehicle, not the object, and the centre of a proposed chiasm is not automatically the point. Report the frame; decline the chiasm. *Moderate on the frame; the chiasm is not claimed.* `[I]`

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative rather than argument, so the connectors are few and mostly temporal — but four of them are load-bearing.

- **25:19** וְאֵלֶּה תּוֹלְדֹת — the book's own structural connector, the eleventh of eleven headings. It links backwards to 25:12 (Ishmael's) in the pattern the overview names: **non-elect first, closed off; then elect.** `[T]`
- **26:1** מִלְּבַד הָרָעָב הָרִאשׁוֹן אֲשֶׁר הָיָה בִּימֵי אַבְרָהָם — "besides the former famine … in the days of Abraham". An explicit narratorial back-reference to 12:10. The narrator is telling the reader, in the first clause of the chapter, to read what follows against Abraham's Egypt. `[T]`
- **26:5** עֵקֶב אֲשֶׁר — "**because** Abraham obeyed My voice". The same causal particle stands at 22:18. ←*for*← : the confirmation of the promise to Isaac is grounded in **Abraham's** obedience, not Isaac's. Note what this does not say: it does not make the promise conditional on Isaac. `[T]`
- **27:46 → 28:1** — the join is causal but the stated cause is false. Rebekah tells Jacob the truth ("Esau is consoling himself concerning you by planning to kill you", 27:42) and tells Isaac a half-truth ("I am tired of living because of the daughters of Heth", 27:46). The murder plot is never mentioned to Isaac. Isaac therefore sends Jacob away for a reason that is real but is not the reason. `[T]` This is a connector that a reader skims and a preacher should not.
- **27:36** הֲכִי קָרָא שְׁמוֹ יַעֲקֹב — "**Is he not rightly** named Jacob?" A rhetorical question functioning as a connector between the name and the two thefts: the name explains the events, and the events justify the name.

**Dropped or smoothed in translation:** the emphatic *ʾak* at 27:30 ("**scarcely** had Jacob gone out") and the infinitive absolute יָצֹא יָצָא in the same clause are two separate intensifiers stacked on a timing detail. Both English versions render the sense but neither shows that the Hebrew has doubled down twice on *how narrowly* Esau missed him. See Tool 8.

### 5. Parallels

Three poetic units sit inside the prose, and they are the only places the tool applies.

**25:23 — a four-colon oracle, chiastically shaped.**

> שְׁנֵי גיים בְּבִטְנֵךְ · וּשְׁנֵי לְאֻמִּים מִמֵּעַיִךְ יִפָּרֵדוּ
> וּלְאֹם מִלְאֹם יֶאֱמָץ · וְרַב יַעֲבֹד צָעִיר

Cola 1–2 are synonymous ("two nations / two peoples"; "in your womb / from your body"). Cola 3–4 are **not** synonymous but **synthetic and specifying**: colon 3 states a general inequality (one people stronger than the other) and colon 4 names which. The movement is from symmetry to asymmetry — which is the oracle's whole point, and is why the last three words carry all the weight. Notice also the **word-order chiasm** binding colon 4 to colon 3: *ûləʾōm miləʾōm yeʾĕmāṣ* (people–from-people–strong) / *wərab yaʿăbōd ṣāʿîr* (greater–serve–lesser). `[T]`

**27:28–29 and 27:39–40 — two blessings in near-mirror.** The second is built from the first, reversed:

| Jacob (27:28) | Esau (27:39) |
|---|---|
| **מִטַּל** הַשָּׁמַיִם | וּ**מִ**טַּל הַשָּׁמַיִם מֵעָל |
| וּ**מִ**שְׁמַנֵּי הָאָרֶץ | הִנֵּה **מִ**שְׁמַנֵּי הָאָרֶץ |

The same two prepositional phrases, in reversed order, with the same preposition מִן. Everything then turns on whether that מִן is partitive ("some of") or privative ("away from") — which the Hebrew does not settle and every translation must. See Tool 8; this is the single most consequential translation decision in the unit.

**Deliberate break in the parallelism at 27:29c.** After two balanced bicola the blessing drops into a flat, unparalleled imperative — הֱוֵה גְבִיר לְאַחֶיךָ, "be lord to your brothers" — before resuming the balanced bless/curse couplet. The break falls exactly on the clause that Esau will quote back in fury (27:37, where Isaac repeats גְבִיר). `[I]` *Moderate.*

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Genesis 25–28 is unusually rich in authorial intrusion, and every intrusion is a verdict he declines to state as one.

| Reference | Comment | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **25:34c** | וַיִּבֶז עֵשָׂו אֶת־הַבְּכֹרָה — "thus Esau despised the birthright" | The classic case, cited in the toolkit's own reference material. Five *wayyiqtol* verbs of pure action (ate, drank, rose, went) and then one verdict. Without it, Esau is a hungry man taken advantage of. |
| **25:28** | Isaac loved Esau … Rebekah loved Jacob | Inside information the characters never state. It supplies the motive for everything in chapter 27 before chapter 27 begins. |
| **26:7b** | "for he was afraid to say 'my wife' … for she was beautiful" | The narrator gives Isaac's private reasoning, and it is fear, not strategy. |
| **26:33b** | עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה — "to this day" | One of the six narratorial "to this day" asides the overview verified (19:37, 38; 26:33; 32:32 [Heb 32:33]; 35:20; 47:26). The narrator's own vantage, long after. `[T]` |
| **26:35** | "and they were a bitterness of spirit to Isaac and Rebekah" | The one thing Isaac and Rebekah agree about, placed immediately before the chapter in which they act against each other. |
| **27:23a** | וְלֹא הִכִּירוֹ — "and he did not recognise him" | The narrator states the deception's success as fact before the reader can wonder. This is the head of the *nākar* chain (see Tool 11). |
| **27:41** | "and Esau bore a grudge … and Esau said **in his heart**" | Inside information again, and the murder plot exists only because the narrator tells us it does — Esau never says it aloud. |
| **28:19b** | "however, Luz was the name of the city formerly" | A gloss for a later reader who knows the place by the wrong name. |

**What the narrator conspicuously does not say.** He never calls Rebekah's plan sin, never comments on Jacob's lie, and never tells the reader whether Isaac's blessing was valid. Given how freely he editorialises about Esau, the silence about the other three is a choice. `[I]` *Moderate — but note that the same narrator who supplies "thus Esau despised" has 90 verses in which to supply "thus Jacob deceived", and does not.*

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory** *(run before triage, per Tool 7c).* Persons: Isaac, Abraham, Rebekah, Bethuel, Laban, Esau, Jacob, Edom, Abimelech, Ahuzzath, Phicol, Judith, Beeri, Basemath, Elon, Ishmael, Mahalath, Nebaioth, Heth, Canaan. Peoples/gentilics: Aramean, Philistines, Hittite. Places: Paddan-aram, Gerar, Egypt, Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth, Beersheba, Haran, Bethel, Luz. Numbers: forty (25:20; 26:34), sixty (25:26), two (25:23 ×2; 27:9; 27:36; 27:45), a hundredfold (26:12), a tenth (28:22).

Triage: routine scenery — Bethuel, Ahuzzath, Phicol, Judith, Beeri, Basemath, Elon, Nebaioth, Paddan-aram, Egypt, Gerar, Haran. Candidates for canonical freight, checked below: **Edom, Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth, Beersheba, Bethel, Luz, Ishmael**, and the number **ten**.

#### The two words the unit is built on

**בְּרָכָה / בְּכֹרָה — *bərākāh* / *bəkōrāh*** `[T]` *High.*
Blessing and birthright: b-r-k and b-k-r, the same three consonants transposed. This is a consonantal metathesis, and the hard rule in `original-languages.md` requires corroboration beyond the sound before it may be reported — which the text supplies in the strongest possible form: **the narrative itself makes the pun explicit**, in Esau's mouth, at 27:36, where both nouns stand in consecutive clauses as the objects of the same verb לָקַח. The corroboration is not thematic inference; it is a character naming it. Counts (WLC): *bārak* 27× / 23 verses in the unit against 73× / 62 verses in Genesis; *bərākāh* 7× here of 16 in the book; *bəkōrāh* 5× here of 7 in the book, four of them in the six verses 25:31–34.

**קְלָלָה — *qəlālāh*, "curse"** `[T]` *High.*
The noun occurs in the whole of Genesis **twice, and both are in this dialogue**: 27:12 (Jacob: "I will bring on myself a *qəlālāh* and not a *bərākāh*") and 27:13 (Rebekah: "your *qəlālāh* be on me, my son"). The verb *ʾārar* (779) likewise appears in the unit only at 27:29, and there only inside the Abrahamic blessing formula. So: **the only cursing in 119 verses is a curse Jacob fears, a curse Rebekah volunteers to absorb, and the curse-clause of a blessing.** Verified by lemma 7045 and 779 across Genesis.

#### The name-words

**יַעֲקֹב / עָקֵב / עָקַב** `[T]` *High.* Jacob's name, the heel he holds (25:26, *baʿăqēb ʿĒśāw*), and the verb Esau coins from it (27:36, וַיַּעְקְבֵנִי). The verb *ʿāqab* (6117) occurs **five times in four verses in the whole Hebrew Bible** — Gen 27:36, Job 37:4, Jer 9:3 [Eng 9:4] twice, Hos 12:4 [Eng 12:3]. Two of those four verses are the canon reading *this* passage back (see Tool 11). The noun *ʿāqēb* (6119) stands in Genesis at **3:15, 25:26, 49:17, 49:19** — the serpent striking the heel, Jacob grasping the heel, Dan the serpent biting the horse's heels. `[T]` on the chain; what it means is `[I]` and held at *moderate*.

**אֲדַמְדָּם / אֱדוֹם** `[T]` *High.* 25:25 he comes out *ʾadmônî*, ruddy; 25:30 he asks for הָאָדֹם הָאָדֹם הַזֶּה, "this red red stuff", and the narrator notes "therefore his name was called Edom". The narrator supplies the etymology himself; nothing is inferred.

**שֵׂעָר / שָׂעִיר — hair, hairy, he-goat.** `[T]` on the data; *moderate* on the link. 25:25 he is *kəʾadderet śēʿār*, like a hairy cloak (*śēʿār*, 8181 — its **only occurrence in Genesis**); 27:11 and 27:23 he is *ʾîš śāʿir* / *śəʿirōt*, hairy. The WLC's tagging distinguishes **8163 a** (the adjective "hairy", at 27:11, 23) from **8163 b** (the noun "male goat", at 37:31). They are separate lexemes on the same consonantal root, and I report them as separate — but the narrator's choice remains striking: the word for Esau's body is the word for the goat whose blood is smeared on Joseph's tunic ten chapters later, in a deception of the same father. And Rebekah's instrument at 27:9, 16 is *gədāyê ʿizzîm* — kids of the goats — while 37:31 reads שְׂעִיר עִזִּים, combining *both* of chapter 27's goat-words in one construct. `[S: audit]` The claim audit already restated this chain on *ʿizzîm* (5795), which stands at 27:9, 16; 37:31; 38:17, 20 — that restatement holds and I have re-verified it.

**יִצְחָק / מְצַחֵק** `[T]` *High.* At 26:8 Abimelech looks out and sees יִצְחָק מְצַחֵק — Isaac *isaac-ing* with his wife. The verb that gave him his name (from the laughter of 17:17, 18:12–15, 21:6) is the verb that gives him away. The piel of 6711 in Genesis stands at 21:9 (Ishmael), 26:8 (Isaac), 39:14, 17 (the charge against Joseph) — a small, consistently uncomfortable set.

#### Three words that occur nowhere else

**סֻלָּם — *sullām*, 28:12.** A **hapax legomenon in the entire Hebrew Bible** (lemma 5551: one occurrence, one verse). Whatever Jacob saw, the language has no other word for it and no other instance to compare. That is a fact the preacher should hold gently: "ladder" (NASB95, ESV, KJV) and "stairway" are both reconstructions from etymology and from the picture, not from usage. *High* on the hapax; *uncertain* on the object. `[T]` / `[I]`

**עֵשֶׂק and הִתְעַשְּׂקוּ — 26:20.** The well-name *ʿĒśeq* (6230) and the verb *ʿāśaq* (6229) from which the narrator derives it are **each a hapax legomenon in the whole Hebrew Bible, and both stand in the same verse**. Verified. A verse that names a well twice over in words the language never uses again.

**שִׂטְנָה — *Śiṭnāh*, 26:21.** As tagged in the WLC this is lemma **7856**, one occurrence in the canon. The identical consonantal form at Ezra 4:6 ("they wrote an *accusation* against the inhabitants of Judah") is tagged as a different lexeme, 7855 — the same word, once as a place-name and once as a common noun. The root is ś-ṭ-n, the root behind *śāṭān*. **This does not make a demon of the Philistine herdsmen**, and the overview's Trap 5 stands unaltered: the noun *śāṭān* (7854) does not occur in Genesis. But the overview's flat statement is worth a footnote — the root is present in this unit, as the name of a well. `[T]` on the data, and *nothing* is claimed beyond it.

#### Two more that repay the time

**תָּם — *tām*, 25:27.** Jacob is *ʾîš tām*. The lemma (8535) occurs **fourteen times in the Hebrew Bible**, and the concentration is in Job — 1:1, 1:8, 2:3 describe Job as *tām*, "blameless", and 8:20, 9:20, 9:21, 9:22 argue about it. The other instances are Song 5:2, 6:9; Ps 37:37, 64:5; Prov 29:10; and Exod 26:24 in a technical architectural sense. **Every major English version softens it here** — NASB95 "peaceful", ESV "quiet", KJV "plain" — and none gives the word its Job sense. Two readings are genuinely available: (a) the domestic/settled sense contrasted with Esau the man of the open country, which the following participle יֹשֵׁב אֹהָלִים supports; (b) a flat narratorial irony, calling the deceiver "blameless" two verses before he buys a birthright. The lexical data will not decide it. *Moderate; named in Open Questions.* `[T]` on the count, `[I]` on the reading.

**מַטְעַמִּים — *maṭʿammîm*, "savoury food".** Eight occurrences in the whole Hebrew Bible: **six of them in Genesis 27** (vv. 4, 7, 9, 14, 17, 31) and two in Proverbs 23 (vv. 3, 6) — where both are warnings not to desire a host's delicacies, and 23:3 adds כִּי הוּא לֶחֶם כְּזָבִים, "for it is deceptive food". The direction of dependence runs the other way (Proverbs is later, and Genesis alludes to nothing), so this is not an allusion Genesis makes; it is a downstream observation, and a striking one. *Moderate; reported as reverse-direction only.* `[T]` on the count.

#### Name-hyperlink checks *(load-the-story rule applied)*

**Bethel.** *High.* Trigger 1 fails in the obvious form and that is the finding: **Gen 28:19 is not Bethel's first occurrence in the canon**. The name stands at **12:8 and 13:3** (twice each), where Abram pitches his tent "with Bethel on the west and Ai on the east" and builds an altar, and later "came to the place of the altar which he had made there at the first". Lemma 1008 in Genesis: 12:8, 13:3, 28:19, 31:13, 35:1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 15, 16 — thirteen occurrences in eleven verses. So when Jacob says at 28:16 "the LORD is in this place **and I did not know it**", the reader knows something Jacob does not: **his grandfather built an altar there.** Note also that 13:3–4 uses *māqôm* twice of that site, the same word Jacob's scene will use six times. Source narrative re-read; the freight is Abram's altar, not Jacob's discovery.

Trigger 2 fires forward rather than backward, and hard: Bethel becomes Jeroboam's calf-shrine (1 Kgs 12:28–33) and one of the two places Amos is sent to denounce. The Torah's own readers hold both — the founding encounter and what was later done at the site. `[S]` on the Kings/Amos material; the point for this unit is that Genesis 28 is the innocent end of a name that does not stay innocent.

**Luz.** *Moderate.* Lemma 3870: eight occurrences in seven verses — Gen 28:19, 35:6, 48:3; Josh 16:2, 18:13 (twice); Judg 1:23, 26. Judges 1:23–26 is worth the preacher's minute: the house of Joseph takes Luz, spares the man who showed them the entrance, and he goes off and **founds another Luz in the land of the Hittites**. The old name survives its renaming.

**Esek, Sitnah, Rehoboth.** *High* on the data. Three wells named in three verses, and the naming formula is the same each time (וַיִּקְרָא שֵׁם / שְׁמָהּ). The first two names mean "contention" and "hostility"; the third means "broad places". *Rəḥōbôt* (7344) also names an Assyrian city at 10:11 and an Edomite one at 36:37 — the same word, in the table of nations and in Esau's genealogy, which is a coincidence of vocabulary rather than a link, and is reported as such.

**Beersheba.** *High.* Named by Abraham at 21:31 after an oath with the same Abimelech and the same Phicol; re-named by Isaac at 26:33 after an oath with an Abimelech and a Phicol. The narrator's "to this day" attaches to Isaac's naming, not Abraham's. And 28:10 has Jacob leaving *from Beersheba* — the place his father secured by treaty — for a place his grandfather had already built an altar at.

**Ten (28:22).** *High* on the data. The verb *ʿāśar* (6237) occurs **nine times in seven verses in the whole Hebrew Bible**, and Gen 28:22 has two of them (עַשֵּׂר אֲעַשְּׂרֶנּוּ, infinitive absolute + imperfect). The others are Deut 14:22 (twice), 26:12; 1 Sam 8:15, 17; Neh 10:38, 39. The noun *maʿăśēr* (4643) first stands at **Gen 14:20**, Abram's tenth to Melchizedek. So Genesis contains exactly two tithes — one given by Abram after a victory, one promised by Jacob before a journey — and they bracket the patriarchal narrative before Joseph.

### 8. Translations

#### The one that matters most: 27:39, the preposition

Isaac's word to Esau begins הִנֵּה מִשְׁמַנֵּי הָאָרֶץ יִהְיֶה מוֹשָׁבֶךָ. The מִן may be partitive or privative, and the two readings are opposites.

| | 27:39 |
|---|---|
| **NASB95** | "Behold, **away from** the fertility of the earth shall be your dwelling, and **away from** the dew of heaven from above" |
| **ESV** | "Behold, **away from** the fatness of the earth shall your dwelling be, and **away from** the dew of heaven on high" |
| **KJV** | "Behold, thy dwelling shall be **the fatness** of the earth, and **of the dew** of heaven from above" |
| **LXX (Swete; Rahlfs-based export agrees)** | ἀπὸ τῆς πιότητος τῆς γῆς ἔσται ἡ κατοίκησίς σου — reading most naturally as partitive, and preserving the ambiguity of the Hebrew |

**Ancient versions check — and the honest classification.** This is **not** a textual variant and **not** one of the three triage categories: the Hebrew and the Greek are rendering the identical consonantal text, and the Greek preserves the same ambiguity the Hebrew has. What has happened is that the **modern versions have closed an ambiguity in one direction and the older tradition closed it in the other**, and every English reader is handed a decision he cannot see was made. Report it as a **construal split**, not as a translation-tradition split over a reading. `[T]`

What is at stake: on the privative reading Esau gets a curse dressed as a blessing and the geography of Edom is the point; on the partitive reading Esau gets a real if lesser blessing, which is what 27:38 ("have you but one blessing?") and 33:9 ("I have plenty, my brother") would both suggest. **The preacher should not present either as what the text says.** *Confidence in the ambiguity: high. Confidence in either resolution: uncertain.* Routed to Open Questions.

#### 25:23 — the ambiguity the Greek closed, and Paul argued from

וְרַב יַעֲבֹד צָעִיר. Three unmarked words, no object marker, and Hebrew word order flexible enough in poetry that "the greater shall serve the lesser" and "the lesser shall the greater serve" are both grammatically available.

- **NASB95 / ESV / KJV** all read "the older shall serve the younger".
- **LXX (Swete):** ὁ μείζων δουλεύσει τῷ ἐλάσσονι — the ambiguity closed, decisively, by Greek case endings.
- **Rom 9:12 (SBLGNT):** Ὁ μείζων δουλεύσει τῷ ἐλάσσονι — **verbatim the LXX.**

**Three-Way Triage: category 3** — the Greek as the NT's own text. Paul's election argument in Romans 9 is built on the Greek rendering of a Hebrew clause that the Hebrew itself leaves open, and it stands whichever text is prior. Two things follow for the preacher. First, the English versions have all made the same choice the LXX made, so a congregation will never hear that a choice was made. Second, **Genesis itself resolves it internally** — 27:29 ("be master to your brothers") and 27:40 ("your brother you shall serve") settle the direction inside the unit, which is the strongest argument for the traditional rendering and does not depend on the Greek at all. *High.* `[T]`

#### 26:8 — the pun the study text loses

NASB95 "Isaac was **caressing** his wife Rebekah"; ESV "Isaac **laughing with** Rebekah his wife"; KJV "Isaac was **sporting** with Rebekah his wife". The Hebrew is יִצְחָק מְצַחֵק. Only the ESV keeps a word from which an English reader might feel the name. NASB95 gives the sense and loses the joke — which is here the entire mechanism of the scene, since it is his name that betrays him. *A rare case in this unit where the ESV is closer to what the Hebrew is doing.* `[T]`

#### 27:36 — where the Greek beats every English version

Esau's pun turns on *Yaʿăqōb / wayyaʿqəbēnî*. NASB95 "he has **supplanted** me"; ESV "he has **cheated** me"; KJV "he hath **supplanted** me". None of them can carry the name. **The LXX coins a verb to do it**: Δικαίως ἐκλήθη τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ Ἰακώβ· **ἐπτέρνικεν** γάρ με — "rightly is he called Jacob, for he has *heeled* me", from πτέρνα, the heel, which is the same word the Greek used of the heel Jacob grasped at 25:26. In the very same verse the Greek **loses** the other pun completely (πρωτοτοκεῖα / εὐλογία for *bəkōrāh* / *bərākāh*). One verse, two puns, and the Greek keeps one and drops one. **Three-Way Triage: category 1 both ways — translation loss, and translation gain; neither is evidence about the LXX's reliability.** `[T]`

#### Smaller divergences worth a note

- **25:27** *tām*: NASB95 "peaceful", ESV "quiet", KJV "plain". See Tool 7 — all three soften a word the same versions render "blameless" in Job.
- **25:28** NASB95 "because he had a taste for game" is a paraphrase of כִּי־צַיִד בְּפִיו, "for game was in his mouth"; ESV "because he ate of his game" is closer.
- **27:12** NASB95 "as a deceiver", ESV "mocking him" — the Hebrew כִּמְתַעְתֵּעַ (from *tāʿaʿ*) allows both; the versions have split.
- **27:41** NASB95 "bore a grudge" is closer to *śāṭam* than ESV's "hated"; *śānēʾ*, the ordinary verb for hate, is a different word and stands at 26:27 in this same unit — so the versions have levelled a distinction the Hebrew keeps.
- **27:30** Neither version shows the doubled emphasis (*ʾak* plus the infinitive absolute יָצֹא יָצָא) on how narrowly Esau missed Jacob.

**Pulpit divergence note:** **N/A — no pulpit text declared.** The ESV appears above as a second literal witness. Should the ESV be the pulpit text at the engagement, three of the notes above become live for the preacher: 25:27 (*tām*), 26:8 (where the ESV is *better*), and 27:39 (where the ESV closes the ambiguity in the same direction as the NASB95, so the congregation will hear a curse and never know a blessing was grammatically available).

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Panel A: brisk, almost brutal.** Sixteen verses cover a marriage, twenty years of barrenness, a pregnancy, an oracle, a birth, a childhood, and a transaction. 25:34 is five verbs in a row — *wayyōʾkal wayyēšt wayyāqom wayyēlak* — and then the verdict. The pace is contemptuous of Esau, and it is meant to be.

**Panel B: settled, then squeezed.** Chapter 26 is the calmest stretch in the Jacob cycle: a famine, a promise, a prosperity, a series of wells. But the tone tightens: envy (26:14), stopped wells (26:15), expulsion (26:16), two quarrels (26:20–21), and only then relief at Rehoboth. The register is **pressure relieved**, and 26:22's naming — "at last the LORD has made room for us" — is the emotional exhale of the whole chapter.

**Panel C: unbearable, and deliberately slow.** The narrator brakes to a crawl at exactly the point of maximum discomfort. Isaac asks four separate identity questions (27:18 "who are you, my son?"; 27:21 "whether you are really my son Esau or not"; 27:24 "are you really my son Esau?"; 27:32 "who are you?"). Jacob's second answer is one word — אָנִי, "I am" — which is as bare as a lie can be made. Then the tone breaks: 27:33 has Isaac **trembling a very great trembling exceedingly** (cognate accusative + adjective + intensifier), and 27:34 has Esau **crying a very great and bitter cry** (the identical construction, one verse later). Two men, one after the other, given the same grammar of collapse. The soundtrack for these two verses is a single sustained note.

**Panel D: hushed and then awed.** A stone, a sunset, a dream, and *mah-nôrāʾ* — "how awesome" (28:17) — the only occurrence of the niphal participle of *yārēʾ* in this unit. The tone is not comfort; it is fear (28:17a, "and he was afraid"). Note what God does **not** say: the *ʾal-tîrāʾ* that accompanies theophanies at 15:1, 21:17, 26:24 and 46:3 is absent at 28:13–15. Jacob is afterwards afraid and nobody tells him not to be. *Moderate* — the fear is reported after the speech, so this is an observation, not a clean pattern-break; the LXX's plus at 28:13 shows an early reader felt the same gap (see Textual Variants).

**The *ʾāhab* finding deserves its own paragraph.** `[T]` *High.* The verb's first occurrence in the whole Bible is Gen 22:2 — a father's love for his only son, spoken as he is asked to give him up (the overview establishes this). In this unit it occurs five times. **25:28 is the only verse in Genesis with the verb twice**, and it is the verse that divides the household: Isaac loved Esau, Rebekah loved Jacob. The other three — **27:4, 27:9, 27:14** — all have the same object, and it is not a person: כַּאֲשֶׁר אָהַבְתִּי / כַּאֲשֶׁר אָהֵב / כַּאֲשֶׁר אָהֵב אָבִיו, "such as I love", "such as he loves", "such as his father loved" — **of a dish of food.** In the chapter where a father's love is what everything turns on, the only thing the text says he loves is dinner.

**Repeated ideas rather than words.** Three of them run the unit:
- **Someone is asked to identify himself** — 27:18, 21, 24, 32, four times in one chapter, plus 25:30's naming and 26:9's "certainly she is your wife".
- **Someone is sent away** — 26:16 (Abimelech sends Isaac), 26:27, 29, 31 (the sending recalled and repeated), 27:43 (flee), 28:5, 6 (Isaac sent Jacob away). The verb *šālaḥ* runs through both halves.
- **A younger displaces an elder** — 25:23 (oracle), 25:33 (birthright), 27:23 (blessing), 27:36 (Esau's summary), 28:1–5 (the sending, with the elder left at home). Four times, and the last is the only one done in daylight.

**`Internal:` The Bethel promise is assembled almost entirely out of earlier Abrahamic wording.** *(answers §earlier; high; `[T]`)* — 28:13's "the land … I will give it to you and to your seed" is the substance of 13:15 and 17:8; **28:14a's כַּעֲפַר הָאָרֶץ, "as the dust of the earth", is Abram's simile from 13:16**; 28:14b is 12:3; 28:14's four directions (west, east, north, south) are 13:14's four directions in a different order. **`Internal:` Genesis 26 replays Genesis 20–21 with the same king, the same commander and the same wells.** *(answers §earlier; high; `[T]`)* — Abimelech, Phicol, the wife-sister claim, the oath, the covenant, the naming of Beersheba: 21:22–34 and 26:26–33 are the same scene twice, and 26:1's own opening clause ("besides the former famine … in the days of Abraham") tells the reader to compare. #### The reverse direction — where this unit is used downstream

**Gen 25:26 and 27:36 → Hosea 12:4 [Eng 12:3]; Jeremiah 9:3 [Eng 9:4]** *(high)* `[T]`

*Source context (Move 1, run on the OT users):* Hosea 12 is a covenant lawsuit — "the LORD has a *rîb* with Judah, and will punish Jacob according to his ways" (12:3 [Eng 12:2]). Into that indictment Hosea drops **בַּבֶּטֶן עָקַב אֶת־אָחִיו**, "in the womb he supplanted his brother" (12:4), then the wrestling, then **בֵּית־אֵל יִמְצָאֶנּוּ וְשָׁם יְדַבֵּר עִמָּנוּ**, "at Bethel he found him, and there He spoke with us" (12:5), and later Jacob's flight to the field of Aram (12:13). **Hosea 12 is the Old Testament's own exposition of exactly this unit** — the grasping, the Bethel encounter, the flight to Paddan-aram — and he reads it as indictment and grace together. Jeremiah 9:3 turns the name into a national diagnosis: כָּל־אָח **עָקוֹב יַעְקֹב**, "every brother utterly supplants", infinitive absolute plus imperfect, two of the verb's five canonical occurrences in one clause.

*Book usage (Move 2, reversed):* the verb *ʿāqab* exists in the canon in four verses, and two of them are prophets reading Genesis 27:36.

*OT-to-OT (Move 3):* Hosea and Jeremiah are not independent. Both use the name as a verb; both apply it to the covenant people rather than to the patriarch alone; both stand in the Latter Prophets. Whether Jeremiah is reading Hosea is not settled here and is left open.

*What it adds:* the prophets did not treat this unit as a family story. They treated Jacob's name as a national character-description — and Hosea, having said it, goes straight to Bethel, where the grace was.

**Gen 25:23 → Romans 9:12** *(high — direct quotation)* `[T]`

*Source context:* the oracle is given to Rebekah before the twins are born, in answer to a question she asked because the pregnancy hurt (25:22). It is unrequested revelation about two peoples, and its last colon is the ambiguous clause discussed under Tool 8.

*Book usage (reversed):* Genesis itself resolves the ambiguity at 27:29 and 27:40, both inside this unit, before any later reader has to.

*OT-to-OT:* Paul pairs the Genesis oracle with **Malachi 1:2–3** ("Jacob I loved, Esau I hated"). The two are already in canonical conversation: Malachi's own question is "was not Esau Jacob's brother?" and his verb for God's choice is **אָהַב**, the very verb Genesis uses at 25:28 of Isaac's and Rebekah's partiality. Malachi transfers to God the word Genesis used of the parents. *Moderate on the transfer being deliberate; high on the lexical fact.*

*What it adds:* Romans 9:11's "though the twins were not yet born and had not done anything good or bad" is not Paul's inference — it is Genesis 25:23's own position in the narrative, before verse 24. And the preacher should know that **the "hated" half of Rom 9:13 is Malachi, not Genesis**; Genesis never says God hated Esau, and blesses him at 27:39–40. See Preaching Pitfalls.

**Gen 25:29–34 → Hebrews 12:16–17** *(high)* `[T]`

*Source context:* the stew scene, with the narrator's verdict at 25:34c.

*What it adds and where it is hard:* Hebrews calls Esau βέβηλος, "profane", and says ὃς ἀντὶ βρώσεως μιᾶς ἀπέδετο τὰ **πρωτοτόκια** ἑαυτοῦ — the only occurrence of that noun in the NT, and the LXX's word at Gen 25:33–34 (Swete: πρωτοτοκεῖα). Then 12:17: "he found no place of repentance (μετανοίας τόπον οὐχ εὗρεν) though he sought it with tears." **The pronoun αὐτήν is feminine and both candidate antecedents — εὐλογίαν and μετανοίας — are feminine.** Genesis 27:34, 38 has Esau seeking *the blessing* with tears; it says nothing about his seeking repentance. This is a real interpretive crux with pastoral consequences and it is treated under Difficult Verses.

**Gen 27 → Hebrews 11:20** *(high)* `[T]` — "By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau **concerning things to come**." Hebrews credits with faith the man Genesis shows blind, fooled, and blessing the wrong son. Also treated under Difficult Verses.

**Gen 28:12 → John 1:51** *(high — the strongest link in the unit)* `[T]` / `[S: NT]`

*Source context:* Jacob, fleeing, asleep on a stone at his grandfather's altar-site, sees a *sullām* with angels going up and down and the LORD standing over it; he wakes and calls the place "the house of God and the gate of heaven".

*The Greek data:* LXX Gen 28:12 (Swete) reads οἱ ἄγγελοι τοῦ θεοῦ **ἀνέβαινον καὶ κατέβαινον ἐπ᾽ αὐτῆς** — and the feminine αὐτῆς agrees with κλῖμαξ, so the Greek has decided that the traffic is on **the ladder**. John 1:51 (SBLGNT) reads τοὺς ἀγγέλους τοῦ θεοῦ **ἀναβαίνοντας καὶ καταβαίνοντας ἐπὶ τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** — the same two verbs in the same order, and the referent changed to a person.

*The Hebrew ambiguity underneath:* MT has בּוֹ, masculine, and both *sullām* and Jacob are masculine. "On it" and "on him" are both grammatically available; the LXX closed it one way and John, as it happens, closes it the other. **Three-Way Triage: category 3.** John's reading is a fact about how the NT reads Genesis and is load-bearing whichever construal is prior; it is not evidence that either text is wrong. *High.*

*What it adds:* Jacob's answer to the vision was to name the stone "the house of God" and promise a tithe. John's answer is that the meeting-point of heaven and earth is a person, and that the disciples will see it. The preacher who takes John 1:51 seriously has to say that Jacob got the right category and the wrong object.

### 12. Genre

Hebrew historical narrative — third-person, *wayyiqtol*-driven, with a genealogical heading — containing **three embedded poetic oracles** (25:23; 27:27b–29; 27:39–40), one **narratorial etymology sequence** (25:25, 26, 30; 26:20, 21, 22, 33; 28:19), and one **vow formula** (28:20–22, the first in Scripture).

Reading rules that matter here: the narrator's asides are authoritative and the characters' speech is not (Tool 6); the poetry inside the prose is where the theological load sits and should be read for parallelism (Tool 5); and the etymologies are the narrator's, not the characters' — he tells you what a name means and thereby tells you what he thinks of the person or the episode.

One genre note the preacher should hold: the birthright transaction at 25:31–33 is framed as a **legal act** — "sell me" (מִכְרָה), "swear to me" (הִשָּׁבְעָה), "and he swore" (וַיִּשָּׁבַע), "and he sold" (וַיִּמְכֹּר). The narrative is not describing a rash promise; it is describing a completed contract with an oath attached. That is why Esau at 27:36 can say "he took my birthright" without disputing its validity.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where on the timeline.** Patriarchal era, second generation after the call: c. 200 years after Abram left Haran, and some 400 years before Sinai. Nothing of Israel exists yet — no nation, no law, no priesthood, no land held, no sanctuary. The one piece of real estate in the family is a burial cave (23) and one re-dug well.

**Canonical position.** Torah, first book, first book of the Tanak (see Phase 0.55).

**Where the reader is, and what has happened since.** Everything. The three things this unit's characters handle without knowing what they are handling all have canonical futures: the **stone pillar** (28:18, 22) is prohibited by Deuteronomy 16:22; **Bethel** becomes a calf-shrine (1 Kgs 12:28–33) and the target of Amos; and the **tithe** (28:22) becomes law (Lev 27:30–32; Num 18:21–28; Deut 14:22–29). A Christian reading has to hold that the vow Jacob makes at a stone is answered not at a stone but at a person (John 1:51), and that the ladder is not something to build but someone to be shown.

**Divergence between canonical and compositional order.** None load-bearing for this unit; the material's place in the book is stable in all traditions. The only ordering question is internal — chapter 26's chronological displacement — and that is treated under Phase 0.6 and the Schnittjer pass, not as a canonical question.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Nobody here is me, and nobody here is the hero.** That is the first thing to say, because the four principals are a father whose senses fail him and who loves his dinner, a mother who is right about the outcome and wrong about the means, a younger son who steals what he had already been promised, and an elder son who sells what he was born with. The unit's only unambiguously admirable actor is God.

| Character | Function |
|---|---|
| **Isaac** | Not a type of Christ in this unit. Recipient of a promise he did nothing to earn, and — at 28:1–4 — a genuine mediator of it. *Foil* to Abraham throughout chapter 26 |
| **Rebekah** | *Recipient of the message* (25:23), and the only one who seeks it. Also a *foil* to Isaac: she knows what God said and he seems not to |
| **Jacob** | *Representative* — of the elect who have nothing to commend them. Not a model; Hebrews' verdict on him elsewhere (11:21) rests on a different scene entirely |
| **Esau** | *Negative example*, explicitly so at Heb 12:16. But not a villain: the narrative gives him the loudest grief in the unit and Isaac does bless him |
| **Abimelech and the Philistines** | *Foil* — outsiders who see plainly what the family cannot: "we have seen that the LORD has been with you" (26:28) |
| **God** | The subject. The one who chooses before birth, keeps the promise through fraud, and commits himself to a fugitive |

**The "Moses-is-me" trap in this unit has two forms and both are common.** The first is "be a Jacob — take hold of God's promises". The second, its mirror, is "don't be a Jacob — don't be a schemer". Both make a human being the subject of a passage whose subject is God's fidelity to a family that cannot be trusted with it.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author is after.** That the reader would stop looking for the reason God kept this promise in the people who carried it, and see that the promise is kept because of who God is. The narrator's method is to remove, one by one, every basis on which a reader might think the transmission deserved: birth order (25:23), paternal judgement (27:23), honest dealing (27:19), and finally the heir's own presence in the land (28:5). Then he has God say "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go."

**Stage 2 — worldview before behaviour.**

*How it tells us to think.* Election is not the reward for anything, including for wanting it badly. Jacob wanted the birthright and the blessing and got both by fraud, and God gave him the actual promise while he was asleep and had nothing. A worldview shaped by this unit expects God's purposes to advance through people who are not equal to them, and does not treat that as scandal.

*What to stop.* Reading providence backwards as approval — assuming that because a plan worked, it was right. Rebekah's plan worked. The narrator does not therefore commend it, and the cost is visible: she never sees her son again.

*What to start.* Distinguishing the blessing that can be seized from the blessing that can only be given. Jacob spent 90 verses acquiring dew, grain and dominion and received the covenant as a gift while unconscious. There is a version of the Christian life that consists in working very hard for what has already been given.

*The motive.* Not "God chose Jacob, so try to be chosen", which is incoherent, but: the God who bound himself to a fugitive with "I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised" is the God whose promises do not depend on the state of the person holding them.

**Four audiences.**

- *For me.* Where am I working to secure something God has already said is mine? And, harder: where has a plan of mine worked, and have I mistaken that for a verdict on it?
- *For a Christian friend.* The believer who thinks their past disqualifies them needs 28:13–15 read aloud with 27 fresh in mind — this is what God said to a man who had just lied to his blind father, and he said it first, unasked.
- *For the church community.* A congregation that only tells stories about the faith of its founders is telling a different story from the one Genesis tells. The honesty of 25–28 about a covenant family is a permission, and a warning.
- *For an unbeliever.* This is not a story about good religious people. The claim of the Bible is not that God's people earned his commitment; it is that he made it before they could and kept it after they had spoiled it.

**Prayer.** Lord, you spoke to Rebekah before her sons had done anything, and to Jacob when he had done everything wrong, and both times you spoke first. Teach us to stop grasping at what you have already promised to give, and to hear you say: *I am with you, and I will keep you wherever you go, and I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised.*

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **Infinitive absolute, three times, all emphatic and all in speech or narration of a decisive moment:** 26:28 רָאוֹ רָאִינוּ ("we have surely seen" — the Philistines' testimony); 27:30 יָצֹא יָצָא ("had hardly gone out" — the timing on which everything hangs); **28:22 עַשֵּׂר אֲעַשְּׂרֶנּוּ** (piel infinitive absolute + piel imperfect, "I will surely tithe" — the emphatic climax of the vow). `[T]` *High.*
- **Cognate accusative twice in consecutive verses,** 27:33 וַיֶּחֱרַד … חֲרָדָה גְּדֹלָה עַד־מְאֹד and 27:34 וַיִּצְעַק צְעָקָה גְּדֹלָה וּמָרָה עַד־מְאֹד — identical construction, identical intensifier, father then son. `[T]` *High.*
- **Unmarked object in poetry, 25:23** — no אֶת on either noun; the ambiguity is structural, not accidental (see Tool 8).
- **Jussive/indicative mixture in the blessings** — 27:28 וְיִתֶּן־לְךָ (jussive, "may he give"), 27:29 יַעַבְדוּךָ (jussive) alongside indicatives. Isaac's blessing is a *wish*, grammatically, not a decree — which makes 27:33's "yes, and he shall be blessed" all the more striking: he cannot revoke a wish, and says so.
- **Ketiv/qere, three in the unit.** 25:23 ketiv גיים (defectively written; the WLC tags it as 1471, *gôy*, "nations") — the occasionally-heard proposal to read *gēʾîm*, "proud ones", is a conjecture, not the Masoretic reading, and should not be preached. 27:3 ketiv צידה / qere ציד. 27:29 ketiv וישתחו (singular) / qere וְיִשְׁתַּחֲווּ (plural), agreeing with the plural verb in the second colon. None affects the sense materially. `[T]`

### Textual Variants

Category-2 divergences only, per the Three-Way Triage; translation loss is reported under Tools 7 and 8, and the John 1:51 material under Tool 11.

**28:13 — the LXX has a plus.** Swete and the Rahlfs-based export both read ὁ δὲ Κύριος ἐπεστήρικτο ἐπ᾽ αὐτῆς καὶ εἶπεν Ἐγὼ ὁ θεὸς Ἀβραὰμ τοῦ πατρός σου καὶ ὁ θεὸς Ἰσαάκ· **μὴ φοβοῦ**· ἡ γῆ… The MT has no מִּירָא-clause. *Evidence:* the phrase is exactly what stands at 26:24 (אַל־תִּירָא) fifteen verses earlier, addressed to Isaac in a night theophany with the same "I am the God of your father Abraham" opening. *Position:* most likely a harmonisation to 26:24, either by the translator or in his *Vorlage*; the shorter Hebrew is likelier original and the harmonising direction is the easier one to explain. But the fact that an early reader felt the gap is itself a datum — Jacob is afraid two verses later and nobody has told him not to be. *Confidence: moderate.* Preaching implication: none directly, but do not say "God told Jacob not to fear" — he did not.

**28:14 — the LXX substitutes the simile.** MT: כַּעֲפַר הָאָרֶץ, "as the dust of the earth" (Abram's simile from 13:16). LXX: ὡς ἡ ἄμμος τῆς θαλάσσης, "as the sand of the sea" (the simile of 22:17 and 32:13). *Evidence:* the Greek has swapped one Abrahamic simile for another, both of which are available in Genesis. This is far more likely harmonisation to the commoner formula than a different *Vorlage*, and the MT reading is the harder one because it is the rarer simile. *Position: MT original; the Greek harmonised. Confidence: moderate-to-high.* Preaching implication: the dust simile is deliberate — it is Abram's word to Jacob, and see Tool 11.

**27:29 — "your mother's sons" or "your father's sons".** MT: בְּנֵי אִמֶּךָ, "the sons of your mother". LXX: οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ πατρός σου, "the sons of your father". *Evidence:* "your father's sons" is exactly the phrase Jacob uses to Judah at 49:8 (יִשְׁתַּחֲוּוּ לְךָ בְּנֵי אָבִיךָ), so the Greek looks harmonised to the later blessing. The MT reading is also the pointed one: "your mother's sons" narrows the bowing to Rebekah's children — that is, to Esau alone, and Isaac's blessing thereby names the very rivalry Rebekah has engineered. *Position: MT original; the harder and more pointed reading. Confidence: moderate.*

**26:3, 4 — plural or singular land.** MT twice reads אֶת־כָּל־הָאֲרָצֹת הָאֵל, "all these lands", with the archaic demonstrative הָאֵל; LXX twice reads πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν ταύτην, singular. *Evidence:* the Hebrew plural is unusual and the demonstrative archaic — both marks of a harder reading; the Greek singular matches the standard formula everywhere else. *Position: MT original. Confidence: moderate.* Preaching implication: minor, but the plural is worth noticing — Isaac is promised *lands*.

**28:3 — El Shaddai.** MT: וְאֵל שַׁדַּי; LXX: ὁ δὲ θεός μου, "and my God", apparently reading אֵלִי or resolving the divine title. *Evidence:* the Greek's usual rendering of *šadday* is παντοκράτωρ, and it is not used here. *Position: unsettled; possibly a Vorlage variant, possibly a translator's simplification. Confidence: uncertain.* Preaching implication: none, but it means the LXX loses the deliberate link to 17:1.

**28:19 — Οὐλαμμαύς.** The Greek has taken the MT's וְאוּלָם לוּז ("but Luz") as a single proper name, "Oulammaus". A translator's misdivision, not a variant reading of the consonants. *High confidence; no exegetical weight.*

**No variant in this unit affects a doctrine or a sermon.** The three that matter for accuracy are 28:13 (do not say God told Jacob not to fear), 28:14 (do not preach "sand of the sea" here), and 27:29 (the MT's "mother's sons" is the sharper text).

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Second-generation patriarchal, semi-nomadic pastoralism at the southern edge of Canaan — Gerar, the Gerar valley, Beersheba — with one journey north-east toward Haran. The family is wealthy in livestock (26:14) and, unusually for the patriarchs, farms as well: **26:12 is the only place in Genesis where a patriarch sows and reaps a crop.** `[T]`

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

1. **What a birthright was.** A double portion of the inheritance and headship of the family. Selling it was legally possible and socially unthinkable. The audience needed no explanation of why 25:34c is a verdict. `[I]`
2. **That a deathbed blessing was a legal act, not a sentiment.** This is why Isaac cannot take it back (27:33, 37) and why Esau's question at 27:36 is about property, not affection. The often-cited **Nuzi tablets are `[S]` and contested** as evidence for the specific practices here; the text supports the general point on its own, through the oath at 25:33 and Isaac's inability to reverse at 27:37. Do not build the sermon on the tablets. `[S]` *Uncertain — the parallel is real but its relevance is disputed.*
3. **What water rights meant.** In this landscape a well is capital, and stopping a well is an act of war (26:15). Isaac's response — move, dig again, move again — reads to a modern hearer as passivity and to the original audience as costly restraint by a man strong enough to fight (26:16, "you are too powerful for us"). `[I]`
4. **Treaty form.** 26:26–31 has the standard shape: the approach, the recitation of past good conduct, the oath, the meal, the departure in peace. The audience recognised a covenant ceremony and would have heard 26:28's "you are now the blessed of the LORD" as the Philistines conceding the point.
5. **Standing stones and anointing.** A *maṣṣēbāh* was ordinary Canaanite cultic furniture. The audience knew that, **and knew Deuteronomy 16:22's prohibition** — which is exactly what makes 28:18 and 28:22 uncomfortable rather than picturesque. See Difficult Verses.
6. **Endogamy.** 26:34–35, 27:46 and 28:1–2, 6–9 form a frame around the marriage question. The audience knew that a Hittite daughter-in-law was not a matter of taste.
7. **What Bethel had become.** The Torah's readers knew the site. See Tool 7.

**What this changes.** Two things above all: chapter 26 stops looking like a digression (a family that farms, holds wells and cuts treaties is a family becoming a people in the land), and Jacob's pillar stops looking like piety and starts looking like a question.

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section:** Torah — the founding story, with nothing before it. The canonical reader arrives with no prior text at all.
- **What the canonical reader knows:** everything in Genesis 1–25, and nothing else. He knows the call, the covenant, the Akedah, the two earlier wife-sister episodes, Abraham's altar at Bethel and his wells at Beersheba, and he has just watched Ishmael's line closed off.
- **The Torah's special case.** Per the extension's rule, for the Torah the compositional and canonical audiences coincide: **the first readers held the founding story and the tabernacle at once.** That is not a later overlay; it is the reading frame the author built in. For this unit it does specific work — a reader with the tabernacle in his camp hears **בֵּית אֱלֹהִים** (28:17, 22) and **שַׁעַר הַשָּׁמַיִם** as a claim about *where God may be met*, and hears Jacob's *maṣṣēbāh* and tithe against the cult he already practises. `[I]` on the specific application; the frame itself is the extension's standing rule.
- **Canonical register:** none available. There are no canonical neighbours to hear this against.

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were:** people who knew Egypt, knew a promise of land they did not yet hold, and could be told that a place-name persisted "to this day" (26:33). `[I]`
- **Their situation:** holding a promise made to ancestors, and asking whether it had survived the generations.
- **What they brought:** the assumption that a firstborn inherits; that a father's blessing is binding; that a well is worth fighting over; and that a man alone at night away from his people is in danger.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking:** that God's word about the two sons comes *before* they are born and *against* the birth order (25:23); and that after 55 verses of fraud, God's speech at Bethel contains not one word of rebuke.
- **Surprising:** that the promise is confirmed to Isaac "**because Abraham obeyed My voice**" (26:5) — the second generation inherits on the first generation's obedience, not its own.
- **Comforting:** "I am with you and will keep you wherever you go, and will bring you back" (28:15) — to hearers who knew what it was to be outside the land, this is the sentence the unit exists for.
- **Disturbing:** that the family of promise is a household of favouritism, fraud and a murder plot, and the narrator does not apologise for it.
- **Funny, and bleakly so:** 26:8. The king looks out of a window and catches Isaac *isaac-ing*. The scene is played for the joke, and the joke exposes the lie.

#### What we bring that they didn't

Individualism (this is a story about a lineage, not a personality); a therapeutic reading of family dysfunction (the text is not interested in healing this family, only in whether the promise survives it); the assumption that a narrative without condemnation is a narrative of approval; and — most distorting here — a sentimental picture of "Jacob's ladder" formed by hymnody rather than by the text, which has no climbing in it at all.

#### Implication for the sermon

Recovering the original reception moves the emphasis off Jacob's character and on to the question the first hearers were actually asking: *will the promise get through?* That is the question the unit answers, and it answers it twice — once before the twins are born and once after everything has gone wrong.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Three, and they converge.

#### 1. God's presence — "I am with you"

- **Where this passage sits:** at the formula's clearest early statement. `[S: audit]` The claim audit established the programme runs to **sixteen** places in Genesis, not the five the sweep claimed, and **five of the sixteen are in this unit**: 26:3 (promise), 26:24 (reassurance), 26:28 (the Philistines' testimony), 28:15 (promise), 28:20 (Jacob's condition).
- **What comes before:** 21:22, where **the same Abimelech** says the same thing to Abraham — "God is with you in all that you do." The formula's first appearance in Genesis is on a foreigner's lips.
- **What comes after:** Exod 3:12, where God's answer to Moses is כִּי־אֶהְיֶה עִמָּךְ, the identical construction to Gen 26:3's וְאֶהְיֶה עִמְּךָ, two verses before אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה makes the verb a name. Then Matt 1:23 and Matt 28:20.
- **What it contributes:** the promise God makes to Jacob is not prosperity but company, and Jacob's counter-offer at 28:20 treats it as a condition to be met rather than a gift to be trusted. *Confidence: high on the data; moderate on the Exod 3 link.*

#### 2. The house of God

- **Where this passage sits:** at the naming. 28:17 and 28:22 are the **first occurrences of בֵּית אֱלֹהִים in the canon**, and 28:18 is the **first *maṣṣēbāh***.
- **What comes before:** Eden as the place God walks (3:8), and the altars of 12:7–8, 13:4, 26:25.
- **What comes after:** the tabernacle, the temple, the departure of the glory (Ezek 10), John 1:14 (ἐσκήνωσεν, "tabernacled"), and John 1:51.
- **What it contributes:** the trajectory begins with a man who thinks a *place* is the house of God and ends with a person who says the traffic between heaven and earth runs over him. *High.*

#### 3. Blessing and curse

- **Where this passage sits:** at the point of maximum lexical concentration in the book (see Tool 10) and at the point where the Abrahamic bless/curse formula (12:3) is passed on inside a deception (27:29).
- **What comes before:** 1:22, 28; 9:1; 12:1–3; 22:17–18.
- **What comes after:** the whole Deuteronomic blessing-and-curse structure (Deut 27–28), and Gal 3:8–14, where the nations-blessing of 12:3 and the curse of the law meet at the cross.
- **What it contributes:** the unit shows blessing being fought over, bought, stolen, wept for and finally simply given. *High.*

**Confluence.** These three are **climactically convergent**, and the convergence has a name in this unit: at Bethel, the *presence* ("I am with you"), the *place* ("the house of God") and the *blessing* ("in you all the families of the ground shall be blessed") are given in one speech, to one man, in fifteen verses. Everything the book has been assembling separately arrives together, on the ground, to a fugitive. That convergence is where the preaching gold is, and it is the reason the sweep was right to name 28:10–22 as the unit's load-bearer.

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton.** Genesis · patriarchal narratives · particularisation via *toledot*; the leading idea served is **seed and blessing narrowing to one line**, and this unit is where it narrows for the second time — Isaac's *toledot* heading (25:19) after Ishmael's (25:12), then within it Jacob after Esau.

**Findings.**

1. *(step 4 — leading word)* **בָּרַךְ is the leading word of the unit beyond dispute**: 27 occurrences in 23 verses, 21 of them in Panel C, against 73 in the whole book. And its shadow, *bəkōrāh*, is the same consonants rearranged — a leading word with an anagram for a rival. `[T]` *High.*
2. *(step 1 — selectivity and time)* **The narrator brakes hardest at the deception**: 55 verses, 46% of the unit, for one afternoon; and accelerates through twenty years of barrenness in half a verse (25:21) and a whole childhood in one (25:27). Braking is weight, and the weight is on the failure, not the fraud's success. `[T]` *High.*
3. *(step 1 — chronological vs thematic order)* **Chapter 26 is out of chronological sequence and placed thematically.** Rebekah is desirable at 26:7 and the twins are unmentioned until 26:34; the chapter belongs earlier. It is here to establish that Isaac holds the promise, that blessing works, and that deceit is inherited. `[I]` *High on the displacement; moderate on the reasons.*
4. *(step 2 — narrative space)* **Every movement in this unit is enforced or evasive, and the last one is out of the land.** Isaac goes to Gerar because of famine, is expelled by Abimelech, moves twice from contested wells, goes up to Beersheba; Jacob is sent to Paddan-aram to escape a brother; and 28:10's "went out from Beersheba" is the first step of a twenty-year exile. Against that, the vertical axis at 28:12 — set on the earth, its head reaching the heavens — is the one movement in the unit that is not flight. `[T]` / `[I]` *High.*
5. *(step 3 — janus)* **28:10–22 faces both ways.** It closes the home narrative (it is the consequence of 27 and the execution of 28:1–5) and opens the exile (28:15's "I will bring you back" is the promise the next seven chapters test, and 28:20–22 the vow that 31:13 and 35:1–7 will call in). A textbook janus. `[T]` *High.*
6. *(step 5 — first vs second reading)* On a **first** reading, 27:36's "he has supplanted me these two times" is Esau's bitterness. On a **second**, it is the narrator's summary of the man's name, and 29:25 ("in the morning, behold, it was Leah") is the reader's promised satisfaction — the elder substituted for the younger, on a deceiver, in the dark. Nothing in the text of this unit anticipates it; everything in it earns it. `[I]` *High.*
7. *(step 6 — polyacoustic intertextuality)* **28:12 builds toward Christ rather than pointing flatly at him.** The echoes accumulate: a place where heaven is open; a house of God; a gate of heaven; and traffic between the two whose referent the Hebrew leaves ambiguous (בּוֹ). John 1:51 is the canon's own resolution, and the honest way to preach it is as arrival, not as decoding. Do not flatten it to "the ladder is Jesus"; the text says the *place* is the house of God and John says the *person* is. `[T]` / `[S: NT]` *High.*

**Echoes and signals.** Leading word: *bārak*. Structural devices: two divine speeches framing a human failure; a janus at 28:10–22; a thematically displaced chapter. Intertextual links (all internal or forward): 22:18 → 26:4; 12:3 + 13:16 → 28:14; 12:8/13:3 → 28:19; 27:9/16 → 37:31; 27:23 → the *nākar* chain; 28:12 → 32:2 → John 1:51.

**Nil returns.** *Step 3, mirror imaging/chiasm:* no chiasm is claimed for the unit (see Tool 3). *Step 3, type-scene:* the betrothal type-scene is **not** run here — Jacob's well-meeting is at 29:1–12, outside this unit; 26:19–22's wells are not a betrothal scene and should not be read as one.

**Irreducibility.** The temptation with this unit is to reduce it to a doctrine — "unconditional election" — and preach the doctrine with the story as illustration. The story will not go quietly: it insists on the smell of Esau's clothes, the goatskin on the wrists, the one-word lie, the great bitter cry, the stone under the head. Let it rule the reader rather than being cashed out.

### Christological Reading

#### Type of connection

**Trajectory** (primary), with one **NT-authorised typological connection** and one strong **contrast**.

*Four-test assessment of the Bethel connection (28:12 → John 1:51):*

1. **Theological-category test — passes.** The connection runs through *presence / house of God / meeting-place*, an established category, not through an incidental detail.
2. **NT-precedent test — passes at the strongest level.** John 1:51 makes this exact connection, using the LXX's own verbs.
3. **Escalation test — passes.** The place becomes a person; the stone becomes flesh; the traffic that Jacob merely saw the disciples are told they *will see* on the Son of Man.
4. **Authorial-pattern test — passes partially.** Genesis itself raises the expectation (28:17's "gate of heaven" is an unresolved claim; 28:22's stone is a promise about a future house), but the resolution is only visible read backwards. Score: passes all four, one partially. **High confidence typology.**

#### How the passage points to Christ

**The place that became a person.** Jacob's conclusion is a claim about geography — "this is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven" — and his response is to make a marker of the stone. John 1:51 does not deny the claim; it relocates it. The category is right; the object was always going to be someone.

**The blessing that could not be stolen.** The structural finding of this unit (Headline 1) is Christologically load-bearing: the blessing Jacob seized was fertility and dominion, and the blessing of Abraham — land, seed, and all the families of the ground — was *given*. Galatians 3:14 names that second blessing as what comes to the Gentiles in Christ: ἵνα εἰς τὰ ἔθνη ἡ εὐλογία τοῦ Ἀβραὰμ γένηται ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ. What could not be seized in Genesis 27 is exactly what is distributed by gift in Galatians 3.

**Contrast — the two who grasped and the one who did not.** Jacob grasps the heel at birth and the blessing in old age, and receives the covenant only as a gift. The contrast is not decorative: the whole unit is about a man taking what he had already been promised. Where the canon takes this is Philippians 2:6 (οὐχ ἁρπαγμὸν ἡγήσατο) — but the preacher should note that **no verbal link exists**; this is a conceptual contrast, and it should be flagged as such rather than smuggled in as an echo. *Moderate; conceptual.*

#### Trajectory

- **What the passage anticipates or longs for:** a meeting-place between heaven and earth that is not a stone, and a bearer of the promise who does not have to steal it.
- **What Christ fulfils:** John 1:51 (the meeting-point); Gal 3:14 (the blessing of Abraham to the nations); Heb 11:20's placing of even Isaac's fumbled blessing inside faith's long look forward.
- **What still remains:** the disciples are told they *will see* — future tense. The full traffic between heaven and earth is not yet in view.

#### Moralism check

- **The "be like X" temptation:** two forms — "grasp God's promises like Jacob", and its mirror, "don't be a schemer like Jacob". Both make a man the subject.
- **The gospel grounding:** 28:13–15 is unconditional and unrequested, spoken over a sleeping fugitive who had lied to his blind father the previous week. That is where the sermon's foundation goes.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** the subject of this passage is the God who chooses before birth and keeps his word after fraud. Jacob is not an example of anything except of what such a God is willing to work with.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**25:23 and Romans 9 — election**

- **Category:** doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** the oracle predates the twins' birth and Paul builds the sharpest paragraph in the NT on it.
- **Common mishandlings:** preaching Rom 9:13's "Esau I hated" as though Genesis said it. **Genesis never does.** The quotation is Malachi 1:2–3, written a thousand years later about two nations, and Genesis has Isaac blessing Esau (27:39–40) and Esau prospering (33:9; 36).
- **Rhetorical function in context:** in Genesis the oracle functions as an *answer to a woman's anguished question* (25:22), not as a doctrinal statement. It explains a pregnancy before it explains a doctrine.
- **Interpretive options:** corporate (two nations, which is what the oracle itself says) or individual (which is how Paul deploys it) — and these are not exclusive.
- **What honest exegesis surfaces:** 25:23 is explicitly about *nations*; Paul's application to individuals is his own move and is legitimate on his own authority. Say both.
- **Suggested handling:** address briefly and accurately; do not make Genesis 25 carry Romans 9's argument.

**27:1–29 — the deception, and what the narrator does not say**

- **Category:** ethical / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** nobody in the passage is rebuked, and the outcome God announced is achieved by fraud.
- **Common mishandlings:** filling the narrator's silence with approval ("Rebekah had faith to act on the oracle"), or with condemnation the text does not supply and then moralising it.
- **Rhetorical function:** the deception's function in the unit is to exhaust the human mechanisms of transmission before Bethel. It is neither commended nor condemned because it is not the point being made.
- **What honest exegesis surfaces:** the consequences are narrated even though the verdict is not. Jacob loses twenty years, his mother, and the household; Rebekah never sees him again; Isaac's last recorded interaction with Esau is a scene of grief. Genesis judges by outcome, quietly.
- **Suggested handling:** acknowledge, and preach the silence as deliberate.

**27:39–40 — is Esau blessed or cursed?**

- **Category:** apologetic / translation-driven.
- **The difficulty:** see Tool 8. The Hebrew preposition is ambiguous and the modern versions and the older tradition have gone opposite ways.
- **Suggested handling:** name the ambiguity in one sentence and do not build on either reading.

**28:18, 22 — a pillar, which Deuteronomy 16:22 forbids**

- **Category:** ethical / doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** לֹא־תָקִים לְךָ מַצֵּבָה אֲשֶׁר שָׂנֵא יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ — "you shall not set up a pillar, which the LORD your God hates." Jacob sets one up, anoints it, and God does not object. `[T]` on both texts.
- **Common mishandlings:** ignoring it; or explaining it away with "the law hadn't been given yet" as though that ended the question.
- **What honest exegesis surfaces:** three points can be made without special pleading. (i) The pillar is not for a rival god — Jacob's problem at 28:20 is which God, not how many. (ii) Deuteronomy's prohibition is Canaanite-cult-specific in its context. (iii) The Torah's own readers held both texts, and the placement of Jacob's pillar at the founding of *Bethel* — later the calf-shrine — is the sort of thing Genesis lets sit without comment. It is a genuine tension, and the honest word is that Genesis narrates and Deuteronomy legislates, and the narrative is not endorsing.
- **Suggested handling:** address briefly if the congregation is likely to notice; do not make it the sermon.

**28:20–22 — is the vow faith or bargaining? And where does the "if" end?**

- **Category:** ethical / pastoral / grammatical.
- **The difficulty:** two questions at once. (i) Does the protasis end at 28:21a, so that "then the LORD will be my God" (21b) is the apodosis — as NASB95 and ESV take it — or does it run through 21b so that the apodosis begins at 22? (ii) Is a vow with an "if" in it an act of faith or an attempt to make terms with God?
- **Rhetorical function:** the vow is the unit's last word, and it is placed immediately after the most unconditional promise in the book so far. That placement is the interpretive datum.
- **What honest exegesis surfaces:** the correspondence is exact and diminishing (Headline 5) — Jacob's vow tracks God's promise clause by clause and asks for less than he was given, with a condition attached. The grammar will not decide the "if"'s extent; the structure suggests that the shape, not the sincerity, is the point.
- **Suggested handling:** do not resolve it into either "Jacob's beautiful faith" or "Jacob the bargainer". Preach the comparison of the two speeches and let the congregation feel the gap.

**Hebrews 11:20 and 12:16–17 — the NT's readings**

- **Category:** apologetic / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** Hebrews credits Isaac with faith in a scene where Genesis shows him deceived (11:20); and Heb 12:17's "he found no place for repentance, though he sought it with tears" is regularly preached as "Esau repented too late and God refused him", which is a hard pastoral word and probably not what the verse says.
- **What honest exegesis surfaces:** on 11:20, Hebrews' point is that Isaac blessed *concerning things to come* — he committed his sons' futures to a promise he would not see, and the blessing stood even though he had the wrong son in front of him. That is faith of a kind Genesis does display (27:33's "yes, and he shall be blessed" is Isaac refusing to revoke). On 12:17, **the feminine αὐτήν has two feminine candidates**, εὐλογίαν and μετανοίας. Genesis 27:34, 38 shows Esau seeking *the blessing* with tears and says nothing of his seeking repentance. The reading "he found no way to reverse [the blessing], though he sought it with tears" fits both the grammar and Genesis.
- **Suggested handling:** if Heb 12:17 comes up, name the ambiguity. Telling a congregation that a man wept for repentance and was refused it is a heavy thing to say on a contested pronoun.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching the stolen blessing as though Jacob stole the covenant

- **What it looks like:** "Jacob stole the Abrahamic promise from his brother."
- **Why it's wrong:** he did not. Read 27:27–29 slowly: dew, fatness, grain, wine, dominion over brothers, and the bless/curse formula. **No land, no seed, no covenant, no God of Abraham.** All of that is at 28:3–4, given by Isaac with his eyes open, to a son he knows is Jacob, and named "the blessing of Abraham". The theft got Jacob a prosperity oracle he then spent twenty years not enjoying, in a foreign country.
- **The corrective:** preach the two blessings side by side. The sermon writes itself: what he grabbed, and what he was given.

### Pitfall 2: "Jacob I loved, Esau I hated" as a quotation from Genesis

- **What it looks like:** "Even before they were born, God said he hated Esau."
- **Why it's wrong:** Genesis 25:23 says nothing of the kind. Romans 9:13 is quoting **Malachi 1:2–3**, written after the exile, about Israel and Edom as nations, in a book whose opening question is "was not Esau Jacob's brother?" Genesis has Isaac bless Esau (27:39–40), has Esau prosper (33:9), and gives him a whole *toledot* of his own (36).
- **The corrective:** cite Malachi as Malachi. If the sermon needs Romans 9, let Romans 9 do its own work rather than back-projecting it into a birth narrative.

### Pitfall 3: The hymnbook version of Jacob's ladder

- **What it looks like:** climbing, ascent, "we are climbing Jacob's ladder", the Christian life as upward progress.
- **Why it's wrong:** nobody climbs anything in Genesis 28. Jacob is asleep. The traffic is angelic and it goes *down* as well as up. The object is a *sullām*, a word that occurs **once in the entire Hebrew Bible**, so "ladder" is a reconstruction; and the LORD is *already at the top* before the vision begins. The passage is about descent to a sleeping man, not ascent by an aspiring one.
- **The corrective:** John 1:51 — the traffic runs over a person, and the disciples are told they will *see* it, not do it.

### Pitfall 4: Rebekah as the woman of faith who acted on God's word

- **What it looks like:** "Rebekah believed the oracle and had the courage to act on it."
- **Why it's wrong:** it is a reading the narrator declines to give. And it does not survive contact with the outcome: her plan works, and it costs her her son for the rest of her life. **Genesis never narrates Rebekah's death** — she has a death-notice for her *nurse* (35:8, with a named burial tree) and none of her own; she is simply mentioned as buried at 49:31. In a book that narrates the deaths of Sarah, Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Rachel and Jacob, that omission is loud.
- **The corrective:** say what she did (she is the only person in the unit who enquires of the LORD, 25:22) and say what it cost, and leave the verdict where the narrator left it.

### Pitfall 5: Preaching Genesis 26 as a filler chapter, or skipping it

- **What it looks like:** running 25:19–34 straight into 27:1, because "chapter 26 is a repeat".
- **Why it's wrong:** it is a repeat, and the repetition is the argument (see Phase 0.6). Without it the reader does not know that Isaac holds the promise, does not know that blessing produces a hundredfold, and does not know that lying about one's wife is what this family does under pressure.
- **The corrective:** if the series has one sermon for the unit, give chapter 26 five minutes as the setup. If it has two, chapter 26 is the first.

### Pitfall 6: Making Jacob's vow the model prayer of commitment

- **What it looks like:** "Like Jacob, let us make our vow to God this morning."
- **Why it's wrong:** the vow asks for less than God has just offered, attaches a condition to a promise that had none, and calls God "the LORD" — not yet "my God" — until the terms are met. Read against 28:13–15 it is a diminishment.
- **The corrective:** put the two speeches in parallel columns and let the congregation see it. Then note that God keeps every clause of his anyway (31:3; 35:1–7), which is the actual good news.

### Pitfall 7: Moralising the whole unit into "don't be a deceiver"

- **What it looks like:** four points on integrity, drawn from the negative example.
- **Why it's wrong:** it makes the subject a man rather than God, and it cannot account for the unit's climax, in which God commits himself to the deceiver without conditions and without rebuke.
- **The corrective:** the overview's moralism check applies directly — *God keeps the promise through people who do not keep faith.* Preach that, and the integrity application arrives on its own, grounded rather than bolted on.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **תָּם at 25:27.** Is the narrator calling Jacob "settled/domestic" (supported by the following participle "dwelling in tents", and by the contrast with Esau the man of the field), or is he flatly ironic, using the Job word for "blameless" of a man about to buy a birthright? Every English version takes the first; the lexical concentration in Job supports the second as at least available. *Recommend BDB/HALOT and a Hebrew-working commentary.*

2. **The מִן of 27:39.** Partitive or privative — the whole content of Esau's blessing turns on it, the ancient and modern traditions have gone opposite ways, and the Hebrew does not decide. *Recommend a grammar on privative min, and the NET translator notes.*

3. **The referent of בּוֹ at 28:12.** "On it" (the *sullām*) or "on him" (Jacob)? The LXX chose the ladder; John 1:51 relocated the traffic to a person; rabbinic tradition preserved the "on him" reading. Nothing in the Hebrew settles it, and it is the difference between a vision of a structure and a vision about a man. *Recommend a commentary that works the Hebrew, and Beale–Carson on John 1:51.*

4. **What the niphal/hithpael alternation means.** The stem question is closed (claim audit) and this unit adds that the stem is inherited from the verse being quoted. What remains open is whether the two formula-families (niphal + "families of the ground" + "in you"; hithpael + "nations of the earth" + "in your seed") carry different theology or are simply two fixed forms of one promise. Galatians 3:8 quotes the passive. *Recommend a commentary on 12:3 and 22:18 that treats the stems together.*

5. **The extent of the protasis at 28:20–21.** Where does the "if" end? NASB95 and ESV put the apodosis at 21b; other traditions put it at 22. The grammar allows both.

6. **Whether the Nuzi material bears on the birthright and blessing scenes.** Frequently asserted in older commentaries and substantially contested since. Flagged `[S]` and *uncertain*; the exegesis above does not depend on it and should not start to.

7. **Whether Jeremiah 9:3 is reading Hosea 12:4 or reading Genesis directly.** Both use the verb *ʿāqab* of the covenant people; the direction of dependence is not settled here.

8. **Isaac never speaks to God.** Genesis records that he *entreated* the LORD (25:21) and that he *called on the name of the LORD* (26:25), but it never quotes a word Isaac addressed to God — where it quotes Abraham at length (15:2–3, 8; 17:18; 18:23–32) and Jacob at length (28:20–22; 32:10–13 [Eng 32:9–12]). The pattern is real and citable; what it means, if anything, is not established here. Routed to Open Questions rather than reported as a finding.

## Book-Overview Tensions

**1. The unit division.** The overview's Preaching Units table gives this material as 25:1–34 / 26:1–35 / 27:1–28:22; the whole-book sweep's §11 gives 25:19–28:22. **The sweep is right, and the overview's own closing note says why** — "the natural seams are the *toledot* headings, not the chapter numbers", and 25:19 is a heading while 25:1 is not. The Masoretic paragraphing in the WLC (setumah after 25:34, 26:33, 26:35, 28:9) further supports the four-panel division used above. *Recommend:* amend the overview's units table to 25:19–28:22 as one ⭐HIGH unit with four preaching panels, and move 25:1–18 (Keturah, Abraham's death, Ishmael's *toledot*) into the preceding unit where it belongs.

**2. The niphal/hithpael alternation.** The overview reports the alternation and rates it "high on the data; moderate on what the alternation means", and the claim audit closed the stem question but left the meaning open. **This unit shows the stem is inherited from the verse being quoted** — 26:4 is 22:18 verbatim (hithpael), 28:14 is 12:3 verbatim plus a clause (niphal) — which moves the question from theology toward citation practice. *Recommend:* record the verbatim-quotation finding in the overview's Christological Trajectory item 4 and in the Early↔Late table.

**3. Trap 5 needs a footnote, not a correction.** The overview states that שָׂטָן does not occur in Genesis, which is correct for lemma 7854. **The root ś-ṭ-n is nonetheless present in this unit**, as the well-name *Śiṭnāh* at 26:21, a hapax as tagged. Nothing about the trap changes; the footnote prevents a congregant with a concordance from thinking the overview overstated.

**4. Bethel's first canonical occurrence.** Any statement that Genesis 28:10–22 is Bethel's first appearance is wrong: 12:8 and 13:3 precede it, and the fact that Jacob is sleeping at his grandfather's altar-site is one of the best things in the passage. *Recommend:* add 12:8/13:3 → 28:19 to the overview's Early↔Late table as a **Replants** relation, high confidence.

**5. Four extensions offered to the Early↔Late table**, all verified here: *mirmāh* 27:35 ↔ 34:13 (two occurrences in Genesis); *śāṭam* 27:41 ↔ 49:23 ↔ 50:15 (three in Genesis, and 50:15 is the fear 50:19–21 answers); מַלְאֲכֵי אֱלֹהִים + *pāgaʿ* 28:11–12 ↔ 32:1–2 (bracketing the exile); and 21:22–34 ↔ 26:26–33 (Abimelech, Phicol, oath, wells, Beersheba — the same scene twice).
