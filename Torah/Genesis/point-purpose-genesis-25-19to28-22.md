## Phase 0 — the scope decision

**Four sermons, one per panel.** At 119 verses the unit is roughly twice the length at which the skill's rule of thumb makes a span a series, and the decision is not merely arithmetic: **the unit's own paragraphing divides it into exactly these four panels.** The Masoretic *setumah* markers in the WLC fall after 25:34, 26:33, 26:35 and 28:9 — which is to say the text itself marks the breaks the series will use. `[T]`

| | Verses | Working title | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 25:19–34 | Before they had done anything | 16 vv |
| **2** | 26:1–35 | On someone else's obedience | 35 vv |
| **3** | 27:1–28:9 | The blessing he did not need to steal | 55 vv |
| **4** | 28:10–22 | The house of God, and a man asleep | 13 vv |

**The overlap, named.** The whole-book sweep's §11 recorded a different decision for this unit — *"Load-bearer: 28:10–22. Decision: Focus (grasping 25–27 makes Bethel land)"* — i.e. one sermon with the weight at Bethel. That remains a good single-sermon plan and is preserved as the alternative split below. Four sermons buys three things the focus plan cannot: chapter 26 gets preached instead of summarised (and it is the chapter that supplies the gospel grammar — see Sermon 2); the two-blessings finding gets the room it needs; and the congregation hears the choice announced (25:23) a fortnight before they hear it honoured (28:13–15), which is the series' whole argument about time.

**The unit's own boundaries.** 25:19 is a *toledot* heading and 25:1 is not; 28:22 is the last word before Jacob's feet leave the land at 29:1. Preaching 25:1–18 with this unit would pull Keturah, Abraham's death and Ishmael's *toledot* across a structural seam — they belong with what precedes. *(This is the one place where the series diverges from the book overview's preaching-units table; see the report's Book-Overview Tensions.)*

## Fallen Condition Focus

> **We try to secure by our own management what God has already promised to give us — and then we are afraid that the way we went about it has cost us the thing itself.**

Anchored, not imported. The two halves are both in the text:

- **The grasping** — Jacob buys a birthright he had already been promised (25:23 precedes 25:31 by eight verses) and steals a blessing that turns out not to contain the covenant at all (27:27–29 against 28:3–4).
- **The fear** — Jacob's vow at 28:20–22 is God's own promise of 28:13–15 shrunk clause by clause and hedged with an "if". A man who is sure of a promise does not restate it as terms.

Shared with the first hearers: people holding a promise made to ancestors, asking whether it had survived the generations, and — after 28:10 — whether being outside the land meant it had been forfeited. `[T]` on the content of 28:15, `[I]` on the felt weight.

Where it differs: this side of the resurrection the promise is not merely reaffirmed to a fugitive but secured in a Son who did not grasp. The preacher should know that is where the trajectory goes and should not import it before Sermon 4.

## Series Teaching Point

> **The promise of God crosses a generation not on the fitness of the people carrying it but on the faithfulness of the God who gives it — announced before the twins have done anything, and handed over whole and unconditional to the fugitive who has done everything wrong.**

**Subject:** what makes God's promise secure as it passes to the next generation?
**Complement:** the God who gives it, not the people who handle it.

*Derived from the convergence of Headline Findings 1, 4 and 5 with the report's Positional Necessity Check, which is the strongest convergence in the unit: the narrator removes, in order, every human mechanism for transmitting the promise — primogeniture (25:23), paternal discernment (27:23), maternal management (27:5–17) and the heir's own initiative (25:31; 27:19) — and then has God give it anyway.*

---

## Series Purpose Statement

> **To move hearers from securing God's promise to receiving it** — freeing the believer who fears his own record has forfeited it, disarming the one who is still working for what has already been given, warning the hearer who would trade it for one meal, and showing the outsider a God who binds himself to people who cannot be trusted with him.

Drawn from the report's So What (Stage 1: "that the reader would stop looking for the reason God kept this promise in the people who carried it"). Each clause names a change in a hearer, and each is reachable by this passage:

| Audience | The change this series seeks |
|---|---|
| The believer with a bad record | To stop reading the past as a disqualification — God spoke *first* at Bethel, unasked, and said nothing about the week before |
| The grasper | To stop working for what is already given, and to count what the working has cost — twenty years, a mother, a household |
| The Esau in the room | To be warned, kindly and plainly, that a birthright can be traded for one meal, and that Hebrews thought this worth saying to a church |
| The outsider | To see that the Bible's claim is not that God's people earned his commitment but that he made it before they could and kept it after they had spoiled it |

---

## Candidate Big Question

> **"What am I still trying to get hold of that God has already promised to give me — and has the way I went about it put it out of reach?"**

Marked *candidate*. It carries both halves of the FCF and the series answers it in two movements: sermons 1–3 show the grasping and its cost; sermon 4 answers the second half with an unconditional promise and no rebuke. The final wording is the preacher's, or the downstream one-sentence step's.

A shorter pulpit form, if the longer one will not carry: **"What are you working for that has already been given to you?"**

## The Four Sermons

### Sermon 1 · Genesis 25:19–34 — *Before they had done anything*

**Teaching Point.** *God settles which son will carry the promise before either twin has drawn breath, and nothing either of them does afterwards alters it.*
**Subject:** when was this decided? **Complement:** before they were born, by God alone, and in answer to a question a woman asked in pain.

**Function in the series.** It establishes that the choice precedes the conduct. Without this week, the next three become a morality tale — and the congregation will spend sermon 3 asking whether Jacob deserved it, which is the question the text has already refused.

**Structure**

1. **25:19–23 · Twenty years, and an answer nobody earned.** A barren wife, a praying husband, and — note it — *Rebekah is the only person in this whole unit who asks God a question* (25:22). The answer she gets is not about her pain; it is about two nations.
2. **25:24–28 · Two boys, and a house already divided.** The narrator gives inside information before anyone acts: "Isaac loved Esau… but Rebekah loved Jacob" (25:28). Everything in sermon 3 is set up here.
3. **25:29–34 · The bowl.** Not a rash moment but a completed contract — *sell me* (v.31), *swear to me* (v.33), *and he swore*, *and he sold*. Then the one verdict the narrator gives in this sermon: "Thus Esau despised his birthright."

**Aim.** That the congregation leaves unable to read the rest of the series as a story about deserving.

**Where Christ comes in.** Romans 9:10–13 takes its stand on 25:23 — and Paul's point ("though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad") is not his inference but Genesis's own placement of the oracle, before verse 24. What Paul does with it there, he does with us at Ephesians 1:4: chosen *in him* before the foundation of the world. Category: **trajectory**, not typology — flag it as such.

**Pulpit note (ESV).** 25:27, "Jacob was a quiet man": the Hebrew is *tām*, the word rendered "blameless" of Job (Job 1:1, 8; 2:3). Say what the word is; do not correct the pew Bible from the pulpit. Whether the narrator means "settled and domestic" or is being flatly ironic is genuinely open, and the report leaves it open.

### Sermon 2 · Genesis 26:1–35 — *On someone else's obedience*

**Teaching Point.** *The promise is confirmed to Isaac on his father's obedience rather than his own, and it proves visible, costly and wholly undeserved in the life of a man who tells the same lie his father told.*
**Subject:** on what footing does the second generation hold the promise? **Complement:** on Abraham's obedience — which is the grammar the gospel will later use.

**Function in the series.** This is the chapter congregations get robbed of, and it does four jobs the deception scene needs (report, Phase 0.6): it shows that Isaac genuinely holds what Jacob will steal; that blessing is a real, visible thing; that deceit is already the house style; and — three verses before Rebekah says "obey my voice" — that God has just commended Abraham in that exact formula.

**Structure**

1. **26:1–5 · A word in the famine.** "Do not go down to Egypt" — the command Abraham never received and never obeyed (12:10). And the reason given for the whole promise: "because Abraham obeyed my voice and kept my charge."
2. **26:6–11 · The same lie, one generation on.** He passes the test his father failed, and fails the test his father failed. The scene turns on his own name: Abimelech looks out and sees *Isaac laughing with Rebekah* (26:8).
3. **26:12–22 · Blessing that outlasts opposition.** A hundredfold in a famine year; wells filled in; expulsion; Esek, Sitnah — *contention* and *hostility* — and then Rehoboth, "for now the LORD has made room for us".
4. **26:23–33 · What the outsiders saw.** "We see plainly that the LORD has been with you" (26:28) — and the same Abimelech had said the same thing to Abraham at 21:22.

**Aim.** To move the hearer off his own record and onto another's — and to let him see that this is not a loophole in the covenant but its pattern.

**Where Christ comes in.** 26:5 is the sermon's gospel hinge and it is not a stretch: *a promise confirmed to one person on the basis of another person's obedience* is the grammar of Romans 5:19 — "by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous." Category: **trajectory**, stated as such; the text is not predicting Christ here, it is establishing the shape into which Christ will step.

**Pulpit note (ESV).** At 26:8 the ESV is the better text: "saw Isaac **laughing** with Rebekah" keeps the pun on his name (*Yiṣḥāq* / *məṣaḥēq*) where the NASB95's "caressing" gives the sense and loses the joke — and the joke is the mechanism of the scene. Use the pew Bible here and say why.

### Sermon 3 · Genesis 27:1–28:9 — *The blessing he did not need to steal*

**Teaching Point.** *What Jacob seized was never the covenant — the blessing of Abraham was not available to theft, and his father hands it to him in daylight sixty verses later.*
**Subject:** what did the theft actually get him? **Complement:** a prosperity oracle; the covenant could only ever be given.

**Function in the series.** The hinge, and the hardest hour. It is also where the series earns its keep, because the finding that governs it is not visible without the exegetical work: read 27:27–29 slowly and it contains dew, fatness, grain, wine, dominion over brothers and the bless-and-curse formula of 12:3 — **no land, no seed, no covenant, no God of Abraham.** All of that is at 28:3–4, named "the blessing of Abraham", given by a father who knows exactly who is in front of him.

**Structure**

1. **27:1–17 · A house at cross purposes.** Four people, four plans, and the only thing the text says Isaac loves is the food: "such as I love" (v.4), "such as he loves" (v.9), "such as his father loved" (v.14). In a chapter about a father's blessing, the verb *love* attaches to dinner three times and to a son not at all.
2. **27:18–29 · Four questions and a one-word lie.** "Who are you, my son?" (v.18) · "whether you are really my son Esau or not" (v.21) · "Are you really my son Esau?" (v.24) · "Who are you?" (v.32). The second answer is a single word: *I am.* Then — and this is the sermon's turn — **read the blessing and say what is not in it.**
3. **27:30–45 · What it cost.** Isaac trembles a great trembling; Esau cries a great and bitter cry — the same construction, one verse apart. Then a grudge, a flight, and a mother who says "I will send and bring you from there" (27:45) and never does.
4. **27:46–28:9 · The blessing of Abraham, in daylight.** "God Almighty bless you and make you fruitful and multiply you… May he give the blessing of Abraham to you and to your offspring with you, that you may take possession of the land."

**Aim.** To stop the hearer working for what has already been given him — and to name honestly what the working costs, without turning the sermon into a warning about dishonesty.

**Where Christ comes in.** Galatians 3:14: "that **the blessing of Abraham** might come to the Gentiles in Christ Jesus." Paul uses the exact phrase Isaac uses at 28:4, and what could not be seized in Genesis 27 is distributed by gift in Galatians 3. That is the strongest Christ-connection in the series after Bethel, and it is verbal, not merely conceptual. *(The contrast with Philippians 2:6, "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped", is conceptual only — there is no verbal link. Use it if it helps; flag it as illustration, not exegesis.)*

**Pulpit note (ESV).** Two, and they pull opposite ways.

- **27:8, 13, 43 — a gift.** The ESV reads "obey my voice" at all three, and "Abraham obeyed my voice" at 26:5 — so **the congregation can hear the chain straight from the pew Bible: God says it of Abraham, and Rebekah says it three times to Jacob.** The NASB95 levels two of the four ("obeyed Me" at 26:5; "listen to me" at 27:8), so here the study text is the weaker witness. Read the four together and let the ESV do the work.
- **27:39 — a warning.** The ESV reads "**away from** the fatness of the earth shall your dwelling be", which the congregation will hear as a curse. The KJV reads it the other way ("thy dwelling shall be the fatness of the earth"), and so, most naturally, does the LXX. The Hebrew preposition will not decide between them. Name the ambiguity in one sentence and build nothing on either reading.

### Sermon 4 · Genesis 28:10–22 — *The house of God, and a man asleep*

**Teaching Point.** *God comes down to a fugitive who is not looking for him, gives him the whole promise unasked and without one word of rebuke, and remains his God when the man's answer is a set of terms.*
**Subject:** what does God do with the man who has just spoiled everything? **Complement:** he comes down to him and gives him the lot.

**Function in the series.** The landing. Everything the previous three weeks have removed — birth order, a father's judgement, a mother's management, the boy's own scheming — is gone, and the promise arrives anyway, in the one place the man had no way of arranging.

**Structure**

1. **28:10–12 · A stone, a sunset, and a place he does not know he knows.** He "came to a certain place" — and the reader knows what Jacob does not: his grandfather built an altar there (12:8; 13:3–4). The *sullām* is the only occurrence of that word in the whole Hebrew Bible, and nobody in the passage climbs it.
2. **28:13–15 · The promise, in his grandfather's own words.** "Like the dust of the earth" is Abram's simile from 13:16; "in you… shall all the families of the earth be blessed" is 12:3 word for word. There is no condition in it and no rebuke, and it ends: "I will not leave you until I have done what I have promised you."
3. **28:16–19 · Fear, a pillar, a name.** "Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it" — the right category, and the wrong object. He makes a marker of a stone.
4. **28:20–22 · The vow that asks for less than it has been given.** Set it against verses 13–15 clause by clause: *I am with you* → *if God will be with me*; *I will keep you wherever you go* → *and will keep me in this way that I go*; *I will bring you back to this land* → *I come again to my father's house*; and against land, offspring and blessing for all the families of the earth — **bread to eat and clothing to wear.**

**Aim.** To press an unconditional promise on people who are still making terms with God, and to send them from the place to the person.

**Where Christ comes in.** John 1:51, and it is the strongest link in the unit: *"you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man."* John uses the same two verbs as the Greek of Genesis 28:12, in the same order, and changes the referent from the ladder to a person. **Jacob had the right category and the wrong object** — the meeting-point of heaven and earth was always going to be someone. Category: **typology**, passing all four tests in the report (theological category, NT precedent, escalation, authorial pattern). Preach it as arrival, not as decoding.

**Pulpit note (ESV).** 28:12 "a ladder": the Hebrew word occurs once in the entire Bible, so "ladder" is a reconstruction and "stairway" is another. Nothing in the text is climbed, and the LORD is already at the top before the vision begins. If the congregation has "We are climbing Jacob's ladder" in its ears, say so kindly and move on.

## Alternative splits

**Two sermons.** 25:19–26:35 (*the promise confirmed, the family exposed*) and 27:1–28:22 (*the blessing seized and the blessing given*). Keeps the two-blessings finding as the spine of the second, but asks one sermon to carry 68 verses and pushes Bethel to the last ten minutes.

**One sermon, focused at Bethel** — the whole-book sweep's own decision. 25:19–28:9 becomes a fast-moving run-up in the introduction and the weight sits on 28:10–22. Use this if the series diary will only take one Sunday. The Teaching Point becomes the series TP above, unchanged; the FCF, PS and Big Question all transfer without alteration; the structure becomes movements 1–4 of Sermon 4 with the grasping compressed into movement 1.

## Keep honest — series-wide

Drawn from the report's Preaching Pitfalls. Four at series level; the sermon-specific ones sit with their sermons above.

**1. "Jacob I loved, Esau I hated" is Malachi, not Genesis.** *(Sermons 1 and 3.)* Romans 9:13 quotes Malachi 1:2–3 — written a thousand years later, about two nations, in a book whose opening question is "Was not Esau Jacob's brother?" Genesis has Isaac bless Esau (27:39–40), has Esau prosper (33:9), and gives him a *toledot* of his own (36). Cite Malachi as Malachi.

**2. Nobody climbs anything at Bethel.** *(Sermon 4.)* Jacob is asleep. The traffic is angelic and goes down as well as up, and the LORD is at the top before the vision starts. The hymn has taught two generations to read this as a passage about ascent; it is a passage about descent to a sleeping man.

**3. Rebekah is not the woman of faith who acted on the oracle.** *(Sermon 3.)* It is a reading the narrator declines to give, and it does not survive contact with the outcome. She is the only person in the unit who enquires of the LORD (25:22), and her plan works, and it costs her her son for the rest of her life — **Genesis never narrates her death.** Her nurse gets a death-notice and a named burial tree (35:8); Rebekah gets a mention in a burial list (49:31). In a book that narrates the deaths of Sarah, Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Rachel and Jacob, the omission is loud. Say what she did and say what it cost, and leave the verdict where the narrator left it.

**4. Neither "be a Jacob" nor "don't be a Jacob".** *(All four.)* Both make a man the subject of a passage whose subject is God's fidelity to a family that cannot be trusted with it. The overview's own moralism check is the line to hold: *God keeps the promise through people who do not keep faith.* Preach that, and the integrity application arrives grounded instead of bolted on.

## Anti-moralism gate — checked

Run on the series TP, each sermon TP, the PS and the structure.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| **Is the payoff "be like X" or "trust the Christ whom X points to"?** | **Passes.** No sermon TP has a human subject. The four subjects are: God settles (1), the promise is confirmed on another's obedience (2), the covenant could only be given (3), God comes down (4). Sermon 3 is the one at risk — an integrity sermon is the obvious wrong turn — and its TP is deliberately framed around what the theft *failed* to get rather than around the theft |
| **Is Christ the subject, or merely an example?** | **Passes.** Christ is the actor in sermons 2 (Rom 5:19 — his obedience, not ours), 3 (Gal 3:14 — the blessing distributed in him) and 4 (John 1:51 — he is the meeting-point). Sermon 1's connection is to God's electing act, with Christ as the sphere of it (Eph 1:4), which is the honest level and is flagged as trajectory |
| **Is the good news grounded in Christ's saving work, not just information about God's character?** | **Passes, with one watch-point.** Sermons 1 and 4 could both be preached as "God is gracious" without a cross in them. The series guards this by placing 26:5 in week 2 — a promise held on another's obedience — so that by week 4 the congregation already has the category, and Bethel's unconditional promise lands on a foundation rather than on a sentiment |

**One further check, specific to this unit.** The report's Headline 1 is a *negative* finding — what the stolen blessing does **not** contain. A negative finding preaches badly if it is left negative. Sermon 3's structure therefore ends on 28:3–4, the positive: the thing itself, given.

## Hand-off

The Big Question and the four structures above are the inputs to the manuscript stage. Nothing downstream is required for this plan to be usable.

Two things worth settling before the first manuscript:

1. **Which Sunday the series starts.** Sermon 1 is the shortest and does the most structural work; if the congregation is new to Genesis it may want ten minutes of the book's shape (the *toledot* spine, the *bārak*/*ʾārar* pressure) in its introduction.
2. **Whether Hebrews 12:16–17 is preached in Sermon 1 or held back.** It is the NT's own verdict on the bowl of stew and belongs there — but its second clause ("he found no place for repentance, though he sought it with tears") is contested: the feminine pronoun has two feminine candidates in the Greek, and Genesis shows Esau seeking *the blessing* with tears and says nothing about his seeking repentance. If it is used, use the first clause and name the ambiguity in the second, or leave it for the manuscript stage to resolve.
