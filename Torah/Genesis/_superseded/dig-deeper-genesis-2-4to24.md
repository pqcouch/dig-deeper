## Headline Findings

1. **The man does the naming God deliberately declined to do in chapter 1 — and the two accounts are therefore one argument, not two versions of one.** `[T]` *verified* Surfaced by: Vocabulary (Tool 7), Structure (Tool 3), Positional Necessity, Copycat (Tool 13), and the companion run on 1:1–2:3. *qārāʾ* occurs **five times in 1:1–2:3, every one of them God's**, and stops dead after 1:10 — he names day, night, heaven, earth, seas, and not one creature. It occurs **four times in 2:4–24, every one of them the man's** (2:19 ×2, 2:20, 2:23). The gap in chapter 1 is the shape of the delegation chapter 2 executes.

2. **The man's job description at 2:15 is, in the Torah's own vocabulary, a Levitical one.** `[T]` *verified* Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Biblical Theology, Original Audience, Schnittjer step 6. *ʿābad* and *šāmar* stand together as a task assignment in the Pentateuch at **Gen 2:15 and then only at Num 3:7, 3:8, 8:26 and 18:7** — every one of those of the Levites serving and guarding the tabernacle. The Eden-as-sanctuary reading does not need an ANE parallel; it is carried by a word-pair the Torah uses nowhere else for anything else.

3. **God does not *form* the woman. He *builds* her — out of a word that is architecture everywhere else in the Torah.** `[T]` *verified* Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Original Languages, Tool 3. The man and the animals are both *formed* (*yāṣar*, 2:7, 2:19) from the ground; the woman alone is **built** (*bānāh*, 2:22), and what she is built from is a *ṣēlāʿ* — which occurs 41 times in the Hebrew Bible, **twice here and 19 times in Exodus of the tabernacle's sides**, plus 7 in 1 Kings and 11 in Ezekiel of the temple's. Add the garden's three materials at 2:12 — gold, bdellium and *šōham* — and the sanctuary vocabulary is running under the whole passage. ***šōham* is the stone of the high priest's ephod** (Exod 25:7; 28:9, 20; 35:9, 27; 39:6, 13).

4. **"It is not good" is the passage's hinge, and it is aimed at chapter 1.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tone, Positional Necessity. The previous unit says *ṭôv* seven times and *ṭôv məʾōd* once. Fifteen verses later comes the Bible's first ***lōʾ-ṭôv*** (2:18) — and what is not good is not a defect in creation but a solitude God has not yet finished addressing. The verse divides the passage in two and the second half exists to answer it.

5. **The compound name *YHWH ʾĕlōhîm* is the garden narrative's signature, and the count is exact.** `[T]` *verified* Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Tool 11 Move 4. It occurs **28 times in Genesis**, of which **19 fall in 2:4–3:24** (11 in this passage) and only nine in the remaining forty-seven chapters — six of those nine in chapter 24. The narrator is welding the covenant name to the creator name for exactly the length of the garden story, and for almost nowhere else in the book.

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

**Section.** Torah, first book; the passage that opens the *toledot* spine. `[T]`

**Reading implication.** 2:4 carries **the first of Genesis's eleven *toledot* headings** — verified in the overview by lemma (13 occurrences of 8435, 11 of them structural). Everything from here to 50:26 is organised by that formula; 1:1–2:3 stood outside it. So this passage is where the book's own architecture begins, and its heading looks *backwards* ("the generations of the heavens and the earth") before every later heading looks forwards to a family. `[T]`

**Presupposes.** Only 1:1–2:3 — and that is the whole canonical furniture available. What it presupposes and uses: the creator, the good creation, the mandate, and the naming God left undone.

**Handoff.** To 2:25–3:24, joined by the *ʿărûmmîm*/*ʿārûm* pun and by the command of 2:16–17 that has nothing to do until the serpent quotes it.

**Neighbours.** 1:1–2:3 precedes; 3:1–24 follows. Remove this passage and chapter 3 has no garden, no command, no tree, and no woman.

**Canonical / compositional divergence.** None at passage level.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** `[S: overview]` Genesis asks whether the blessing can outrun the curse and through whom, narrowing from earth to one man to one seed.

**Passage-level.** This unit supplies the *specifics* that chapter 1 gave only in outline, and it supplies them in the order the following chapter will need them: a place (the garden), a vocation (serve and keep), a word (the command), a naming authority (delegated), and a partner. `[T]` Chapter 1 said humanity was made in God's image to rule; chapter 2 shows what that looks like on the ground, and it looks like a priest in a garden under a word.

**The relation between the two accounts is the interpretive question, and the text answers it structurally rather than by assertion.** `[I]` The second account does not repeat the first; it fills four gaps the first deliberately left — the naming of the creatures, the *place*, the *command*, and the woman as a distinct act. A reading that treats them as rival versions has to explain why the second supplies exactly what the first withheld. *Moderate–high confidence.*

---

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement (1:1–2:3).** Humanity made in God's image, blessed, mandated to be fruitful and to rule; God named the realms on days one to three and then named nothing else; the week arrived at a blessed, sanctified, unclosed seventh day. `[T]`

**Necessity answer — why this passage, here?** Because chapter 1 left four things open that the story cannot proceed without, and this passage closes all four:

1. **The naming.** God stopped naming at 1:10. The creatures are unnamed for nine verses of chapter 1 and remain so until 2:19–20, when the man names them. The delegation is the point, and it is executed *here*. `[T]` *verified*
2. **The place.** Chapter 1 has no location in it at all — no direction, no geography, no place-name. This passage has eight proper nouns in five verses (2:8–14). The move from a world to a garden is the move from cosmos to covenant ground.
3. **The command.** Chapter 3's disobedience requires a prohibition, and chapter 1 gives none. 2:16–17 supplies it. Without this passage the fall is a snack.
4. **The partner.** 1:27 says "male and female he created them" without narrating it. 2:18–24 narrates it, and in doing so produces the only "not good" in the creation account.

**Implication.** The passage is not a second creation account competing with the first; it is the first account's **unfinished business**, and reading it that way changes what the sermon is about. Its subject is not *how* humanity was made but *what humanity was made for* — vocation, word, and relationship, in that order.

#### General context

**Immediately before:** 2:1–3, the sanctified seventh day.
**Immediately after:** 2:25, the janus verse (see the boundary note above), then the serpent.
**Section:** the first *toledot* panel, 2:4–4:26, of which this is the opening movement.

**Commonly read out of context.** 2:24 is the most-quoted verse in the passage and is regularly preached as a standalone marriage text. In context it is the **narrator's aside** — the only sentence in the passage that leaves the garden and addresses the reader's own world — and it draws its conclusion (*ʿal-kēn*, "for this reason") from the *manner of the woman's making*. The verse is an inference from 2:21–23, not a free-standing ordinance.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 2:4 | **Heading** | The first *toledot*. Looks back over 1:1–2:3 and opens the spine |
| 2:5–7 | **The double lack, and the forming** | Two "not yet"s (no shrub, no plant) with two causes (no rain, no man); answered by the man formed from the ground |
| 2:8–14 | **The garden, and where it is** | Planted, the man placed, two trees named; then a river, four heads, and eight proper nouns |
| 2:15–17 | **Vocation and word** | Placed *to serve and to keep*; permitted everything but one; the penalty stated |
| 2:18–20 | **The single "not good", and the search** | The solitude named; the animals formed and named; no *ʿēzer* found |
| 2:21–24 | **The building, the acclamation, and the aside** | Deep sleep; the *ṣēlāʿ* built into a woman; the man's only speech; the narrator's "for this reason" |

**Device: a problem–solution pattern, run twice, the second time with a delay.** 2:5's lack is answered within two verses (2:7). 2:18's lack is *not* — the narrative goes through the whole animal creation and a naming ceremony before the answer arrives at 2:22. `[T]` The delay is deliberate and it is doing work: the search has to fail before the gift can land, and 2:20's "but for Adam there was not found a helper suitable for him" is the hinge on which the second half turns.

**The passage is framed by ground.** *ʾădāmāh* opens it (2:5, twice implied by the lack) and the man is taken *from* it (2:7), the trees grow *from* it (2:9), the animals are formed *from* it (2:19) — and then, at 2:21–22, God stops using the ground. The woman alone is made from something already living. `[T]` *verified — ʾădāmāh occurs 5× in the passage, at 2:5, 6, 7, 9, 19, and not once after 2:19.*

---

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative Hebrew; few connectors, and three that matter:

- **2:5 *kî* ("for")** — the causal clause explaining the double "not yet": no rain, **and** no man to work the ground. The absence of the man is given as a *cause* of the earth's barrenness, on a level with the absence of rain. `[T]` That is a striking thing to say fifteen verses after a chapter in which the earth brought forth vegetation without any man at all, and it sets up 2:15.
- **2:17 *kî*** — grounding the prohibition in its penalty: do not eat, **for** in the day you eat you shall surely die.
- **2:24 *ʿal-kēn* ("for this reason")** — the passage's only inferential connector and its only address to the reader. It draws a conclusion about all marriages from the single event of 2:21–23. `[T]` Everything the verse claims rests on that inference, which is why the manner of the woman's making is not a decorative detail.

---

### 5. Parallels

Not poetry — but **2:23 is the passage's one poetic line, and it is the first recorded human speech in the Bible.** `[T]`

> זֹ֣את הַפַּ֗עַם עֶ֚צֶם מֵֽעֲצָמַ֔י
> וּבָשָׂ֖ר מִבְּשָׂרִ֑י
> לְזֹאת֙ יִקָּרֵ֣א אִשָּׁ֔ה כִּ֥י מֵאִ֖ישׁ לֻֽקֳחָה־זֹּֽאת

Synonymous parallelism in the first two cola (bone from my bones ‖ flesh from my flesh), then a third colon that turns to naming and supplies the wordplay *ʾiššāh* / *ʾîš*. `[T]` The line is bracketed by *zōʾt* — "**this** one, this time… **this** one was taken" — the demonstrative used three times, which after 2:20's fruitless survey of every creature reads as recognition rather than description.

Elsewhere the patterning is narrative rather than parallel.

---

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Four intrusions, and they carry disproportionate weight:

- **2:4's heading** — the narrator's structural signpost, not part of the action. `[T]`
- **2:10–14, the river and the four heads** — a geographical excursus in the present tense ("a river *flows*", "it *flows around*"), stepping outside the narrative's time to locate the garden in the reader's world. `[T]` The narrator wants this garden mapped, not mythologised.
- **2:12 "the gold of that land is good"** — an evaluative aside about a place, using the chapter-1 evaluation word. `[T]`
- **2:24 "For this reason a man shall leave…"** — the decisive one. `[T]` The narrator steps out of Eden entirely and speaks about the reader's own institution, in the imperfect, as a standing pattern. It is the only sentence in the passage not about the garden, and it is the sentence Jesus and Paul both quote.

**Distinguish narrator from character.** 2:23 is the *man's* speech and 2:24 is the *narrator's* — a distinction the NASB95's paragraphing makes easy to miss. Jesus attributes 2:24 to the Creator (Matt 19:4–5, "he who created… said"), which is a claim about the narrator's authority, not a claim that Adam said it. `[T]` / `[S: NT]`

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory.** Eden, Pishon, Havilah, Gihon, Cush, Tigris (Heb. Hiddekel), Assyria, Euphrates — **eight, all clustered in 2:8–14** — plus the divine name. `[T]` The contrast with the previous unit is total: 1:1–2:3 has **no proper nouns whatever**. Triage: *Eden* is load-bearing (below); the four rivers and their lands are the narrator's locating apparatus and are treated together; *Assyria* and *Euphrates* carry forward-freight (the empire that will destroy Samaria; the river named as the covenant boundary at 15:18) but are not doing work *here* — inventory only, and flagged rather than developed, because a hyperlink that is not load-bearing does not get the load-the-story treatment.

**(a) *ʾādām* / *ʾădāmāh* (120 / 127) — the passage's leading wordplay.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* *ʾādām* 15×, *ʾădāmāh* 5×, and **2:5 puts them in one clause**: "there was no *ʾādām* to work the *ʾădāmāh*". The man is named for the stuff he is made of and the job he is given, and the pun is the passage's spine. It also sets the fuse for 3:17–19 and 3:23, where the same pair returns under a curse. English cannot carry it; "man from the ground" loses the sound entirely, and the preacher should say it aloud in Hebrew once.

**(b) *ʿābad* (5647) and *šāmar* (8104) — "to serve" and "to keep", 2:15.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* This is the passage's strongest lexical finding. **As a paired task assignment the two verbs occur in the Pentateuch at Gen 2:15 and then only at Num 3:7, 3:8, 8:26 and 18:7** — all four of the Levites' duty at the tabernacle: to keep guard and to do the service. Swept by lemma across all five books. The other Torah verses containing both words (Deut 11:16; 12:30; 13:5) use them in unrelated senses ("take care lest you serve other gods"), and are excluded. *What it adds:* Eden is described as a sanctuary and the man as its priest **in the Torah's own technical vocabulary**, without any appeal to ANE parallels — and for a reader who holds Genesis and Numbers together, which the Torah's audience does, the description is not subtle.

**(c) *ṣēlāʿ* (6763) — "rib", or "side", 2:21, 2:22.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence on the distribution; moderate on what it implies.* 41 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible: **2 here, 19 in Exodus, 7 in 1 Kings, 11 in Ezekiel, 1 each in 2 Samuel and Job.** Every occurrence outside Genesis is architectural — the *sides* of the ark and the altar, the boards of the tabernacle, the chambers of the temple. "Rib" is a possible anatomical sense but it is not the word's home; "side" is. Combined with *bānāh* (below), the picture is of God taking a *side* and constructing.

**(d) *bānāh* (1129) — "built", 2:22.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* One occurrence, and it is the only creative verb in the passage not used of the man or the animals. God *forms* (*yāṣar*, 2:7, 2:19) the man and the beasts from the ground, like a potter; he **builds** the woman, like a builder, from living material. The verbal distinction is exact and it is invisible in most sermons.

**(e) *ʿēzer* (5828) — "helper", 2:18, 2:20.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* Swept across the whole Hebrew Bible: **21 occurrences.** Two are here. Of the other nineteen, **the great majority are of God as helper** — Exod 18:4 (Eliezer, "the God of my father was my help"); Deut 33:7, 26, 29; Ps 20:3; 33:20; 70:6; 89:20; 115:9, 10, 11; 121:1, 2; 124:8; 146:5. The remainder (Isa 30:5; Ezek 12:14; Hos 13:9; Dan 11:34) are of military or national help. **There is not one occurrence in the canon where *ʿēzer* denotes a subordinate assistant.** The qualifier *kənegdô* — "as opposite him", "corresponding to him" — supplies the correspondence the word alone does not. *What the pair does not settle:* the question of role or order, which this passage does not raise and which the report will not import.

**(f) *ṭôv* (2896) — and the first *lōʾ-ṭôv*.** `[T]` *verified* Five occurrences here (2:9 ×2, 2:12, 2:17, 2:18), against seven in 1:1–2:3. 2:18's **"it is not good"** is the first negative evaluation in Scripture, and it is spoken by God about a state of affairs he has himself brought about and not yet completed. `[I]` *That reading — a deliberate incompleteness rather than a fault — is an inference; what is `[T]` is that God says it and then acts to remedy it.*

**(g) *yāṣar* (3335) — "formed", 2:7, 2:8, 2:19.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* The potter's verb, used of the man **and of the animals** (2:19), both "from the ground". The man's distinction in this passage is therefore not his material and not his forming; it is the breath (2:7) and the vocation (2:15) and the naming authority (2:19). Preaching human uniqueness from the *dust* gets it exactly backwards.

**(h) *nəšāmāh* (5397) — "breath", 2:7.** `[T]` One occurrence, and it is what the animals do not receive. *High confidence on the count; the theological weight is carried by the contrast with 2:19, not by the word alone.*

**(i) *tardēmāh* (8639) — "deep sleep", 2:21.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* Seven occurrences in the canon; **the only other one in Genesis is 15:12**, the deep sleep that falls on Abram while God alone passes between the pieces. Both of Genesis's *tardēmôt* are moments when God acts unilaterally and the man is unconscious for it. *Moderate confidence that the link is designed; the distribution is exact either way.*

**(j) *qārāʾ* (7121) — "called", 2:19 ×2, 2:20, 2:23.** `[T]` *verified* *High confidence.* Four occurrences, **all of them the man's**. See Headline 1.

**(k) The garden's three materials, 2:12 — gold, *bədōlaḥ*, *šōham*.** `[T]` *verified* *High on the distributions; moderate on the inference.* ***šōham* (7718) occurs 11 times: here, then Exod 25:7; 28:9, 20; 35:9, 27; 39:6, 13 — all of the ephod and breastpiece — plus Ezek 28:13, Job 28:16 and 1 Chr 29:2.** *bədōlaḥ* (916) occurs **twice in the canon: here and Num 11:7**, where the manna looks like it. And **Ezek 28:13, the other Eden passage in the canon, lists *šōham* among the stones that covered the figure in "Eden, the garden of God".** Gold, priestly stone, and a substance otherwise known only from the wilderness bread: the garden's mineralogy is sanctuary mineralogy.

**Paronomasia check (words recurring three or more times).** Run on *ʾādām* (15), *ʾădāmāh* (5), *ʾĕlōhîm*, *ḥay* (6), *ʿēṣ* (5), *gan* (5), *ṭôv* (5). **Two fire:** *ʾādām* / *ʾădāmāh*, treated above; and *ʾîš* / *ʾiššāh* at 2:23, which the text itself puns explicitly. **Nil** on the rest. The *ʿărûmmîm* / *ʿārûm* pair belongs to 2:25–3:1 and is reported in the boundary note.

### 8. Translations

**NASB95 against the KJV:**

- **2:5 "no shrub of the field… no plant of the field"** — both versions keep the two terms distinct. Worth preserving: the pair is not decorative, it is the double lack the verse then explains with a double cause.
- **2:6 "a mist" (NASB95, KJV)** — Hebrew *ʾēd*, a word of genuinely uncertain meaning (it occurs elsewhere only at Job 36:27). "Mist", "spring", "flow" and "stream" are all on offer. *Uncertain — flag for a lexicon.* The LXX read πηγή, "spring", which is the reading most modern versions have moved toward.
- **2:15 "to cultivate it and keep it" (NASB95) / "to dress it and to keep it" (KJV)** — both render *ʿābad* with an agricultural verb, which is a legitimate sense and, in the light of (b) above, the weaker half of the word's range here. Neither version can show the reader that this pair is Levitical vocabulary. **This is the single place in the passage where the English cannot carry the finding**, and the preacher has to supply it.
- **2:18 "a helper suitable for him" (NASB95) / "an help meet for him" (KJV)** — the KJV's "help meet" is the source of the noun "helpmeet", which is a mistranslation by accretion; "meet" was an adjective meaning *fitting*. Worth one sentence from the pulpit if the congregation reads the KJV.
- **2:21 "one of his ribs" (NASB95, KJV)** — both choose the anatomical sense. Given the word's distribution (c above), "one of his sides" is at least as defensible, and the ancient versions took it as a body part too (LXX πλευρά, which is also "side"). Not an error; a narrowing.
- **2:23 "Woman… Man"** — both versions capitalise to signal the wordplay, and both add a footnote. The pun cannot be reproduced in English; "*ʾiššāh* from *ʾîš*" has to be said aloud.

**Ancient versions check.** The trigger holds at 2:18 and 2:24, and it fires at both.

**Translation-tradition split — 2:18.** MT reads אֶעֱשֶׂהּ, first person **singular** (parsed `Vqi1cs`): "**I will make** him a helper." **Both Greek editions read ποιήσωμεν — first person plural, "let us make."** `[T]` *verified in Swete and in the Rahlfs-based export* The Greek has assimilated 2:18 to 1:26's "let us make", producing a second "let us" that the Hebrew does not have. Under the Three-Way Triage this is a **category 2 substantive variant** — see Textual Variants. *It bears directly on the Genesis 1 run's handling of 1:26: anyone arguing the plural of 1:26 from the Greek should know that the Greek supplies a plural here too, where the Hebrew is singular.*

**The NT's own text — 2:24.** MT ends וְהָי֖וּ לְבָשָׂ֥ר אֶחָֽד, "and they shall become one flesh", with **no numeral**. **Both Greek editions read καὶ ἔσονται οἱ δύο εἰς σάρκα μίαν — "and *the two* shall become one flesh."** `[T]` *verified* And **every New Testament citation follows the Greek**: Matt 19:5, Mark 10:8, 1 Cor 6:16, Eph 5:31 all read οἱ δύο (checked in SBLGNT). Mark 10:8 then draws the inference explicitly — "so they are no longer **two** but one flesh" — from a word the Hebrew does not contain. This is **category 3**: the Greek as the NT's own text, and it stands whichever reading is prior. *An honest qualification:* the Greek supplies οἱ δύο at 2:25 as well ("and *the two* were naked"), where the Hebrew has *šənêhem* — so the addition at 2:24 is plausibly the translator's idiom rather than a different *Vorlage*, and the MT's "they" implies two in any case. The NT's argument is not damaged; but the report will not call this a variant when translator habit explains it. *Moderate confidence on the cause; high on the fact.*

**Pulpit divergence note:** N/A — no pulpit text declared. When one is named, check 2:15 (whether the version's verb obscures the Levitical pair), 2:18 ("helper"), and 2:21 ("rib" or "side").

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Intimate, close-focused, and — for the first time in the Bible —*unresolved*.** `[T]` Chapter 1 was a processional viewed from a great height; this passage is at ground level with one man in it. God stoops, forms, breathes, plants, takes, puts, commands, brings animals to see what he will call them, puts him to sleep, builds, and brings her to him. The verbs are those of a craftsman and a host.

Devices carrying it:

- **Anthropomorphism, unapologetically.** God is a potter (2:7), a gardener (2:8), and a builder (2:22). Chapter 1 had God speaking; chapter 2 has him with his hands in the soil.
- **The pause at 2:20.** "But for Adam there was not found a helper suitable for him." A whole creation has been paraded and named, and the sentence ends in a lack. It is the first disappointment in Scripture and it is God-authored.
- **The one exclamation.** 2:23 is the only human speech and the only raised voice: *zōʾt happaʿam* — "**this** one, at last!" The English "This is now" (NASB95) is flat; the Hebrew is relief.
- **The narrator's step outside.** 2:24's "for this reason" changes the camera angle entirely, from a garden to every household that will ever exist.

**Soundtrack:** a single instrument — something with breath in it — and a long silence at 2:20 before the last phrase.

**A note for the preacher.** The passage's emotional centre is a *lack that God names and then fills*. A sermon that hurries from 2:18 to 2:22 loses the two verses of searching in between, which is where the text puts its weight.

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew narrative prose, and of a different texture from the previous unit.** `[T]` Chapter 1 was formulaic and symmetrical; this is scene-based, anthropomorphic, and built on a problem-and-delay. Both are prose; the difference in texture is a difference of *camera*, not of kind, and treating it as a difference of *source* is a decision the report does not need to make and does not make.

**One embedded sub-genre:** 2:23 is a short poetic line inside the narrative — the first human speech, and the first poetry in the Bible.

**One embedded genre-shift:** 2:10–14 is a geographical excursus in the present tense, a different register from the narrative around it.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits:** pre-fall, at the origin. Everything is downstream.

**Where the reader stands:** this side of the cross, in the already/not-yet.

**What has happened in between that changes the reading:**

- **The fall.** The vocation of 2:15 is now exercised under 3:17–19's curse; the marriage of 2:24 is now exercised under 3:16's disorder. Preaching either as though the reader could simply step into it skips a chapter.
- **The tabernacle and temple.** The sanctuary vocabulary of 2:12 and 2:15 is *anticipatory* for the reader of Genesis and *retrospective* for the reader who has Exodus and Kings — and it terminates in a city with no temple in it (Rev 21:22) and the tree of life back in the middle of it (Rev 22:2).
- **Christ, and the two Adams.** 1 Cor 15:45 quotes 2:7 and makes the first man a type by contrast. What the reader inherits from 2:7 is a living soul; what he needs is a life-giving spirit.
- **Ephesians 5.** Paul quotes 2:24 and then says the mystery is "great" and that he is speaking of Christ and the church. The verse's ultimate referent is disclosed only this side of the resurrection.

**Canonical position:** Torah, first book, first *toledot*. See Phase 0.55.

### 15. Who Am I?

**The man (*hā-ʾādām*)** is generic and representative — the noun carries the article throughout the passage, "the man", not a personal name. `[T]` So the identification is genuinely available: the reader is the creature formed from dust, given breath, given a place, given a charge, given a word, and given a partner.

**But the identification runs into 1 Corinthians 15 immediately.** `[S: NT]` Paul takes the man of 2:7 as the *first* Adam precisely in order to contrast him with the last. So the honest position: **the reader identifies with the man as creature and vocation-holder, and does not identify with him as the one who keeps the charge.** The garden's guard failed to keep the garden; the reader is on that side of the story, not the other.

**The woman** is not a subordinate character in the passage's own terms — the word used of her is the word used of God's help — and she is not the reader's foil. Where a sermon needs a single identification point, "the man" as generic humanity is the passage's own, and 2:24's "a man… his wife" is the narrator's application of it to every reader.

**Where the passage is commonly misread as "me".** The courtship reading (Tool 13) and its mirror, the reading that makes the woman's making a statement about women's *nature*. The text says what she is made *from* and what she is *for* (correspondence, help) and does not offer an ontology.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the passage seeks.** Not an action first, but a self-understanding: to receive one's life as *breathed*, one's work as *charged*, one's limits as *given*, and one's company as *gift*. The only imperative in the passage is God's command not to eat, and the only instruction is the narrator's at 2:24.

**Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.**

*Worldview.* Four shifts, in the passage's own order:

1. **You are dust with breath in it**, and so is the animal beside you as to the dust. The difference is not the material.
2. **Work is pre-fall.** The charge at 2:15 comes before the curse at 3:17, and it is priestly language: serve and guard. Work is not the consequence of sin; toil is.
3. **A limit is part of a good world.** The one prohibition stands in a garden of unrestricted permission — "from *any* tree… you may eat freely" — and the limit is the smaller half of the sentence by a long way.
4. **Solitude was the first thing God called not good**, and he fixed it by making another person, not by giving the man more to do.

*Behaviour — start.* Take the charge of 2:15 seriously in its own terms: serve the place you have been put, and guard it. That is domestic, congregational and vocational at once.

*Behaviour — stop.* Stop treating the limit as the enemy of the gift. The serpent's first move in the next chapter is to make the prohibition sound like the whole sentence, and it works.

*Motivation.* Everything in the passage is *given* — breath, place, food, work, word, partner — before anything is asked. The command arrives in a garden already planted.

**Four audiences.**

- *For me:* What has been entrusted to me to serve and to guard, that I have been treating as mine to use? And where am I hearing a good limit as a grudging one?
- *For a Christian friend:* The one who believes work is a curse to be endured needs 2:15 before 3:17 — and the one who is alone needs to hear that God said it first, and called it not good, and did something about it.
- *For the church community:* A congregation that hears "serve and guard" as its own charge over what it has been given — a place, a people, a deposit — is reading the verse the way Numbers reads it.
- *For an unbeliever:* The passage claims that human beings are made, addressed, and given both a job and a limit. That is a coherent account of why work matters and why boundaries are not oppression, and it can be laid alongside the alternatives.

**Prayer.** *You formed us from the ground and breathed into us; you put us in a place and gave us something to serve and to keep; you set one limit in a garden full of gifts; and when you saw that we were alone you said it was not good, and you made another. Teach us to hold what you have entrusted as stewards and not as owners, to hear your limits as kindness, and to receive one another as gift.*

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **2:5–6 — two disjunctive clauses** setting the scene before the first *wayyiqtol* at 2:7, exactly as 1:2 did before 1:3. `[T]` The narrator uses the same device to open both accounts.
- **2:17 — *môt tāmût*, infinitive absolute + imperfect.** `[T]` The doubled-up emphatic: "dying you shall die." NASB95's "you will surely die" carries the force; the KJV's "thou shalt surely die" the same. The construction is *emphatic*, not durative, and does not by itself decide the timing question (see Difficult Verses). *High confidence on the grammar; the timing is a separate matter.*
- **2:18 — *ʾeʿĕśeh*, `Vqi1cs`.** `[T]` *verified* First person **singular**. The Greek's plural is a divergence, not a rendering. See Tool 8 and Textual Variants.
- **2:19 — *wayyiṣer*, and the vexed tense.** `[T]` on the form; `[S]` on the debate. The verb is a *wayyiqtol* ("and the LORD God formed"), which if read sequentially places the forming of the animals *after* 2:18 and therefore after the man — the apparent conflict with chapter 1's order. Some versions render it pluperfect ("had formed"; so the NIV) to harmonise. **The Hebrew form is the ordinary narrative past**, and the pluperfect rendering is an interpretive choice, not a translation of a different form. *Say so honestly rather than resolving it silently.* The alternative resolution — that chapter 2 is not narrating in chronological sequence but assembling a scene around the man — does not require the grammar to be bent.
- **2:23 — *ʾiššāh* / *ʾîš*.** `[T]` Folk-etymological wordplay: the two words are not in fact from the same root, and the text is punning on sound, which is what Hebrew narrative habitually does. Note that *ʾîš* has not appeared before this verse in the passage — the man is *hā-ʾādām* throughout until the woman exists, and only then is he *ʾîš*. `[T]` *verified — ʾîš occurs at 2:23 and 2:24 only.*
- **2:24 — *dābaq*, "cling".** `[T]` One occurrence. Elsewhere in the Torah it is the verb for clinging to the LORD (Deut 10:20; 11:22; 13:4; 30:20). *Moderate confidence that the covenantal register is in view here; the distribution is suggestive and the passage does not say so.*

#### Wordplay and sound

*ʾādām* / *ʾădāmāh* (the passage's leading pun); *ʾîš* / *ʾiššāh* (2:23, explicit); *ʿărûmmîm* / *ʿārûm* (2:25 → 3:1, just outside the boundary). None survives into English.

### Textual Variants

**2:18 — "I will make" (MT) against "let us make" (LXX).**

- **The issue:** MT אֶעֱשֶׂהּ, `Vqi1cs`, first person singular. Both Greek editions in the corpus read ποιήσωμεν, first person plural.
- **Evidence:** verified in Swete and in the Rahlfs-based Logos export, which agree. Not checked against BHS's apparatus or Göttingen — flagged.
- **Triage: category 2**, a substantive variant (or a translator's harmonisation, which on the evidence available cannot be distinguished from one).
- **Most likely original:** the **MT**. The Greek reading is the assimilating one — it conforms 2:18 to 1:26 — and assimilation to a nearby famous phrase is a well-attested direction of change. *Moderate confidence.*
- **Preaching implication:** small in itself, real for anyone building on 1:26's plural. If the Greek supplies a "let us" at 2:18 where the Hebrew has "I", then the Greek's "let us" at 1:26 is at least worth holding more loosely as evidence of anything. Worth one sentence to a congregation that has heard 1:26 preached as a Trinity text.

**2:24 — "the two".** Not a variant in the reportable sense. **Category 3**, the NT's own text, handled under Tool 8 and Tool 11, with the translator-habit qualification stated there.

**A versification trap, recorded because this run nearly fell into it.** `[S: method]` In the corpus's **Swete** layer, the content of MT 2:25 is numbered as part of **Gen 3:1**; in the **Rahlfs-based export** it stands as 2:25, as in the MT. A first pass read the Swete numbering as evidence that the Greek tradition attached the nakedness verse to the serpent scene — a tidy finding, and false. **The two Greek editions differ in verse numbering, not in text.** The corpus README warns that versification manufactures false failures; this is what that looks like in practice.

**No other significant textual variants in this passage.**

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Not recoverable from the text; the presenting situation is `[S]`, per the overview.

**What the ancient reader knew that we do not.**

- **The four rivers were a real-world map, not a mythic gesture.** `[S]` *moderate.* Tigris and Euphrates are identifiable; Pishon and Gihon are not, and the lands (Havilah, Cush) are debated. The narrator's present-tense excursus (2:10–14) is an attempt to *locate* the garden for a reader who is expected to know two of the four. What that changes: the passage resists being read as a myth set nowhere.
- **A garden in the ancient Near East was a royal institution**, attached to palaces and temples, walled, irrigated, and planted with rare trees. `[S]` *moderate–high.* The word the LXX chooses — παράδεισος, from a Persian term for a walled royal park — reflects that. So "garden" is closer to *estate* or *sanctuary precinct* than to allotment.
- **Naming was an exercise of authority**, well attested in and outside the Bible. `[I]` It is what makes 2:19–20 a delegation rather than a vocabulary exercise.
- **Gold, onyx and aromatic resin are temple materials**, and a reader who knows Exodus knows it. `[T]` on the distributions; `[S]` on the cultural weight.
- **A "deep sleep" is not ordinary sleep.** `[T]` *tardēmāh* is used of divinely-induced unconsciousness (Gen 15:12; 1 Sam 26:12; Isa 29:10; Job 4:13; 33:15). The man is not merely asleep; he is put beyond participation.

**The ANE parallels, and where to put them.** Mesopotamian garden and creation-of-man material exists and is `[S]` in every particular. Nothing in the text refers to it. **The report's position, as with the previous unit:** the sanctuary reading here does not need it — the *ʿābad*/*šāmar* pair and the *ṣēlāʿ*/*šōham* vocabulary do the work from inside the Torah, which is a stronger and more preachable warrant than a comparative one.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Torah — readers of the founding story, holding creation and cult together. **This is the passage where that frame pays off most directly in the whole book.** A reader who has Numbers in his ears hears 2:15 as a Levitical commission; a reader who has Exodus hears 2:12's stones as ephod stones. The extension's fourth category — intra-Pentateuchal mutual illumination — is not a theoretical possibility here; it is how the passage's central vocabulary works.

**What they brought.** A world in which sacred space was guarded, entry was regulated, and an intruder into a holy place died for it (Num 18:7). And a world in which marriage was an alliance between households, not primarily an affection.

**Shocking.** That the man is put in the sanctuary as its servant rather than kept out of it. And 2:24's *direction*: a **man** leaves his father and mother — in a patrilocal society where the woman left her household, the verse states the reverse. `[T]` That is the sharpest culturally-counter statement in the passage and it is usually passed over.

**Surprising.** That God calls something "not good" while creation is still unfinished; and that the remedy for the man's solitude is not more work but another person.

**Comforting.** That work is given before any fall, and that the limit is one tree in a garden of permission.

**Disturbing.** The penalty at 2:17, and the fact that the guard is being told what to keep out before there is anything to keep out.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A romantic conception of marriage, which makes 2:24 sound like a wedding text; and an individualism that hears "helper" as a job title rather than a relation.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1 — Temple and God's presence.** *Where this passage sits:* the origin, and the passage that supplies the sanctuary vocabulary. `[T]` on the lexical data; `[I]` on the reading. *Before:* only 1:1–2:3. *After:* the tabernacle (Exod 25–40, where *ṣēlāʿ* and *šōham* both live), the temple, Ezek 28:13's Eden-and-precious-stones oracle, Ezek 47's river from the sanctuary, Christ as the dwelling (John 1:14; 2:21), and Rev 22:1–2, where a river and a tree of life return to a city that needs no temple. *Payoff:* it explains why the garden has minerals in it at all — 2:11–12 is otherwise an odd digression — and it re-describes the man's job. *Confidence: high on the vocabulary; moderate on the full sanctuary reading.*

**2 — Image, vocation and the two Adams.** *Where it sits:* the first Adam's charge. `[T]` *After:* the charge broken (3:1–24); Israel given a land to serve and keep and losing it; **1 Cor 15:45 quoting 2:7 to name Christ the last Adam**; Rom 5:12–21. *Payoff:* it is the only route by which 2:15's vocation can be preached without moralism. *Confidence: high (NT-authorised).*

**3 — Marriage and divine-human union.** *Where it sits:* the institution, and the narrator's own generalisation of it. `[T]` *After:* Hosea and Ezekiel's covenant-marriage; **Eph 5:31–32, which quotes 2:24 and says the mystery refers to Christ and the church**; Rev 19:7–9. *Payoff:* Paul does not *apply* 2:24 to Christ by analogy; he says the verse was about that. *Confidence: high (explicit).*

**Confluence — and it is (c), climactic convergence.** The three meet in one place and the NT says so. **Ephesians 5:31–32 quotes this passage's marriage verse and calls it a mystery about Christ and the church; Ephesians 2:21–22 has already called that same church a temple in the Lord.** So the sanctuary the man was to serve and guard, the vocation the last Adam takes up, and the union the narrator generalises at 2:24 arrive together in one image — a people who are both bride and temple. That convergence is not this report's construction; it is two adjacent moves in one letter.

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton:** Genesis · primeval history, the first *toledot* panel · serving the book's leading idea by particularising: from cosmos to garden to one pair.

**Findings:**

1. **Structure & shape — the passage is built on a delayed answer.** `[T]` The first lack (2:5) is met in two verses; the second (2:18) is held open across the animal creation and a naming ceremony before 2:22 answers it. Braking is weight, and the weight is on 2:20's failure. *Step 3.*
2. **Repetition & linkage — the leading word is *ʾādām*, and it is a pun on the ground.** `[T]` *verified* 15 occurrences against 5 of *ʾădāmāh*, joined in one clause at 2:5, and both detonated at 3:17–19. *Step 4.*
3. **Narrative space — the passage is intensely located, and its geography is centrifugal.** `[T]` A garden planted *in the east* (*miqqedem*), a river going *out* from Eden and dividing into four heads that reach Havilah, Cush and Assyria. The previous unit had no space at all; this one has a map, and the map runs outward from a centre. *Step 2.* The eastward vector begun here is the one the overview tracks to 3:24, 4:16, 11:2 and 13:11 — **with the caution the claim audit attached: it is a narrative pattern, not a lexical rule, and 2:8 is the counter-instance, since Eden itself is *miqqedem*.** `[S: audit]`
4. **First vs second reading.** `[T]` On a first reading 2:16–17 is a generous permission with one exception, and 2:25 is an innocent detail. On the re-read both are fuses: the command is the thing the serpent will edit, and the nakedness is the word the next verse puns on. *Step 5.*
5. **Intertextuality (polyacoustic) — the Levitical register.** `[T]` *verified* *ʿābad*/*šāmar* with Num 3:7–8; *ṣēlāʿ* with Exodus; *šōham* with the ephod. Building echoes, heard by a reader who holds the whole Torah — not flattened into a single equation. *Step 6.*
6. **Selectivity — what is not told.** `[T]` The passage does not say how long any of this took, what the man did in the garden, or how much time passed before 2:18. The narrator gives a place, a charge, a word and a partner, and omits everything else. *Step 1.*

**Echoes and signals:** leading word — *ʾādām*/*ʾădāmāh*; structural device — problem–delay–answer, twice; intertextual links — Num 3:7–8; Exod 25–39; cross-reported to Tool 11.

**Nil returns:** *type-scene* (nothing yet to repeat); *chiasm* at unit level; *symbolic numbers* (the four rivers are geography, not numerology — and claiming otherwise would be exactly the over-reading the guardrails forbid).

**Irreducibility.** The passage is not a set of propositions about anthropology and marriage. It is a story in which a lack is named, a search fails, and a gift is given — and the sermon that reduces it to "work is good, marriage is good, limits are good" has kept the conclusions and lost the shape that makes them land.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: typology (one, NT-authorised), trajectory (two), and contrast (one).**

**Typology — marriage.** 2:24 → **Eph 5:31–32**, where Paul quotes the verse and says "this mystery is great, but I am speaking with reference to Christ and the church". *Tests:* theological category ✓ (covenant union); NT precedent ✓ (explicit, and it is Paul who makes the identification, not the interpreter); escalation ✓ ("great mystery" — the reality exceeds the sign); authorial pattern — **partial**: Genesis generalises the union but does not signal a further referent. Three and a half of four → **typology, at high confidence, on NT authorisation.** `[T]` / `[S: NT]`

**Trajectory 1 — sanctuary.** The garden's vocabulary (2:12, 2:15) runs to tabernacle, temple, Christ as the dwelling, and the new creation's temple-less city with the tree of life in it. *High confidence on the vocabulary; moderate on the whole arc.* `[T]` / `[I]`

**Trajectory 2 — the two Adams.** 2:7 → **1 Cor 15:45**, quoted almost verbatim from the Greek. The first man became a living soul; the last became a life-giving spirit. *High confidence (explicit quotation).* `[S: NT]`

**Contrast — the guard who did not keep.** The man is charged to *šāmar* the garden and, in the next scene, does not. Christ is the one who keeps what he is given and loses none of it (John 17:12; 18:9 use φυλάσσω and ἀπόλλυμι of exactly that). `[I]` *Moderate confidence — the Genesis vocabulary is verified, the John link is thematic rather than a quotation, and the report does not press it further than that.*

**Christophany:** none.

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation here is unusually strong and unusually specific:** *be a good steward* (from 2:15), *be a good husband* (from 2:24), and *wait for God's provision* (from 2:18–22). All three are sermons that could be preached from this passage without mentioning Christ at all, and all three would be moralism with a Genesis text attached.
- **The gospel grounding:** everything in the passage is given before anything is asked — breath, garden, food, work, word, partner. The command arrives *inside* a garden already planted, and the woman arrives *after* the search has failed. Nothing here is earned.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** the last Adam has taken up the vocation the first was given (1 Cor 15:45); the true guard keeps what the first did not; and the union the narrator generalised at 2:24 turns out, on Paul's testimony, to have been about him all along.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 2:17 — "in the day that you eat from it you will surely die"

- **Category:** apologetic / doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** they eat, and do not die that day. Adam lives to 930 (5:5).
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the clause is the *ground* of the prohibition (*kî*), stating the stake. Its work in the passage is to make the limit serious, not to schedule an event.
- **The options:** (i) *bəyôm* as an idiom for "when", not "on that very day"; (ii) death as immediate spiritual alienation, with physical death following; (iii) the sentence passed that day and the execution deferred by mercy (3:21–24 as commutation); (iv) *môt tāmût* as "you will become mortal".
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the infinitive-absolute construction is **emphatic, not temporal** `[T]` — it strengthens the verb, it does not date it. And *bəyôm* + infinitive is used elsewhere in this very passage as a general temporal marker (2:4, "in the day that the LORD God made"), where it plainly does not mean a single day. `[T]` *verified* That is a text-internal answer and it is the strongest one available.
- **Suggested handling:** address briefly; the 2:4 parallel does most of the work and is satisfying to a congregation.

#### 2:18–23 — the woman: *ʿēzer kənegdô*, the rib, and the order

- **Category:** doctrinal / pastorally sensitive.
- **The difficulty:** the passage is a primary text in a live and painful debate, and hearers arrive with strong prior commitments and, sometimes, with injuries.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the whole second half of the passage exists to *remedy* something God calls not good. Its function is gift, not hierarchy — whatever else is true.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces, and the report will state both halves:** `[T]` (i) ***ʿēzer* is not a subordinate word.** Twenty-one occurrences in the canon; the great majority of God; not one of a subordinate assistant. Any sermon that glosses "helper" as "assistant" is wrong on the lexicon. (ii) **The passage does establish an order and a derivation**, and 1 Tim 2:13 argues from it. The man is formed first, names the animals, and names the woman; she is taken from him and brought to him. Flattening that is as inaccurate as flattening the first half. **The passage gives correspondence and derivation, and it does not give either a hierarchy of worth or a job description.** The report stops there, deliberately.
- **Suggested handling:** name both halves, name the debate as a real one among people who believe the Bible, and do not settle it from a text that does not raise it. **Pastoral note:** in most congregations there will be someone for whom this passage has been used as a weapon. Say the *ʿēzer* finding out loud.

#### 2:19 — the order of the animals

- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** read sequentially, 2:19 has the animals formed after the man, against chapter 1's order.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the verb is an ordinary *wayyiqtol*; the pluperfect rendering ("had formed", NIV) is an interpretive smoothing rather than a different reading of the form. `[T]` The better answer is that chapter 2 is not narrating a sequence but assembling a scene: its subject is the man, and the animals enter when they become relevant to him.
- **Suggested handling:** mention only if the congregation reads a version that has smoothed it, or if someone asks.

#### 2:10–14 — the geography

- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** two of the four rivers cannot be identified, and the described hydrology (one river dividing into four) does not correspond to any known system.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the narrator plainly *intends* location — present tense, named lands, identifiable rivers — and equally plainly does not give enough to find the place. `[T]` Both facts are data. Attempts to fix Eden on a map go beyond what is given; so does treating the passage as deliberately un-located.
- **Suggested handling:** sidestep with a note, unless the sermon is making the sanctuary point, in which case 2:11–12's minerals are the reason the excursus is there at all.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching 2:18–24 as a marriage seminar

- **What it looks like:** the sermon is about marriage, 2:24 is the text, and the garden is scenery.
- **Why it's wrong:** 2:24 is the narrator's *inference* from the manner of the woman's making, and Ephesians 5:31–32 says the verse's referent is Christ and the church. A sermon that arrives at "here is how to have a good marriage" has stopped one move short of where Paul takes it — and has left the unmarried, the widowed and the divorced with nothing.
- **The corrective:** preach the making, then the inference, then the mystery. The marriage counsel then arrives as a consequence and everyone in the room is in the sermon.

### Pitfall 2: "Helper" as "assistant"

- **What it looks like:** a passing gloss — "God made her to help him" — with the modern sense of *help* doing all the work.
- **Why it's wrong:** *ʿēzer* occurs 21 times in the Hebrew Bible and the great majority are of God. There is no occurrence where it means a subordinate assistant. The lexicon simply does not support the gloss.
- **The corrective:** say the count. Then say what *kənegdô* adds — correspondence, a counterpart — which is the part the word alone does not carry. And leave the role question to the texts that actually raise it.

### Pitfall 3: Making the two accounts rivals — in either direction

- **What it looks like:** either "these are two different creation stories" offered as a problem to be managed, or a strained harmonisation that flattens the second account into a replay of the first.
- **Why it's wrong:** the second account supplies precisely what the first withholds — the naming, the place, the command, the partner. That is not the profile of a rival version; it is the profile of unfinished business.
- **The corrective:** show the *qārāʾ* counts. Five, all God's, stopping at 1:10; four, all the man's, at 2:19–23. The congregation can see the join.

### Pitfall 4: Work as the curse

- **What it looks like:** "work came in with the fall" — usually implied rather than stated, and usually by starting at 3:17.
- **Why it's wrong:** the charge is given at 2:15, before any disobedience, and in priestly vocabulary. Toil is cursed at 3:17; work is given here.
- **The corrective:** preach 2:15 before 3:17, and say what the words mean — serve, and guard.

### Pitfall 5: The courtship template

- **What it looks like:** "God will bring her to you, as he brought her to Adam — wait for his timing."
- **Why it's wrong:** a unique event in a unique garden with one man and no alternatives, converted into a method. The text draws its own inference at 2:24, and it is about leaving parents, not about finding a spouse.
- **The corrective:** let the passage's own *ʿal-kēn* set the scope of the application.

### Pitfall 6: Human uniqueness from the dust

- **What it looks like:** "God formed *man* from the dust — see how personally he made us, unlike the animals."
- **Why it's wrong:** 2:19 says the animals were formed from the ground too, by the same verb. `[T]` *verified* The distinction in this passage is the breath, the charge, and the naming authority — not the material or the method.
- **The corrective:** the contrast is available and it is better than the one usually drawn; make it accurately.
