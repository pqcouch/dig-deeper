Claim Audit 2: Genesis

Passages audited: Gen 29:1–35:29, with the later texts each claim names. The whole Genesis queue: 22 claims (G1–G22), thirteen from the 29:1–31:55 dig and nine from the 32:1–35:29 dig, both of 25 October 2026. Date: 25 October 2026 · Version: 1.0 Purpose: queue trigger 1 had fired (22 queued; six digs since the audit of 7 September 2026), and trigger 2 was armed by two Headline Findings that rest on synthetic designs — the release-law pattern of 29–31 (G1) and the Cain–Abel reversal of 32–33 (G14). This audit decides what each claim may carry into a sermon backbone, the digs’ v1.1 patches and the next overview upgrade. Method: two blind auditors, run one after the other (the two-auditor rule of 16 October 2026). Auditor A took the fourteen single contacts (wording, rarity, rivals, the Greek). Auditor B took the eight field and design claims, with block tests against every Genesis window of the same length, at two or more frequency ceilings and window lengths, plus reverse tests across the canon. Neither saw the digs, the overview, the sweep, the earlier audit or the other’s report; each was given only the claim as the queue states it. The main session reproduced the decisive results.

Primary texts: WLC Hebrew with its lemma index (every count); Swete LXX (observation, all books); the Rahlfs LXX and BHS with its apparatus for Genesis (Logos exports, the citation of record); SBLGNT for the New Testament. There is no Rahlfs export for any book but Genesis, so every Greek reading outside Genesis below is Swete’s. References follow the Hebrew versification, with the English in brackets where it differs (Gen 32; Hos 12; Ps 20; Mic 4:14–5:2).

Warrant tags: [T] the text itself · [I] an inference from it · [S] a secondary source · [S: audit] a verdict of this or an earlier audit.

The release law and the household (29–31)

G1. The release-law / exodus pattern

Claim tested. Jacob’s twenty years (29:15–31:55) are told in the vocabulary of the Hebrew-servant release law and of the exodus.

What held. [T] - רָאָה (“see”) + עֳנִי (“affliction”) in one verse in the Torah: Gen 29:32; 31:42; Exod 3:7; 4:31; Deut 26:7 — exact. Across the canon it is fifteen verses, so it is a Torah claim, not a Bible-wide one. - חִנָּם (“for nothing”): in Genesis only 29:15; in Exodus only 21:2, 11. - “It was told … that he had fled” (the hophal of נָגַד (“tell”) with בָּרַח (“flee”)): Gen 31:22; Exod 14:5; 1 Sam 27:4 only. - רֵיקָם (“empty-handed”) is not rare (16 verses), but “send away” (piel of שָׁלַח (“send”)) + רֵיקָם (“empty-handed”) is, and in the Torah it occurs only at Gen 31:42 and Deut 15:13. - New, and the strongest single node: עָבַד (“serve”) + “six” + “year” in one verse — Gen 31:41; Exod 21:2; Deut 15:12, 18; Jer 34:14. Every occurrence but Gen 31:41 is a release law. With a wage word as well: Gen 31:41 and Deut 15:18 only. - New — the daughters’ half: נָכְרִי (“foreign”) + מָכַר (“sell”) in one verse — Gen 31:15 (“are we not reckoned foreigners by him? for he has sold us”); Exod 21:8 (the daughter sold as a maidservant: “to a foreign people he has no right to sell her”); Deut 14:21. The Greek keeps it (ἀλλότριαι … πέπρακεν; ἀλλοτρίῳ … πωλεῖν).

What did not. - “Pursue” + “overtake” are two verses apart in Gen 31 (31:23, 25), not in one verse; and the pair is common (fourteen one-verse instances, Gen 44:4 and Exod 14:9; 15:9 among them). - Laban’s 31:43 against Exod 21:4 shares only “sons … daughters”. It is a thematic echo.

Baseline. Gen 29:15–32:1 (119 verses) was ranked against about 1,300 Genesis windows of the same length. - Against the release laws alone (Exod 21:1–11 with Deut 15:12–18): first with words in at most 50 verses of the canon and at most 400; with words in at most 150, the only windows ahead overlap the same Gen 31 material. Stable. - Against Exod 3 alone or Exod 14 alone: between the 25th and 65th percentiles. Against the full composite (the laws with Exod 3 and 14): top 13–30 %. Unremarkable. - In reverse, across the canon: Deut 15:12–18 is in the top 2 % (50-verse ceiling) and 7 % (150-verse ceiling) of partners for Gen 29–31; Ruth 2 ranks higher, on harvest, wage and maidservant words — a genre neighbour, not a rival design.

Greek. δωρεάν (“for nothing”) at Gen 29:15 and Exod 21:2, 11; Gen 31:42 κενόν με ἐξαπέστειλας (“you would have sent me away empty”) against Deut 15:13 οὐκ ἐξαποστελεῖς αὐτὸν κενόν (“you shall not send him away empty”) — tighter than the Hebrew. The exodus phrases break: Exod 3:7 has κάκωσιν, Exod 4:31 θλῖψιν, against ταπείνωσις at Gen 29:32; 31:42.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Release law high; exodus layer moderate. Wording: “Jacob’s service is told in the vocabulary of the release law (Exod 21:1–11; Deut 15:12–18): ‘for nothing’ (29:15), ‘six years’ with ‘serve’ (31:41), ‘sent away empty’ (31:42), and the daughters’ ‘foreigners … sold’ (31:15, cf. Exod 21:7–8). Two exact phrases — ‘saw my affliction’ (29:32; 31:42) and ‘it was told … that he had fled’ (31:22) — also tie it to the exodus, but at block level Gen 29–31 is no closer to Exod 3 or 14 than chance.” [S: audit]

G2. Hos 12:13–14 [Eng 12:12–13]

What held. “Jacob fled (בָּרַח, the Genesis verb, 27:43; 31:20–27; 35:1, 7) to the field of Aram; Israel served (עָבַד) for a wife, and for a wife he kept (שָׁמַר)”; then “by a prophet the LORD brought Israel up from Egypt, and by a prophet he was kept (נִשְׁמָר)”. עָבַד (“serve”) with בְּ (“for”) of the price matches 29:18, 20 (“for Rachel”); שָׁמַר (“keep”) is Jacob’s own word for his shepherding (30:31). The double שָׁמַר (“keep”) — Jacob kept for a wife, Israel was kept by a prophet — is Hosea’s antithesis, and it is in the text. [T] שְׂדֵה אֲרָם (“the field of Aram”) occurs only here; Genesis says Paddan-aram. Hosea is retelling, not quoting.

What did not. “Kept sheep” — the Hebrew has no object; the English versions supply it.

Greek. Swete keeps the wordplay (ἐφυλάξατο … διεφυλάχθη, “he kept … he was kept”).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. High. Present it with G18: Hos 12 reads the whole Jacob cycle — the heel (12:4a; Gen 25:26; 27:36), the Jabbok, the meeting with Esau and Bethel (12:4b–5), the flight and service (12:13), set against the exodus (12:14). [S: audit]

G3. Michal replays Rachel

What held. וְשֵׁם הַקְּטַנָּה (“and the name of the younger”): Gen 29:16; 1 Sam 14:49 only. הַבְּכִירָה (“the firstborn daughter”): Gen 19:31–37; 29:26; 1 Sam 14:49 only. The teraphim: in the Torah only Gen 31:19, 34, 35; in Samuel 1 Sam 15:23; 19:13, 16. The elder promised and the younger loved (1 Sam 18:17, 20); a bride-price (1 Sam 18:25). [T]

What did not. רָמָה (“deceive”, piel) at Gen 29:25 and 1 Sam 19:17 is not exclusive (eight verses; with “why”, four). “Goats’ hair + garment” (1 Sam 19:13, 16) has no counterpart in Gen 31: Rachel sits on the teraphim, she does not disguise them. The parallel is in the act, not the words.

Baseline. Gen 29–31 is the best Genesis partner for 1 Sam 18:17–19:17 (with 14:49) at both ceilings and both window lengths. In reverse, 1 Sam 18–19 is only in the top 8 % (50-verse ceiling) or the top third (150-verse ceiling) of canon partners for Gen 29–31; the Song and Ruth rank higher.

Greek. παρελογίσω με (“you deceived me”) with ἵνα τί (“why”) at Gen 29:25 and 1 Kgdms 19:17 tightens it; the teraphim (εἴδωλα against κενοτάφια) and “the younger” break.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Words high; design moderate. Wording: “1 Samuel introduces Saul’s daughters in Laban’s formula (Gen 29:16, 26 ↔︎ 1 Sam 14:49, exclusive), and Michal’s deception of her father with the teraphim recalls Rachel’s (Gen 31:19–35). A replay of the whole Jacob–Laban story is not shown.” [S: audit]

G4. Exod 22:8–12 [Eng 22:9–13] behind 31:36–39

What held. פֶּשַׁע (“transgression”): its first three occurrences are Gen 31:36; 50:17; Exod 22:8. טְרֵפָה (“torn animal”) + “bring” in one verse: Gen 31:39; Exod 22:12; Ezek 4:14 only (in Ezekiel, of food entering the mouth). Jacob says he did not bring the torn animal — which is exactly what the law lets a keeper do instead of paying. Theft by day and by night (31:39; Exod 22:6, 11); “goods” (31:37; Exod 22:6); “between the two of us” (31:37; cf. Exod 22:8, 10); God as judge. [T]

What did not. The Hebrew “bear the loss” verbs differ (31:39 the piel of חָטָא (“bear the loss”); Exodus the piel of שָׁלַם (“repay”), “repay”).

Greek — it strengthens. ἀδίκημα (“wrong”, 31:36; Exod 22:8), θηριάλωτον (“torn by beasts”, 31:39; Exod 22:12), σκεύη (“goods”) and ἀποτίνω (“repay”) in both: the Greek supplies the shared verb the Hebrew lacks (Exodus in Swete).

Rival. Lev 5:20–26 shares the forensic frame (ἀδίκημα also at Lev 5:23), more weakly.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Moderate–high. Wording: “Jacob’s speech (31:36–39) uses the vocabulary of the custody law of Exod 22:6–12 — ‘transgression’, the torn animal that need not be ‘brought’, theft by day and night — and claims to have borne what the law says a keeper need not repay.” [S: audit]

G5. Lev 15:20 behind 31:34–35

What held. “She sat on them” (31:34) has the construction of Lev 15:20, “everything on which she sits is unclean”. In the Greek, ἐπικαθίζω (“sit upon”) occurs in the Torah only at Gen 31:34 (Rahlfs) and Lev 15:20 (Swete). [T]

What did not. Gen 31 has no נִדָּה (“menstruation”); its phrase “the way of women” (31:35) is not Levitical. And Swete’s Genesis does not read ἐπικαθίζω at 31:34 — the Greek link is Rahlfs’.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Moderate (down from moderate–high). Cite Lev 15:19–26 rather than 15:20 alone. Wording: “Rachel ‘sat on’ the teraphim pleading ‘the way of women’: by the standard of Lev 15:19–26 whatever a menstruant sits on is unclean. One construction and the idea in the Hebrew; Rahlfs sharpens it.” [S: audit]

G6. Deut 21:15–17 and Lev 18:18

What held — Deut 21. The feminine participle שְׂנוּאָה (“unloved, hated”) occurs eight times: Gen 29:31, 33; Deut 21:15 (twice), 16, 17; Isa 60:15; Prov 30:23 — six of the eight in Gen 29 and Deut 21. Deut 21:15–17 legislates Jacob’s case: two wives, loved and unloved, the firstborn from the unloved (Reuben, 29:32); 1 Chr 5:1–2 records Reuben’s birthright passing to Joseph. The Greek keeps it (μισεῖται; μισουμένη). [T]

Correction. 37:3 is a loved son, not a loved wife: the consequence, not the parallel.

What did not — Lev 18:18. Its idiom אִשָּׁה אֶל־אֲחֹתָהּ (“a woman to her sister”) elsewhere means “one to another” (Exod 26:3–17), and צָרַר (“make a rival”) is not in Gen 29–30. The contact is conceptual (Rachel “envied her sister”, 30:1). Rival for the loved/unloved pattern: 1 Sam 1:2–6.

Verdict. Deut 21: Confirmed with nuance, moderate–high. Lev 18:18: Needs reframing, moderate — a concept, not a verbal link. [S: audit]

G7. “Portion and inheritance” (31:14)

What held. In the Torah חֵלֶק (“portion”) + נַחֲלָה (“inheritance”) in one verse occurs only at Gen 31:14 and of Levi (Num 18:20; Deut 10:9; 12:12; 14:27, 29; 18:1) and in Deut 32:9 — exact. [T]

What did not. Across the canon it is a fixed formula (18 verses). The closest in form is the cry of secession: “we have no portion in David, no inheritance in the son of Jesse” (2 Sam 20:1; 1 Kgs 12:16 = 2 Chr 10:16) — like Rachel and Leah’s, a renunciation of a house by those leaving it. The Levitical texts state a status; they do not leave anything.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Low–moderate as a Levitical echo; moderate as a shared formula. Wording: “Rachel and Leah use the formula of disinheritance, ‘portion and inheritance’, which the Torah otherwise uses of Levi; the same formula marks secession from a house (2 Sam 20:1; 1 Kgs 12:16).” [S: audit]

G8. Ruth 4:11 and 30:3

What held. Rachel and Leah named together outside Genesis: only Ruth 4:11. The niphal of בָּנָה (“be built”) with a person as subject — “obtain children” — occurs only at Gen 16:2 and 30:3, of thirty niphals. Ruth 4:11’s “who both built the house of Israel” uses the root of the same women, and names Ephrathah and Bethlehem (35:16, 19). [T]

What did not. “Build a house” is the levirate idiom of Deut 25:9 (“who will not build up his brother’s house”), which is Ruth’s own setting. The Greek loses the pun (τεκνοποιήσομαι, “I shall bear children”, at 30:3).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. High that Ruth 4:11 recalls Rachel and Leah; moderate for a specific answer to 30:3. [S: audit]

G9. Jer 31:15 → Matt 2:18 and 30:1

What did not hold. The shared words with 30:1 (“give me sons, or else I die”) are only Rachel, “sons” and “there is none” (אַיִן).

The stronger source. Jer 31:15’s distinctive words come from Jacob’s grief for Rachel’s sons: - “Refused to be comforted” (מָאֵן + נָחַם (“be comforted”)): Gen 37:35; Jer 31:15; Ps 77:3 only. - “They are not” (אֵינֶנּוּ): Gen 42:36 (“Joseph is not … and you would take Benjamin”), with 42:13, 32; 44:30, 34. - Rachel’s grave on the way to Ephrath (35:16–20; cf. 1 Sam 10:2) locates the image. [T]

Greek. Matthew’s οὐκ ἤθελεν παρακληθῆναι (“would not be comforted”) is closer to Rahlfs Gen 37:35 (οὐκ ἤθελεν παρακαλεῖσθαι) than to Swete Jer 38:15 [Heb 31:15] (οὐκ ἤθελεν παύσασθαι, “would not cease”). Check against Rahlfs Jeremiah before it is cited — the library has it; the corpus does not.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Low–moderate for 30:1; moderate–high for 37:35 and 42:36 (with 35:16–20) as Jeremiah’s sources. Wording: “Jer 31:15 (Matt 2:18) gives Rachel the grief Jacob felt for her sons — ‘refused to be comforted’ (Gen 37:35), ‘they are not’ (42:36) — at her grave on the way to Ephrath (35:16–20).” [S: audit]

G10. John 4 and the well scene of 29:1–14

What held. John names Jacob three times (4:5, 6, 12) and the plot “Jacob gave to his son Joseph” (Gen 33:19; 48:22; Josh 24:32). φρέαρ (“well”) in both.

What did not. The scene’s Greek wording is Gen 24’s, not Gen 29’s: ἀντλῆσαι ὕδωρ (“to draw water”, Gen 24:13, 20), πηγή (“spring”, 24:13, 43), ὑδρία (“water jar”, nine times in Gen 24, none in Gen 29), “give me a drink” (24:17, 43, 45). In Gen 29 no drink is asked. The “betrothal type-scene” is a modern category [S].

Verdict: Needs reframing. Low–moderate for Gen 29; moderate for the Genesis well scenes together. Wording: “John 4 is set at Jacob’s well on Jacob’s land (Gen 33:19; 48:22) and uses the wording of the Genesis well scenes — chiefly Gen 24 — with Gen 29 supplying Jacob himself.” [S: audit]

G11. A five-part symmetry with the births at the centre

What held. An outer frame: kiss (29:11, 13 ↔︎ 31:28; 32:1), stone (29:2–10 ↔︎ 31:45–46), kinsman (29:4–15 ↔︎ 31:23–54). [T]

What did not. The wage words do not frame the centre; they run through it — שָׂכָר (“wages”) at 30:18 (Issachar) and the verb at 30:16 (Leah “hires” Jacob), both inside the births. The parts are badly unbalanced (14 verses against 55).

Baseline. The rare words shared by the mirror pairs (A–A′, B–B′) are fewer than those shared by the cross pairs, at every ceiling. Of about 1,300 random Genesis stretches cut in the same proportions, 70–92 % score as well or better; of about 67,000 other ways of dividing the same stretch, 77–94 %.

Verdict: Needs reframing. Low–moderate. Wording: “The Laban cycle is framed by the well, the stone and the kiss (29:1–14 ↔︎ 31:45–32:1); the wage dispute escalates in a straight line (29:15 → 30:28–34 → 31:7, 41); and the births are the hinge — ‘when Rachel had borne Joseph, Jacob said … send me away’ (30:25).” [S: audit]

G12. The search complex, 31:19–35 ↔︎ 44:1–12

What held. חָפַשׂ (“search”) in Genesis only 31:35; 44:12. Theft, pursuit and overtaking (31:23, 25 ↔︎ 44:4, 6), a death sentence on “him with whom it is found” (31:32 ↔︎ 44:9), and a search that ends at Rachel or Rachel’s son. Steal + search + find within eight, twelve or seventeen verses occurs only at Gen 31 and Gen 44 in the whole canon. In reverse, 44:1–12 is in the top 0.5–1.2 % of canon windows as a partner for 31:19–35. [T]

Care. 31:33 is a household order (Jacob, Leah, the maids, Rachel last), not eldest to youngest; the parallel is in the ending.

Greek — it tightens. ἐρευνάω (“search”, 31:33, 35; 44:12); παρ᾽ ᾧ ἂν εὑρεθῇ (“with whom it is found”, 31:32 ↔︎ 44:9), nearly verbatim; and “from the elder … to the younger” (44:12) recalls Laban’s “the younger before the elder” (29:26).

Verdict: Confirmed. High. [S: audit]

G13. Stones and shepherd

What held. אֶבֶן (“stone”) in Genesis: 15 occurrences in 13 verses — 2:12; 11:3; then 28:11, 18, 22; 29:2, 3, 8, 10; 31:45, 46; 35:14; 49:24. After 11:3 every one is Jacob’s. The pillars (מַצֵּבָה) likewise. God as the subject of רָעָה (“shepherd”) in Genesis only 48:15; 49:24. The Joseph story, as long, has no stone (its pit is a בּוֹר (“pit”)). [T]

What did not. “Leads to 49:24” is a reading of a distribution, not a marked chain; the syntax of 49:24 is disputed. The Greek has neither title (49:24 “he who strengthened Israel”; 48:15 “who nourished me”).

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Distribution high; the “leads to” reading moderate; Hebrew only. [S: audit]

Jacob and Esau, Shechem and Bethel (32–35)

G14. Cain–Abel reversed in Jacob–Esau

What held. [T] - מִנְחָה (“gift, offering”) in Genesis: 4:3, 4, 5; 32:14, 19, 21, 22; 33:10; 43:11, 15, 25, 26 — exact. - פָּנִים (“face”) sixteen times in 32:2–33:20, four of them in 32:21 — exact. - כָּפַר (“atone”): 32:21 is the first piel in the canon; 6:14 is the qal of a homograph (“cover with pitch”). - רָצָה (“accept”): in Genesis only 33:10; next Lev 1:4.

What did not. - 4:7 שְׂאֵת (“lifting up”) is a noun, not the idiom “lift up the face”. In Genesis that idiom is 19:21; 32:21 (and 43:34 in another sense). - Block test: null at every setting. Gen 4:1–16 and 32:2–33:20 share nothing among words in at most 50 verses, two words at 150, seven at 400. Ranked among same-length Genesis windows, both sit between the bottom and the 80th percentile. The best partners of Gen 4 are Gen 2–5 and 47–50. - The set gift + brother + face within five verses occurs in Genesis in Gen 4, Gen 32–33 and Gen 43 — the brothers bring a מִנְחָה (“gift, offering”) to Joseph, and “you shall not see my face” (43:3, 5). It is a three-member chain, not a two-text reversal.

New, and better than the claim. אוּלַי (“perhaps”) + כָּפַר (“atone”) in one verse: Gen 32:21 and Exod 32:30 only, in the identical form אֲכַפְּרָה (“perhaps I may atone”). Jacob says it of Esau’s face, Moses of the LORD’s anger after the calf. The Greek keeps it (ἐξιλάσομαι / ἐξιλάσωμαι).

Verdict: Needs reframing. Words high; the Cain–Abel reversal as a design low–moderate. Wording: “מִנְחָה (”gift, offering“) is used in Genesis only of Cain and Abel, of Jacob’s gift to Esau and of the brothers’ gift to Joseph: three brother-conflicts. Jacob’s ‘perhaps I may atone’ (32:21, the first piel of כָּפַר (”atone“)) is said again, in the same form, only by Moses (Exod 32:30). Gen 32–33 is not shown to reverse Gen 4 in particular.” [S: audit]

G15. Mic 4:8–5:2 [Eng 4:8–5:3] and Gen 35:16–21

What held. [T] - מִגְדַּל־עֵדֶר (“tower of the flock”): Gen 35:21; Mic 4:8 only (by phrase; the index files the Genesis form as one place name — see Summary). - Birth-pang words (חוּל, “writhe”; קָשָׁה, “have hard labour”; the participle יוֹלֵדָה, “she who is in labour”) within five or eight verses of Ephrath(ah) or Bethlehem: Gen 35:16–19 and Mic 4:9–5:2 only. - Ephrath(ah): Gen 35:16, 19; 48:7; Mic 5:1; Ps 132:6; Ruth 4:11; 1 Chr 2:19, 24, 50; 4:4 — the claim omitted 1 Chr 2:19. - Matthew quotes Mic 5:1 at 2:6 and Jer 31:15 at 2:18; he adds ποιμανεῖ (“shall shepherd”), picking up Micah’s flock (4:8).

What did not — the order. The 32–35 dig said the two texts stand “in the same order”. They do not: - Genesis: Ephrath (35:16) → labour (35:16–17) → Bethlehem (35:19) → tower (35:21). - Micah: tower (4:8) → labour (4:9–10) → Bethlehem Ephrathah (5:1) → labour (5:2).

The tower closes Genesis’ scene and opens Micah’s: a reversed frame.

Baseline. A bag-of-words test cannot see this link, because it rests on place names and a common verb; the exclusive phrase and the set test carry it.

Greek. Both Greek editions move the tower to 35:16 as πύργου Γαδερ, a transliteration, so the shared phrase is lost (Micah has πύργος ποιμνίου); and in the Greek order of Gen 35, tower–labour–Bethlehem, the sequence now matches Micah’s.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Words high; design moderate–high. Wording: “Micah gathers the tower of the flock (Gen 35:21; Mic 4:8, exclusive), a woman in labour and Bethlehem Ephrathah — a combination found only here and at Rachel’s death — in a reversed frame: the tower that ends Genesis’ scene opens Micah’s.” [S: audit]

G16. Gen 34 and Israel’s later scandals

What held. [T] - נְבָלָה (“folly”) + “Israel” in one verse: Gen 34:7; Deut 22:21; Josh 7:15; Judg 20:6, 10; 2 Sam 13:12; Jer 29:23 — Gen 34:7 is the first. - “Such a thing is not done” (לֹא + niphal of עָשָׂה (“do”) + כֵּן (“so”), of custom or morals): Gen 29:26; 34:7; 2 Sam 13:12 only. - עָכַר (“trouble”): in Genesis only 34:30; add Josh 6:18 to Josh 7:25. - “Spoke to her heart”: the claimed nine verses are exact. - עָנָה (“violate”) piel (“violate”): add Deut 21:14; Ezek 22:10, 11; Lam 5:11 to the list. - טָמֵא (“defile”, the verb) first at 34:5; טָהֵר (“purify”, the verb) first at 35:2 (the adjective “clean” is already at 7:2, 8). - New: מֹהַר (“bride-price”): Gen 34:12; Exod 22:16 (the seduction law); 1 Sam 18:25 only.

Baseline. For each later text, the strongest Genesis partner (31-verse windows; ceilings 50 and 150): - Deut 22:13–29: Gen 34, at both. - Josh 7: Gen 34 first at 50; second at 150, behind Gen 18–19. - Judg 19–20: Gen 18–19 (Sodom), by a wide margin; Gen 34 in the top 8–25 %. - 2 Sam 13: Gen 37–38 (Judah and Tamar); Gen 34 in the top 6–31 %.

Greek. The נְבָלָה (“folly”) chain dissolves — ἄσχημον (Gen 34:7), ἀφροσύνη (Deut 22:21; 2 Kgdms 13:12), ἀνόμημα (Josh 7:15), ἀπόπτωμα (Judg 20:6) — and “trouble” is “made me odious” against “destroyed”. ταπεινόω (“humble, violate”) survives.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. Words high; design moderate. Wording: “Gen 34 is the first place in the canon for Israel’s vocabulary of sexual and communal outrage — ‘folly in Israel’, ‘such a thing is not done’, ‘defile’, ‘trouble’. It is the closest Genesis partner of the virginity laws (Deut 22) and of Achan (Josh 7). For the Levite’s concubine the closer source is Sodom (Gen 19), and for Amnon and Tamar it is Judah and Tamar (Gen 38): there Gen 34 is one source among several.” [S: audit]

G17. Josh 24 and 1 Sam 7:3 at 35:2–4; 33:19

What held. “Put away the foreign gods” as an imperative: Gen 35:2; Josh 24:23; 1 Sam 7:3 only. קְשִׂיטָה (“a unit of value”): Gen 33:19; Josh 24:32; Job 42:11 only; Gen 33:19 and Josh 24:32 also share “the plot of the field” and “the sons of Hamor, the father of Shechem”. The oak at Shechem (35:4; Josh 24:26). [T]

Care. “Among you” differs in each (בְּתֹכְכֶם / בְּקִרְבְּכֶם (“among you”) / מִתּוֹכְכֶם (“from your midst”)), and the two oak words are filed under different lemmas. Shechem’s tree is a site-tradition (12:6; Judg 9:6).

Greek. “Foreign gods” survives, and 1 Sam 7:3 shares ἐκ μέσου ὑμῶν (“from among you”) with Gen 35:2. The oak is τερέμινθος in both Gen 35:4 and Josh 24:26.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. High. [S: audit]

G18. Hos 12:4–5 [Eng 12:3–4]

What held. שָׂרָה (“strive”): Gen 32:29; Hos 12:4 only; Hos 12:5 וַיָּשַׂר (“and he strove”) is filed under another root (with only Judg 9:22), but plays on the sound. יָכֹל (“prevail”) and “with God” are shared. “At Bethel he found him, and there he spoke with us” answers “the place where he spoke with him” (35:13–15). [T]

G19. Luke 15:20

What held. “Fall on the neck” in Hebrew: Gen 33:4; 45:14; 46:29 only; with “run” and “kiss” in the same verse, Gen 33:4 only. [T]

Greek. The exact Lukan phrase ἐπέπεσεν ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον αὐτοῦ (“he fell on his neck”) is Rahlfs Gen 46:29 (father and son); Gen 33:4 has προσέδραμεν … ἐφίλησεν … προσέπεσεν (“ran … kissed … fell on”), different compounds. G21. Ps 20:2 [Eng 20:1]

Critical assessment — the gaps in this audit

  1. History of interpretation was not checked for any claim. Hays’s sixth criterion is empty throughout. The design verdicts most in need of it are G1 (the release law), G15 (Micah) and G16; the questions belong in a Logos round.
  2. Rahlfs exists in the corpus for Genesis only. Every Greek reading of another book is Swete’s. Two findings turn on that: G9 (Matthew against Swete Jeremiah) and G5 (Swete’s Genesis lacks “sit upon” at 31:34). Neither should be cited until Rahlfs or Göttingen has been read for the other book.
  3. Bag-of-words baselines are blind to names and order. They could not test G15, whose evidence is a place name and a sequence; the exclusive phrase and the set test carry it, which is a narrower kind of proof. And their nulls are autocorrelated: “twelve windows ahead” usually means shifted copies of the claimed block.
  4. G1’s composite partner. The release laws were tested whole and in parts, but the reverse test uses contiguous windows, which only approximate a composite of Exodus and Deuteronomy.
  5. Two auditors, not three. Each claim was tested once. They overlapped only on Gen 31:41–42 and Hos 12, and agreed there.
  6. The main session’s reproduction found two auditor errors (the stone count; the Migdal-eder index entry) and one edition fact the auditor had attributed to the wrong edition (Rahlfs, not Swete, at 31:34). None changed a verdict; all three are the kind the positive-control rule exists for.
  7. Blindness. The auditors were barred from the Torah folder, the overview, the sweep, every earlier audit and the session’s own working folder; each kept its scripts in a separate folder of its own. The claims they were given were the queue’s one-line summaries, which can drop a dig’s own qualifications (the Job audit 3 lesson). In two cases the dig had already made the correction the auditor made: the 32–35 dig says the weeping of Hos 12:5 belongs to Gen 33, and it notes the LXX’s moved tower.
