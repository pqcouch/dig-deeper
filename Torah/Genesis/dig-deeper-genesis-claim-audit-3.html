Claim Audit: Genesis — audit 3

Passages audited: Genesis 32:3–33:17 [Heb 32:4–33:17], the claims arising from the dig of 21 November 2026. Date: 21 November 2026 Mode: two blind auditors in turn — A the fourteen single contacts and distributions (G32–G45), B the seven design claims (G46–G52) with block baselines at two or more settings. Neither auditor was permitted to open the dig, the 32:1–35:29 report, the whole-book sweep, the book overview, or either earlier audit. Decisive results reproduced in the main session. Purpose: to test the claims of a fresh, quarantined dig before any of them reaches the book overview or a sermon — and, because the dig was itself a re-cut of ground already covered, to establish which of its findings are new and which the earlier work already held. Logos round: three Study Assistant questions, 21 November 2026, with every load-bearing citation ownership-checked in the Library panel. Results folded in at G35/G47, G46/G51 and under The apparatus question below.

Editions. Every count is from the WLC (Open Scriptures morphhb) reading text and its lemma index; Greek counts from Swete for the Greek OT and the SBLGNT for the New Testament. Citations of record from the Logos exports: BHS, the BHS apparatus, Rahlfs 1979, NASB95, ESV. Where a finding rests on a reading rather than on wording generally, the edition is named at the point of use.

The shape of the result. Fourteen of twenty-one claims hold with their figures reproduced exactly; the damage is concentrated in the designs. Of the seven design claims, one is discarded outright, four need reframing, and the two that survive do so with their anchors moved. Five of the dig’s six Headline Findings are touched. This is the fourth consecutive Genesis audit in which the failures are counts, superlatives and patterns rather than claims about meaning — with one new variety recorded here: a claim that was true of the words and false about the syntax joining them (G46).

Claims audited

G32 — פָּנִים אֶל־פָּנִים stands in five verses; Gen 32:30 is the canon’s first

Source: dig, Tool 7 and Headline 3. Claim: the idiom stands in exactly Gen 32:30, Exod 33:11, Deut 34:10, Judg 6:22, Ezek 20:35.

Verdict: Confirmed. Reproduced by ordered phrase search, plain and skeletal passes identical, and independently by lemma adjacency — “face” + “to” + “face” as three consecutive index tokens — which returns the same five and nothing else. Two methods agreeing means the exclusivity is not an artefact of spelling or of the search.

Nuance the claim must carry. The idiom has a near-twin the claim does not exclude: Deut 5:4 has פָּנִים בְּפָנִים, the only occurrence of that form in the canon. Any statement of exclusivity must say the אֶל form.

Rating now: words high, distribution high. But note what the five do not show: two are seeing a divine figure and surviving (Gen 32:30, Judg 6:22), two are divine speech to Moses (Exod 33:11, Deut 34:10), one is judgement. Gen 32:30 sits with Judg 6:22, not with the Moses pair — which is the finding that undoes G46 and makes G51.

G33 — run + neck + kiss in exactly two verses of the Greek canon

Source: dig, Headline 5 and the reverse table; sharpens the standing queue item G19 (audit 2: “Luke 15:20 — moderate for 33:4; Tob 11:9 a rival”). Claim: Gen 33:4 and Luke 15:20 are the only two verses in Swete and the SBLGNT together carrying a running verb, falling on the neck, and kissing.

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. The exclusivity reproduces — two of the 97 verses carrying τραχηλ-. But the claim is more brittle than it sounds, and the auditor broke it in three places.

What breaks it. The verbatim string ἐπέπεσεν ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον αὐτοῦ matches Gen 46:29 and Tob 11:9, not Gen 33:4. Acts 20:37 — same author — is the closest verse in the Greek Bible to Luke 15:20 on the words. And the “exactly two” is partly an artefact of the verse as the unit: widened to two verses, neck + kiss adds Gen 45:14–15. The set is two because two single elements happen to be missing elsewhere, not because the combination is deeply distinctive.

Rating now: words possible only (down from the dig’s moderate–high); design possible-to-strong on the scene-shape rather than the diction. G19’s “moderate, Tob 11:9 a rival” stands and is not displaced.

G34 — צַוָּאר in six verses of Genesis, three of them Jacob and Esau

Verdict: Confirmed as a distribution — eight occurrences in six verses: 27:16, 27:40, 33:4, 41:42, 45:14, 46:29; 38 verses canon-wide. The three Jacob–Esau verses are confirmed with the senses claimed. The design built on them is G49, and it does not survive.

G35 — יָטַב in nine verses of Genesis, and God never speaks it

Source: dig, Headline 4 and Tool 11 Move 4(e). Verdict: first half Confirmed; second half Needs reframing — as stated it is false.

The distribution reproduces: eleven occurrences in nine verses — 4:7; 12:13, 16; 32:9, 12; 34:18; 40:14; 41:37; 45:16. The verb טוֹב is absent from Genesis entirely, so no second verb confuses the count.

Why the second half fails. Gen 4:7 is direct divine speech. Gen 4:6 opens “and the LORD said to Cain”, and 4:7 continues that speech with no new speech-marker: הֲלוֹא אִם־תֵּיטִיב שְׂאֵת וְאִם לֹא תֵיטִיב — God uses the verb twice in one verse. The first of the nine verses contradicts the claim.

The claim that survives is sharper. God never speaks the verb as a promise in Genesis; the only divine use is conditional and addressed to Cain. And the form Jacob attributes to God — הֵיטֵב אֵיטִיב — stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Gen 32:12. Jacob quotes God using a form God is never recorded as using, anywhere. Of the nine Genesis verses, only 32:9 and 32:12 have God as the subject, and both are in Jacob’s mouth.

Gen 31:3, checked: “Return to the land of your fathers and to your kindred, and I will be with you.” Two substitutions in Jacob’s version at 32:9: “the land of your fathers” becomes “your land”, and “I will be with you” becomes the near-homophone “I will do you good”. Presence becomes profit.

Rating now: distribution high; the “no divine speech” formulation withdrawn; the promise formulation high.

G36 — חוֹל in three verses of Genesis; Jacob was promised dust, not sand

Verdict: Confirmed with nuance. The distribution reproduces — 22:17, 32:12, 41:49 — and 28:14’s promise to Jacob is כַּעֲפַר הָאָרֶץ, dust. Confirmed: Jacob was never promised sand.

The nuance, and it matters for how the dig words it. Jacob’s phrase is כְּחוֹל הַיָּם; Gen 22:17’s is כַּחוֹל אֲשֶׁר עַל־שְׂפַת הַיָּם. They are not the same wording, and an ordered search for Jacob’s phrase returns 32:12, 41:49, Isa 10:22 and Hos 2:1 — not 22:17. So the sand simile is a compression of 22:17, not a quotation of it, and the compressed form is Jacob’s own coinage; within Genesis it recurs only of Joseph’s grain.

Rating now: “the only sand promise in Genesis is Abraham’s at 22:17, which Jacob neither received nor quotes verbatim” — high. The dig’s “the simile Jacob quotes is the one given to Abraham” — withdrawn as wording, kept as substance.

G37 — “which cannot be numbered for multitude” in three verses; Gen 16:10 to Hagar

Verdict: Confirmed. Three verses: Gen 16:10, Gen 32:12, 1 Kgs 3:8, and Gen 16:10 is the angel of the LORD to Hagar, about Ishmael. The reported plain/skeletal behaviour reproduces exactly, and the auditor identified the cause: the conjunctive waw on לֹא. Gen 32:12 has the asyndetic, maqqef-bound לֹא־יִסָּפֵר; Gen 16:10 and 1 Kgs 3:8 both have וְלֹא יִסָּפֵר. Normalising maqqef does not help, because the prefixed waw makes the word a different word; only the skeletal pass’s deletion of waw brings them together. Both recovered verses were read in the text and are genuine presences, not collapses.

Nuance for any statement of exclusivity: the same idiom in the plural stands at 1 Kgs 8:5 and 2 Chr 5:6. Say “in this singular form”.

What the claim walked past, and it is better than the claim. הַרְבָּה אַרְבֶּה stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 3:16 (to the woman, of pain), Gen 16:10 (to Hagar), Gen 22:17 (to Abraham) — and Gen 16:10’s first half is therefore shared with Gen 22:17. So Jacob’s sentence welds the counting clause spoken to Hagar about Ishmael onto the sand simile spoken to Abraham about Isaac’s line. Queued as G53.

G38 — פָּגַשׁ twice in Genesis, both inside; פָּגַע outside

Verdict: Confirmed. פָּגַשׁ: two verses in Genesis, 32:17 and 33:8, both inside the passage; 14 canon-wide; in the Torah only those two plus Exod 4:24 and 4:27. פָּגַע: three verses in Genesis — 23:8, 28:11, 32:1 — and zero inside the passage.

The correction the claim needs. The dig says “פָּגַע stands at 32:1, immediately outside”. It also stands at 28:11, at Bethel. And that is the better observation: פָּגַע at 28:11 outbound and 32:1 returning brackets the whole Mesopotamian sojourn — which supports the preceding Masoretic block as a real unit rather than supporting this one’s boundary. Queued as G54.

G39 — חָצָה twice in Genesis, both inside

Verdict: Confirmed. Two verses, 32:7 and 33:1; 14 canon-wide. The only other Torah uses are legal apportionment (Exod 21:35; Num 31:27, 42), which makes the Genesis pair distinctively narrative.

G40 — קָטֹן as a verb in four verses; 32:10 the only first person

Verdict: Confirmed; the root-play Confirmed with nuance as a resonance only.

The distribution reproduces — Gen 32:10, 2 Sam 7:19, Amos 8:5, 1 Chr 17:17 — and 32:10 is the only first-person form. Sharper than the claim: Gen 32:10 is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where a person is the subject of the verb. In 2 Sam 7:19 and 1 Chr 17:17 the subject is “this thing”; in Amos the object is an ephah. “I have become small” has no parallel.

The root-play with הַקָּטָן (27:15, 42) rates weak on words. One shared root, two Strong’s entries, two parts of speech, no shared phrase; and the adjective stands in 19 verses of Genesis, so its recurrence carries little information. The clause Jacob is in — “I am too small for all the ḥesed and all the faithfulness you have shown your servant” — is the covenantal self-abasement idiom, and its two closest relatives (2 Sam 7:19 // 1 Chr 17:17) sit in exactly that setting: a man confronted with promised ḥesed saying he is not equal to it. One further datum the dig did not have: Gen 25:23’s oracle uses רַב / צָעִיר, not קָטָן — so the קָטָן vocabulary for Jacob is Rebekah’s alone, in one chapter.

Rating now: the verb’s rarity and unique personal subject, high; the root-play, possible — offer it as an overtone, and hang the chapter-27 recall on בִּרְכָתִי at 33:11, which is securely that chapter’s vocabulary.

G41 — Succoth: Gen 33:17 first in the canon, Exod 12:37 next

Verdict: distribution Confirmed; the implied identification Needs reframing.

Eighteen occurrences in sixteen verses, Gen 33:17 first, Exod 12:37 and 13:20 next. But the texts place the two in different countries. Gen 33:17 is reached after crossing the Jabbok, and 33:18 marks a fresh arrival at Shechem “in the land of Canaan” — so Succoth is outside Canaan, east of the Jordan; Josh 13:27 puts it among Gad’s towns “beyond the Jordan eastward”; Judg 8:4–5 has Gideon cross the Jordan and then reach it; 1 Kgs 7:46 puts it in the Jordan plain. Exod 12:37 and Num 33:5–6 put their Succoth in Egypt, between Rameses and Etham. Eleven of sixteen occurrences are Transjordan or the Jordan valley; four are Egypt; Genesis belongs with the eleven.

And the coincidence has a mundane explanation the dig did not give: סֻכּוֹת is a common noun, “booths”, and Gen 33:17 supplies an etymology local to itself — using both the common noun and the place-name in one verse, the only verse in Genesis with the common noun.

Rating now: words weak; design possible, as canonical echo only (down from the dig’s moderate). What may be said: the name Israel’s first camp out of Egypt bears is the name Israel’s ancestor gave to his first camp back in the land. What may not: that they are the same place, or that either is named for the other.

G42 — Penuel and Succoth both punished by Gideon in one campaign

Verdict: Confirmed. All eleven Judges occurrences of the two names fall inside Judg 8:4–17, a single pursuit. Judges 8 is the only chapter in the Hebrew Bible holding both place-names.

A method note worth keeping. The phrase search for פְּנִיאֵל is a clean illustration of the skeletal-pass trap in reverse: the plain pass returns Gen 32:30 alone, the skeletal pass returns all eight verses — because deleting waw and yod makes Pᵉnîʾēl and Pᵉnûʾēl indistinguishable. Those seven extra hits are not occurrences of the spelling Peniel. Genesis spells the name two ways in consecutive verses, and every other verse in the canon uses the second.

G43 — the Jabbok in seven verses, six of them boundaries

Verdict: Confirmed, with one qualification. Four of the six later verses carry גְּבוּל in the same clause; all six are statements of territorial limit; the soft case is Deut 2:37, where the Jabbok marks what Israel was forbidden to approach — a boundary by function, not by label. Five of the six name the Arnon in the same breath, so the Jabbok is almost never a lone datum but the northern term of a fixed Transjordan pair.

What the claim did not reach. The other side of that line is, in every one of the six, the sons of Ammon or the Amorite who held their land — and Ammon descends from Lot. The Jabbok is a kinship boundary in the later texts, which is what Gen 32–33 is about. Queued as G55.

G44 — the covenant name once; Elohim seven times

Verdict: Confirmed on the counts; the contrast the pair invites half fails.

Both figures reproduce exactly: יְהוָה once, at 32:9, against 163 occurrences in Genesis; אֱלֹהִים seven times in six verses. But the extent is 3.07 % of Genesis, so the pro-rata expectation for Elohim is 6.7 against 7 observed — exactly the book’s own rate. Only the YHWH figure is a datum, and on one cell with an expected count of five it is suggestive rather than significant.

The sharper form, which the dig did not have. The single יְהוָה is on Jacob’s lips, not the narrator’s — and so is every one of the seven אֱלֹהִים. No divine name of any kind appears in the narrator’s own voice anywhere in Gen 32:3–33:17. Exactly reproducible, and it does not depend on a rate comparison that half fails.

G45 — פָּנִים fifteen times; 32:20 one of four verses with four or more

Verdict: Confirmed, with a counting convention that must be declared.

Fifteen occurrences in nine verses; the canon-wide census of verses with four or more returns exactly the claimed four: Ezek 10:14 (×7), Ezek 1:10 (×5), Gen 32:20 (×4), Isa 21:15 (×4). Read for sense: Gen 32:20 has three substantive and one prepositional; Ezekiel’s twelve are all substantive (the cherubim’s faces, a catalogue); Isaiah’s four are all the preposition מִפְּנֵי.

The convention. The figure “four” counts לְפָנָי as an instance — morphologically correct, and audibly right since all four end alike. But count only the substantive and Gen 32:20 has three, tying with Exod 34:35, 1 Kgs 6:3 and Ezek 10:22 — and Exod 34:35 is narrative prose. So “four occurrences, matched or exceeded in only three other verses” is true on the lemma count; “the only narrative one” is true on the lemma count and false on the substantive-only count. The dig must say which count it is using.

The better internal observation: the passage turns on its own axis — “face” is a direction of travel in the first half and a thing seen in the second, with 32:20 the hinge holding both senses in one sentence.

G46 — the Moses cluster: convergence on Exodus 32–34

Source: dig, Headline 3. Verdict: DISCARD as a design claim. The two distributions survive as distributions.

First, a syntactic correction that also touches a standing queue item. The claim describes “אוּלַי with the piel of כָּפַר — perhaps I may make atonement”. That collocation exists only at Exod 32:30. At Gen 32:20 the אוּלַי does not govern אֲכַפְּרָה at all; it governs יִשָּׂא פָנָי at the end of the verse — “perhaps he will lift up my face”. The NASB95 renders the syntax correctly, and so does the Greek: Rahlfs 1979 has ἴσως γὰρ προσδέξεται τὸ πρόσωπόν μου at Gen 32:20 and ἵνα ἐξιλάσωμαι with no ἴσως at Exod 32:30. The two verses share two words in a verse, not an idiom.

This corrects standing queue item G23, entered by audit 2 on 25 October 2026 and rated high (words): “Exod 32:30 — ‘perhaps I may atone’ (אוּלַי + אֲכַפְּרָה), only Gen 32:21 / Exod 32:30”. The co-occurrence is right and reproduces; the gloss, which implies a shared idiom, is wrong. G23 is reworded in the queue and its rating held at high for the co-occurrence only. Audit 2’s filed text is left standing, per the project’s practice of recording a correction in the newer document rather than editing the older — as Round 2 did with Round 1’s stale status table.

The alternative the brief asked for turns out to be generous to the claim. It is not that Exod 32–34 is such a rich theophany-vocabulary field that everything appears to converge there — it does not even rank as a good general partner. The honest description is narrower and worse: two isolated lexical contacts, with no surrounding contact at all. A partner that ranks 2nd at one fine setting and 93rd at chapter level has no stable signal.

G47 — Jacob’s assembled promise

Source: dig, Headline 4 and Move 4(e). Verdict: Needs reframing. The constituents are confirmed; the design is real but is a Genesis-wide convention, not a feature of this passage.

Constituents, each verified: וְשַׂמְתִּי אֶת־זַרְעֲךָ stands in exactly two verses — Gen 13:16 (to Abram, a dust promise) and Gen 32:12; כְּחוֹל הַיָּם is a compression of 22:17 and not a quotation of it (G36); לֹא יִסָּפֵר מֵרֹב is the Hagar clause (G37); 28:14 gave Jacob dust; 31:3 said “I will be with you”; הֵיטֵב אֵיטִיב stands in one verse of the canon.

The strongest datum, which the dig did not have, and it is local rather than cross-referential. Three verses earlier Jacob has already quoted the command and already altered it: at 32:9 “the land of your fathers” has become “your land”, and “and I will be with you” has become the near-homophone “and I will do you good” — a cohortative. By 32:12 that cohortative has become the infinitive absolute, “I will surely do you good”. The escalation happens inside one prayer, across three verses. That is far better evidence of deliberate composition than the cross-references are.

The control that decides the claim. How do other Genesis characters quote earlier divine speech? Every case checked is inexact, and several are composite in the same way:

  • Gen 3:2–3, Eve quoting 2:16–17 — the Torah’s first quotation of divine speech: the infinitive absolute dropped, “fruit” added, “every” dropped, the tree re-identified from 2:9, a clause added (“neither shall you touch it”), and “you shall surely die” weakened. Jacob performs the mirror-image operation: he adds an infinitive absolute.
  • Gen 48:3–4, Jacob again — attributes to one appearance at Luz a composite of Gen 35:11 (El Shaddai, be fruitful and multiply, a company) and Gen 17:8 (an everlasting possession, said to Abraham), while 28:13 names YHWH, not El Shaddai. The same operation as 32:12, by the same speaker.
  • Gen 24, three versions of one speech in one chapter (24:3–4 against 24:38; “in its midst” becoming “in his land” at 24:37; and 24:40 adding “and prosper your way”, which Abraham never said at 24:7).
  • Gen 24:7, Abraham calling a promise an oath when the only oath in Genesis says nothing about land.
  • Gen 50:24, Joseph citing an oath to Isaac and Jacob that Genesis never narrates.
  • Even God quotes selectively: at 26:4 he takes the stars of 22:17 and leaves the sand.

So the technique is a convention of Genesis speech. Gen 32:12 is the densest and most self-serving instance, not a singular designed move.

History of interpretation — the Logos round’s strongest return. Three commentators notice the misquotation, and they split on motive. Mathews (Genesis 11:27–50:26, NAC 1b:551) reads it charitably: “I will make you prosper” paraphrases the earlier promises of divine presence. Two others read it as manipulation — one calling it “a subtle attempt to redefine God’s words to his advantage”, the other noting that the recorded words of the Lord to Jacob “contain no such thing”. Ownership check: Mathews is in the library; the two manipulative readings are from works that are not. So the only verifiable witness among the three takes the charitable reading, which argues for keeping both readings open rather than tilting to the critical one. None of the three mentions the sand, the dust, or the Hagar clause — the lexical substitutions appear to be unremarked.

A reader-response datum that strengthens the perception of the gap. Swete’s Genesis (Vaticanus) reads at Gen 28:14 ὡς ἡ ἄμμος τῆς θαλάσσης — “as the sand of the SEA” — where Rahlfs 1979 reads τῆς γῆς and no Hebrew witness reads sand at all (the BHS apparatus at 28:14 has only one note, on the nations-blessing clause). A Greek manuscript tradition harmonised Bethel to Jacob’s quotation, making the quotation accurate. That is ancient readers noticing precisely this discrepancy. Cite it as a Vaticanus-tradition reading, never as “the LXX”.

Two further Greek notes: the וְשַׂמְתִּי link does not survive into Greek (Rahlfs has ποιήσω at 13:16, θήσω at 32:12); the Hagar link does (οὐκ ἀριθμηθήσεται ἀπὸ τοῦ πλήθους against οὐκ ἀριθμήσεται ἀπὸ τοῦ πλήθους).

G48 — Hosea reads the passage as one episode

Source: dig, Headline 1 ground (iv), described there as “the single best argument for the extent”. Verdict: the dependence Confirmed; the unit-delimiting use DISCARDED.

What verified. שָׂרָה stands in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 32:28 and Hos 12:3 — confirmed, and the Greek strengthens it, since Rahlfs and Swete render both with the same verb. So Hos 12:3 depends on Gen 32:28; that much is secure.

Why the delimiting use fails — five independent reasons.

  1. The weeping is a formula, not a fingerprint. נָפַל + צַוָּאר + בָּכָה stands in exactly three verses of the canon, all Genesis: 33:4, 45:14, 46:29. At 45:14 the weeping verb is בָּכָה, singular — Hosea’s exact form; at 46:29 it is also singular. Hosea’s singular matches the Joseph reunions better than it matches Gen 33:4, where the text reads “and they wept”, plural.
  2. The apparatus cuts the other way from what the claim needs. BHS carries a bare conjecture at Gen 33:4 — read “and he wept”, singular — and no note at all on Hosea’s singular. So the contested reading is MT’s plural in Genesis, not MT’s singular in Hosea; and the conjecture has no versional support, Swete reading “they both wept”.
  3. The Greek of Hosea is corporate and plural — “they wept and besought me… they found me, and there it was spoken to them.” An ancient reader took the verse of Israel the people, not of Jacob the man in one scene.
  4. Hosea 12 ranges across the whole cycle, and within a single verse. Hos 12:3 has “in the womb he grasped his brother’s heel” — עָקַב, whose Genesis verse is 27:36 — before it has “he strove with God”. So v.3 moves from Gen 25/27 to Gen 32 inside one verse. And v.4 ends “at Bethel he found him”, which is Gen 28 or 35, neither in the proposed unit; 12:12 is Aram, 12:13 the prophet. Hosea 12 is a compressed biography; it cannot delimit anything.
  5. Hosea has almost no measurable contact with this passage. Of 813 chapters ranked by rare-contact density, Hos 12 ranks 548th at the tight threshold and 352nd at the loose one — and its single rare contact is with Gen 32:3, the passage’s outer frame, not with the Jabbok or the embrace.

Also: Hosea’s second “strove” is a different Hebrew verb (a lemma found only at Hos 12:4 and Judg 9:22), so “he strove with God… he strove with the angel” renders two distinct verbs and only the first is the Genesis word. BHS at Hos 12:4 further proposes that “angel” is a gloss.

Rating now: the dependence on Gen 32:28, high; as evidence for the extent of the unit, withdrawn entirely. Hosea is an early witness to the naming scene, inside a prophetic compression of a whole life.

G49 — the neck as a designed Jacob–Esau arc

Source: dig, Headline 5. Verdict: Needs reframing — the arc is discarded; a tighter formula replaces it.

The auditor censused all 38 canon verses of צַוָּאר by idiom: yoke or subjugation 18, ornament worn on the neck 7, water or ruin “up to the neck” 3, pride or defiance 3, tower simile 2, falling on the neck 3, clothing laid on the neck 1, bodies of the slain 1.

The claimed arc cross-cuts three unrelated idioms. 27:16 is a clothing verse; 27:40 is the canon’s commonest neck idiom, the yoke, 18 of 38 verses; 33:4 is an embrace verse. Nothing but the bare noun joins them — and the distribution shows that yokes and embraces are simply where necks occur, in Hebrew as in English.

What is real, and exclusive:

  • נָפַל עַל־צַוָּאר stands in exactly three verses of the whole Hebrew Bible, all in Genesis: 33:4, 45:14, 46:29 — and all three weep. Add the weeping verb and the set is unchanged. The link holds in Greek too.
  • לָבַשׁ + צַוָּאר stands in three canon verses: Gen 27:16, Gen 41:42, Job 39:19. So 27:16 pairs with 41:42 — deception clothed, then investiture clothed — and not with 33:4.

Rating now: the six-verse Genesis distribution, high; the Jacob–Esau arc, discarded; the reconciliation formula — נָפַל עַל־צַוָּאר with weeping, three verses, Genesis only, 33:4 its first instance — confirmed, high.

What the claim also missed: the kiss at 33:4 carries the puncta extraordinaria and BHS’s note reads frt add cf punct extr, “perhaps an addition”; and BHS note ᵃ records a ketiv/qere at the same verse, the ketiv singular “his neck”, the qere of some manuscripts plural. A claim about the embrace at 33:4 that does not mention these is incomplete.

G50 — the extent as a four-ground convergence

Source: dig, Headline 1. Verdict: Confirmed with nuance for the extent; Needs reframing for the convergence argument.

Ground (i), the Masoretic boundaries — confirmed, verified twice independently. The WLC prints a bare פ closing Heb 32:3 and a bare ס closing 33:17; independently confirmed in the OSIS source as an x-pe milestone and an x-samekh milestone. No marker of any kind stands between, and the full marker census for Genesis 30–37 is: 32:3 (petuchah), 33:17, 33:20, 34:31, 35:8, 35:22, 35:29, 36:19, 36:30, 36:43, 37:36 — with chapters 30 and 31 carrying none at all.

But the nuance inverts part of the argument. Genesis has 93 such blocks, with a median of 8 verses; this one of 47 is the 7th longest; and the block immediately preceding it runs 148 verses — Gen 28:10 to 32:3, the longest in the book. The Masoretes are paragraphing very coarsely here, so “no division in 47 verses” is not positive evidence of unity. What is evidence is the two boundaries, and they are exactly where the dig puts them.

And the chapter divisions disagree with the paragraphing at the top, in both systems. The petuchah closes Eng 32:2; the English chapter break falls two verses earlier, the Hebrew chapter break three verses earlier, and both fall inside the 148-verse block. The Masoretic layout assigns the Mahanaim angels to the Laban unit, against both chapter systems — which is a real argument for the dig’s choice of 32:3 over the commoner 32:1.

Grounds (ii) and (iii) do not support the stated extent. Both are “a rare word recurs across the span”, which is one method counted twice. Baselined against every 47-verse window of Genesis (1,487 of them), asking how many lemmas occur nowhere else in Genesis and recur across the window, proper nouns excluded:

Setting This window Rank Windows beating it
One occurrence in each half 2 233 of 1,487 180
One in the first and last quarter (a true inclusio) 0 853 of 1,487 126

The quarters result is a diagnosis rather than a null: neither frame word reaches the ends of the unit. חָצָה spans verses 5 and 31 of 47; פָּגַשׁ spans 15 and 38; the “four hundred men” pair brackets Eng 32:6–33:1 (and is not verbatim — the maqqef and the position of “with him” differ). The lexical frame encloses roughly 32:6–33:1, the approach and the Jabbok, leaving the first three verses and the last sixteen outside it.

Ground (iv) is discarded — see G48.

Independence — where the convergence argument fails. (ii) and (iii) are the same method twice, drawn from the same small pool of unusual words. (ii), (iii) and (iv) each delimit something narrower than, and different from, the claimed extent — 32:17–33:8, 32:6–33:1, and 32:22–33:11 respectively. Only (i) sets the claimed boundaries. A convergence requires the lines to converge on the same extent, and these do not.

Rating now: the extent 32:3–33:17 rests on one ground — the Masoretic boundaries — and that ground is strong. Grounds (ii) and (iii) are restated as evidence of internal cohesion around the Jabbok. Ground (iv) goes. Two further Genesis-exclusive recurring lemmas the dig did not count: Penuel and אָבַק — four in all, not two.

But the geographical confound is real and must be conceded, and on the claim’s own five items the verdict would be “words, and geography, and no demonstrated design”: Succoth and Penuel are the two towns on the Jabbok route, so any Transjordan pursuit names them; Gideon’s necks are camel ornaments, not embraces; only חָצָה matches in sense.

The item that rescues it, and it was in none of the claims. כִּי עַל־כֵּן רָאִיתִי stands in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 33:10 and Judg 6:22 — confirmed by two independent methods that agree, an ordered phrase search with plain and skeletal passes identical, and a four-lemma co-occurrence search. The particle chain כִּי עַל־כֵּן itself stands in nine canon verses, and in only these two is it followed by “I have seen” — and in both the object is a face in which God is seen. Judg 6:22 fuses the particle chain and verb of Gen 33:10 with the idiom of Gen 32:30, and v.23’s “you shall not die” answers 32:30’s “my life was delivered.” The BHS apparatus has no note at Judg 6:22, so the text is secure.

This settles the rivalry with G46: among the five “face to face” verses, Judg 6:22 is demonstrably the closest to this passage and Exod 33:11 is not. The chapter ranking agrees — Judg 6 is 5th and 17th of 813, Judg 7 is 47th and 12th, while Judg 8 is 163rd and 227th.

History of interpretation — thin. The Logos round found no commentator arguing the link. What exists: the Holman cross-reference apparatus links Gen 32:30 directly to Judg 6:22, and links Judg 13:22 to Gen 33:10; and a dictionary article groups Exod 33:20, Judg 6:22–23 and Judg 13:22 as the “seeing God is mortal danger” motif — but that work is not in the library, so it cannot be cited. The two-verse collocation appears unremarked.

The Greek cannot carry the place-name half: Rahlfs and Swete translate Peniel as Εἶδος θεοῦ at Gen 32:30–31 while Judg 8:8 transliterates Φανουήλ, so the Penuel link is invisible in Greek.

Rating now: words high at Judg 6:22; design moderate, capped pending a second independent pass. The anchor moves from Gideon’s campaign to Gideon’s theophany. Judges 8’s two place-names corroborate at the level of setting, not of design.

Verdict: Needs reframing. The foundation is exact; “nearest” fails.

Verified: פָּגַשׁ in the Torah stands only at Gen 32:17, Gen 33:8, Exod 4:24 and Exod 4:27 — and Exod 4:14/24–27 carries with it “to meet” (לִקְרַאת, as at Gen 32:6 and 33:4), “kissed” (נָשַׁק, as at 33:4), “touched” with a lower-body euphemism (נָגַע “at his feet”, as at Gen 32:25, 32 of the thigh), a night lodging on the road home, a brother, and “the mountain of God” against “the camp of God”.

The apparatus question — and why the gate is still shut

The dig’s puncta extraordinaria finding at Gen 33:4 (six upper dots, one over every letter of “and he kissed him”; BHS’s note frt add cf punct extr) was tagged [unchecked — apparatus spread] and barred from the book overview under gate item (e4), because a corpus holding one manuscript tradition cannot report how widely an apparatus feature is attested. The Logos round did not open that gate.

What the round returned that is citable from owned works: the dots originated as scribal notations marking material for deletion, a convention of many Qumran texts, and were reappropriated within the Masoretic corpus to indicate doubtful letters, as ʾAbot de Rabbi Nathan A 34 explains (Tov, “Textual Criticism: Old Testament”, Anchor Yale Bible Dictionary 6:397 — owned); and Butin held them to be as old as the Christian era and probably marking a letter for deletion (Gesenius’ Hebrew Grammar, ed. Kautzsch and Cowley, p. 31 — owned).

What the round returned that cannot be used: the one answer to the spread question — that medieval Hebrew manuscripts from around AD 920–950 onward consistently display the dots — rests on a single source not in the library, and so does the suggestion that the dots express the scribes’ own incredulity at the reunion. Nothing in the library reached Genesis Rabbah or Sifre on the point.

So the finding stays exactly where it was: witness named (“in Leningrad, as the WLC prints it”; “as BHS prints it”), stated as reading tradition rather than authorial, tagged [unchecked — apparatus spread], capped at moderate, and barred from the overview. A printed Ginsburg, or dedicated Masoretic scholarship, would be needed to settle it.
