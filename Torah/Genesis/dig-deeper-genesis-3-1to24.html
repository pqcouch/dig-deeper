Headline Findings

  1. Neither the man nor the woman is cursed. [T] verified Surfaced by: Vocabulary (Tool 7), Structure (Tool 3), Linking Words, Difficult Verses. ʾārar occurs twice in the chapter — 3:14, of the serpent, and 3:17, of the ground. The sentences on the woman (3:16) and the man (3:17–19) are consequences announced, not curses pronounced, and the text is careful about which word it uses where. A sermon that says “God cursed Adam and Eve” is contradicting the vocabulary at the one point where the chapter is most precise.

  2. The word for “naked” changes at the fall, and the change is total. [T] verified Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Original Languages, Tool 11 Move 4, Tone. 2:25 has ʿārôm (6174) — its only occurrence in Genesis, and the word that puns with ʿārûm “crafty” (6175, 3:1, also its only occurrence in Genesis). From 3:7 the narrator switches to a different lemma, ʿêrōm (5903), at 3:7, 3:10 and 3:11. Elsewhere in the canon that second word sits in Deuteronomy’s curse-list (28:48) and six times in Ezekiel’s oracles of shameful exposure. The pre-fall word was a pun; the post-fall word is a judgement word.

  3. In the temptation dialogue the covenant name is never spoken. [T] verified Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Narrator’s Comment. The narrator says YHWH ʾĕlōhîm eleven times in Genesis 2 and again at 3:1, 8, 9, 13, 14, 21, 22, 23. But inside the conversation of 3:1–5 the name is absent: the serpent says “has ʾĕlōhîm said” (3:1), the woman says “ʾĕlōhîm has said” (3:3), the serpent says “ʾĕlōhîm knows” and “like ʾĕlōhîm” (3:5). Neither speaker uses the covenant name once. The name returns at 3:8, when its bearer arrives.

  4. The chapter is about eating. [T] verified Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Tone, Tool 16. ʾākal occurs seventeen times in twenty-four verses — more than any other word in the chapter and more than “tree” (11), “woman” (13) or “serpent” (5). Every movement turns on it: the prohibition quoted (3:1–3), the promise (3:5), the act (3:6), the interrogation (3:11–13), and three of the sentences (3:14, 17, 18–19, 22). The serpent eats dust; the man eats bread in sweat.

  5. The guard who failed is replaced by guards who do not fail — and they are the tabernacle’s. [T] verified Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Tool 11 (forward-within-Torah), Biblical Theology, Schnittjer. The man was put in the garden lə-šomrāh, “to keep it” (2:15). The chapter’s single occurrence of šāmar is 3:24, where the cherubim are stationed lišmōr, “to keep the way to the tree of life”. And Gen 3:24 is the first occurrence of kərûb in the canon; the next eleven are all Exodus 25–36, the ark and the curtains. The creatures posted at Eden’s gate reappear over the mercy seat.

Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

Section. Torah, first book; the second half of the first toledot panel (2:4–4:26). [T]

Reading implication. The canonical reader arrives here having read exactly two units and nothing else. Every term the chapter turns on has been defined within the last fifty verses: the garden, the command, the trees, the man, the woman, the ground. Nothing is imported.

Presupposes. 1:1–2:3 and 2:4–25 — and this is the first passage in the canon for which that field is not empty. Specifically: the command (2:16–17), the trees (2:9), the naming authority (2:19–20), the woman’s making (2:21–22), the vocation to serve and keep (2:15), and the closing state of unashamed nakedness (2:25). Every one of those is load-bearing here.

Handoff. To 4:1–26 — Eve bearing “with the help of YHWH” (4:1), the ground that will open its mouth for blood (4:11), and təšûqāh + māšal returning at 4:7 in a moral key.

Neighbours. 2:4–25 precedes; 4:1–26 follows.

Canonical / compositional divergence. None at passage level.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. [S: overview] Genesis asks whether the blessing can outrun the curse and through whom.

Passage-level. This chapter creates the problem the rest of the book exists to answer. It does three things and no more: it narrates how the command was broken; it records what God said in consequence; and it plants, inside the first of those sentences, a promise of enmity and a bruised head. [T]

What it conspicuously does not do. It does not explain the serpent’s origin, motive or identity; it does not moralise; it does not tell the reader what the tree of knowledge was; and it offers no theodicy. [T] The narrator’s restraint is near-total — he supplies four short asides and otherwise reports speech and action. Any sermon that answers the questions the chapter declines to answer has left the text.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 2:4–25 gave the garden, the vocation to serve and keep, the single prohibition with its stated penalty, the delegated naming, the woman built and brought, and — as its last word — the two of them naked and unashamed. [T]

Necessity answer — why here? Because everything this chapter destroys was established in the previous one, item for item, and in the same order:

Established in 2:4–25 Undone or reversed in 3:1–24
The command, with permission vastly larger than prohibition (2:16–17) Quoted back with the permission shrunk and the prohibition enlarged (3:1–3)
Naked and not ashamed, mutually (2:25) Naked, known, covered, hidden (3:7–10) — and with a different word
God walking in the garden — implied by 2:15’s placement God walking in the garden, and the pair hiding from him (3:8)
The man names the animals and the woman (2:19–23) The man names his wife again, and this time after judgement (3:20)
Put in the garden to keep it (2:15) Driven out, and cherubim posted to keep the way (3:24)
The ground the man was taken from and put to serve (2:5, 7, 15) The ground cursed, and the man returned to it (3:17–19, 23)

Implication. The chapter is not a free-standing story about temptation. It is the systematic dismantling of the preceding unit, and the preacher who has not preached chapter 2 cannot preach chapter 3 without supplying it first. The reversal-table above is the sermon’s structural spine if the series has taken 2:4–25 the week before.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 4 confirms and extends this: the plants of the two preceding units (the nakedness word, the command, the ʾādām/ʾădāmāh pun, the tree of life, the šāmar charge) all detonate here, and none of them detonates anywhere else.

General context

Immediately before: 2:25, the unashamed pair — joined to 3:1 by the ʿărûmmîm/ʿārûm sound-pair. [T] verified Immediately after: 4:1, Eve bearing Cain. The chapter’s last word is exclusion; the next chapter’s first act is generation.

Commonly read out of context. 3:16b (“your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you”) is regularly lifted as a statement of created order. It sits inside a sentence of consequence following a transgression, and the same rare word-pair (təšûqāh + māšal) recurs eighteen verses later at 4:7, of sin’s desire for Cain and his duty to master it. [T] verified — təšûqāh occurs 3× in the whole Hebrew Bible: Gen 3:16, 4:7, Song 7:11.

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
3:1–5 The question and the contradiction The serpent’s opening question; the woman’s answer; the flat denial and the counter-promise
3:6–7 The taking, and the opening Saw, took, ate, gave; eyes opened; the new knowledge is nakedness; fig leaves
3:8–13 The hiding and the interrogation God walking; the pair hidden; four questions; two answers, each ending “and I ate”
3:14–19 The sentences Serpent, woman, man — in the order of the transgression
3:20–21 Naming and clothing The man names his wife; God makes garments and clothes them
3:22–24 The expulsion The divine deliberation; sent out; driven out; the way guarded

Device: two chains, run in opposite directions. [T] The transgression runs serpent → woman → man (3:1–6). The interrogation runs the other way, up the chain: God addresses the man (3:9–12), then the woman (3:13), and never questions the serpent at all. The sentencing then runs back down in the original order: serpent (3:14–15), woman (3:16), man (3:17–19). The serpent gets no question because it is given no opportunity to answer; the humans get questions because they are being invited to speak. That asymmetry is doing theological work and it is visible in the arrangement alone.

Two hinges, both marked by a verb of seeing or knowing. 3:6 (“the woman saw… and took”) and 3:7 (“their eyes were opened, and they knew”). The chapter’s turn is not the eating but the knowing that follows it. [T]

On the “chiasm”. The whole-book sweep called this chapter “a tight chiastic movement of approach/withdrawal”. [S: sweep] The observation that God’s questions mirror the serpent’s question is real and worth keeping. But the Schnittjer guardrail applies — structure is the vehicle, never the object; do not force patterns; the centre of a chiasm is not automatically the point — and the two-chains description above accounts for the same data without requiring a centre. This run reports the chains and declines the chiasm. Moderate confidence that the chiastic label over-reads.

4. Linking Words

  • 3:1 ʾap kî — “Indeed, has God said…?” [T] The chapter’s first word out of a character’s mouth is a particle of uncertain force — “even though”, “indeed”, “is it really that”. English versions must choose, and every choice is an interpretation. What is clear is that it is not a straight question: it is an insinuation with a question mark on the end, and the NASB95’s “Indeed, has God said” preserves the oddity better than a smoother rendering would.
  • 3:5 kî — “For God knows…” [T] The serpent’s only stated reason, and it is a claim about divine motive. The whole temptation rests on this one causal clause.
  • 3:14 and 3:17 kî — “Because you have done this” / “Because you have listened”. [T] The two sentences that use the curse-word both begin by naming the ground of the sentence. The woman’s sentence (3:16) has no such clause: it opens without a stated reason. That asymmetry is in the text and is worth noticing before anyone builds on 3:16.
  • 3:22–23 pen … wə — “lest he stretch out his hand… therefore the LORD God sent him out”. [T] The expulsion’s stated motive is preventive, not punitive: it is to stop him eating from the tree of life and living for ever in this condition. The connector makes that explicit and most sermons get it the other way round.

5. Parallels

Prose, but the three sentences (3:14–19) are cast in elevated, quasi-poetic form — short cola, parallel members, no wayyiqtol chain. [T] The clearest case is 3:15:

וְאֵיבָ֣ה אָשִׁ֗ית בֵּֽינְךָ֙ וּבֵ֣ין הָֽאִשָּׁ֔ה וּבֵ֥ין זַרְעֲךָ֖ וּבֵ֣ין זַרְעָ֑הּ ה֚וּא יְשׁוּפְךָ֣ רֹ֔אשׁ וְאַתָּ֖ה תְּשׁוּפֶ֥נּוּ עָקֵֽב

Two synonymous cola establishing the enmity at two levels (persons, then seeds), then a chiastic third: he — bruise-you — head ‖ you — bruise-him — heel. [T] The same verb both ways; only the body part changes. The parallelism is what makes the asymmetry audible: identical action, different target, and head and heel are not equivalent.

3:19 is likewise chiastic at its close: “for dust you are, and to dust you shall return” — the sentence ends where the man began (2:7).

6. Narrator’s Comment

Four intrusions in twenty-four verses — the narrator is more restrained here than anywhere in the two preceding units. [T]

  • 3:1a “Now the serpent was more crafty than any beast of the field which the LORD God had made.” [T] The chapter’s only characterisation, and it does two things at once: it makes the serpent shrewd, and it makes it a creature. The relative clause is not decoration — it forecloses dualism in eight words before the serpent has spoken.
  • 3:6b “…and she gave also to her husband with her, and he ate.” [T] Two words (ʿimmāh) that most sermons pass over. The man is present. The narrator does not say he was elsewhere and returned; he says he was with her. High confidence on the wording; what it implies about his passivity is an inference and is flagged as such. [I]
  • 3:7b “…and they knew that they were naked.” [T] The narrator reporting the content of the promised knowledge. The serpent said their eyes would be opened and they would know good and evil; the narrator reports what they actually knew.
  • 3:20b “…because she was the mother of all the living.” [T] The narrator explaining the name — and doing so in the past tense about a woman who has not yet borne a child, immediately after a sentence of death. It is the one hopeful aside in the chapter and the narrator supplies it himself.

What the narrator does not say. He offers no verdict on the man, no verdict on the woman, and no identification of the serpent. [T] In a book that will later tell us plainly that “Esau despised his birthright” (25:34), the silence here is a choice.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. Eden (3:23, 24) and Ḥawwāh/Eve (3:20). Two, and one of them is coined in the passage. [T] The chapter has almost no onomastic freight — no place is entered, no people named. Eden was established in the preceding unit; Ḥawwāh is new and is treated below. Routine: none. This is a nil-return for the Tool 7c hyperlink machinery, and the third consecutive unit for which that is true.

(a) ʿārûm (6175) “crafty” and ʿārôm / ʿêrōm (6174 / 5903) “naked” — the pun, and the word that replaces it. [T] verified High confidence. This is the chapter’s most consequential lexical fact and it is invisible in translation.

Word Lemma Occurrences in Genesis Elsewhere
ʿărûmmîm “naked” (2:25) 6174 1 — 2:25 only 16 in the WLC: Job, Isaiah, Hosea, Amos, Micah, Ecclesiastes
ʿārûm “crafty” (3:1) 6175 1 — 3:1 only Proverbs, mostly, of the prudent
ʿêrummim / ʿêrōm “naked” (3:7, 10, 11) 5903 3 — 3:7, 3:10, 3:11 10 in the WLC; Deut 28:48 (the curse-list) and six times in Ezekiel 16, 18, 23 (oracles of shameful exposure)

So: the narrator uses the pun-word once, immediately before the serpent; the serpent is described with its homophone; and from the moment the eyes are opened the narrator never uses the pun-word again — he switches lemma. The nakedness of 2:25 and the nakedness of 3:7 are not the same word in Hebrew, and the second word is the one the canon later uses for exposure under judgement. What it adds: the change of state is registered by a change of vocabulary, and it happens in the same verse as the opened eyes.

(b) ʾārar (779) “to curse” — twice, and not where most sermons put it. [T] verified High confidence. 3:14 (the serpent) and 3:17 (the ground). The whole book has nine occurrences and two of them are here. Neither the woman nor the man is the object of the verb. 3:16 contains no curse-word at all; 3:17’s curse falls on the ʾădāmāh, “because of you”. See Headline 1 and Difficult Verses.

(c) ʾākal (398) “to eat” — seventeen times in twenty-four verses. [T] verified High confidence. The chapter’s leading word by a distance: 3:1, 2, 3, 5, 6 ×2, 11 ×2, 12, 13, 14, 17 ×3, 18, 19, 22. Every stage of the chapter is an eating: forbidden, promised, done, confessed, sentenced. The serpent will eat dust; the man will eat bread in sweat; and 3:22 fears one more eating that is prevented.

(d) ʿiṣṣābôn (6093) “toil, pain” — and it occurs three times in the whole Hebrew Bible. [T] verified High confidence. Gen 3:16, 3:17 and 5:29 — and nowhere else in the canon. The word binds the woman’s sentence and the man’s with a single term (a link most English versions break, rendering it “pain” in 3:16 and “toil” in 3:17), and then it appears once more, at Lamech’s naming of Noah: “this one will give us rest from our work and from the ʿiṣṣābôn of our hands, because of the ground which YHWH has cursed” (5:29). The only later use of the curse’s own word is a father’s hope that his son will end it. Cross-reported to Move 4.

(e) šûp (7779) “to bruise/strike” — 3:15 twice, and four times in the canon. [T] verified High confidence on the count; genuinely uncertain on the sense. The only other occurrences are Ps 139:11 and Job 9:17, and neither settles the meaning: “crush”, “bruise”, “strike at”, “snap at” and “gasp after” have all been proposed, and some derive the two Genesis instances from different roots. The text uses the same verb of both parties, which is the observation that matters and which survives whatever the gloss. Do not preach a gloss as though the lexicon were settled.

(f) kətōnet (3801) “garment”, 3:21 — and its only other occurrences in Genesis. [T] verified High confidence. 3:21, then 37:3, 23, 31, 32, 33 — all of Joseph’s tunic. The first garment in the Bible is made by God from skins, which requires a death; the next garment in the book is stripped off a son and dipped in the blood of a goat and shown to his father. The distribution is exact; the connection is the reader’s to make, and the report flags it as available rather than asserting design. [I] moderate

(g) šāmar (8104) “to keep” — once, at 3:24. [T] verified High confidence. The chapter’s only occurrence, and it is the verb of the man’s commission at 2:15. The cherubim are posted lišmōr, “to keep”, the way the man was placed lə-šomrāh, “to keep it”. See Headline 5.

(h) kərûb (3742), 3:24 — the first occurrence in the canon. [T] verified High confidence. The next eleven occurrences in the WLC are Exod 25:18, 19 ×3, 20 ×2, 22; 26:1, 31; 36:8, 35 — the ark and the tabernacle curtains. The creatures that bar the way here are the creatures embroidered on the veil and overshadowing the mercy seat.

(i) hithallēk (1980, hithpael), 3:8 — “walking about”. [T] verified High confidence on the distribution. God is not said to walk but to walk about, in the reflexive stem. In Genesis that stem is used of Enoch (5:22, 24), Noah (6:9), Abraham (17:1), Abraham’s God (24:40) and Jacob’s fathers (48:15) — the walking-with-God vocabulary. And in the rest of the Torah it appears at Lev 26:12 — “I will walk about among you and be your God” — and Deut 23:15, of God walking in the camp. What it adds: what the pair hide from at 3:8 is the thing Leviticus later promises to restore.

(j) ḥawwāh / ḥay (2332 / 2416), 3:20. [T] High confidence on the wordplay; the etymology is debated. The man names his wife Ḥawwāh “because she was the mother of all ḥāy”. The narrator supplies a sound-explanation, as he habitually does, without claiming a derivation. The LXX does not transliterate: it translates — Ζωή, “Life”. [T] verified in Swete Note the placement: the naming falls between the sentence of death (3:19) and the clothing (3:21). The man’s response to being told he will return to dust is to call his wife Life.

Paronomasia check (words recurring three or more times). Run on ʾākal (17), ʾiššāh (13), ʿēṣ (11), ʾādām (7), ḥay (7), nāḥāš (5), yādaʿ (4). Two fire: ʿārûm/ʿārôm (a), across the unit boundary; and ʾādām/ʾădāmāh (3:17, 19, 23), the previous unit’s leading pun now under a curse. Nil on the rest. nāḥāš has a canonical partner in nəḥōšet (bronze) and the bronze serpent of Num 21, but nothing in this passage activates it, and the hard rule on sound-based claims applies — the observation is not reported as a finding here.

8. Translations

NASB95 against the KJV:

  • 3:1 “Indeed, has God said” (NASB95) / “Yea, hath God said” (KJV) — both preserve the particle’s oddity. Smoother versions (“Did God really say…?”) make it a plain question and lose the insinuating force. The literal awkwardness is the point.
  • 3:6 “desirable to make one wise” (NASB95) / “to be desired to make one wise” (KJV) — agreement. Note the three-fold description echoes 2:9’s “pleasing to the sight and good for food” with a third item added: the tree was also desirable for gaining insight. The addition is the woman’s, not the narrator’s earlier description. [T]
  • 3:15 “He shall bruise you on the head” (NASB95) / “It shall bruise thy head” (KJV) — a real divergence, and it is about the antecedent of hûʾ. The Hebrew pronoun is masculine singular agreeing with zeraʿ (a masculine noun), so “he” is grammatically ordinary and “it” is equally defensible in English. NASB95’s capitalised “He” is an interpretive decision the Hebrew does not require. Say so from the pulpit rather than resting weight on the version’s capital letter.
  • 3:16 “your desire will be for your husband” (NASB95) / “thy desire shall be to thy husband” (KJV) — agreement, and both leave təšûqāh unglossed. Some modern versions render “contrary to” or “against”, importing the 4:7 sense. The Hebrew preposition is the ordinary ʾel; the rendering “contrary to” is an interpretation, not a translation. [T]
  • 3:20 “Eve” (both) — transliteration. The LXX translates instead (Ζωή), which shows the pun operating.
  • 3:24 “He stationed the cherubim” (NASB95) / “he placed at the east of the garden of Eden Cherubims” (KJV) — agreement on the sense; see the ancient-versions note.

Ancient versions check. The trigger holds at 3:15, 3:16 and 3:24, and it fires at all three.

3:15 — the LXX’s αὐτός. [T] verified in Swete and in the Rahlfs-based export The Greek reads αὐτός σου τηρήσει κεφαλήν — a masculine singular pronoun with the neuter antecedent σπέρμα. The mismatch is grammatically anomalous in Greek in a way the Hebrew’s agreement is not, and it personalises the seed. Under the Three-Way Triage this is category 1 shading into category 3: the Greek cannot carry the Hebrew’s collective-with-agreement, and the reading it substitutes is the one standing behind the NT’s handling of the seed. The divergence: high confidence. The translator’s messianic intent: genuinely disputed — do not preach the mismatch as proof. Note also that the LXX renders šûp both times with τηρήσει (“will watch/lie in wait for”), which is a different picture from “bruise” and shows that the verb’s obscurity is ancient.

3:16 — təšûqāh → ἡ ἀποστροφή σου. [T] verified in Swete The Greek reads “your turning” — not “desire”. ʾApostrophē is a turning-toward or a returning. Translation-tradition split: the modern versions have a word of desire; the Greek tradition has a word of turning. Under the triage this is category 1, translation loss with interpretive substitution — the Greek translator faced the same rare word modern translators face and made a different guess. What it adds for the preacher: the ancient uncertainty about təšûqāh is a reason for humility, not a licence for confidence in either direction.

3:24 — who is settled where. [T] verified MT: “he drove the man out, and he stationed (wayyaškēn) east of the garden of Eden the cherubim and the flaming sword”. LXX: “he cast out Adam and settled him (κατῴκισεν αὐτόν) opposite the paradise of delight, and stationed (ἔταξεν) the cherubim…”. The Greek has God settling the man opposite the garden; the Hebrew has God stationing the cherubim. Translation-tradition split, and it is category 2, a substantive difference in the object of the verb — see Textual Variants.

Pulpit divergence note: N/A — no pulpit text declared. When one is named, check 3:15 (the pronoun and any capitalisation), 3:16 (“desire” against “contrary to”), and 3:1 (whether the particle has been smoothed into a plain question).

9. Tone and Feel

Conversational, then forensic, then elegiac — three registers in twenty-four verses, and the shifts are the structure. [T]

  • 3:1–6 is dialogue, and it is quiet. No narrator interjections, no exclamations, no divine voice. The chapter’s most consequential act is narrated in seven Hebrew words at 3:6b. There is no drama in the telling and the restraint is what makes it terrible.
  • 3:8–13 is a courtroom, and the pace changes. Four questions in five verses (“Where are you?”, “Who told you?”, “Have you eaten?”, “What is this you have done?”). Both answers end with the same three syllables — wāʾōkēl, “and I ate” — after a clause of blame. [T] The repetition is exact and it is the chapter’s most quietly damning device.
  • 3:14–19 is formal sentence, in elevated diction, unhurried, with no reply from any of the three.
  • 3:20–24 is aftermath. A naming, a clothing, a deliberation broken off mid-sentence (3:22’s wəʿattāh pen… trails away without an apodosis in the Hebrew), and an expulsion. The chapter ends with a flaming sword turning, and no human speech since 3:12.

The single most affecting detail is 3:8: the sound of God walking about in the garden, and two people among the trees. The narrator gives no interior monologue; he lets the sound do it.

Soundtrack: a conversation at normal volume, a long silence, then something with a slow, formal tread — and at the end, no resolution, just the sword.

A note for the preacher. This chapter is often preached loudly. Its own tone is level. The horror is in what is reported flatly — “and he ate”, “and I ate”, “and he drove the man out” — and a raised voice will not add to it.

12. Genre

Hebrew narrative prose with three embedded oracles. [T] The frame (3:1–13, 3:20–24) is ordinary wayyiqtol narrative; the sentences (3:14–19) are elevated, parallelistic, and free of the narrative chain — a different register inside the same chapter.

Reading rules that matter here. It is presented as something that happened, and it is theologically loaded to a degree unmatched in the book. Neither of those facts settles the other, and the genre tool’s own principle applies: when something is presented as historical fact, pause to consider that it really happened — and when a text is this heavily patterned, read the patterning too. The chapter is not a fable with a moral and it is not a bare chronicle. Where a reader’s tradition presses hard in either direction, say so and preach what the text does.

One sub-genre to name: 3:14–19 are sentences of a court, not curses in the magical sense. They are pronounced after questioning, in order, by a judge who has heard the parties.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits: the hinge of the whole timeline. Everything before it is two chapters long; everything after it, until the new creation, is lived under 3:17–19.

Where the reader stands: after the cross and resurrection; in a creation still groaning (Rom 8:22); with the tree of life promised open again (Rev 22:14) and not yet entered.

What has happened in between that changes the reading:

  • Romans 5. The chapter is read in the NT as the entry point of sin and death for a race, not as one couple’s misfortune. Preaching it as a cautionary tale about disobedience underplays what Paul does with it.
  • The tabernacle. The cherubim of 3:24 reappear over the mercy seat, and the way that was barred is the way the high priest goes once a year — and then, at the tearing of the veil, the way that is opened.
  • Revelation 12:9 and 20:2. The serpent is identified. The identification is made there, not here — this is the overview’s Trap 5, and it is this chapter’s principal preaching trap.
  • Revelation 22. The tree of life, guarded at the end of Genesis 3, stands in the middle of the city with its leaves for the healing of the nations, and there is no sword.

15. Who Am I?

The man and the woman are representative, and the identification is direct — this is the passage where the reader is most clearly in the story. [T] Romans 5:12–21 makes it explicit: what happened here happened to a race. There is no Moses-is-me danger; the danger is the opposite one, of standing outside the chapter as a spectator judging two people who fell for an obvious trick.

The woman is not the chapter’s villain, and the text guards against making her one. [T] She is deceived (3:13, and 2 Cor 11:3 and 1 Tim 2:14 both say deceived); the man is not said to be deceived, is present throughout (3:6b), and is the one God addresses first (3:9) and the one whose sentence is longest. The chapter distributes responsibility carefully and a sermon should distribute it the same way.

The serpent is not “me” and is not a character to psychologise. It is a creature (3:1a), it is shrewd, it speaks, and the narrator says nothing else about it.

Christ is not in this chapter as a character. He is in it as the one whose seed-promise is planted at 3:15 and whose work answers everything the chapter breaks — which is a different claim from finding him in the text, and the report keeps them apart.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the passage seeks. Not primarily a resolution to do better. The chapter contains no imperative addressed to the reader at all. What it seeks is recognition: this is what happened, this is what we are, this is why the ground fights back and why we hide — and, in one verse, this is what God said he would do about it.

Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.

Worldview. Four shifts:

  1. The tempter’s method is a question about God’s goodness, not an argument against his existence. [T] “Has God said…?” then “God knows that…” The attack is on motive, and it is made to someone who can quote the command.
  2. Sin’s first consequence is not punishment but hiding. The eyes open, the knowledge arrives, and it is knowledge of exposure. Fig leaves come before God does.
  3. The curse lands on the serpent and on the ground — not on the man and the woman. [T] verified God sentences the human pair; he curses the snake and the soil. That distinction is not a technicality; it is the difference between a race under judgement and a race under a curse.
  4. The last acts of the chapter are mercy. A promise inside the serpent’s sentence (3:15); a name that means Life spoken over a woman just told she will bear in pain (3:20); garments God makes and puts on them (3:21); and an expulsion whose stated reason is to stop them living for ever in this condition (3:22).

Behaviour — stop. Stop the two things the chapter shows: editing God’s word (both the serpent and the woman do it), and answering for ourselves by naming someone else first.

Behaviour — start. Receive the covering. The chapter’s one act of clothing is God’s, it costs a life, and the pair do nothing but stand still for it.

Motivation. Not fear of the sword. The chapter’s own last word is a guarded way — but the guarding is stated as a mercy (3:22), and the canon ends with the way open.

Four audiences.

  • For me: Where am I quoting God’s word back with the permission shrunk and the prohibition enlarged? And what am I still covering with fig leaves that he has offered to clothe properly?
  • For a Christian friend: The one who thinks their sin has put them beyond God’s approach needs 3:8–9 — God came walking, and the first word out of his mouth was a question that let the man answer.
  • For the church community: A congregation that reads this chapter as a story about two foolish people has not read Romans 5. A congregation that reads it as their own story will handle each other’s failures differently.
  • For an unbeliever: The chapter offers an account of why the world is as it is that does not blame the world’s maker and does not pretend the damage is superficial. It can be laid alongside the alternatives.

Prayer. You made us to walk with you in the cool of the day and we hid. We have edited your word to suit ourselves and we have answered for ourselves by blaming others. Thank you that you came looking, that you asked instead of accusing, that you clothed what we could not cover, and that in the sentence on the serpent you hid a promise. Bring us back to the tree you guarded.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • 3:3 — the woman’s three alterations. [T] Against 2:16–17 she (i) drops “any/every tree” from the permission (2:16 has mikkōl ʿēṣ, “from every tree”; she has “from the fruit of the trees”), (ii) adds “nor shall you touch it”, and (iii) softens the infinitive-absolute môt tāmût (“dying you shall die”) to pen-təmutûn (“lest you die”). The alterations run in both directions — the permission shrinks and the prohibition grows — and they are visible only against the previous chapter. High confidence on the three differences; the interpretation of them is the preacher’s.
  • 3:4 — the serpent’s exact negation. [T] lōʾ-môt təmutûn: he takes the infinitive absolute the woman had dropped and puts it back, negated. He quotes God’s construction more accurately than she did, in order to deny it. High confidence — this is visible in the Hebrew and invisible in most English.
  • 3:6 — three participial descriptions. ṭôv lə-maʾăkāl (good for food), taʾăwāh… lāʿênayim (a delight to the eyes), neḥmād lə-haśkîl (desirable for insight). The first two reproduce 2:9’s description of the trees; the third is new and is the woman’s own. [T]
  • 3:16 — no causal clause. [T] The serpent’s sentence opens “because you have done this” (3:14) and the man’s “because you have listened” (3:17). The woman’s opens with neither. The asymmetry is in the text; the report does not interpret it further.
  • 3:22 — aposiopesis. [T] “And now, lest he stretch out his hand and take also from the tree of life and eat and live for ever—” and the sentence stops. There is no main clause in the Hebrew; the dash in NASB95 is right. God does not finish the sentence; he acts (3:23).
  • 3:24 — two verbs of expulsion. [T] wayšallaḥ (3:23, “sent out”, the Piel of the ordinary verb “send”) and wayəgāreš (3:24, “drove out”, a much stronger verb used of divorce and of expelling nations). The second escalates the first, and both are in consecutive verses.

Textual Variants

3:24 — who is settled opposite the garden.

  • The issue: MT has God stationing (wayyaškēn) the cherubim east of the garden. The LXX has him casting out Adam and settling him (κατῴκισεν αὐτόν) opposite the paradise, and then stationing the cherubim with a different verb (ἔταξεν).

  • Triage: category 2, a substantive difference, though it could also arise from a translator reading the same consonants with a different object.
  • Most likely original: the MT, on the ground that the Greek’s reading resolves an ambiguity (who is being settled?) in the easier direction, and that the MT’s construction — a long object phrase following the verb — is the harder reading. Moderate confidence.
  • Preaching implication: small. It does not change what happens; it changes whether the verse also tells us where the man ended up. Worth a footnote, not a paragraph.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. Not recoverable from the text; the presenting situation is [S], per the overview.

What the ancient reader knew that we do not.

  • A speaking serpent needed no explanation and no apology in the ancient world, and the narrator gives none. [S] moderate. What is striking to the original audience is not that it speaks but that the narrator calls it a creature that God made (3:1a) — flattening it out of the divine category it would otherwise occupy in a neighbouring literature.
  • Serpents carried associations of wisdom, chaos and life-renewal across the ANE. [S] moderate. The chapter engages none of them explicitly. As with chapter 1, the report’s position is that the demotion is on the page and the comparative material is background, not the sermon’s subject.
  • Nakedness in an honour–shame culture is a public category, not merely a private one. [S] high. 3:7’s covering and 3:8’s hiding are the two proper responses to exposure in that world: cover yourself, then get out of sight.
  • Cherubim were not infants. [S] high. In ANE iconography and in the Bible’s own descriptions (Ezek 1, 10), they are composite guardian figures flanking thrones and doorways. A congregation whose mental image is a Renaissance putto is reading 3:24 wrongly.
  • A garment given by a superior was an act of investiture — clothing as status conferred, not merely covering supplied. [S] moderate. It makes 3:21 more than a kindness.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Torah — readers of the founding story, holding creation and cult together. The frame is live here in a specific and verifiable way: the cherubim they know from the ark and the curtains make their first appearance barring a way, and the walking-about God they know from Leviticus 26:12’s promise is the one being hidden from. [T] verified Both links are Torah-internal.

What they brought. A world in which sacred space was guarded and entry was regulated on pain of death; a tabernacle with cherubim woven into the veil; and a curse-list (Deut 27–28) whose vocabulary for shameful nakedness is the word this chapter switches to at 3:7.

Shocking. That the serpent’s promise was, in a limited sense, true — the eyes were opened (3:5, 3:7 use the same verb) and God himself says “the man has become like one of us, knowing good and evil” (3:22). [T] The chapter does not say the tempter lied about the effect; it shows him lying about the cost.

Surprising. That God comes walking and asks a question rather than pronouncing immediately; and that the sentence on the serpent contains the only promise in the chapter.

Comforting. 3:21. God clothes them himself, and does it after the sentences, not before.

Disturbing. The sword at the end, and the silence of the man from 3:12 onward.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1 — Adversary, sin, death and new creation. Where this passage sits: the origin of the whole theme, and the extension’s own worked case. [T] Before: nothing. After: death reigning (Rom 5:12–14); the adversary named as a figure (Job 1–2; Zech 3); the serpent identified at Rev 12:9 and 20:2; the cross as the answer (Heb 2:14–15; Col 2:15); death abolished last (1 Cor 15:26); the serpent’s end (Rev 20:10). Payoff: it supplies the cosmic frame without which 3:15 is only a note about snakes. Confidence: high.

2 — Temple and God’s presence. Where it sits: the loss. [T] verified on both links. The walking-about God (3:8, hithallēk) is hidden from; the cherubim (3:24) bar the way. After: Lev 26:12 promises the walking restored; the cherubim move to the ark and the veil; the veil is torn; Rev 21:22 has no temple and Rev 22:2 has the tree. Payoff: the two verified Torah links make this the chapter’s strongest biblical-theological thread, and both come from the text’s own vocabulary rather than from a comparative argument. 3 — Seed. Where it sits: planted at 3:15. [T] After: zeraʿ runs 59 times through Genesis (12 in chs. 1–11, 47 after) [S: overview], becomes the vehicle of blessing at 22:17–18, and is read singularly at Gal 3:16. Payoff: it is the thread that turns a curse-scene into the book’s opening move. Confidence: high that the word is planted; the reading of 3:15 itself is treated below.

Confluence — and it is (c), climactic convergence. All three meet at the cross and, before that, they meet at the veil. The adversary is defeated, the presence is restored, and the seed arrives — and the tabernacle’s cherubim-woven curtain is where a Torah reader would first feel the three converge: the barrier of 3:24, embroidered, with a way through it once a year, torn open when the seed of the woman had his heel bruised. That convergence is a canonical reading, not a claim this chapter makes.

Nil returns: type-scene; symbolic numbers; comparison/interchange between parallel characters (the three parties are sequential, not paralleled).

Irreducibility. The chapter is not the proposition “humanity fell”. It is a conversation, a taking, a hiding, four questions, three sentences, a naming, a clothing, and a sword — in that order. The order is the theology: mercy appears inside the sentence and after the judgement, not instead of it, and a summary loses exactly that.

Christological Reading

Type of connection: direct promise (contested), typology (one, weak), trajectory, and contrast.

Direct promise — 3:15, handled carefully. [T] / [S] The text says: enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their seeds; hûʾ will bruise your head, and you will bruise his heel.

What is [T]: the enmity, the two seeds, the same verb both ways, and the asymmetry of head and heel. What is not [T]: that hûʾ denotes an individual. zeraʿ is a collective noun and the pronoun agrees with it grammatically; a collective reading (“humanity will strike at the serpent’s brood”) is available on the Hebrew alone. What the canon supplies: the LXX’s masculine αὐτός against neuter σπέρμα, which personalises it — a verified divergence whose intent is disputed; the seed-thread that Genesis itself narrows to a line and Paul reads singularly (Gal 3:16); and Rom 16:20’s crushing language.

The report’s position: 3:15 is rightly preached as the first promise, and the argument for it should be made from the canon rather than from the verse’s grammar. Staking the messianic reading on the pronoun hands a thoughtful hearer an easy objection and is not necessary. Confidence: high that the verse is the seed-promise’s origin; moderate on any individual reference in the Hebrew alone.

Typology — the garments of skin (3:21). Tests: theological category ✓ (covering/atonement); NT precedent — weak (no NT text cites 3:21); escalation ✓ if the category holds; authorial pattern — weak (Genesis does not develop it). Two of four → reclassify. This is a trajectory, not a type: an animal dies and God clothes, and the canon’s sacrificial and clothing language runs from there (Lev 16; Isa 61:10; Gal 3:27; Rev 19:8). Moderate confidence, and labelled honestly — the “first sacrifice” reading is a canonical trajectory, not something the verse asserts.

Trajectory — the guarded way. [T] verified on the vocabulary Cherubim bar the way to the tree of life (3:24); cherubim are woven into the veil and overshadow the mercy seat (Exod 25–36); the veil is torn; Rev 22:2, 14 has the tree accessible. High confidence on the Torah links; the arc is a canonical reading.

Contrast — the two Adams. The man is with the woman, silent, and eats (3:6b); he is questioned and blames God’s gift (3:12). Rom 5:12–21 and 1 Cor 15:21–22 make the contrast explicit and the NT does the work.

Christophany: none. The walking-about God of 3:8 is not identified.

A word about the serpent. [T] verified śāṭān does not occur in Genesis. nāḥāš occurs in six verses in the book (3:1, 2, 4, 13, 14; 49:17). The identification is a canonical judgement made at Rev 12:9 and 20:2. Preaching it as though Genesis 3 states it teaches people to read later theology as earlier text — and it is unnecessary, because Revelation says it plainly.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptation: there is no positive human model in the chapter, so the moralism takes a different shape here — “don’t be like Eve”, “resist temptation”, “guard your heart”. All three make the chapter a cautionary tale and the hearer the hero of their own resistance.
  • The gospel grounding: the chapter’s only good news is spoken by God — a promise inside a sentence (3:15) and a garment made and put on (3:21). The humans contribute fig leaves.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the one whose heel was bruised; the one who did not grasp equality with God (Phil 2:6, against 3:5’s “you will be like God”); the one who kept what he was given (John 17:12) where the first guard did not.

Difficult / Contested Verses

3:15 — the protoevangelium

  • Category: doctrinal.
  • The difficulty: how much the verse itself carries, as against how much the canon reads into it.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it sits inside the serpent’s sentence, not as a separate announcement. The promise is delivered as part of a judgement, addressed to the guilty party — the humans overhear it. [T] That is a homiletically valuable observation and it is often missed.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: set out under Christological Reading. High on the seed-promise’s origin here; moderate on individual reference from the Hebrew alone.
  • Suggested handling: make it the sermon’s hinge, and build the case canonically.

3:16 — “your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you”

  • Category: pastorally sensitive / doctrinally contested.
  • The difficulty: whether this prescribes an order or describes a disorder; and what təšûqāh means.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is a sentence pronounced after a transgression, in a series of three, in a chapter whose other two sentences describe damage (a serpent on its belly, a ground that fights back). Its function in the sequence is consequence, not institution. [T]
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces, both halves: (i) təšûqāh occurs three times in the canon and the nearest one is 4:7, of sin’s desire for Cain, which is hostile — that is a real argument for reading 3:16 as disorder, and it is text-internal. [T] verified (ii) But 4:7 is eighteen verses later in a different register, and the Song of Songs instance (7:11) is plainly not hostile, so the 4:7 parallel constrains less than it is often made to. And the LXX read the word as “turning”, not desire at all. The honest position is that the term is not securely known and the verse’s force depends on it. Moderate confidence that disorder is described rather than order prescribed; the report does not resolve the lexical question.
  • Suggested handling: name it as a sentence in a series of sentences; name the 4:7 parallel and its limits; do not build a doctrine of marriage on a word whose sense the ancient translators already disagreed about. Pastoral note: this verse has been used to justify real harm. Say what it is — a description of what went wrong — out loud.

3:17–19 — the curse on the ground, and what it means for work

  • Category: ethical / apologetic.
  • The difficulty: hearers conclude either that work is cursed, or that the physical world’s harshness is a moral verdict on them personally.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the curse falls on the ʾădāmāh, not on work and not on the man. [T] verified Work was given at 2:15 before any of this; what changes is that it now resists. Romans 8:20–22 generalises it to creation’s futility.
  • Suggested handling: address directly, and pair with 2:15.

3:22 — “the man has become like one of Us”

  • Category: doctrinal / apologetic.
  • The difficulty: God appears to confirm the serpent’s claim, and the plural returns from 1:26.
  • Rhetorical function: it is the stated ground of the expulsion — the reason a way must be barred.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the serpent’s prediction about the effect is confirmed and his claim about the cost was false. [T] On the plural, see the Genesis 1 run’s Difficult Verses — and note the finding recorded there: the Greek supplies a plural at 2:18 where the Hebrew has a singular, which is a reason to hold the “let us” texts more loosely as evidence.
  • Suggested handling: name it; do not smooth it. The chapter is willing to say that something true was gained at a catastrophic price.

3:20 — Eve named “Life” between a death sentence and a covering

  • Category: none of the standard ones — flagged because it is easily passed over.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the placement is the point. [T] The man has just heard “to dust you shall return”, and he names his wife Life; and the narrator, not the man, supplies the explanation. Whether this is faith or irony the text does not say — and a preacher should notice that before deciding.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Saying God cursed Adam and Eve

  • What it looks like: “God cursed the serpent, the woman and the man” — usually said in passing, as a summary of 3:14–19.
  • Why it’s wrong: ʾārar occurs twice in the chapter, at 3:14 (the serpent) and 3:17 (the ground). [T] verified The word is never applied to either human being, and the chapter is precise about it in a way the sermon usually is not.
  • The corrective: say what the text says — the serpent is cursed, the ground is cursed, and the man and woman are sentenced. The distinction preaches.

Pitfall 2: Naming the serpent Satan as though Genesis did

  • What it looks like: “Satan comes to Eve in the form of a serpent.”
  • Why it’s wrong: śāṭān does not occur in Genesis — nought occurrences [T] verified. The identification is made at Rev 12:9 and 20:2, and the narrator here goes out of his way to say the serpent is a creature God made (3:1a).
  • The corrective: preach the serpent Genesis gives, then say where the canon takes it. The canonical route is more impressive than the shortcut.

Pitfall 3: Making the woman the villain

  • What it looks like: the sermon’s energy falls on Eve’s gullibility; the man appears as a bystander who was unfortunately led astray.
  • Why it’s wrong: 3:6b says he was with her; he is not said to have been deceived (2 Cor 11:3 and 1 Tim 2:14 both say she was); God addresses him first (3:9); and his sentence is the longest in the chapter. [T]
  • The corrective: distribute responsibility as the chapter does. And in most congregations there is someone for whom this passage has been used against women; the accuracy is pastoral as well as exegetical.

Pitfall 4: Preaching 3:16b as the design for marriage

  • What it looks like: “God’s order for marriage is stated here — her desire, his rule.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it is a sentence in a chapter of sentences, following a transgression; the same rare word-pair describes sin’s designs on Cain eighteen verses later; and the ancient translators already disagreed about what təšûqāh means.
  • The corrective: preach it as damage described. If the sermon needs a doctrine of marriage, 2:24 is the text and Eph 5 is its interpreter.

Pitfall 5: The moralised cautionary tale

  • What it looks like: “Here is how temptation works — recognise the pattern, and resist it.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the chapter contains no imperative to the reader and no human being who resists. Romans 5 reads it as the entry of sin and death for a race, not as a case study in poor decision-making. A sermon that ends on the hearer’s resistance has made the hearer the hero of a chapter whose only good news is spoken by God.
  • The corrective: let 3:15 and 3:21 be the destination. Fig leaves are what the humans manage; garments are what God makes.

Pitfall 6: Reading the expulsion as pure punishment

  • What it looks like: “God threw them out of the garden as their punishment.”
  • Why it’s wrong: 3:22 states the reason and it is preventive — lest he eat from the tree of life and live for ever in this condition. [T] The connector makes the logic explicit.
  • The corrective: the sword at the gate is a mercy with an edge on it, and the canon opens the way at Rev 22.
