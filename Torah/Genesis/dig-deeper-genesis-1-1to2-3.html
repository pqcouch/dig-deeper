The Passage

Headline Findings

  1. The passage is built on sevens, and four of the five popular counts verify exactly — while one, widely repeated, does not. [T] Surfaced by: Repetition (Tool 10), Vocabulary (Tool 7), Structure (Tool 3), Original Languages. Counted by lemma in the WLC: 1:1 = 7 words; 1:2 = 14; ṭôv “good” 7×; ʾĕlōhîm 35× (5 × 7); ʾereṣ 21× (3 × 7); “and God said” 10×; 2:1–3 = 35 words; and 2:2a, 2:2b and 2:3a are three consecutive seven-word clauses. But šāmayim (11) plus rāqîaʿ (9) comes to 20, not the 21 the popular “heaven 21×” claim requires. Preach the sevens that hold; drop the one that does not.

  2. God names only what he makes on days one to three, and never names a single living creature. [T] Surfaced by: Repetition (Tool 10 — qārāʾ 5×, all of them in 1:5–1:10), Structure (the forming/filling symmetry), Copycat (Tool 13), Who Am I (Tool 15). God names day, night, heaven, earth, seas — the realms — and then stops. The naming of the creatures is left undone until 2:19–20, when the man does it. In a chapter whose whole grammar is God speaking and it being so, the naming God deliberately does not do is the space he leaves for his image.

  3. The evening-and-morning refrain runs exactly six times and then stops. [T] Surfaced by: Repetition (ʿerev 6×, bōqer 6×, each at 1:5, 8, 13, 19, 23, 31), Structure, Tone, Positional Necessity. Six days close; the seventh does not. The day the passage blesses and sanctifies is the only one it leaves open, and every canonical use of that seventh day afterwards — Exodus 20, Psalm 95, Hebrews 4 — is reading an unclosed door.

  4. The rare verb of 1:2 meets its rare partner again only in the Song of Moses, and they are adjacent there. [T] verified Surfaced by: Original Languages, Tool 11 Move 4 and the adjacency check, Biblical Theology. rāḥap “hover” occurs three times in the whole Hebrew Bible — Gen 1:2, Deut 32:11, Jer 23:9 — and bōhû occurs three times — Gen 1:2, Isa 34:11, Jer 4:23. Deuteronomy 32:10–11 puts tōhû and rāḥap in consecutive verses, of YHWH finding Israel in a howling waste and hovering over her like an eagle over its young. Within the Torah, that is Genesis 1:2’s own commentary: the Spirit over the formless deep is the same care that later hovers over a formless people.

  5. The syntax of the first sentence is the passage’s first theological decision, and the Masoretic analysis takes a side. [T] / [S] Surfaced by: Original Languages, Tool 8, Difficult Verses. bərēʾšît is anarthrous, which is what allows the construct reading (“When God began to create…, the earth was…”). But the WLC morphology parses rēʾšît here as absolute (Ncfsa), which is the traditional reading — an independent main clause, “In the beginning God created.” The choice determines whether 1:1 is a summary title, the first act, or a temporal clause subordinate to 1:2 — and whether the chapter teaches creation ex nihilo on its own page or leaves that to the canon.

Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

Section. Torah, first book, first passage — and the first passage of the whole Tanak. [T]

Reading implication. This is the only pericope in Scripture with nothing canonical behind it. Every category it uses is being defined, not assumed: God, heaven, earth, light, day, blessing, image, rule, rest, holiness. A reading that imports later theology as background has the direction of dependence backwards.

Presupposes. Nothing. The overview’s most useful line applies with full force here: the field that normally carries the testable claims is empty, and the emptiness is the argument.

Handoff. To 2:4 and the first toledot, which narrows from cosmos to the human pair; and, at book scale, to Exod 1:7, which reports this passage’s blessing as having taken effect using four of its own five verbs. [T] verified

Neighbours. Nothing precedes; 2:4–25 follows. Remove this passage and 2:4 opens on a garden with no maker named and a command with no authority behind it.

Canonical / compositional divergence. None to report at passage level: the passage narrates the canon’s first events and stands first in the canon, so the two orders coincide. This is the one place in the Torah where that is trivially true, and worth stating precisely because it is nowhere else.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. [S: overview] Genesis exists to ask whether the blessing can outrun the curse, and through whom — narrowing from the whole earth to one man to one seed, and ending unresolved, with the promise owed and the family in a coffin in Egypt.

Passage-level. This passage supplies the blessing that the rest of the book will spend fifty chapters defending. It establishes, in order: that there is one God who is not part of what he makes; that he makes by speaking; that what he makes is ordered, filled, and good; that humanity is made in his image to rule and to multiply; and that the whole movement arrives at rest. [T]

The purpose is foundational rather than polemical. The chapter is often preached as an argument against something — Babylonian cosmogony, or modern naturalism. It does not argue. It states. There is no rival named, no opposing view engaged, no “unlike the nations” anywhere on the page. [T] The demotion of sun, moon and sea to creatures is real, but it is achieved by not mentioning their names — the great lights are called “the greater light” and “the lesser light” rather than šemeš and yārēaḥ, and the sea-monsters are simply among the things God made and called good. That is a demotion by silence, and reading it as an argument converts a statement into a debate the text declines to have. [I] moderate

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. There is none. This is the one passage in the canon for which Question 1 has no material to work on. The check therefore runs forward, and that is not a shortcut — it is the honest form of the question here. [T]

Necessity answer — what does this passage exist to make possible? Four things, each of which a later passage in Genesis requires and cannot supply for itself:

  1. The command that chapter 3 breaks is only intelligible because 1:26–28 has established humanity as a ruler under a word. Without the mandate, the fall is a snack.
  2. The categories the curse inverts. bārak is spoken three times here (1:22, 1:28, 2:3); by 3:14 the governing word is ʾārar. The curse is legible only as the reversal of this blessing. [T] verified
  3. The order the flood unmakes. 7:11’s “fountains of the great deep… and the floodgates of the sky” undoes the day-two separation of waters, and 8:1’s wind over the earth re-runs 1:2’s Spirit over the waters. The flood is not a disaster narrative; it is a de-creation, and it needs this passage to be one.
  4. The mandate Exodus reports fulfilled. Exod 1:7 stacks pārû (6509), wayyišrəṣû (8317), wayyirbû (7235), wayyaʿaṣmû (6105), wattimmālēʾ hāʾāreṣ (4390 + 776) — four of the five roots from 1:20–28 and 9:1, 7. [T] verified by lemma

Implication. The chapter’s function is establishing, not arguing. It is the only passage in Scripture that has to define its own terms from nothing, and preaching it as a defence of something forfeits the one thing it uniquely does.

General context

Immediately after (2:4–25): the first toledot, which zooms from cosmos to the pair — and which supplies, in different vocabulary, what this passage conspicuously withholds: the man’s naming of the animals (2:19–20), the “not good” of aloneness (2:18), and the specific prohibition (2:16–17). The two accounts are not rivals but a wide shot and a close-up, and the second fills three gaps the first leaves deliberately open.

Section: 1:1–2:3 is the book’s prologue and the only unit in Genesis with no toledot heading. [T] The eleven headings begin at 2:4; this passage stands outside the genealogical spine that organises everything else. Its structural isolation is a claim: what follows is generations, and this is not.

Commonly read out of context: 1:28 (“be fruitful and multiply… subdue it”) is regularly pulled loose in both directions — as an environmental mandate and as an environmental licence. In context it is one of three blessings, spoken to creatures who have just been made in God’s image, in a world declared good six times before they arrive.

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
1:1 Title, or first act Seven words naming the whole. Whether it summarises or begins is the passage’s first crux (Tool 8)
1:2 The condition addressed Fourteen words. tōhû wābōhû, darkness, deep, and the Spirit hovering — the twin problem the six days answer
1:3–13 Days 1–3: forming the realms Light/dark; waters above/below; sea/land with vegetation. God separates and God names
1:14–31 Days 4–6: filling the realms Luminaries; fish and birds; land creatures and humanity. God makes and God blesses; he names nothing
2:1–3 Day 7: the goal Completion, cessation, blessing, sanctification. No evening

Device: forming and filling, in strict correspondence, arriving at rest. Days 1–3 answer tōhû (unformed) by making three realms; days 4–6 answer bōhû (unfilled) by populating them in the same order. Day 1’s light is filled by day 4’s luminaries; day 2’s sky and sea by day 5’s birds and fish; day 3’s land by day 6’s animals and man. [T] The symmetry is in the text and does not need to be argued for; the two words of 1:2 are the two problems, and the two triads are the two solutions.

Day 7 stands outside the frame. It is not a fourth in a series of threes and not the closing member of a set of six; it has no creative act, no “it was good”, and no evening. [T] The structure’s own testimony is that the six days are the means and the seventh is the end.

Three verbal boundary-markers confirm the sections: qārāʾ (naming) occurs 5×, all of them in 1:5–1:10 — that is, only in the forming triad; bārak (blessing) occurs 3×, all in the filling triad and the seventh day (1:22, 1:28, 2:3); ʿerev/bōqer close each of the six but not the seventh. [T] all verified by lemma

4. Linking Words

Narrative Hebrew, so the connective work is carried by the wayyiqtol chain rather than by conjunctions, and there is little to trace. Three things are worth naming:

  • “And it was so” (wayhî kēn) — six times: 1:7, 9, 11, 15, 24, 30. [T] verified Not eight. It is absent from day 1 (the light simply is: “and there was light”, 1:3) and absent at 1:20–21. The formula is not a mechanical refrain; where it is missing, the fiat and its execution are collapsed into a single clause, and the effect is immediacy.
  • “Then God said” (wayyōʾmer ʾĕlōhîm) — ten times: 1:3, 6, 9, 11, 14, 20, 24, 26, 28, 29. [T] verified Ten divine speeches across six days. The count is exact and often misreported as eight (the number of creative acts); two of the ten (1:28, 1:29) are addressed to humanity rather than about creation, which is itself the point — the last two things God says in the chapter, he says to someone.
  • “Because” (kî) at 2:3 — the passage’s one causal connector, and it governs the reason for the blessing: the seventh day is blessed and sanctified because in it God ceased. [T] The holiness of the day rests on what God did, not on what anyone else must do — a note worth carrying to the pulpit before any application of sabbath is attempted.

5. Parallels

Not poetry, but the passage is strongly patterned prose, and one verse is genuinely poetic:

1:27 — a tricolon, and the only verse in the passage with poetic shape:

וַיִּבְרָ֨א אֱלֹהִ֤ים ׀ אֶת־הָֽאָדָם֙ בְּצַלְמ֔וֹ בְּצֶ֥לֶם אֱלֹהִ֖ים בָּרָ֣א אֹת֑וֹ זָכָ֥ר וּנְקֵבָ֖ה בָּרָ֥א אֹתָֽם׃

Three cola, each governed by bārāʾ. Lines 1 and 2 are chiastic: created / God / man / in his image ‖ in the image of God / created / him. Line 3 breaks the pattern — it does not repeat “image” but supplies “male and female”, and it shifts from singular ʾōtô to plural ʾōtām. [T]

The break is the meaning. The parallelism sets up an expectation of a third image-statement and delivers instead the sexed plurality of humanity — so that “male and female” arrives grammatically in the slot where “image” belongs. Whatever else the image is, the verse’s own structure will not let it be predicated of a solitary man. High confidence on the structure; moderate on how much weight the slot-substitution will bear. [T] / [I]

Elsewhere the patterning is formulaic rather than parallel — announcement, fiat, execution, evaluation, naming or blessing, day-formula. The variations in that pattern are Tool 10’s business.

6. Narrator’s Comment

Narrative, so the tool applies — but the passage is unusual in that the narrator’s comments and the divine speech are almost the whole text, and the narrator’s own asides are few and load-bearing:

  • 1:2 — the entire verse is narratorial background. [T] The disjunctive wə- + noun construction (wəhāʾāreṣ hāyətāh) breaks the wayyiqtol chain to give a state, not an event: this is the narrator setting the stage before the first “and God said”. Standard translations render it as narrative; grammatically it is a circumstantial clause.
  • “And God saw that it was good” (7×) — this is the narrator reporting God’s evaluation, not God speaking. [T] The distinction matters: the goodness of creation is not a divine announcement to anyone; it is an assessment the reader is given access to. Nobody in the story hears it.
  • 1:31 “and behold, very good” — the seventh and only intensified evaluation, and the only one introduced by hinnēh. [T] The narrator has been keeping a tally, and this is where he closes it.
  • 2:3’s kî clause — the narrator explaining the sanctification of the seventh day. [T] This is the passage’s only explanatory aside, and it is the one the rest of the canon quotes (Exod 20:11).

What the narrator conspicuously does not say: he offers no comment on the man, no evaluation of the image, and no verdict on the mandate. The one thing modern readers most want explained — what the image is — the narrator declines to define. [T]

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. There are, remarkably, no proper nouns in the passage at all apart from ʾĕlōhîm. No place, no person, no people, no named heavenly body. [T] This is worth stating rather than passing over: Genesis is a book of names — 86 distinct proper-noun lemmas in chapter 10 alone — and its opening pericope has none. The name-hyperlink machinery of Tool 7c therefore has nothing to work on here, which is itself a finding: the passage is deliberately pre-onomastic, and the first name given to anything is given by God at 1:5, to a time.

(a) bārāʾ (1254) — “create”. Six occurrences: 1:1, 1:21, 1:27 (×3), 2:3. [T] verified High confidence. In the Hebrew Bible the subject of this verb is always God; it never takes an accusative of material. What the count shows is where the verb clusters: half of its six occurrences fall in one verse, 1:27, on the making of humanity. The chapter’s ordinary verb is ʿāśāh (“make”, 10×); bārāʾ is reserved for the beginning (1:1), the sea-creatures (1:21), humanity (1:27 ×3), and the summary at 2:3. The distribution is the emphasis.

(b) ʾĕlōhîm (430) — 35 occurrences, five times seven. [T] verified High confidence. It is the grammatical subject of virtually every clause in the passage. There is no other agent: no assistants, no consort, no opponent. The chapter’s monotheism is carried by its syntax before it is carried by any statement.

(c) tōhû wābōhû (8414 + 922) — “formless and void”, 1:2. [T] High confidence on the count; moderate on the sense. The pair occurs nowhere else; bōhû occurs three times in the whole Hebrew Bible — here, Isa 34:11 and Jer 4:23 — and both later uses are visions of un-creation, Edom reduced to the measuring-line of chaos and Jeremiah seeing the earth returned to tōhû wābōhû. [T] verified The words denote non-order and non-occupancy, not a rival power: the darkness and the deep are conditions, not deities, and the text neither combats nor personifies them.

(d) rāḥap (7363) — “hovering”, 1:2. [T] verified High confidence on the count; moderate on the freight. Three occurrences in the whole Hebrew Bible: Gen 1:2, Deut 32:11, Jer 23:9. Jeremiah’s is of shaking bones. Deuteronomy 32:11’s is of an eagle over its young — and Deut 32:10, the immediately preceding verse, contains tōhû. The Torah’s only other pairing of these two rare words is a picture of YHWH finding Israel in a howling waste and hovering over her. See Tool 11 Move 4 and Biblical Theology.

(e) ṣelem (6754) and dəmût (1823) — “image” and “likeness”, 1:26–27. [T] High confidence on the range; the referent is the passage’s great uncertainty. ṣelem occurs 3× here (1:26, 1:27 ×2), dəmût once (1:26). In ordinary Hebrew usage ṣelem is a physical representation — a statue, a model — and dəmût a resemblance. In the ANE a ṣelem of a king stood in territory the king ruled but did not physically occupy. The text supplies function (rule, 1:26, 28) and pairing (male and female, 1:27) and supplies no definition. The report should not manufacture one. What is [T] is that the image is (i) of God, (ii) borne by humanity as male and female, and (iii) immediately connected to ruling.

(f) qārāʾ (7121) — “called”, five occurrences, all at 1:5, 1:8, 1:10. [T] verified High confidence. God names day, night, heaven, earth and seas — and then never names again. The naming is confined to the forming triad; not one creature is named by God in this passage. In Hebrew narrative naming is an act of authority (Adam over the animals, 2:19–20; Pharaoh renaming Joseph, 41:45; God renaming Abram, 17:5), which makes the silence at 1:20–25 a deliberate withholding rather than an oversight. See Headline 2.

(g) šābat (7673) — “ceased”, 2:2, 2:3. [T] verified High confidence. Its first two occurrences in the canon, and its only occurrences in Genesis apart from 8:22 (“shall not cease”). The verb means to stop, not to rest from fatigue; there is no word for tiredness anywhere in the passage, and Isa 40:28 says flatly that God neither faints nor grows weary. The noun šabbāt does not appear — the institution is not named here, only the act from which it will later take its name.

And the verb changes when the canon picks it up, which is worth knowing before quoting Exodus as though it repeated Genesis. [T] verified Genesis 2:2–3 has God šābat (cease). Exodus 20:11 has him wayyānaḥ — from nûaḥ, the verb of coming to rest — before it names “the sabbath day”. Exodus 31:17 has both, šābat wayyinnāpaš (“ceased and was refreshed”). So the Decalogue grounds the command in this passage while rendering its central act with a different verb, and the one place Genesis itself uses nûaḥ is 8:4, of the ark coming to rest on Ararat. Preaching “God rested because he was tired” is a lexical error against Genesis; preaching “God instituted the Sabbath here” is a canonical anticipation the passage has not yet made.

Paronomasia check (words recurring three or more times). Run on ʾĕlōhîm (35), ʾereṣ (21), yôm (14), mayim (11), šāmayim (11), ʿāśāh (10), mîn (10), rāqîaʿ (9), ṭôv (7). Nil return: no root in the passage shares its consonantal pattern with another significant word in a different semantic domain. The one candidate — šāmayim / mayim, heaven and waters, which do share -ayim — is a dual/plural ending, not a root echo, and is not reported. [T]

8. Translations

NASB95 against the KJV, on the load-bearing renderings:

  • 1:1 — NASB95 “In the beginning God created”; KJV identical. Both take rēʾšît as absolute. The alternative — “When God began to create the heavens and the earth, the earth being formless…” — is a real reading held by real scholars, and neither of these versions gives it. The WLC morphology parses rēʾšît as absolute (Ncfsa), which supports the traditional rendering; the argument for the construct reading rests on the word’s being anarthrous and on the analogy of Gen 2:4 and 5:1. [T] on the morphological tag; [S] on the debate. Moderate confidence that the absolute reading is correct — this is a genuine crux and the report does not resolve it.
  • 1:2 “was moving over” (NASB95) / “moved upon” (KJV) — both render məraḥepet with a verb of motion. The participle is feminine (agreeing with rûaḥ) and durative; “hovering” (so ESV, NIV) is closer to what Deut 32:11 shows the verb doing. NASB95’s “moving” is the weaker of the two English options here, and the preacher should say “hovering”. High confidence.
  • 1:2 “the Spirit of God” (NASB95, KJV) — rûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm can be rendered “the Spirit of God”, “the wind of God”, or, taking ʾĕlōhîm as a superlative, “a mighty wind”. All three are grammatically available. Both literal versions choose the first. [T] on the ambiguity; the choice is interpretive and should be named as such rather than assumed.
  • 1:26 “Let Us make” — NASB95 and KJV agree. The plural is the passage’s other great crux (see Difficult Verses).
  • 2:2 “By the seventh day God completed” — NASB95’s “by” is an interpretive smoothing of bayyôm haššəbîʿî, which is simply “on the seventh day”. The KJV has “on the seventh day God ended his work”. NASB95 is trying to avoid the appearance that God worked on the seventh; the Hebrew does not make that move, and neither does the KJV. This is where the ancient-versions check fires.

Ancient versions check. The trigger holds: the moderns agree on 2:2, and the rendering carries weight. The LXX does not agree — it reads ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ἕκτῃ, “on the sixth day”. Verified in Swete and in the Rahlfs-based Logos export; the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Peshitta are reported to agree with the Greek [S]. Under the Three-Way Triage this is a category 2 substantive variant — see Textual Variants — and it is the tidier reading, which is a mark against it. Translation-tradition split: MT and the English versions have God finishing on the seventh day and ceasing on it; the Greek tradition has him finishing on the sixth and ceasing on the seventh. High confidence on the divergence; moderate on which is prior.

A second ancient-versions finding, and it is a large one. LXX 1:2 renders tōhû wābōhû as ἀόρατος καὶ ἀκατασκεύαστος — “invisible and unformed”. [T] verified in Swete That is not a translation of the Hebrew pair but a philosophical construal of it, and it is the reading through which most patristic exegesis of Genesis 1 was done. Under the triage this is category 1 with a category 3 tail: the Greek cannot carry the Hebrew’s concrete waste-and-emptiness, and the rendering it substitutes went on to do real work in the tradition. Report it as a fact about the Greek, not as a corruption.

Pulpit divergence note: N/A — no pulpit text declared. When one is, the two renderings to check first are 1:2 (rûaḥ, and “hovering” against “moving”) and 2:2 (“by” against “on”).

9. Tone and Feel

Measured, unhurried, regal — and entirely without strain. The register is that of a sovereign at work, not a combatant. Nothing resists; nothing has to be defeated; there is no effort anywhere in the passage and no adversary. [T]

The devices carrying it:

  • Formulaic repetition — the announcement/fiat/execution/evaluation cycle runs six times with small variations, producing a liturgical cadence rather than a narrative arc. The reader is not held in suspense; there is no tension to resolve.
  • Accumulation — “and God said… and God saw… and there was evening and there was morning” builds by addition, and the additions are all positive. Seven “good”s, no reversals.
  • Deceleration at the end. The six days occupy 31 verses; the seventh occupies three — but those three contain no action, three occurrences of “the seventh day”, and the passage’s only “because”. The narrative brakes hard exactly where nothing happens. [T]
  • The two moments of raised voice. 1:26’s “Let Us make” breaks the impersonal jussive pattern of every previous fiat (“let there be”, “let the waters bring forth”) with a cohortative in the first person plural — the only self-address in the passage. And 1:31’s hinnēh ṭôv məʾōd is the only intensified evaluation. Both fall on the sixth day.

Soundtrack: not a fanfare, and certainly not a battle. Something closer to a slow processional that gains an instrument on each entry and then, at the end, holds a single sustained chord it does not resolve.

A note for the preacher. The chapter’s emotional architecture is calm, and calm is difficult to preach. The temptation is to import drama the text does not have — a cosmic battle, a debate with the ancients, an argument with modernity. Everything the passage does, it does without raising its voice, and a sermon that shouts is not honouring its tone.

Deuteronomy 32:10–11 ← Genesis 1:2 (high confidence on the lexical data; moderate on the intended link) [T] verified

The data. rāḥap occurs 3× in the Hebrew Bible: Gen 1:2, Deut 32:11, Jer 23:9. tōhû occurs 20×, of which Deut 32:10 is the only other Torah instance. The two words stand in consecutive verses at Deut 32:10–11, and nowhere else in the canon do they meet.

Source context (Move 1, run on the Deuteronomy end). Deut 32:10–11 sits in the Song of Moses, in the section rehearsing YHWH’s finding of Israel: “He found him in a desert land, and in the howling waste (bətōhû) of a wilderness; he encircled him, he cared for him, he guarded him as the pupil of his eye. What it adds. For a reader holding both texts — which the Torah’s audience does — the Spirit hovering over the formless deep and YHWH hovering over Israel in the wilderness waste are the same picture. Genesis 1:2’s hovering is not brooding incubation (the folk-etymology “brooding like a hen”, which the Hebrew does not support) but protective attendance over something not yet ordered. And Deuteronomy is doing the borrowing: it describes the making of a people in the vocabulary of the making of a world. The direction of dependence is not resolvable from the texts alone and is left open.

12. Genre

Hebrew narrative prose, highly patterned — not poetry, and not a scientific report. [T] The wayyiqtol chain, the absence of parallelism outside 1:27, and the formulaic cycle all mark it as prose; the strict symmetry, the numerical structure and the refrains mark it as prose of a very deliberate kind. “Exalted prose narrative” is a fair description; “poetry” is not, and calling it poetry is often a way of lowering the stakes rather than reading the genre.

The days are genuinely contested, and the report does not resolve it. The main options — twenty-four-hour days; a literary framework of forming and filling; a functional-ordering account; a day-age scheme — are held by serious readers, and the passage’s own signals point in more than one direction at once. For the ordered-literary reading: the forming/filling symmetry, light on day 1 before luminaries on day 4, the numerical architecture, the unclosed seventh. For the plain-sequence reading: yôm with an ordinal and with “evening and morning” ordinarily means a day; Exod 20:11 argues from the six-and-one to Israel’s working week. Name the options; do not let the question consume the sermon (see Preaching Pitfalls).

13. Copycat

Almost the entire passage is descriptive of God’s action and therefore not available for imitation at all. God speaks, separates, makes, sees, names, blesses, ceases, sanctifies. None of that is a template. [T]

Three things in the passage are addressed to humanity, and each needs its own label:

Element Label Note
1:28 “Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth” Blessing, in the form of a command It is introduced by “God blessed them, and God said to them” — the imperative is the content of a blessing, not a bare law. It is re-issued to Noah (9:1, 7) after the flood and reported fulfilled at Exod 1:7. Treating it as an obligation laid on every individual mistakes a corporate blessing for a personal duty.
1:28 “subdue it… and rule” Normative pattern, mandated kābaš (1×) and rādāh (2×) are strong verbs. What constrains them is their setting: the rule is exercised by an image, on behalf of the one imaged, over a world already declared good six times. A rule that damages what God called good has stopped representing him. [I]
2:2–3 God ceased and sanctified Descriptive of God — and not yet prescriptive The passage gives no command to anyone to rest. The sabbath command is Exod 20:8–11, which grounds itself in this verse. Preaching the command here rather than the ground is a timeline error.

The passive-receipt observation. Humanity does nothing in this passage. It is made, blessed, spoken to and given food. The first thing said about the man is what God did; the first thing said to him is a blessing. Whatever posture the passage models, it is reception before activity. [I]

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits: at the origin — before the fall, before covenant, before Israel, before law. Everything on the timeline is downstream. [T]

Where the reader stands: this side of the cross and the resurrection, in the already/not-yet, with the whole canon in hand.

What has happened in between that materially changes the reading:

  • The fall. The world the passage calls “very good” is not the world the reader inhabits. The most common timeline error with this passage is preaching creation’s goodness as a description of present experience. It is a description of a state, not of the reader’s world — which is why Romans 8:20–22 has to speak of creation’s subjection to futility.
  • The image, marred and restored. 1:26–27 is pre-fall; 9:6 shows the image surviving the fall; Col 1:15 and Rom 8:29 show it fulfilled in Christ and being restored in his people. Preaching 1:27 without that arc yields either a naive anthropology or a moral demand.
  • The rest, still open. Exodus 20:11 turns the seventh day into a command; Psalm 95 warns a generation that failed to enter God’s rest; Hebrews 4:4 quotes 2:2 and argues that a sabbath rest therefore remains. The unclosed seventh day of this passage is what makes the Hebrews argument possible.

Canonical position: Torah, first book, first passage — see Phase 0.55. [T]

15. Who Am I?

God is the only agent and is not a character to identify with. [T]

Humanity (ʾādām, 1:26–30) is where the reader stands — and the identification is genuinely direct, which is unusual for an OT narrative. There is no individual protagonist here to mistake for oneself, no hero whose courage might be moralised. ʾādām is generic and collective (“let them rule”, 1:26; “male and female he created them”, 1:27), so the identification is with humanity as such, not with a figure. The Moses-is-me error has no purchase on this passage, which is a mercy.

But there is a Christological correction to make. The reader is not the true image. Colossians 1:15 gives that title to Christ, and 1 Cor 15:45–49 makes the first ʾādām the type and the last the antitype. So the honest identification is: the reader is the image-bearer who has not kept the mandate, reading about the vocation that Christ has taken up and fulfilled. Identification with 1:26–28 without that qualification produces exactly the moralism the overview’s trap list warns against. [I]

The creatures are neither examples nor foils; they are the world the mandate concerns.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the passage seeks. The passage issues no command to its reader, so the response it seeks is not first an action but a settled way of seeing: that the world is made, made by word, made good, and made for rest — and that the reader is in it as a representative, not an owner. The one imperative in the passage (1:28) is the content of a blessing.

Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.

Worldview. Three shifts, in the order the passage makes them:

  1. There is one God and he is not part of what he made. Thirty-five occurrences of ʾĕlōhîm as subject, no rival, no consort, no struggle. The sea-monsters are among the things he made and called good; the great lights are not even named.
  2. The world is good because he says so, and its goodness is reported, not announced. Seven times the narrator lets the reader overhear a verdict nobody in the story hears. The goodness of things is not a human evaluation and does not depend on human agreement.
  3. The point of the six days is the seventh. The structure arrives at rest, and the rest is blessed and set apart before any human being has done a day’s work.

Behaviour — start. Exercise the rule of 1:28 as a representative: over a world already called good, on behalf of the one whose image you bear. That is a genuine and specific implication, and it is the passage’s only imperative.

Behaviour — stop. Stop treating creation’s goodness as a thing to be argued about rather than received. And stop, in the plain sense: the passage’s own destination is cessation, and a reader who cannot stop has not yet believed that the work was finished by someone else.

Motivation. Not duty. The mandate arrives inside a blessing (1:28), and the rest arrives before any work of ours (2:3). Application here flows from gift, or it is not this passage’s application.

Four audiences.

  • For me: Which part of my life am I holding as owner rather than as image-bearer? And can I stop — actually stop — for a day, on the ground that the work was finished without me?
  • For a Christian friend: The one exhausted by a life that never closes needs the seventh day that has no evening, and needs to hear that it was blessed before anyone had earned it.
  • For the church community: A congregation that receives the world as good, and its own vocation as representation rather than possession, will handle money, work, bodies and the created order differently from one that treats Genesis 1 as a battleground.
  • For an unbeliever: The chapter makes no argument and asks for none. It states that the world is made and good and going somewhere. That claim can be laid alongside the alternatives without heat.

Prayer. You spoke and it was. You looked at what you had made and called it good, and you said so before anyone was there to hear it. Teach us to receive this world as a gift, to rule it as those who bear your image and not as owners, and to stop — because you finished the work, and the day you blessed is still open.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. Not recoverable from the text, and the report will not manufacture it. [S] Genesis names no author, audience, place or occasion; the presenting situation the sweep and most commentaries assume — wilderness Israel with the tabernacle standing — is a reconstruction, and this run follows the overview in tagging it [S] rather than [T].

What the ancient reader knew that we do not.

  • A three-tier cosmos was the shared furniture of the ancient world: waters above, a solid expanse holding them back, waters below. rāqîaʿ is from a root meaning to hammer something flat, and the LXX’s στερέωμα (“firm thing”) reads it that way. The passage uses that furniture without arguing about it, which tells us the cosmology is the medium, not the message. [S] high confidence on the term; moderate on how the first readers pictured it.
  • Naming was an exercise of authority, not a labelling convenience. This is well attested across the ANE and inside the Bible itself. It is what makes God’s refusal to name the creatures a live observation rather than an argument from silence. [I]
  • An image (ṣelem) of a king stood in territory the king ruled but did not occupy, representing his authority in his absence. That this is the background against which 1:26 would have been heard is widely held and, unlike much ANE material, materially changes the reading: “image” is first a matter of function and standing, and only then of resemblance. [S] moderate–high.
  • Sun, moon and sea were gods for everyone else. Here the sun and moon are not named — only “the greater light” and “the lesser light” — and the sea-monsters (tannînîm), which elsewhere in the canon are the stuff of chaos-combat imagery (Ps 74:13; Isa 27:1; 51:9), are here simply among the creatures God made and called good. [T] on the text; [S] on the background.

The ANE parallels, and where to put them. Enuma Elish, Atrahasis and the Egyptian cosmogonies are real, and they are [S] in every particular. Nothing in the text refers to them. Where a commentary treats the chapter as a polemic against a specific Babylonian text, that is a reconstruction of setting, not a reading of the page. The report’s position: the demotion of sun, moon and sea is on the page and can be preached; the identification of a target is not, and should not become the sermon’s subject. See Tool 1 and Preaching Pitfall 2.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Torah — readers of the founding story, who by definition know nothing before this. This is the only passage in Scripture where the canonical reader arrives with an empty hand. Everything the passage does is establishing.

The Torah cult-context, named as the extension requires. For the Torah the compositional and canonical audiences coincide: readers who hold the creation account and the tabernacle simultaneously. That frame is live here in a specific way — the seven divine speeches, the pattern of command-and-compliance, the sequence ending in a sanctified seventh, and the verbs of separating and blessing are the vocabulary of the cult, and Exod 39–40 will narrate the tabernacle’s construction in a form that answers this passage’s shape (a work completed, seen, blessed). This is intra-Pentateuchal mutual illumination, not later theology read back. [I] moderate–high — the shape correspondence is real; the strength of the claim depends on how tightly Exod 39–40’s formulae track these, which this run has not verified reference by reference. Flagged for a solo check before it enters a sermon.

What they brought. A world in which the heavens were populated by powers and the sea by monsters, in which order was won rather than spoken, and in which humanity existed to feed the gods.

Shocking. That the great lights are lamps with a job — to separate, to mark times, to give light — and are not even named. That the sea-monsters are on the good list.

Surprising. That humanity is made last and blessed, rather than made to relieve the gods of labour; and that the ṣelem is borne by everyone, male and female, rather than by a king.

Comforting. That the world is not a battlefield and its order is not provisional. Nothing in the chapter has to be defended.

Disturbing. The mandate. To be given rule is to be given account.

What we bring that they didn’t. A question about mechanism. Every modern reader arrives asking how, and the passage is answering who and what for. That is not a reason to refuse the how-question elsewhere; it is a reason not to make it this passage’s business.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1 — Temple and God’s presence. Where this passage sits: at the origin. [T] on the passage; [I] on the temple reading. The cosmos is built as a sanctuary is built — realms, then occupants, then a sanctified seventh — and the ordering, separating and blessing vocabulary is the vocabulary later used of the tabernacle. What comes after: the tabernacle (Exod 25–40), the temple, the departure of the glory (Ezek 10), Christ as the dwelling (John 1:14; 2:21), the church as temple, and finally Rev 21:22, where there is no temple because God and the Lamb are it. Payoff: it explains why the account ends in sanctification rather than in achievement. Confidence: moderate — the correspondence is real and widely held, but this run has not verified the Exod 39–40 formulae reference by reference.

2 — Image of God and new humanity. Where it sits: the origin of the theme. [T] After: the image marred but not erased (5:3; 9:6); Israel as God’s son; Christ as the image (Col 1:15) and the last Adam (1 Cor 15:45); believers conformed (Rom 8:29). Payoff: it is the only route by which 1:26–28 can be preached without moralism. The mandate is not a standard to meet; it is a vocation Christ has taken up.

3 — Land, rest and inheritance. Where it sits: the first and defining member. [T] The seventh day is blessed and sanctified with no command attached and no evening. After: Exod 20:11 makes it a command; Josh 21:44 gives rest in the land; Ps 95 warns a generation that did not enter it; Heb 4:4 quotes 2:2 directly and argues that a sabbath rest therefore remains. Payoff: the unclosed seventh is what the whole later argument is built on, and it is a feature of the text, not an inference from it.

Confluence — and it is (c), climactic convergence. These three do not run in parallel. They meet in the seventh day. The sanctuary-shaped week arrives at a sanctified day; the image-bearers are blessed on the sixth and then given a seventh they did not make; and the rest that the land never delivered is the rest this day opens. Hebrews 4 is the passage where the convergence is finally stated — a rest, entered by a people, secured by a priest — and it gets there by quoting Genesis 2:2. The preacher who traces only one of the three will preach a smaller sermon than the text supports.

Schnittjer Pass

Skeleton: Genesis · primeval prologue, standing outside the toledot spine · serving the book’s leading idea by establishing the blessing that particularisation will narrow.

Findings:

  1. Structure & shape — the seventh day is the janus and the goal, not an appendix. It faces back (completing the six) and forward (opening what Exodus, the Psalms and Hebrews will enter). It is the only day with no evening. [T] Step 3.
  2. Narrative selectivity and time — the narrator brakes hardest where nothing happens. Six days of creation in 31 verses; three verses for a day with no act, containing three occurrences of “the seventh day” and the passage’s only causal clause. Braking is weight; the weight is on the rest. [T] Step 1.
  3. Repetition & linkage — the numerical architecture is the leading device, and it verifies: 7-word opening, 14-word second verse, ʾĕlōhîm 35, ʾereṣ 21, ṭôv 7, three consecutive seven-word clauses closing the unit. [T] verified Step 4.
  4. Repetition & linkage — the leading word is ʾĕlōhîm itself, 35 times, subject of nearly every clause. In a passage with no proper nouns, the one name carries everything. [T] Step 4.
  5. First vs second reading. On a first reading 1:31’s “very good” is simply the summit. On the re-read — from 3:17, from 6:5, from a coffin in Egypt — it is the high-water mark of a world that will not stay that way, and 2:18’s “not good” arrives fifteen verses later. [I] Step 5.
  6. Narrative space — the passage has no place in it. No location, no direction, no up or down, no east. The spatial vocabulary that will carry so much of Genesis (eastward exile, going down to Egypt, going up to the mountain) is entirely absent. Space here is made, not travelled. [T] Step 2.

Echoes and signals: leading word — ʾĕlōhîm; structural device — forming/filling symmetry answering tōhû/bōhû, arriving at an unclosed seventh; intertextual link — Deut 32:10–11’s tōhû + rāḥap, cross-reported to Tool 11.

Nil returns: type-scene (no repeated scene-pattern; there is nothing yet for a scene to be repeated from); chiasm at unit level (1:27 is chiastic within itself, but the unit is not); ellipsis; comparison/interchange between characters (there is only one agent).

Irreducibility. The strongest temptation with this passage is to reduce it to a proposition-list — God made everything, it was good, we bear his image, rest matters. Everything on that list is true and none of it is the passage, which is a told sequence arriving somewhere. What the story does that the list cannot is make the reader wait six days for a seventh, and then not close it.

Christological Reading

Type of connection: trajectory (two strands), with one contrast. No typology claim is made, and that is a deliberate finding.

Trajectory 1 — the image. 1:26–27 establishes the category that Col 1:15 (“the image of the invisible God”) and 1 Cor 15:45–49 (first and last Adam) fill. Tests: theological category ✓ (image/sonship is established); NT precedent ✓ (explicit and repeated); escalation ✓ (Christ is the image, not an image-bearer); authorial pattern — partial: Genesis raises the category and leaves it unresolved but does not itself signal a coming fulfilment. Three of four → trajectory, not typology, and high confidence as a trajectory. [T] / [S: NT]

Trajectory 2 — the rest. 2:2–3 establishes a blessed, sanctified, unclosed seventh day. Heb 4:4 quotes 2:2 verbatim and argues from the absence of a closing formula that the rest remains open. Tests: category ✓ (sabbath/rest); NT precedent ✓ (Hebrews argues from this verse); escalation ✓ (a rest entered through a priest, not a day observed); authorial pattern ✓ — the text itself creates the expectation by breaking its own six-fold refrain. Four of four. But the fulfilment runs through an institution, not a person or event, so it is classified trajectory with a strong authorial signal. High confidence. [T] / [S: NT]

Contrast — the mandate not kept. Humanity is given rule over a good world and by 3:17 the ground is cursed because of the ruler. The passage’s vocation is the standard the rest of Genesis measures failure against. Christ answers it as the last Adam who does keep the word, and as the one through whom all things hold together (Col 1:17). [I]

Christophany: none. The rûaḥ ʾĕlōhîm of 1:2 is not an appearance; treating it as one overreaches.

A word about “Let Us make” (1:26). It is regularly preached as a Trinitarian text. The report’s position: the plural is genuinely unexplained in the passage, the main readings are set out under Difficult Verses, and the Trinitarian reading is a legitimate canonical reading but not a grammatical finding. Preaching it as though Genesis 1:26 proves the Trinity gives an unbeliever a very easy objection and is not needed — John 1:1–3 does the work far better, and does it by rewriting this passage’s opening words.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptation here: there is no human character to imitate, which removes the usual danger — and creates a subtler one. The moralism this passage invites is “steward creation well” as bare ethics, or “take a day off” as bare discipline. Both convert a blessing into a duty.
  • The gospel grounding: the mandate arrives inside a blessing (1:28), and the seventh day is blessed and sanctified before any human being has worked at all (2:3). Nothing in the passage is earned.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the true image has come and has kept the vocation; the rest that the seventh day opened has been entered by a forerunner. Preach 1:26–28 through Col 1:15 and 2:2–3 through Heb 4, and the imperatives land as consequences of grace rather than as conditions of it.

Difficult / Contested Verses

1:1–2 — the syntax, and creation ex nihilo

  • Category: doctrinal / apologetic.
  • The difficulty: whether 1:1 is an independent clause (“In the beginning God created…”), a summary title for the whole account, or a temporal clause subordinate to 1:2 or 1:3 (“When God began to create…, the earth being formless…, God said…”). On the third reading the chapter opens with unformed matter already present, and creatio ex nihilo is not taught here.
  • Rhetorical function in context: 1:1 functions as the passage’s opening statement of agency — whatever its syntax, the sentence’s work is to name God as the one who made the heavens and the earth. That function is stable across all three readings.
  • The main options: absolute/independent (traditional; supported by the WLC’s own parsing, the ancient versions, and John 1:1’s reuse); summary title (1:1 heads the account, 1:2 gives the state, 1:3 begins); construct/temporal (grammatically possible on the anarthrous form; analogous to 2:4 and 5:1).
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the WLC parses rēʾšît as absolute [T], the LXX read it as absolute, and John 1:1 reuses Ἐν ἀρχῇ as an independent statement. That is a strong cumulative case, and it is not a knock-down one. Moderate–high confidence. And a doctrinal note worth making from the pulpit: creatio ex nihilo does not stand or fall with this verse. Heb 11:3, Rom 4:17 and John 1:3 carry it, and a sermon that stakes the doctrine on a contested Hebrew construction is building on the weakest available foundation.
  • Suggested handling: address briefly, name the options honestly, and root the doctrine where the canon roots it.

1:26 — “Let Us make”

  • Category: doctrinal.
  • The difficulty: a first-person plural cohortative in a passage whose every other verb of God takes singular concord — including in the very next verse (“God created… in his image”).
  • Rhetorical function in context: it marks the sixth day’s climax by breaking the fiat pattern. Whatever the plural means, its function is to slow the narrative at the making of humanity — the only place God deliberates aloud. [T]
  • The main options: a divine council of heavenly beings (cf. 1 Kgs 22:19; Job 1:6; Ps 82:1 — and note 3:22’s “like one of us”); a plural of deliberation or self-address; a plural of majesty (weakest — poorly attested for Hebrew verbs); an intra-Trinitarian address (the classic Christian reading).
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the singular of 1:27 constrains any reading that makes the plural a plurality of creators — whoever is addressed, God alone creates. [T] Beyond that the passage does not say, and the report does not resolve it. The divine-council reading has the most OT support; the Trinitarian reading has the most canonical warrant. They are not mutually exclusive.
  • Suggested handling: acknowledge; do not make it the sermon’s proof-text for the Trinity.

1:1–2:3 — the days, and the science question

  • Category: apologetic / pastoral / tribal.
  • The difficulty: the relation between this account and modern cosmology and biology. Real people in the congregation hold real and opposed positions, and some have been told their faith depends on one of them.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the day-sequence functions as architecture — six working days arriving at a seventh — and the architecture is what Exod 20:11 later argues from.
  • The main options: twenty-four-hour days; a literary framework; a functional-ordering account; day-age. Each is held by people who believe the Bible.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the passage’s own signals point more than one way (Tool 12), and the text nowhere raises the question it is being asked. The report takes no position.
  • Suggested handling: name the question honestly and early, say plainly that orthodox Christians differ, say what the passage is actually doing — and then do that. The pastoral point is the important one: hearers who have been taught that their faith stands on a particular reading of the days need to hear that it does not, and hearers who have quietly concluded the chapter is indefensible need to hear what it actually claims.

1:28 — “subdue it… and rule”

  • Category: ethically troubling / weaponised.
  • The difficulty: kābaš is a strong verb (elsewhere of subjugating land or people), and 1:28 has been used to license exploitation and, from the other side, cited as evidence that the Bible is ecologically irresponsible.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the content of a blessing (1:28 opens “God blessed them, and God said to them”), spoken to image-bearers, over a world the narrator has just called good six times.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the verbs are strong and the report will not soften them — this is dominion language and flattening it into “gentle care” is not accurate. What constrains it is not a weaker verb but the setting: rule exercised by an image, on behalf of the one imaged, over what he called good. [T] on the verbs and the setting; [I] on the constraint.
  • Suggested handling: address directly. Name the misuse in both directions once, then preach the setting.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Spending the sermon on the days

  • What it looks like: the first sermon of a Genesis series adjudicating young-earth against old-earth, and the congregation leaving having heard about geology.
  • Why it’s wrong: the question is real but it is not this passage’s question, and the passage’s own signals do not settle it. Meanwhile everything the chapter does say — one God, ordered world, spoken into being, good, image, mandate, rest — goes unpreached.
  • The corrective: name the question in two honest minutes, say that Christians who believe the Bible differ, and then preach the text. And say the pastoral thing: nobody’s faith rests on this.

Pitfall 2: Making the chapter a polemic

  • What it looks like: “Genesis 1 was written to refute Enuma Elish” — delivered with more confidence than the evidence carries, and often as the sermon’s organising idea.
  • Why it’s wrong: it sounds scholarly, which makes it harder to spot. Nothing in the text names a rival, and the ANE material is [S] in every particular. The demotion of sun, moon and sea is achieved by silence — the lights are not named — and converting silence into argument turns a statement into a debate the chapter declines to have.
  • The corrective: preach the demotion, which is on the page. Leave the target off it.

Pitfall 3: “God rested because he was tired”

  • What it looks like: a sermon on rest built on divine fatigue, usually kindly meant and aimed at exhausted people.
  • Why it’s wrong: šābat means to cease, not to recover; there is no vocabulary of tiredness anywhere in the passage, and Isa 40:28 says flatly that God does not grow weary.
  • The corrective: the seventh day is about completion, not recuperation — God stops because the work is finished. Which is, in fact, far better news for exhausted people than the sentimental version.

Pitfall 4: Preaching the sabbath command from 2:2–3

  • What it looks like: “Here God institutes the Sabbath, so you should keep it.”
  • Why it’s wrong: a timeline error. The passage gives no command to anyone, the noun šabbāt does not occur, and the institution belongs to Exod 20:8–11 — which grounds itself in this verse. Preaching the command here skips the ground.
  • The corrective: preach what is here: a day blessed and sanctified before anyone had worked, and left open. Then let Heb 4 do what it does with it.

Pitfall 5: Using 1:26 as a Trinity proof-text

  • What it looks like: “‘Let Us make’ — there is the Trinity in the first chapter of the Bible.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the very next verse has singular concord throughout (“God created… in his image”), and the plural has serious alternative explanations, the divine council chief among them. The claim hands a thoughtful hearer an easy objection and gains nothing.
  • The corrective: the plural marks the sixth day’s climax — the only place God deliberates aloud. If a Trinitarian reading of creation is wanted, John 1:1–3 rewrites this passage’s opening words and gives it far more securely.

Pitfall 6: Moralising the mandate

  • What it looks like: the sermon lands on “so steward creation well” or “so take a day off”, and stops there.
  • Why it’s wrong: both convert a blessing into a duty. The mandate arrives inside a blessing; the seventh day is sanctified before anyone has worked. Application that does not run through the true image (Col 1:15) and the rest that remains (Heb 4) is ethics with a Genesis text attached.
  • The corrective: preach the vocation Christ has taken up and the rest he has entered — then the imperatives are consequences of grace instead of conditions of it.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. The 1:1 syntax. Absolute, summary title, or construct. The corpus morphology favours absolute and the ancient versions read it so, but the question is genuinely open. Verify against a grammar that works the crux.
  2. The referent of ṣelem / dəmût. Functional, relational, ontological, representative — the passage supplies function and pairing and no definition. This report declines to supply one.
  3. The plural of 1:26. Divine council, deliberation, majesty, or intra-Trinitarian. Unresolved, and 1:27’s singular constrains it more than it settles it.
  4. The 2:2 variant. Position stated (MT, on the harder-reading ground) at moderate confidence; not checked against BHS’s apparatus or the Göttingen edition.
  5. The tabernacle correspondence. The Exod 39–40 shape-correspondence is asserted at moderate confidence and has not been verified formula by formula. It should be before it enters a sermon — this is exactly the kind of synthetic claim the September audit found failing.
  6. The Deut 32:10–11 link. The lexical data are verified and exact. Whether Deuteronomy is deliberately reaching back to Genesis 1:2, or the shared vocabulary is a common register for wilderness-and-care, is not resolvable from the texts and is left open.
