## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools. All five are verified against the corpus; the verification commands are recorded in the Text-First Declaration.

1. **In the Leningrad tradition, the paragraph divisions give Deuteronomy's Ten Words ten units and Exodus's nine — and the extra division is a setumah *inside* Deuteronomy 5:21, separating "your neighbour's wife" from "your neighbour's house".** `[T]` for the divisions; `[S: Logos — Keil & Delitzsch]` for the manuscript spread. Surfaced by: Structure (Tool 3), Original Languages, Translations (Tool 8), Schnittjer Pass step 3. In the WLC, Exodus 20 breaks after vv. 6, 7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 — nine units across vv. 2–17. Deuteronomy 5 breaks after vv. 10, 11, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, **within 21**, and after 21 — ten units across vv. 6–21. So the two texts, as Leningrad transmits them, **do not merely permit different countings of the ten; they are paragraphed so as to produce them** — Deuteronomy's division separating a ninth word (the wife) from a tenth (the household), which is what the Augustinian, Roman and Lutheran numbering requires, and Exodus's paragraphing declining to yield it.

   **Two qualifications, both material, both added at the checking stage.** First, **the parashoth are not authorial.** They pre-date the Masoretes (the Mishnah already knows them) but they are an early Jewish reading tradition, not part of the text — so this finding is evidence about how the words were *received and divided*, never about how they were *given*. Second, **the coveting-word setumah is not universal in the Hebrew manuscripts:** Keil & Delitzsch report it absent from **234 of the 694 manuscripts Kennicott collated**, and from many printed editions. `[S: Logos — K&D, *Commentary on the OT* 1:393–94]` **The nine-versus-ten contrast is therefore a fact about Leningrad and BHS, not about "the Hebrew" — and this report says only the former.** *High confidence that the internal break at Deut 5:21 stands in both the WLC and the Logos BHS export; moderate that the contrast between the two books holds across the wider manuscript tradition; and see Open Questions 1 and 1a, one of which this qualification sharpens and the other of which it creates.*

2. **Deuteronomy's Decalogue is introduced by two consecutive first-person "I"s with different speakers; Exodus's is introduced by the narrator. The mediation Patrick asked about is in the grammar, not merely in the setting.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Vocabulary (Tool 7), Narrator's Comment (Tool 6), Structure, Positional Necessity. Exodus 20:1 is narration — *"and God spoke"* — and every אָנֹכִי in the chapter (20:2, 5) is God's. Deuteronomy 5 has אָנֹכִי five times (5:1, 5, 6, 9, 31), and vv. 5 and 6 are consecutive: **"*I* was standing between the LORD and you" is immediately followed by "*I* am the LORD your God."** Two speakers, the same word, adjacent verses, with only the word לֵאמֹר between them. Deuteronomy 5:4 heightens it further: the LORD spoke to you פָּנִים בְּפָנִים — a phrase that occurs **once in the entire Hebrew Bible**, where the ordinary idiom פָּנִים אֶל־פָּנִים occurs five times (Gen 32:31; Exod 33:11; Judg 6:22; Ezek 20:35; Deut 34:10), and in Exodus and Deuteronomy is used **of Moses**. Deuteronomy asserts unmediated encounter for the people in a form found nowhere else, and in the very next breath puts a mediator in the middle of it. *High confidence.*

3. **The verb צוה ("command") occurs five times in Deuteronomy 5 and not once in Exodus 20.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition (Tool 10), Tool 11 Move 2, Linking Words. Deuteronomy 5:12, 15, 16, 32, 33 — including the twice-repeated **כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוְּךָ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ**, "as the LORD your God commanded you" (5:12, 16), a clause Exodus cannot contain because in Exodus the commanding is happening now. The whole of Exodus 20 (all twenty-six verses) has no form of צוה at all. Deuteronomy's Decalogue is therefore **not presented as a parallel giving but as a re-issue that names the prior giving** — which settles the methodological question in the text's own favour: these are not two versions to be harmonised but an original and its authorised re-preaching. *High confidence.*

4. **The whole theological difference in the fourth word can be read off the subject of one verb.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Original Languages, Repetition, So What?, Biblical Theology. נוח ("rest") occurs once in Exodus 20 and once in Deuteronomy 5. At **Exodus 20:11 the one who rests is the LORD** (וַיָּנַח); at **Deuteronomy 5:14 the one who rests is the slave** (לְמַעַן יָנוּחַ עַבְדְּךָ). Deuteronomy keeps the command and swaps the ground — creation for exodus — and the swap carries the verb with it. Reinforcing this: עֶבֶד occurs three times in Exodus 20 (2, 10, 17) and **four** in Deuteronomy 5 (6, 14, 15, 21), the extra link being 5:15's "you were a slave", so that Deuteronomy's slave-chain runs unbroken from the prologue's "house of slaves" to the neighbour's slave one must not covet. *High confidence.*

5. **The Greek tradition harmonised the two Decalogues toward Deuteronomy, and three New Testament citations follow Greek Deuteronomy's order of the short prohibitions.** `[T]` for the readings; `[I]` for "harmonised toward Deuteronomy" as the direction. Surfaced by: Translations (ancient-versions check), Tool 11, Textual Variants. In Rahlfs, **Greek Exodus 20:10 already has the ox, the donkey and all your cattle; 20:12 already has "that it may be well with you"; and 20:17 already has the wife first and the field** — three Deuteronomic pluses standing in Greek Exodus where the Hebrew has none of them. And the order of the short prohibitions differs three ways: Hebrew (both books) murder–adultery–steal; Greek Exodus adultery–steal–murder; Greek Deuteronomy adultery–murder–steal. **Luke 18:20, Romans 13:9 and James 2:11 all put adultery before murder** — Greek Deuteronomy's order — while Matthew 19:18 and Mark 10:19 keep the Hebrew order. The apostles are quoting a Decalogue that has already been shaped by the very comparison this dig is running. *High confidence on the readings (verified in Rahlfs and SBLGNT); moderate on any account of why the Greek moved as it did.*

## Phase 0.55 — Canonical Position

**Section.** Both books stand in the **Torah** — Exodus second of five, Deuteronomy fifth and last. In BHS order the Torah opens the canon, so neither book has any earlier Scripture to quote except what precedes it within the Torah itself.

**Reading implication.** The Torah is the canon's fountainhead, not a borrower. For this passage the consequence is exact and unusual: **the "source text" of Deuteronomy 5 is another passage in the same five-volume work.** The Citation Triad therefore runs *inside* the Torah — which is why the Exodus overview's finding that every completed Exodus dig found its richest material in Move 4 rather than Move 2 applies here in an inverted form: for Deuteronomy 5, Move 2 (book usage) and Move 4 (internal echo) are almost the same operation, because Deuteronomy's "source" is its own corpus.

**Presupposes.** Deuteronomy 5 presupposes, with checked references: Exodus 19:16–19 (the fire, cloud, and voice on the mountain — Deut 5:4, 22–23); Exodus 20:1–17 (the words themselves); Exodus 20:18–21 (the people's terror and request for a mediator — Deut 5:23–27, where Moses reports it as their initiative and adds God's approval, which Exodus does not record); Exodus 31:18 / 34:28 (the writing on the tablets — Deut 5:22); Genesis 2:1–3 (the creation ground Deuteronomy *drops*, and which Exod 20:11 carries). Exodus 20 in turn presupposes Genesis 2:1–3 and Exodus 19:4–8.

**Handoff.** Deuteronomy 5 hands the rest of the book its warrant: the people's request and God's endorsement of it (5:28–31) are what authorise chapters 6–26 as Moses' exposition, and 5:29's "oh that they had such a heart" plants the diagnosis that 10:16 and 29:4 state and 30:6 promises to cure.

**Neighbours.** Exodus 20 is bounded by the theophany (19:16–25) and the Book of the Covenant (20:22–23:33). Deuteronomy 5 is bounded by ch. 4's imageless-God argument and the Shema (6:4–9), which is not a new topic but the first commandment expounded.

**Canonical/compositional divergence.** None to report between these two texts: the canonical order (Exodus before Deuteronomy) and the narrative order (Horeb before Moab) agree, and Deuteronomy 5:12 and 5:16 explicitly mark themselves as posterior with כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוְּךָ. This is the rare case where the direction of dependence is stated by the later text rather than reconstructed. `[T]`

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Two passages, so the check runs twice.

### Exodus 20:1–17

**Preceding movement.** Israel has arrived at Sinai, received the eagles'-wings proposal (19:4–6), sworn "All that the LORD has spoken we will do" (19:8), been consecrated (19:10–15), and stood at the foot of a mountain wrapped in fire while God descended (19:16–20). A covenant has been offered and accepted and now awaits its terms. God has also announced a purpose: he comes in the cloud *"that the people may hear when I speak with you, and may also believe you forever"* (19:9).

**Necessity answer.** Exodus 20:1–17 exists here because a covenant sworn without terms is not yet a covenant, and because 19:9's stated purpose requires that the people actually hear God's own voice before they ask for it to stop. The Ten Words are the covenant's constitutional core, spoken by God directly to the whole people — the only body of law in Scripture given that way — and they are spoken *inside* the theophany, bracketed by the same phenomena at 19:16 and 20:18. The 19:9–20:21 dig established this bracket as the unit's architecture; this dig accepts it and works the centre panel.

**Implication.** Exodus 20 is not a legal appendix to a narrative but the speech-act the narrative was built to deliver — and the terror in which it is delivered is the reason the people immediately ask for a mediator. **The Decalogue in Exodus is the last thing the people ever hear God say directly.** That is what makes Deuteronomy's re-preaching necessary rather than merely convenient.

### Deuteronomy 5:1–33

**Preceding movement.** Chapters 1–3 have retold the journey; chapter 4 has argued at length from a single datum — *"you saw no form"* (4:12, 15) — that Israel must make no image, and has closed with the heading *"This is the law which Moses set before the sons of Israel"* (4:44) and the geographical note that fixes the sermon at Moab.

**Necessity answer.** Deuteronomy 5 exists here because 4:44 has announced *torah* and torah must begin with the ten words that constitute it; because chapter 4's imageless argument requires the second word to be restated in the form that grounds it; and, most importantly, because **the covenant has to be transferred to a generation that was not there.** That is the work 5:2–4 does — *"not with our fathers… but with us, with all those of us alive here today"* — and it is why the chapter opens with the collapsing of the generations before a single word of the Decalogue is quoted.

**Implication.** Deuteronomy's Decalogue is embedded in an act of contemporising, and that act is the reason for every one of its changes. The additions are not glosses; they are what re-preaching looks like. The chapter then earns the rest of the book: 5:23–31 retells the people's request, adds God's endorsement of it (*"they have done well"*, 5:28) — which Exodus does not report — and has God commission Moses to receive "all the commandment and the statutes and the judgments" (5:31). **Chapters 6–26 are legally downstream of Deuteronomy 5:31.**

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens both answers. Deuteronomy has been quarrying Exodus continuously since 1:6; the Decalogue is the first place it quotes Exodus *verbatim and at length* rather than summarising it. The switch from paraphrase to quotation is itself positional: Moses retells the story in his own words and quotes the words of God in God's.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level, Exodus.** Per the finalised overview: to reveal the LORD as the covenant-keeping Redeemer who defeats the powers, binds his people at Sinai, and comes down to dwell — the book's own thesis stated at 29:46. `[S: overview]`

**Passage-level, Exodus 20:1–17.** To deliver, in God's own voice and in the hearing of the whole people, the terms of the covenant they have already sworn to keep — grounded first in the rescue that has already happened. The purpose is achieved as much by the *mode* (direct divine speech, from fire, to everyone) as by the content: the Decalogue's authority in the rest of Scripture rests on the fact that no prophet mediated it. `[T]` The narrator marks this at 20:1 (*God spoke*) and Deuteronomy confirms it at 5:22 (*to all your assembly… and He added no more*).

**Book-level, Deuteronomy.** To bind the second generation to the one LORD in wholehearted love, and — in naming a heart-disease only God can heal — to lean the Torah forward. `[S: overview]`

**Passage-level, Deuteronomy 5:1–33.** To make the Horeb covenant *this* generation's covenant, by three moves in order: assert that it was made with them (5:2–5), quote it to them with the applications a landed, redeemed, slave-owning generation needs (5:6–21), and establish that Moses' ongoing exposition carries the same authority because the people themselves requested it and God approved (5:22–33). `[T]`

**The two purposes are not in competition, and this is the payoff of running them together.** Exodus's purpose requires that the words be *unmediated*; Deuteronomy's requires that they be *mediated and applied*. The two texts are the two halves of one claim about how God's word reaches a people who cannot bear to hear it directly. A dig on either alone will state one half and imply the other; only the synoptic run can show that the tension is authorial and deliberate.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above — both passages worked.*

#### General contextual notes

**Exodus 20 — immediately before:** 19:16–25, the theophany and the repeated warnings not to break through. **Immediately after:** 20:18–21 (terror and the request for a mediator) and 20:22–26, the altar law, which begins *"You yourselves have seen that I have spoken to you from heaven"* — the narrative's own summary of what has just happened. **Section:** the Sinai block, 19:1–24:18. **Chapter division:** the break at 19/20 hides a single scene, as the 19:9–20:21 dig established; the Decalogue has no independent narrative frame in Exodus at all.

**Deuteronomy 5 — immediately before:** 4:44–49, the heading and the geographical setting. **Immediately after:** 6:1–3 (the purpose clause) and 6:4–9, the Shema. **Section:** the second discourse, 4:44–28:68. **Chapter division:** unusually well placed here; 5:1's summons and 6:1's heading both mark real seams.

**Read out of context?** Constantly, and in a specific way. The Decalogue is the most frequently excerpted passage in the Old Testament, and it is nearly always excerpted *from verse 3 or verse 7* — that is, with the prologue removed. Both overviews name this as the trap. What the immediate context corrects is not a detail but the genre: with 20:2 / 5:6 attached these are covenant stipulations addressed to the already-rescued; without it they are a moral code addressed to anyone. **The excerpting is what makes the moralism possible.** `[T]`

**The specific content of the preceding failure — Deuteronomy only.** Deuteronomy 5:5 gives the reason Moses stood between: *"for you were afraid because of the fire and did not go up the mountain."* The failure is named, and it is not sin — 5:28 has God call the same response *"well"*. This is an unusual case: the preceding failure is a fear the text endorses. Naming it precisely matters, because a sermon that treats the people's terror as unbelief will contradict God's own verdict three verses later.

### 3. Structure

Both Decalogues have the same five-part shape, and the differences are all in parts three and four.

| Part | Exodus | Deuteronomy | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame (opening) | 20:1 — narrator | 5:1–5 — Moses' summons, the covenant-transfer claim, the mediation note | Establishes who is speaking, and to whom |
| Prologue | 20:2 | 5:6 | Redemption before demand — **identical** |
| God-ward words | 20:3–11 | 5:7–15 | No other gods · no image · the Name · the sabbath |
| Neighbour-ward words | 20:12–17 | 5:16–21 | Parents · murder · adultery · theft · witness · coveting |
| Frame (closing) | 20:18–21 — terror, request, endorsement by narration | 5:22–33 — terror, request, endorsement by divine speech, and the commission | What the hearing produces |

**The structural device in Exodus is the theophany bracket** (19:16 ‖ 20:18): the same four phenomena — thunder, lightning, trumpet, smoking mountain — before and after, so that the Decalogue is spoken *within* the terror. `[S: dig 19:9–20:21]` **The structural device in Deuteronomy is a quotation frame**: 5:5 ends לֵאמֹר ("saying") and 5:22 resumes *"These words the LORD spoke"* — Moses' speech opens a pair of quotation marks at 5:6 and closes them at 5:21. Everything between is God's voice inside Moses' voice.

#### The parashoth architecture

The paragraph divisions (Headline Finding 1) are the strongest structural datum either text supplies, because they are the tradition's own answer to a question **neither text states**: where does one word end and the next begin? The two answers differ, and they differ at exactly one place — the coveting word. `[T]`

**What the markers are.** The **פ** (*petuchah*, "open") and **ס** (*setumah*, "closed") are the *parashoth* — the Pentateuch's oldest system of textual division, functioning much as English paragraphs do. An open section began a new line; a closed section could begin part-way along one, after a gap. The Pentateuch carries 669 of the smaller kind (379 closed, 290 open) alongside the 54 larger sabbath-reading divisions. `[S: Logos — Hastings, *Dictionary of the Bible* 1:288; Kutz & Josberger]` **Their date matters for how much they can carry:** they pre-date the Masoretic pointing — the Mishnah already knows them, and the Gemara treats them as inviolable and ascribes them to Moses — **but they are not original to the text.** `[S: Logos — K&D 1:394; Murphy]` So they are strong evidence about how the Ten Words were read and divided in antiquity, and no evidence at all about how they were given. Nothing in this report treats them as authorial.

**What they do to the count, and the problem they create.** Keil & Delitzsch state the mechanism exactly as the corpus shows it: the Masoretic text groups **the words against other gods and against images, together with the jealousy-and-*ḥesed* clause, as a single section**, and this forces the counting problem — *"if the prohibitions against polytheism and idolatry are treated as one unit, the only way to maintain the total of ten is to divide the coveting commandment into two."* And they name the difficulty this dig found independently: the division is hard to make cleanly **because the two texts reverse the order of wife and house.** `[S: Logos — K&D 1:393–94]`

**This is an earned confirmation.** The grouping of the first two words into one paragraph, the split of the coveting word, and the wife/house reversal as the obstacle were all derived from the WLC before any commentary was opened — and K&D reach the same three points in print. The alignment with the Roman and Lutheran numbering is likewise noted independently. `[S: Logos — Bullinger, *Companion Bible* 2:34]`

**But the corpus sharpens the commentary at one point.** K&D describe "the Masoretic text" as if it behaved uniformly. It does not, across the two passages: **in Leningrad the coveting word is split in Deuteronomy 5:21 and not in Exodus 20:17.** If K&D are describing Exodus — the citation falls in the Pentateuch volume and the question put to Logos concerned Exodus — then either they are working from an edition whose Exodus carries the setumah, or the description has been generalised from Deuteronomy. **Kennicott's figures make the first entirely possible:** 460 of his 694 manuscripts *do* carry the disputed setumah. Either way the consequence for the preacher is the same and is worth stating plainly: **the Roman and Lutheran numbering is fully supported by Leningrad's Deuteronomy and only half-supported by its Exodus** — which groups words one and two but declines to split the tenth. Routed to Open Questions.

**Deuteronomy binds where Exodus separates.** Words six to ten in Deuteronomy are joined by waw (5:18, 19, 20, 21: *and* you shall not commit adultery, *and* you shall not steal, *and* you shall not bear witness, *and* you shall not covet); Exodus juxtaposes them asyndetically. `[T]` The effect is that Deuteronomy reads the second table as a single connected sentence and Exodus as a series of separate strokes. This is a structural difference no English version I know reproduces — the NASB95 renders both asyndetically. Note that the waw does not begin the run: 5:17 (murder) has no waw, so the chain hangs off the sixth word and gathers the last four to it.

### 4. Linking Words

Sparse in both — apodictic law does not argue — but the few that are present are load-bearing, and they are one of the two places the texts diverge most.

**אֲשֶׁר (Exod 20:2 // Deut 5:6).** *"I am the LORD your God, **who** brought you out."* The relative clause is the entire ground of everything that follows, and it is identical in both. `[T]`

**כִּי (Exod 20:5, 7, 11 // Deut 5:9, 11).** Three grounding "for"s in Exodus, two in Deuteronomy. **The one that goes missing is Exodus 20:11's** — *"**for** in six days the LORD made…"* — which Deuteronomy replaces not with another כִּי but with a different construction entirely (וְזָכַרְתָּ כִּי־עֶבֶד הָיִיתָ, "and you shall remember **that** you were a slave"). The particle is the same; its function is not. In Exodus כִּי introduces a fact about God; in Deuteronomy it introduces a fact about the hearer. `[T]` *High confidence — this is the syntactic form of Headline Finding 4.*

**עַל־כֵּן (Exod 20:11 // Deut 5:15).** *"Therefore the LORD blessed the sabbath day"* against *"therefore the LORD your God commanded you to observe the sabbath day."* **Same connector, different consequent.** In Exodus the therefore-clause tells you what God did to the day; in Deuteronomy it tells you what God did to you. `[T]` This is the single tidiest illustration in either text of what re-preaching does: the logic is preserved and the terminus is moved.

**כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוְּךָ (Deut 5:12, 16).** Not a logical connector but a temporal-authorising one, and absent from Exodus by necessity. See Headline Finding 3.

**לְמַעַן (Exod 20:12 once // Deut 5:14, 16 ×2 within one word, plus 5:29, 33).** Deuteronomy multiplies purpose clauses. In the fifth word alone it has two where Exodus has one: *"that your days may be prolonged"* **and** *"that it may go well with you."* `[T]`

**וְ chaining the last five words (Deut 5:18–21).** See Tool 3.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as Hebrew poetic parallelism** — neither passage is verse. `[T]`

But a structural parallelism *between the two texts* operates in its place, and it is worth naming because it is the object of this dig. The relation is not synonymous (the texts are not saying the same thing twice), nor antithetical. It is closest to **synthetic** in Tool 5's sense: the second member extends and develops the first, and must be read *with* it rather than instead of it. Where Tool 5 would say "use the second half to interpret the first," the synoptic form of the rule is: **Deuteronomy is the authorised second colon of Exodus, and Exodus is the reason Deuteronomy's additions land where they do.** The one place where the relation is genuinely chiastic is the coveting word, where the two objects change places:

> Exodus: **house** … **wife**, servants, ox, donkey
> Deuteronomy: **wife** ‖ **house**, field, servants, ox, donkey

That is an A-B / B-A inversion at the passage's last words, and in Deuteronomy a paragraph break falls in the middle of it. *Moderate–high confidence that the inversion is deliberate; high that it is present.* `[T]` for the datum; `[I]` for intentionality.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**Exodus 20:1** — *"And God spoke all these words, saying"* — is the narrator's only intrusion into the Decalogue, and it is doing heavy work in four words: it names the speaker as God (not the Angel, not a voice), it names the content as *words* (הַדְּבָרִים, the noun that will be counted at 34:28), and it says **all**. Everything from 20:2 to 20:17 is inside that quotation. `[T]`

**Exodus 20:18–21** carries the passage's real narratorial verdict, and it is a verdict by narration rather than by comment: *"the people stood at a distance, while Moses approached the thick cloud where God was."* The narrator does not say the request was right. He shows the geography. `[T]` `[S: dig 19:9–20:21 reaches the same reading independently]`

**Deuteronomy 5** is the more interesting case, because **the narrator's function has been transferred to a character.** The book's third-person frame ("These are the words that Moses spoke", 1:1) hands the floor to Moses at 1:6 and does not take it back within this chapter. So the interpretive asides at 5:3 (*"not with our fathers"*), 5:5 (*"while I was standing between"*) and 5:22 (*"and He added no more"*) are formally Moses' comments, not the narrator's — yet they carry narratorial weight because the book's frame has vouched for Moses.

**The strongest instance is God's own aside at 5:28–29**, which is a comment inside a quotation inside a speech: *"They have done well in all that they have spoken. Oh that they had such a heart in them…"* This is the passage's deepest theological datum and it comes at three removes from the narrator — reported by Moses, quoting God, commenting on the people. Exodus preserves nothing of it. `[T]` *High confidence.* A dig on Exodus 20 alone cannot know that God commended the people's terror; a dig on Deuteronomy 5 alone will not feel how much Exodus withheld.

### 7. Vocabulary

Six items, all verified against the WLC lemma index.

**עֵד שָׁוְא / עֵד שֶׁקֶר (Deut 5:20 // Exod 20:16) — the strongest lexical finding in the passage.** `[T]`
עֵד שָׁוְא, "a vain/empty witness," occurs **once in the Hebrew Bible**: Deuteronomy 5:20. The ordinary formula עֵד שֶׁקֶר, "a false witness," occurs **five times**: Exodus 20:16, Deuteronomy 19:18, Proverbs 6:19, 14:5, 25:18. So Deuteronomy has the standard phrase available and uses it — **in its own false-witness law at 19:18** — but declines it in the Decalogue. Why is answerable from the text: שָׁוְא occurs in Deuteronomy **three times in two verses, and both verses are inside the Ten Words** (5:11 twice, of the Name; 5:20 once, of the witness). Deuteronomy's ninth word is bound by vocabulary to its third. **In Deuteronomy, to bear empty witness against your neighbour is done with the same word as taking the Name emptily** — the two are one offence against truth, one God-ward and one neighbour-ward, and the second table is stitched to the first by a single noun. Exodus keeps the roots separate (שָׁוְא at 20:7 and 23:1; שֶׁקֶר at 5:9, 20:16, 23:7). *High confidence — lemma-verified, and the distributional argument is decisive.*

**חָמַד / הִתְאַוָּה (Exod 20:17 // Deut 5:21).** `[T]`
Exodus uses חמד twice; Deuteronomy uses חמד once (of the wife) and אוה once (of the household). The distributions settle a question that is often asked and rarely checked: **אוה does not occur anywhere in Exodus** (0 occurrences), and occurs three times in Deuteronomy (5:21; 12:20; 14:26), where its other two uses are of ordinary, licit desire — craving meat, and spending your tithe-money on *"whatever your heart desires."* So Deuteronomy's second verb is not a synonym drafted for variety; it is a Deuteronomic word whose other uses are morally neutral. The effect is to make the wife the object of the *illicit* verb and the property the object of the *appetitive* one — a distinction Exodus does not draw and the Greek does not carry (both Greek Decalogues use ἐπιθυμήσεις twice). *High confidence on the distribution; moderate on the weight the distinction should bear, since two neutral uses are a small base.*

**נוּחַ (Exod 20:11 // Deut 5:14).** See Headline Finding 4. One occurrence each; the subject changes from God to the slave. `[T]` *High confidence.*

**זָכוֹר / שָׁמוֹר (Exod 20:8 // Deut 5:12).** Both infinitive absolutes used as commands — an emphatic, timeless form that English cannot carry and that both NASB95 and the ESV render as bare imperatives. `[T]` The semantic difference is real but easily overstated: *remember* faces the day as something already instituted (which Exodus 16 has in fact already done, before Sinai), and *observe* faces it as something to be kept. Note that Deuteronomy uses זכר too, four verses later and of the exodus (5:15, וְזָכַרְתָּ): **Deuteronomy has not dropped "remember" — it has moved it from the day to the slavery.** `[T]` *High confidence on the move; the standard rabbinic harmonisation (both spoken in one utterance) is a reception datum, not a textual one.*

**אָנֹכִי and פָּנִים.** See Headline Finding 2. In Deuteronomy 5 פָּנִים clusters three times in four verses (5:4 face-to-face; 5:5 "from before the fire"; 5:7 "before my face"); in Exodus 20 it occurs at 20:3 and then not again until 20:20, outside the Decalogue. `[T]` The claim audit's note that HALOT gives six live senses for עַל־פָּנָי, with the ancient versions rendering it differently between Exod 20:3 and Deut 5:7, is confirmed from the corpus: **Rahlfs has πλὴν ἐμοῦ at Exodus 20:3 and πρὸ προσώπου μου at Deuteronomy 5:7** — "besides me" against "before my face." `[T]` for the Greek; `[S: Exodus claim audit]` for the HALOT range. Swete agrees with Rahlfs at both points.

**עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים — "the ten words", and what the collection is actually called.** `[T]`
The phrase occurs **three times in the whole Hebrew Bible — Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13; Deuteronomy 10:4 — and never inside either Decalogue.** Two of the three are in Deuteronomy, so **the book that re-preaches the Ten Words is also the book that names them**, and both texts that give them leave them unnamed and uncounted. `[T]` *High confidence — accent- and maqqef-insensitive search of the whole WLC.*

The designation matters more than it looks. **In Hebrew the collection is never called "commandments."** It is דְּבָרִים, "words" — the same noun the narrator uses at Exodus 20:1 (*"God spoke all these* words") and Moses at Deuteronomy 5:22 (*"these* words *the LORD spoke"*). The noun מִצְוָה *is* present in both Decalogues, but of God's commandments in general — "those who love me and **keep my commandments**" (Exod 20:6 // Deut 5:10) — never as the name of the ten. And Deuteronomy 4:13 keeps the two apart in a single clause: *"his covenant, which he **commanded** (צוה) you to perform, the **ten words**."* **The collection is commanded; it is called words.**

**A checking-stage correction.** A Logos query returns the claim that *"the biblical texts themselves consistently employ 'Ten Commandments' when naming this collection"*, citing Exod 34:28; Deut 4:13; 10:4. `[S: Logos]` **That is an artefact of reading an English version's rendering as though it were the biblical designation** — the same answer concedes, two paragraphs earlier, that "in the Hebrew and Greek texts, the 'ten commandments' are, literally, the 'ten words'." The three verses cited are precisely the three the corpus returns, and in every one of them the Hebrew is הַדְּבָרִים. So the answer's own evidence tells against its conclusion. *The finding stands as the corpus gives it: the Bible names this collection "the ten words," three times, all outside the Decalogues themselves.* The scholarly preference for "Decalogue" over "Ten Commandments" — on the ground that the latter creates the misimpression that the collection is entirely prohibition — is well taken and is confirmed here from the other direction: **two of the ten are not prohibitions at all** (זָכוֹר/שָׁמוֹר and כַּבֵּד), as Tool 10 records. `[S: Logos — Johnstone, *Eerdmans Commentary* 92]`

**Proper-noun inventory.** Exodus 20:1–17: מִצְרַיִם (Egypt, v.2) — the only proper noun besides the divine names. Deuteronomy 5:1–33: יִשְׂרָאֵל (5:1), חֹרֵב (Horeb, 5:2), מִצְרַיִם (5:6, 15). **One name carries canonical freight and it is Horeb.** Deuteronomy's consistent name for the mountain is Horeb, not Sinai; the choice is the book's throughout (1:2, 6, 19; 4:10, 15; 5:2; 9:8; 18:16; 28:69), so it is not a passage-level signal and receives no separate weight here. *Load-the-story rule not triggered: no name-hyperlink in either passage is load-bearing for the exegesis.* Egypt is invoked in both texts, but as event rather than place, and the event is named in the clause itself.

### 8. Translations

**No pulpit text is declared** (study only), so the pulpit-divergence check is N/A. The study text is compared with the ESV below only where the divergence bears on a finding, because every other document in the Dig Deeper folder is ESV-based and the checking stage runs against them.

**Where NASB95 obscures a finding.**

1. **The waw-chain (Deut 5:18–21).** NASB95 renders Deuteronomy's five chained prohibitions exactly as it renders Exodus's five unchained ones — *"You shall not…"* five times in both. The ESV does the same. **The single most systematic structural difference between the two Hebrew texts is invisible in both English versions.** `[T]` A congregation reading either will not see that Deuteronomy binds the second table into one sentence.

2. **עֵד שָׁוְא (Deut 5:20).** NASB95: *"false witness"*, identical to its rendering of Exodus 20:16. ESV likewise. The lexical bridge to 5:11 — the strongest finding in Tool 7 — is levelled to nothing. `[T]` *This is the place where an English-only dig would have missed the most.*

3. **תִתְאַוֶּה (Deut 5:21).** NASB95 does preserve the distinction — *"you shall not covet… and you shall not desire"* — and deserves credit for it; the ESV also distinguishes ("covet"/"desire"). The NIV levels both to "covet". Since no pulpit text is declared this is noted, not scored.

4. **The infinitive absolutes (Exod 20:8 // Deut 5:12).** Both flattened to imperatives in NASB95 and ESV; nothing in English can carry them.

5. **פָּנִים בְּפָנִים (Deut 5:4).** NASB95 and ESV both render *"face to face"*, which is correct and also exactly what they render for the five occurrences of the *other*, ordinary idiom. The hapax is invisible. `[T]`

**Ancient versions check — trigger met** (NASB95, ESV and KJV substantively agree throughout, and the renderings carry weight). The check returns a great deal, and it must be triaged before it is reported.

**Category 1 — translation loss.** The waw-chain, the two infinitive absolutes, the שָׁוְא/שֶׁקֶר bridge, the חמד/אוה pair, and the שׁוֹר–חֲמוֹר repetition are all features of the Hebrew that no translation into any language carries; the Greek breaks them exactly as English does. Both Greek Decalogues use ἐπιθυμήσεις for both coveting verbs and μαρτυρίαν ψευδῆ for both witness-formulas. **These are facts about the Hebrew, not judgements about the Greek.** Every count in this report is a count *in the Hebrew*, in the WLC.

**Category 2 — substantive variants.** Reported in the Textual Variants extension below; there are four.

**Category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text.** Reported under Tool 11.

**Translation-tradition split (category 2, and the only one that qualifies here):** **the ancient and modern traditions divide over the ox and the donkey in the Sabbath word.** The Hebrew of Exodus 20:10 lists "your male or female servant or your cattle"; Deuteronomy 5:14 adds "your ox or your donkey or any of your cattle." **Rahlfs and Swete both give Greek Exodus 20:10 the full Deuteronomic list** — ὁ βοῦς σου καὶ τὸ ὑποζύγιόν σου καὶ πᾶν κτῆνός σου. Every modern English version follows the Hebrew and prints the shorter list at Exodus. The reading is not trivial: the ox and the donkey are the same pair Exodus reserves for the coveting word (20:17), so the Greek's plus removes a distinction the Hebrew maintains. *Position: the shorter Hebrew at Exodus 20:10 is likelier original, because it is the harder reading and because assimilation to the parallel is the commonest scribal movement in synoptic material — but this is a judgement on internal evidence alone, since no Qumran or Samaritan evidence has been consulted in this run. **Moderate confidence**, and routed to Open Questions.*

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Exodus 20:1–17 — the register is sheer, unbroken declaration.** Sixteen verses of second-person-singular address with no vocative, no appeal, no reason offered beyond three grounding clauses, and not one word of warmth. The tone is set by the frame rather than the words: this is being said out of fire, to a people who have been fenced off from the mountain on pain of death, and it stops because they beg for it to stop. **The absence of tenderness is the tone.** `[T]`

**Deuteronomy 5:1–33 — the register is a preacher's.** It opens with a vocative (*"Hear, O Israel"*), argues (*"not with our fathers, but with us"*), explains itself (*"for you were afraid"*), and adds motive clauses the original lacked (*"that it may go well with you"*; *"as well as you"*). Then, in the closing frame, it does something Exodus never does: **it lets God sound wistful.** מִי־יִתֵּן — literally "who will give" — introducing 5:29, is the Hebrew of longing, and what God longs for is a heart in the people that they do not have. `[T]`

**The tonal contrast is the theological contrast.** In Exodus the words are terrifying because God is speaking. In Deuteronomy the same words are pastoral because Moses is speaking — and the one moment God's own voice returns unmediated, at 5:28–29, it is not terrifying at all but grieved. **Soundtrack:** Exodus, a single sustained brass chord that does not resolve; Deuteronomy, the same material scored for strings, with a solo line at 5:29 that trails off. *A sermon on either text that borrows the other's emotional register will misrepresent it.*

### 10. Repetition

**Within Exodus 20:1–17.**
- **Eight of the ten words open with לֹא**; only two open with a positive imperative (זָכוֹר, v.8; כַּבֵּד, v.12). The proportion is identical in Deuteronomy. `[T]`
- יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ "the LORD your God" ×5 (vv. 2, 5, 7, 10, 12).
- רֵעֶךָ "your neighbour" ×4, all in vv. 16–17 (once at 16, three times at 17) — the second table's closing word cluster; Deuteronomy has the same four, at 5:20 and 5:21. `[T]`
- שֵׁשֶׁת יָמִים "six days" ×2 (vv. 9, 11), binding the command to its ground.

**Within Deuteronomy 5:1–33.**
- הַיּוֹם "today" ×3 — twice in the opening (5:1, 3) and once in the people's report (5:24) — the book's janus-word, planted here.
- צִוָּה ×5 (see Headline Finding 3).
- שָׁמַע ×7 verses (5:1, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28) — **all in the frames, none in the Ten Words.** `[T]`
- דִּבֶּר ×8 verses (5:1, 4, 22, 24, 26, 27, 28, 31) against three in Exodus 20 (1, 19, 22). **Deuteronomy's chapter is saturated with speaking-and-hearing vocabulary that its Decalogue proper does not contain.** The words are quoted; the talk about the words surrounds them. `[T]` *High confidence — lemma-verified.*
- יטב ×3 (5:16, 28, 29): the fifth word's added promise (*"that it may go well with you"*), God's verdict on the people (*"they have done **well**"*), and God's longing (*"that it may be **well** with them… forever"*). **Zero occurrences in Exodus 20.** The root that Deuteronomy adds to the fifth commandment is the root God uses to bless the people's fear and to voice his own wish for them. `[T]` *High confidence — this is a genuine three-link chain inside the chapter, and it is Deuteronomic through and through.*
- אָב ×3, in three different senses (5:3 the fathers with whom the covenant was *not* made; 5:9 the fathers whose iniquity is visited; 5:16 the father to be honoured). Exodus 20 has two (5, 12). `[T]`
- יָסַף ×2 (5:22, 25): *"He added no more"* / *"if we hear the voice of the LORD our God **any longer** [lit. if we add to hear]"*. God stopped adding; the people cannot bear that he should. Same root, four verses apart. `[T]` *High confidence — see Tool 11 Move 4.*

**Across the two texts.** שׁוֹר and חֲמוֹר: **once each in Exodus 20 (v.17 only); twice each in Deuteronomy 5 (vv. 14 and 21).** `[T]` Deuteronomy imports the ox-and-donkey pair from the tenth word into the fourth, so that the animals a man must not covet from his neighbour are the animals he must give rest to in his own yard. *Moderate–high confidence that the import is deliberate: the pair is verbatim, and Deuteronomy's Sabbath word is the only place either Decalogue names specific animals in a command about rest.*

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources.** For Exodus, the Torah master and the Exodus overview agree: Genesis and the Abrahamic oath, with Move 4 carrying most of the weight. For Deuteronomy, the overview lists Genesis–Numbers as confirmed live and marks "the Decalogue (Exod 20) re-given at 5" as live/high. **This passage is the one place in Deuteronomy where the live source is quoted at length rather than retold.**

**Independent candidate-finding.** Before testing the overview's named candidate (Exod 20), candidates were derived from the passages' own distinctive vocabulary: פָּנִים בְּפָנִים (→ Gen 32:31; Exod 33:11; Deut 34:10 — the only other occurrences of the idiom in any form); עֵד שָׁוְא / עֵד שֶׁקֶר (→ Deut 19:18; Prov 6:19; 14:5; 25:18); חמד (→ Exod 34:24; Deut 7:25); נוּחַ + the seventh day (→ Gen 2:2–3); בְּיָד חֲזָקָה וּבִזְרֹעַ נְטוּיָה (→ the Deuteronomic exodus formula). Each was checked against the corpus before the overviews were consulted.

**Exodus 20:1–17 → Deuteronomy 5:6–21** *(high confidence — sustained direct quotation, marked as such by the quoting text)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Exodus 20 sits inside the Sinai theophany, spoken by God's own voice from fire to the assembled people, immediately after a covenant proposal they have already sworn to accept (19:8) and immediately before they beg for the speaking to stop (20:19). Its function within its own passage is to be **the terms of a covenant already agreed and the event that proves a mediator necessary.** Four things travel with it into Deuteronomy: the direct divine voice; the fire; the whole people as addressee; and the terror. Deuteronomy retains all four and says so (5:4, 22–23).

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Deuteronomy has been using Exodus since 1:6, but by **retelling**: the departure from Horeb (1:6–8 ← Exod 33–34; Num 10), the appointment of judges (1:9–18 ← Exod 18), the spies (1:19–46 ← Num 13–14), and — most relevantly — chapter 4's argument from *"you saw no form"* (4:12, 15), which presupposes Exodus 20:4 and 20:18–21 without quoting either. **Deuteronomy 5 is the switch from retelling to quotation, and the switch is the point.** The book paraphrases what Moses saw and quotes what God said. Deuteronomy will return to this same source twice more, and both times to *name the collection*: 4:13 and 10:4, both עֲשֶׂרֶת הַדְּבָרִים. `[T]` *High confidence.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Exodus 20 is itself already in canonical conversation with Genesis 2:1–3, and citing it activates that earlier link: **Exodus 20:11's ground for the Sabbath is a quotation of the creation account's own logic** (six days of making, rest on the seventh, blessing and hallowing of the day — Gen 2:2–3 supplies all four elements, including both verbs בָּרַךְ and קָדַשׁ). This is the finding with the sharpest consequence for the synoptic question: **when Deuteronomy replaces the Sabbath's ground, it is not editing Exodus but declining to reactivate Genesis.** The creation-link is not weakened, contradicted, or denied; it is simply not the ground that a generation about to become slave-owners in a land of their own most needs to hear. `[T]` for the Genesis elements; `[I]` for the "declines to reactivate" formulation. *High confidence on the datum; moderate on the formulation.*

*What it adds.* The triad turns the central methodological question into an exegetical one. Deuteronomy is not offering a variant text of the Decalogue; it is performing an authorised re-issue that (a) names its source in the text (כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוְּךָ), (b) preserves the source's four defining circumstances, (c) reproduces four of sixteen verses consonant for consonant and the prologue exactly, and (d) changes only the motive clauses, the lists, and two words. **Every change is at a point where the source left the application open.** The bare prohibitions — no other gods, no image, the Name, murder, adultery, theft — are transmitted with no substantive change at all. That distribution is the finding: **Deuteronomy adds nothing to the commands and everything to the reasons.**

**Genesis 2:1–3 → Exodus 20:11** *(high confidence — sustained conceptual and verbal allusion)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Genesis 2:1–3 closes the creation account: the work is finished, God rests on the seventh day, and he blesses and sanctifies it. Its function there is to make the seventh day the goal of the account rather than an appendix to it. Exodus 20:11 reproduces its logic in one sentence and adds "and the sea and all that is in them" — a merismus for the whole created order.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Genesis is Exodus's confirmed live source, and the Sabbath has already appeared in the book **before Sinai**, at Exodus 16:23 — which the Exodus claim audit records as the first שַׁבָּת in Exodus and apparently in the Torah, strengthening the law-before-Sinai argument. `[S: Exodus claim audit]` So the fourth word is not instituting the Sabbath but codifying an institution the narrative has already shown in operation. Exodus will return to it at 31:12–17, again with the creation ground.

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Genesis 2:1–3 and Exodus 31:12–17 are already in conversation with each other through the tabernacle's seven-speech structure, which the Exodus overview treats as established. Deuteronomy stands outside this conversation entirely: **it never grounds the Sabbath in creation, anywhere.** `[T]`

*What it adds.* It locates the difference precisely. Exodus's Sabbath is one node in a creation-and-tabernacle network that runs through the whole book; Deuteronomy's is one node in an exodus-and-mercy network that runs through the whole of *its* book (cf. 15:15; 16:12; 24:18, 22 — "you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt" as the recurring ground of Deuteronomic social law). **Neither ground is a correction of the other. Each is native to its book.** *High confidence.*

### 12. Genre

**Both passages are covenant stipulation — apodictic law — inside a host genre, and the host genres differ.** Exodus 20's host is **historical narrative**: the Decalogue is a speech reported by a narrator inside a scene. Deuteronomy 5's host is **sermon embedded in narrative**, which the genre table names as its own category (Acts speeches, Deuteronomy, Joshua 24) — and the reading rule for that category is that the sermon must be analysed *within* its narrative setting.

The consequence is not decorative. **Apodictic law in a narrative frame is a datum about what happened; apodictic law in a sermon is a datum about what is being urged.** Both are God's words; they are doing different work in their hosts. This is the genre-level form of the whole dig: Exodus reports the Decalogue's *giving*, Deuteronomy performs its *preaching*, and a reader who flattens the genre difference will read Deuteronomy's additions as textual variants rather than as homiletic moves.

One further note. The suzerain-vassal treaty form that the Deuteronomy overview names as the book's shape is a **comparative-literary reconstruction**, not a text-observation, and nothing in this dig rests on it. `[S]`

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passages sit.** Exodus 20 at Sinai, in the third month after the exodus; Deuteronomy 5 on the plains of Moab, forty years later, in the last month of Moses' life. **Both are pre-conquest, pre-monarchy, pre-temple, pre-exile — and both are inside the same book.** Between them lie the golden calf, the tabernacle, Leviticus, the spies, the wilderness generation's death, and the arrival at the edge of the land. Deuteronomy's re-preaching is spoken to people who have watched their parents die under this covenant.

**Canonical position (Tanak).** See Phase 0.55.

**Where the reader sits, and what has happened since.** This side of the cross, and four things have happened that materially change the reading. (1) **The mediator the people asked for has come**, and the request Deuteronomy records God commending is what the office of prophet was built on (18:15–19 → Acts 3:22). (2) **The Sabbath's two grounds have both been taken up and both relativised** — Hebrews 4 argues from the creation rest that a rest remains, and Colossians 2:16–17 calls the day a shadow whose substance is Christ; the New Testament handles the fourth word differently from the other nine, and the preacher should know that before the sermon rather than during it. (3) **The heart Deuteronomy 5:29 wished for has been promised and given** (30:6 → Jer 31:33; 2 Cor 3:3). (4) **The tenth word has been identified as the one that exposes the others** — Romans 7:7, *"I would not have known about coveting if the Law had not said, 'You shall not covet'"* — Paul citing the word that both texts place last and that both leave without an object in the Greek.

**Timeline-driven misreading to watch.** The Decalogue is the passage most often applied with the timeline switched off, in both directions: as a code binding Christians unchanged, or as an abolished law with nothing to say. Neither text supports either. **Deuteronomy is itself the Bible's first demonstration that the Ten Words are re-applied when the situation changes** — which is a timeline argument made inside the Torah.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Exodus 20.** The addressee is second-person **singular** throughout the Ten Words in both books (לְךָ, אֱלֹהֶיךָ, בִּנְךָ) even though the audience is the whole nation — a grammatical feature English preserves only accidentally. `[T]` So the identification is direct and personal by design: each Israelite is addressed alone, in a crowd.

**Are we the addressee?** Directly, no: the prologue's ground is *"who brought you out of the land of Egypt"*, and no Christian was. But the identification is not therefore blocked, because **Deuteronomy 5:3 is itself an argument about exactly this problem** — a generation that was not at Horeb is told the covenant was made *with us*. The text models the transfer it is asking for. What Deuteronomy does across forty years, the New Testament does across the cross: *"you were bought with a price"* is the Christian prologue, and the imperative rides on it in the same grammar.

**Moses is not us** (Exodus overview, trap 2). **The people are** — including in their fear, and including in God's verdict that their fear was right and their hearts were not equal to it (5:28–29). That last is the sharpest identification the passage offers: the group the reader is invited to stand with is the group God commends and then grieves over in the same breath.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** In Exodus: that a redeemed people should hear the terms of their covenant in the voice of the God who redeemed them, and know they cannot bear it. In Deuteronomy: that a new generation should own that covenant as theirs *today*, and keep it in the specific shape their new life requires — with rest for the slave, honour that goes well with them, and truth that is not empty.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

*Worldview.* Two claims, one from each text. From the identical prologue: **obligation follows rescue and never produces it** — grace-before-law is not a doctrine imposed on the Decalogue but the Decalogue's own opening sentence in both books. From the differences: **God's unchanging word is re-preached, not repeated.** The same ten words, given twice by the same author, differ — and the differences track the hearers. A congregation that has been taught the Bible is a fixed text and nothing else will find this unsettling; a congregation that has been taught it is infinitely adaptable will find it disciplining. It is neither. The bare prohibitions do not move. The reasons do.

*Behaviour.* Deuteronomy's own applications are the model, and they are pointed: give rest to the people who work for you, **and give it on the ground that you were once worked**; keep your neighbour's marriage and your neighbour's property in separate categories; do not empty out your speech about your neighbour any more than you would empty out the Name.

*Motive.* Both texts supply it and Deuteronomy doubles it: *that your days may be prolonged* **and** *that it may go well with you* (5:16), and behind both, the sentence God says to himself — *"Oh that they had such a heart"* (5:29). **The motive Deuteronomy finally offers is not a threat or a reward but God's own longing**, and a sermon that reaches the Decalogue's motives without reaching 5:29 has stopped one verse short of the best one.

**Four audiences.**
- *For me:* which of the ten do I keep in Exodus's register — as a rule given from fire — and which have I never let Deuteronomy re-preach to me in the specifics of my own household, wages, speech, and wanting?
- *For a Christian friend:* the one who reads the Ten Commandments as the thing they must do to be accepted needs the nine words that precede all ten, in both books.
- *For the church community:* the fourth word is where the two texts differ most and where Christians differ most; a congregation is better served by being shown that the Bible itself grounds the Sabbath two ways than by being given one ground as if it were the only one.
- *For an unbeliever:* the passage's own claim is that these words come from the God who acted first. Take away the prologue and it is ethics; leave it in and it is a covenant, and covenants have a party on the other side.

**Prayer.** *You spoke, and we could not bear it. You wrote what you spoke and gave it to a mediator, because we asked. You commended the asking and grieved that our hearts were not equal to what we had asked for. Give us the heart you wished for us, which you have promised and have given in your Son.*

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **The double accentuation.** BHS prints the Decalogue in **both** Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 under two competing accent systems — the *taʿam ʿelyon* ("upper"), which divides by commandment, and the *taʿam taḥton* ("lower"), which divides by ordinary verse length. This is visible in the Logos BHS export, where words carry two accents (e.g. Exod 20:2 אָֽנֹכִ֖י֙, 20:13 לֹ֥֖א תִּֿרְצָֽ֖ח) and the ת of תִּרְצָח, תִּנְאָף and תִּגְנֹב carries a rafe in both books. **The Masoretes did not settle how many verses the Ten Words occupy, and preserved both answers in the pointing of both texts.** `[T]` *High confidence that the double accentuation is present in the export; the analysis of what each system yields should be checked in BHS proper or in a Masorah study — see Open Questions.*
- **Infinitive absolute as command** (זָכוֹר, Exod 20:8; שָׁמוֹר, Deut 5:12): an emphatic, tenseless imperatival use. Both books use the same construction; only the verb differs.
- **Second-person singular throughout the Ten Words in both books**, against second-person plural in Deuteronomy's frame (5:1 אָזְנֵיכֶם, 5:4 עִמָּכֶם). The number shifts exactly at the quotation marks. `[T]` *High confidence — this is a further, independent confirmation of Tool 3's quotation-frame analysis.*
- **The maqqef moves at Exod 20:3 // Deut 5:7.** Exodus binds יִהְיֶה־לְךָ; Deuteronomy binds לֹא־יִהְיֶה. Consonantally identical, accentually different. A small datum, but it belongs to the same family as the paragraph divisions: **the two texts are pointed independently.** `[T]`
- **Ketiv/qere at Deut 5:10.** The WLC prints מצותו unpointed; the Logos BHS export prints מִצְוֹתָֽו with an apparatus siglum. Exodus 20:6 has מִצְוֺתָי. So Exodus reads "**my** commandments" and Deuteronomy's consonantal text reads "**his**" — inside a clause where God is speaking in the first person. See Textual Variants.

No paronomasia is claimed in either passage. The repetition-triggered check was run on the words recurring three or more times (לֹא, יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, רֵעֶךָ, צִוָּה, אָב) and **no root echo across semantic domains was found that meets the hard rule's corroboration test.** Recorded as a nil-return rather than omitted. The one sound-adjacent finding that does hold is not paronomasia but recurrence: שָׁוְא at Deut 5:11 and 5:20 (Tool 7).

### Textual Variants

Category 2 of the three-way triage only — substantive variants. Four are worth the preacher's knowing; a fifth cluster is flagged as unchecked.

#### Deuteronomy 5:10 — "my commandments" or "his"?

- **The issue:** Exodus 20:6 reads מִצְוֺתָי, "my commandments." Deuteronomy 5:10's consonantal text reads מצותו, "his commandments," inside first-person divine speech. The WLC leaves it unpointed (the classic ketiv signal); the Logos BHS export points it מִצְוֹתָֽו and attaches an apparatus siglum.
- **Evidence:** Not assessed in this run — the apparatus entry itself was not read, only its siglum. **Rahlfs and Swete both read τὰ προστάγματά μου ("my commandments") at Deut 5:10**, agreeing with Exodus. `[T]`
- **Position:** The qere/versional reading "my" is almost certainly what is to be read; the ketiv "his" is likelier a genuine consonantal variant than a scribal slip, given that BHS flags it. *Moderate confidence; the apparatus must be read before this is stated in an overview.*
- **Preaching implication:** None material — both English versions read "my", and the sense is unaffected. Worth knowing only if a hearer compares a Hebrew interlinear.

#### Exodus 20:10 — the Greek's fuller Sabbath list

Treated at Tool 8 as the passage's one **Translation-tradition split**. *Moderate confidence that the shorter Hebrew is original.*

#### Exodus 20:12 — the Greek's two pluses

- **The issue:** Rahlfs and Swete both give Greek Exodus 20:12 **ἵνα εὖ σοι γένηται** ("that it may be well with you") and **ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς τῆς ἀγαθῆς** ("the good land"). Hebrew Exodus has neither; Hebrew Deuteronomy 5:16 has the first (וּלְמַעַן יִיטַב לָךְ) but not the second. `[T]`
- **Evidence:** Both Greek editions agree; no Hebrew witness consulted in this run.
- **Position:** Assimilation of Greek Exodus to Deuteronomy for the first plus is the natural explanation, and the second is a further expansion attested at neither. **The direction is uniform:** every Greek plus in Exodus 20 moves it toward Deuteronomy 5, and none moves the other way. That uniformity is itself evidence. *Moderate–high confidence on the direction; moderate on any single reading.*
- **Preaching implication:** Real, if indirect. **Ephesians 6:2–3 quotes the fifth commandment with "that it may be well with you" attached** and calls it "the first commandment with a promise" — quoting a form of the fifth word that Hebrew Exodus does not have. Paul is quoting Deuteronomy, or a Greek Exodus already conformed to it.

#### Exodus 20:17 — "his field" in the Greek

- **The issue:** Both Greek editions give Exodus 20:17 the wife-first order **and** τὸν ἀγρὸν αὐτοῦ, "his field" — both of which are Deuteronomic in Hebrew and absent from Hebrew Exodus. `[T]`
- **Position:** Assimilation, in the same direction as the two above. *Moderate–high.*
- **Preaching implication:** The wife-first order in Greek Exodus removes the Hebrew distinction on which the ninth/tenth division depends, so **in Greek there is no basis for the Augustinian numbering from Exodus rather than Deuteronomy** — the two Greek texts read alike at exactly the point where the two Hebrew texts differ most.

#### Unchecked and flagged

The **Samaritan Pentateuch** (which harmonises the two Decalogues extensively and adds a tenth commandment concerning Gerizim), the **Nash Papyrus**, and **4QDeut^n** are all directly relevant — each is a witness to how ancient readers handled precisely this comparison — and **none has been consulted in this run**, since none is in the corpus. Every statement in this report about the Hebrew is a statement about the Masoretic tradition as the WLC and the Logos BHS export present it. Routed to Open Questions.

**A note on the apparatus density, offered as an observation and not a finding.** Counting the apparatus sigla in the Logos BHS export (the sigla only — **the apparatus entries themselves were not read**), Exodus 20:1–17 carries 33 and Deuteronomy 5:1–22 carries 39. Their distribution is not even: **the four verses carrying eight sigla each are Exod 20:10, Exod 20:17, Deut 5:21 — and Deut 5:14 carries four.** These are exactly the Sabbath household-list and the coveting word in both books: the two places where the Hebrew texts diverge most. **BHS's apparatus is densest precisely where this dig found its material**, which is to say the ancient witnesses were already working on the same problem. *Moderate confidence — a siglum count is a proxy for apparatus weight, not a measure of it, and the entries should be read before this is repeated.*

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Exodus 20: Sinai/Horeb, third month of the first year, a newly redeemed slave population encamped at a mountain. Deuteronomy 5: the plains of Moab east of the Jordan, forty years later, a generation born in the wilderness about to cross into settled agricultural land.

**What the original audience knew, that we do not.**

1. **The difference between owning nothing and owning a field.** The single addition to Deuteronomy's coveting word is שָׂדֵהוּ, "his field." A slave population at Sinai had no fields; a people about to receive allotted land would have neighbours whose boundaries they could see. **The addition is not an expansion of principle but a naming of the specific property that was about to exist.** `[I]` *High confidence that the field is new; moderate on the settlement explanation, which is inference from the setting rather than statement.*
2. **The ox and the donkey as capital.** In a subsistence agrarian economy the plough-ox and the pack-donkey are a smallholder's most valuable movable assets and his labour force. Deuteronomy's Sabbath word legislates rest for them; its coveting word forbids wanting a neighbour's. Naming these two animals in both places is naming the household's economic engine twice.
3. **Slave-owning as the audience's near future.** Deuteronomy's Sabbath ground — *you were a slave* — is addressed to people who will shortly own slaves, and the book legislates for that (15:12–18, with the same Egypt-memory as its ground). The fourth word in Deuteronomy is the first appearance of what becomes the book's characteristic social-ethical move.
4. **Covenant documents were read aloud, kept in a sanctuary, and periodically re-read to a new generation.** That the Decalogue is deposited on tablets (Deut 5:22) and re-preached at a covenant renewal is not literary artifice; it is what covenant documents were for. *Moderate confidence — this is comparative ANE background rather than text-observation, and nothing in the report rests on it.* `[S]`

**The cult-context frame.** For any Torah passage the original readers held the creation account and the tabernacle simultaneously, and this passage is a clear instance: **the Sabbath's creation ground (Exod 20:11) is native reading-frame material for readers whose tabernacle was built on a seven-speech, "finished-and-blessed" pattern**, not a later theological overlay. The Exodus overview treats that pattern as established. So Exodus's fourth word is legible to its first readers as part of a network they lived inside. Deuteronomy's readers, addressed on the edge of the land, are being handed the ground they will need when the tent is replaced by farms.

**What this changes about how we read.** It removes the main reason people find the two Sabbath grounds troubling. Read as competing explanations of why the Sabbath exists, they conflict. Read as two grounds addressed to two situations by one author — a camp around a tabernacle, and a nation about to farm and to employ — they do not.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Torah — readers of the founding story, who know creation, fall, flood, patriarchs, exodus and covenant, and who hold the tabernacle in view. For this passage the canonical reader has a specific and unusual advantage: **the reader of the Torah meets both Decalogues, in order, inside one work.** Whatever the compositional history, the canonical reader is *supposed* to notice the differences, because the same book presents both. Nothing in the report requires a view about how the Torah reached its final form.

**The first hearers.**
- *Exodus 20:* a recently enslaved people who have seen ten plagues and a sea, have sworn obedience to terms they have not yet heard, and are standing at a fenced mountain that is on fire.
- *Deuteronomy 5:* their children, who have buried them; who know Egypt only by report and the wilderness by experience; who are being told the covenant is theirs.

**Where the passage fits their world.** Deuteronomy 5:3's *"not with our fathers"* is a startling sentence to a people whose fathers demonstrably *were* at Horeb — and Deuteronomy 4:9–10 has just told this generation *"your eyes have seen"* and reminded them of the day they *"stood before the LORD your God at Horeb."* The book collapses the generations deliberately and repeatedly. `[T]`

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** at Exodus, that God speaks the terms himself, to everyone, and that it is unbearable. At Deuteronomy, 5:3 — a claim about their own presence at an event most of them missed.
- **Surprising:** God's verdict at 5:28. The people's request to be spared God's voice is the sort of thing a reader expects to be rebuked, and it is commended.
- **Comforting:** *"as well as you"* (5:14) to anyone in the audience who works for another; and, for the whole assembly, that the covenant is theirs *today*.
- **Disturbing:** 5:29. God's wish is a diagnosis — the people do not have the heart the covenant requires, and God says so while approving of them.

**What we bring that they didn't.** Three things distort this passage in particular. **Individualism**: we hear "you shall not" as addressed to me alone, when the singular grammar is addressed to each within a summoned assembly. **The idea of a law-code**: we bring the notion of a free-standing list of rules, which makes the prologue look like a preamble instead of the ground. **A settled canon of one text per passage**: we expect duplicates to be errors, so the differences read as a problem to solve rather than as an author's second sermon.

**Candidate Fallen Condition Focus.**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **What they felt** | That God's own voice was more than they could survive, and that they wanted his words without his presence (Exod 20:19; Deut 5:25–27) — a fear God calls right; and, beneath it, that they did not have the heart to keep what they had agreed to (Deut 5:29, God's own diagnosis). `[T]` |
| **Candidate FCF (shared concern)** | We want God's word without God's nearness — and we agree to more than our hearts can carry. |
| **Shared / differs** | Shared entirely as to the condition. Differs as to the remedy: they were given a mediator who would die outside the land, and a wish. We are given the Mediator and the heart, which is what 5:29 was wishing for. |
| **Confidence** | Anchored — both halves are stated in the text (5:27–29), not inferred. |

**Implication for the sermon.** The FCF here is unusually well-attested because **the text supplies the diagnosis in God's own voice rather than leaving it to the preacher.** A sermon on either Decalogue that names the condition from 5:29 is quoting God, not psychologising the congregation.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Three themes, and they converge.

#### Covenant

**Where these passages sit.** The Mosaic covenant's constitutional core, given at Sinai (Exod 19–24) and renewed at Moab (Deut 5; 29). **What precedes:** the Abrahamic oath, which both texts presuppose and neither cites here. **What follows:** the covenant's failure, the prophets' indictment on precisely these terms (Jer 7:9 and Hos 4:2 both list Decalogue offences), and the new covenant in which the words are written on the heart (Jer 31:33) — which is the answer to Deuteronomy 5:29 in the vocabulary Deuteronomy itself uses. **What it contributes:** the Decalogue is covenant *stipulation*, not moral philosophy, and the identical prologue in both books is what makes it so. *Confidence: high.*

#### Sabbath / rest

**Where these passages sit.** The two canonical grounds of the Sabbath, one in each text. **What precedes:** Genesis 2:1–3 (the divine rest) and Exodus 16 (the Sabbath in operation before Sinai). **What follows:** Exodus 31:12–17 (creation ground again, as covenant sign); Deuteronomy 15 (the Egypt-memory as the ground of release); Isaiah 58:13–14; and then the New Testament's two-handed treatment — Hebrews 4 arguing from the *creation* rest that a rest remains, Colossians 2:16–17 calling the day a shadow. **What it contributes:** the two grounds are not rivals, and the New Testament picks up both. *Confidence: high on the OT trajectory; moderate on how tightly Heb 4 depends on Exod 20:11 specifically rather than on Gen 2:2.*

#### Image of God / the imageless God

**Where these passages sit.** The second word, and Deuteronomy 4's argument for it. **What precedes:** Genesis 1:26–27 — humanity is God's image, which is the reason no other image may be made. **What follows:** the calf (Exod 32), Deuteronomy 4:15–19, the prophets' idol-satire, and Colossians 1:15, where **the image the second word forbids making turns out to be a person God has given.** *Confidence: high on the OT; moderate–high on the Colossians link, which is trajectory rather than citation.*

**Confluence — and its type.** These three do not merely run in parallel. The relation is **climactic convergence** (Tool-extension category c): the covenant that these words constitute is broken by an image (Exod 32) within twelve chapters of being given; the rest it commands is grounded once in the God who made and once in the God who freed; and all three converge on a single unmet condition that Deuteronomy 5:29 names — a heart. **The covenant needs the heart, the image-prohibition exposes that the heart makes images anyway, and the rest is what the heart cannot enter.** Hebrews 3–4 makes exactly this convergence, arguing from a hardened heart to a rest not entered, and it does so quoting Psalm 95 — which is itself a meditation on the wilderness generation between these two chapters.

### Schnittjer Pass

**Skeleton.** Two books, two divisions. **Exodus** — the mountain section of the from-Egypt / through-wilderness / at-the-mountain arc; the leading idea is the progressive proximity of God, and the Decalogue is the moment proximity becomes speech. **Deuteronomy** — the second of the four heading-marked discourses (opened at 4:44, "This is the torah"); the leading idea is the "today" janus, and the Decalogue is where "today" is applied to a covenant made forty years ago.

**Findings.**

1. **Narrative braking (step 1).** Both books brake hard here, and differently. Exodus accelerates through the theophany in ten verses and then stops dead for sixteen verses of quoted speech. Deuteronomy brakes *before* the quotation — five verses of preamble arguing about who the covenant belongs to — and again *after* it, eleven verses on what the hearing produced. **Deuteronomy gives more space to the reception of the words than to the words.** `[T]` *High confidence — the proportions are countable: 16 verses quoted, 17 of frame.*
2. **Janus (step 3).** Deuteronomy 5 is the book's structural janus and the Decalogue is its hinge: it faces back to Horeb (5:2–5, 22–27) and forward to the exposition (5:31 → chs. 6–26). **הַיּוֹם at 5:1 and 5:3 is the janus-word the whole book runs on, and this is where it is planted.** `[T]` *High confidence; the overview and sweep both have the "today" janus at book level, this dig locates its first load-bearing use.*
3. **Framing/inclusio (step 3).** Deuteronomy's quotation frame (לֵאמֹר at 5:5 → *"these words the LORD spoke"* at 5:22) is a clean inclusio around the Ten Words, and the second-person number shifts from plural to singular and back at exactly its edges (Original Languages). `[T]` *High confidence.*
4. **Extended echo effect / type-scene (step 3).** The Sinai-theophany-and-terror sequence is told twice in the Torah — Exod 19:16–20:21 and Deut 5:22–27 — with the same elements in the same order: fire, voice, terror, request, mediator. The second telling adds one element the first lacks: **God's spoken approval.** A type-scene whose second instance completes the first rather than repeating it. *Moderate–high confidence.*
5. **First vs second reading (step 5).** On a first reading, Exodus 20:19 is the people flinching. On a second — after Deuteronomy 5:28 and 18:16–17 — it is the origin of the prophetic office. **This is the clearest case of narrative surprise in either passage, and it is only available to a reader who has both texts.** `[T]` *High confidence.*
6. **Polyacoustic intertextuality (step 6).** Resist flattening. The Decalogue's forward echoes build rather than resolve into a single type: Jer 7:9 and Hos 4:2 (indictment by list), Ps 50 (the covenant lawsuit), Ezek 18 (the generational clause revisited), Matt 5:21–48 (intensification), Matt 19 / Mark 10 / Luke 18 (the rich man), Rom 7:7 and 13:9 (the tenth word as diagnostic and the whole as summed by love), Jas 2:11 (the indivisibility of the ten). **Six or seven notes, not one.** *Moderate–high.*

**Nil returns.** No chiasm in either passage. No wordplay meeting the corroboration test (Original Languages). No symbolic-number finding: "ten" is stated only outside both passages (Exod 34:28; Deut 4:13; 10:4), and neither Decalogue counts itself.

**Irreducibility.** The strongest guard this pass supplies is against the standard treatment of the Decalogue, which is precisely reduction — ten propositions extracted from two stories. **Both texts refuse it structurally:** neither can be read without its frame, and the frames are what differ. Keep the told story as the theological statement: God spoke and it was unbearable; Moses stood between; God approved and grieved.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** trajectory and contrast, with one typological figure and no direct prophecy.

**The mediator (typology).** The mediator whose office is instituted at Exodus 20:19–21 and endorsed at Deuteronomy 5:28–31, and whose institution Deuteronomy itself traces to this moment (18:16–17, quoting the same request). *Tests:* **category** — covenant mediation, an established theological category (passes); **NT precedent** — Acts 3:22 and 7:37 cite Deut 18:15 of Christ, Heb 12:18–24 sets Sinai against Zion, 1 Tim 2:5 names one mediator (passes, explicitly); **escalation** — the mediator who stood between and could not enter the land against the one who entered heaven itself (passes); **authorial pattern** — Deuteronomy raises the expectation and 34:10 leaves it unfulfilled (passes). **All four tests pass: high-confidence typology.**

**The words themselves (trajectory).** The Ten Words are intensified rather than abolished at Matthew 5:21–48 — where the two words Jesus takes first are murder and adultery, the first two of the second table in *Greek Deuteronomy's* order — summed at Romans 13:9 and Matthew 22:37–40 by the two commands that stand behind the two tables (Deut 6:5, from the chapter immediately after this one, and Lev 19:18), and written on the heart at Jeremiah 31:33. *Confidence: high.*

**The unmet condition (contrast).** Deuteronomy 5:29's *"Oh that they had such a heart"* is the sharpest Christological datum in either passage, and it is a **contrast, not a type**: the passage's own diagnosis of a lack. Its answer is Deuteronomy's own (30:6), and the New Testament's (2 Cor 3:3; Heb 8:10). **The Torah's ache, which the master overview names, is audible here in God's own voice.** *Confidence: high.*

**The two grounds of rest (trajectory).** Creation-rest → Hebrews 4:9–10; exodus-rest-for-the-slave → the rest Christ gives to the labouring and heavy-laden (Matt 11:28), which the Exodus overview has already set against Moses distributing his burden at Exodus 18. *Confidence: moderate–high; the Matthew link is thematic, not cited.*

**Moralism check.**

*The "be like X" temptation here* is not about a character — there is no hero to imitate in either passage — but about the commands themselves: **"here are ten rules; keep them."** That is the Decalogue's characteristic misuse and both overviews name it.

*The gospel grounding* is in the text and needs no importing. Both books open the Ten Words with nine identical Hebrew words about what God has already done. **Grace-before-law is not a hermeneutical principle applied to this passage; it is verse two of it, in both books, word for word.** The Exodus overview reaches the same conclusion and adds that it operates inside the case law too (22:21, 27; 23:9).

*Christ as hero, not example:* the passage's own movement is toward a mediator, and both texts end with the people at a distance and one man in the middle. Deuteronomy adds that God approved of the arrangement and wished the people had a heart. **The Decalogue does not end with ten things to do; in both books it ends with a man standing between God and a people who cannot bear him, and a wish that hangs unanswered until it is answered.**

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### The numbering of the Ten — Exod 20:2–17 // Deut 5:6–21

- **Category:** doctrinal / catechetical.
- **The difficulty:** Neither text numbers the words, and the three main traditions divide them differently — Jewish (the prologue as the first word), Reformed and Orthodox (no other gods / no images as first and second, coveting as one tenth), and Roman and Lutheran (no other gods and no images as one, coveting split into ninth and tenth).
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the division is never the text's own concern; the count appears only in the retrospective texts (Exod 34:28; Deut 4:13; 10:4), which give the number and not the division.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **in Leningrad the paragraphing of the two texts supports different divisions, and Deuteronomy's is the one that yields a separate ninth and tenth** (Headline Finding 1, with its two qualifications; Tool 3 §The parashoth architecture). Meanwhile the paragraphing of *both* texts keeps the prologue, the no-other-gods word and the no-images word in a **single** unit, which supports neither the Reformed division of first from second nor a clean Jewish first-word. Keil & Delitzsch describe the same mechanism and name the same obstacle — that the two texts reverse wife and house — and report the disputed setumah missing from a third of Kennicott's manuscripts. `[T]` for the divisions; `[S: Logos — K&D 1:393–94; Bullinger 2:34]` for the manuscript spread and the tradition-alignment. *High confidence on the Leningrad datum; moderate on the wider tradition; and the inference to any numbering scheme should be made cautiously, since paragraph divisions and commandment-numbering are related but not identical questions.*
- **Suggested handling:** address briefly and honestly. Three points are worth a congregation's hearing, and none of them is unsettling once said plainly. **The Bible gives the number and never gives the division.** **The division systems are ancient but not authorial** — pre-Masoretic reading tradition, and variable between manuscripts. And **a congregation whose catechism numbers them one way is not thereby wrong**; the numbering traditions are downstream of a genuine ambiguity in the text, not of anyone's carelessness. Say it before a hearer discovers it and concludes something has been hidden.

#### "Visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children" — Exod 20:5 // Deut 5:9

- **Category:** ethical / pastoral / apologetic. Identical in both texts apart from one waw.
- **The difficulty:** it reads as inherited punishment, and lands hard on anyone who believes their family's damage is a sentence.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** it is the *negative* half of a deliberately unbalanced pair — three or four generations of those who hate me, against **thousands** of those who love me. The asymmetry is the point and it is in both texts.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the qualifier לְשֹׂנְאָי, "of those who hate me," is attached in both books, so the clause is not about innocent descendants; and Deuteronomy itself limits inherited penalty at 24:16, with Ezekiel 18 taking the question up at length. **Deuteronomy 5:3's "not with our fathers, but with us" sits four verses before 5:9's "the iniquity of the fathers" and pulls hard against it** — the same word, the same chapter, in tension. That tension is a feature of Deuteronomy's chapter that Exodus's does not have, and a preacher working from Deuteronomy should name it rather than smooth it.
- **Suggested handling:** address, with the asymmetry and the qualifier, and without flattening the tension in either direction.

#### The Sabbath's two grounds — Exod 20:11 // Deut 5:15

- **Category:** doctrinal; the live intra-Christian question.
- **The difficulty:** whether the fourth word binds Christians, and if so on which ground. A creation ground suggests a creation ordinance; an exodus ground suggests a covenant sign for a redeemed nation.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **both grounds are canonical and neither text offers both.** The New Testament also takes both hands: Hebrews 4 reasons from the creation rest, Colossians 2:16–17 relativises the day. **A preacher who has only one of the two texts will find the question easier than it is.**
- **Suggested handling:** name the two grounds; do not adjudicate the sabbatarian question from this passage, which cannot settle it. This is a case where the report's job is to show the preacher the shape of the disagreement.

#### Coveting — Exod 20:17 // Deut 5:21

- **Category:** pastoral, and doctrinally load-bearing.
- **The difficulty:** the only word addressing an interior state; and both texts list a wife among the things not to be coveted, in Exodus alongside servants and livestock.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **Deuteronomy moves her out of that list and gives the household a different verb** (Tool 7). This is not a modern sensitivity read back into the text; it is a change the second text makes, marked by a paragraph break in both Hebrew witnesses. *High confidence.* Guard against the opposite over-correction: Deuteronomy separates the wife from the property, it does not remove her from a list of a neighbour's things — she stands at the head of the neighbour's household either way, and a sermon that presents Deuteronomy as modern will overstate what has changed.
- **Suggested handling:** this is the clearest single payoff of preaching the two texts together, and it is worth the congregation's hearing.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching a harmonised Decalogue that is neither text

- **What it looks like:** "The Ten Commandments say…" followed by a composite — Exodus's creation ground with Deuteronomy's servant clause, or Deuteronomy's wife-first ordering with Exodus's single verb.
- **Why it's wrong:** the composite exists nowhere in Scripture. It also removes the two frames, which is where each text's meaning lives — the terror at Sinai, the transfer at Moab.
- **The corrective:** preach one text, with its frame, and let the other be the commentary. If the differences are worth mentioning, mention them as the author's own re-preaching, not as variants to be reconciled.

### Pitfall 2: Starting at verse 3 (or verse 7)

- **What it looks like:** the sermon opens with "You shall have no other gods before me."
- **Why it's wrong:** the prologue is not a preamble; it is the covenant's ground and the grammar's antecedent, and it is identical in both books — the one place where the two texts have not moved a letter. Both overviews name law-divorced-from-grace as the trap that bites here, and the Exodus overview says flatly that 20:2 forecloses it.
- **The corrective:** never read the Ten Words without verse 2 / verse 6. If time forces a cut, cut elsewhere.

### Pitfall 3: Treating the differences as a problem to be defended

- **What it looks like:** an apologetic excursus — "some say these are contradictory, but in fact…" — which puts the congregation on the defensive about a feature the text is not embarrassed by.
- **Why it's wrong:** the two texts stand in the same five-volume work, and the later one openly says it is re-issuing the earlier (כַּאֲשֶׁר צִוְּךָ, 5:12, 16). Nothing is being concealed. The New Testament quotes both, sometimes in mixed forms, without comment.
- **The corrective:** preach the differences as evidence of how Scripture applies itself. **Deuteronomy 5 is the Bible's first sermon on the Ten Commandments, and it was preached by the man who received them.**

### Pitfall 4: Preaching Deuteronomy's Sabbath as a correction of Exodus's — or the reverse

- **What it looks like:** "Exodus grounds the Sabbath in creation, but Deuteronomy shows us what it's *really* about — mercy." Or, from the other side, treating the exodus ground as a secondary, culturally bound application of the permanent creation ordinance.
- **Why it's wrong:** each ground is native to its book and to its book's whole network (Tool 11 Move 3), and the New Testament reasons from both. Neither text presents itself as improving the other.
- **The corrective:** preach the ground your text gives, and if you name the other, name it as the other text's, addressed to the other situation.

### Pitfall 5: Treating the people's fear at 20:19 as unbelief

- **What it looks like:** "If only they had been braver, they could have gone on hearing God for themselves."
- **Why it's wrong:** God says the opposite — *"they have done well in all that they have spoken"* (Deut 5:28) — and builds the prophetic office on the request (18:16–17). Moses in Exodus distinguishes the fear they must not have from the fear they must (20:20).
- **The corrective:** preach the request as right, and the mediator as given in answer to it. This pitfall is *only* avoidable if the preacher has both texts, which is the strongest practical argument for the joint dig.

### Pitfall 6: Stopping the Deuteronomy sermon at verse 21

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on Deuteronomy 5 that treats vv. 22–33 as narrative tailpiece.
- **Why it's wrong:** the chapter's own weight is in the frame — seventeen verses of frame against sixteen of quotation — and its best sentence is at 5:29. The chapter also earns the rest of the book at 5:31.
- **The corrective:** preach to 5:29 at least. The wish is the point the whole book turns on.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **The setumah at Deuteronomy 5:10.** The WLC marks a paragraph division at the end of 5:10; the Logos BHS export does not show one there (an apparatus siglum sits where the marker would fall). The nine-versus-ten count in Headline Finding 1 depends on it: without it, Deut 5:6–11 is a single unit and Deuteronomy yields nine like Exodus. **The internal break at 5:21 is present in both witnesses and is unaffected.** *Check the printed BHS at Deut 5:10.* **The Kennicott evidence (below) makes this look less like a transmission error in one of my two witnesses and more like an ordinary instance of the variation the parashoth tradition carries everywhere.**

1a. **Does the coveting-word setumah stand in Exodus 20:17 in any substantial part of the tradition?** Keil & Delitzsch describe the Masoretic text as splitting the coveting commandment by a setumah, and report it **absent from 234 of Kennicott's 694 manuscripts** — which means present in 460. `[S: Logos]` **Leningrad's Exodus 20:17 has no such break**; Leningrad's Deuteronomy 5:21 does. So either K&D are generalising from Deuteronomy, or the Exodus setumah is well attested outside Leningrad and the nine-versus-ten contrast is narrower than it looks. **This is now the single most consequential open question in the report, because Headline Finding 1 rests on it.** *Needed: which text K&D are discussing at 1:393–94, and whether any critical edition or manuscript list records a setumah at Exod 20:17.*
2. **The double accentuation.** BHS prints both accent systems in both Decalogues (the export shows the doubled accents). What each system yields — how many verses, and where the commandment boundaries fall in each — should be read off BHS or a Masorah study rather than reconstructed. *This bears directly on the numbering question and may sharpen or qualify Headline Finding 1.* **Note that the accentuation and the parashoth are two independent division systems**, and a Logos query on the parashoth returned nothing on the accents; they need asking about separately.
3. **The Deuteronomy 5:10 ketiv/qere.** The apparatus entry was not read, only its siglum. *What does BHS's note say, and does any Hebrew witness support "his"?*
4. **The Samaritan Pentateuch, the Nash Papyrus and 4QDeut^n.** None is in the corpus and none was consulted. All three are direct witnesses to how ancient readers handled the Exodus/Deuteronomy comparison — the Samaritan text harmonises the two extensively and adds a Gerizim commandment. **This is the largest single gap in the report, and it is a gap in the evidence, not in the argument:** every claim made here is a claim about the Masoretic tradition and is labelled as such.
5. **The Greek pluses at Exodus 20:10, 12 and 17.** The direction of assimilation is uniform and the internal argument favours the shorter Hebrew, but no Qumran or Samaritan evidence has been weighed, and Göttingen Exodus exists. *Worth a Logos pass before this enters the Exodus overview.*
6. **The NT citation orders in NA28.** Verified here in SBLGNT, which is an observation layer. The corpus's NA28 export covers Acts only. *Confirm Luke 18:20, Rom 13:9, Jas 2:11 and the Mark 10:19 variant in NA28 before the finding enters an overview.*
7. **The apparatus-density observation.** A siglum count is a proxy. *Read the entries at Exod 20:10, 20:17, Deut 5:14 and 5:21 before repeating the claim.*
8. **The weight of the חמד/אוה distinction.** Two neutral uses of אוה elsewhere in Deuteronomy is a small base on which to build "the appetitive verb". *A lexicon pass (HALOT, NIDOTTE) would settle whether the neutral colouring is real or an accident of a three-occurrence sample.*
