Presenting Situation

Speaker and mediator. Leviticus is not narrated so much as spoken. Thirty-seven divine-speech openings carry the book, from 1:1 to 27:1, and the overwhelming majority run “And the LORD spoke to Moses, saying…” [T] The book opens mid-sentence in the grammar of Hebrew narrative — וַיִּקְרָא, “And he called” — with the subject unstated until the second clause supplies it. [T] Leviticus therefore presents itself not as a fresh start but as the continuation of something already under way.

Four speeches are addressed to Moses and Aaron together (11:1; 13:1; 14:33; 15:1) — and every one of them falls inside the clean/unclean block, where the priest is the diagnostician and must hear the rule himself. [T] Exactly one speech in the whole book is addressed to Aaron alone (10:8), and it comes immediately after his two eldest sons are killed at the altar; its content is the prohibition of wine before service. [T] The addressee list is doing structural work, not decoration.

Authorship. The book presents Moses as the recipient and mediator of every speech and, twice, as the one who then transmits it (21:24; 23:44). It does not name its own author. Mosaic authorship is the traditional and canonical ascription and is [I]/[S], never [T]. The label “the Holiness Code” for chapters 17–26 is likewise [S] — a nineteenth-century critical designation, not a heading the book supplies; the phenomena it points at are real and countable (below), but the name is borrowed and should be used with that noted.

Setting, as the text states it. “On Mount Sinai” (7:38; 25:1; 26:46; 27:34) and “from the tent of meeting” (1:1). [T] That is all the book itself says about place. The wider chronology — the first month of the second year after the exodus, the tabernacle newly erected — comes from Exodus 40:17 and Numbers 1:1, not from Leviticus. [I]

Audience. Narrative-world: a just-redeemed people encamped around a tent that now has God in it. Canonical: every later reader who needs the grammar of atonement and holiness — which is to say, the rest of Scripture, and above all Hebrews, 1 Peter, and the Gospels’ purity controversies. Default to the canonical audience. [I]

Pressure. One question, asked in every chapter: how can a sinful people live at close quarters with a holy God without being destroyed? The book itself states the stakes twice in the same idiom — “so that he may not die” (16:2, 13) and “lest you die” (8:35; 10:6, 7, 9) — and gives the principle over two corpses: “Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified” (10:3). [T] Proximity raises privilege and peril together.

Occasion (inferred). Exodus closes with the glory filling the tabernacle so completely that Moses could not enter (Exod 40:35). Leviticus opens with the LORD calling Moses and speaking to him from that tent. The book exists to solve the impasse the previous book ended on. [T] for Lev 1:1’s wording and location; [I] (high confidence) for the link to Exodus 40:35, whose wording is supplied from standard reference knowledge rather than from a text in front of me this session.

Purpose. To make the resident presence survivable and then enjoyable: atonement deals with what would destroy the approaching sinner (1–16), and holiness fits the atoned people to host the God who lives among them (17–26), with the whole thing grounded, eight times over, in “I am the LORD who brought you out of the land of Egypt.” [I]

A versification note worth putting in a handout. The Hebrew and English chapter divisions part company once: BHS 5:20–26 = ESV 6:1–7, so Hebrew 6:1 = English 6:8. Both BHS and the LXX give chapter 5 twenty-six verses and chapter 6 twenty-three; the English division is the outlier. Commentaries working from the Hebrew will cite the priests’ manual as 6:1–7:38 — which is also where the real seam falls. [T]

Structural Arc Map

The seams below are the ones the text marks itself: divine-speech openings, colophons, addressee changes, and the four occurrences of “Sinai”.

Device. Two great panels — approach and atonement (1–16) and holiness (18–26) — pivoting on the Day of Atonement (16) and joined by the blood hinge (17), the whole set inside the story of a God who has just moved in. The movement runs outward: from the altar, to the priest’s body, to the Israelite’s table, to the marriage bed, to the courtroom, to the calendar, to the field, to the balance-sheet. [T]

Three structural signals, counted.

  1. “Sinai” appears in only four verses in the entire book — 7:38; 25:1; 26:46; 27:34 — and every one of them is a seam: one closing colophon for the offerings, one speech-heading, and two closing colophons for the book. The name is absent from chapters 8–24 altogether. [T]
  2. The divine-speech formula occurs 37 times but never in chapter 26, which means the blessings-and-curses chapter is not its own address but the continuation of the speech begun “on Mount Sinai” at 25:1. Sabbath-year, jubilee, and covenant sanctions are one utterance. [T]
  3. Chapter 27 opens a new speech after 26:46 has already closed the book. The appendix is deliberate and marked, not accidental. [T]

On calling chapter 16 “the centre”. It is not the arithmetic centre, and the popular claim should be dropped. Of the book’s 859 verses, 456 stand before chapter 16 and 369 after; the midpoint verse falls inside chapter 15, and by chapter count the middle chapter is 14. [T] The pivot claim has to be made on design, and on design it is very strong indeed:

  • kipper (the verb “atone”) occurs 52 times in Leviticus; 16 of them — 31% — are in chapter 16. The Greek agrees independently: ἐξιλάσκομαι occurs 51 times, 16 of them in chapter 16. [T]
  • kappōret (the mercy seat) occurs 7 times, all of them in chapter 16 (vv. 2, 13, 14, 15) and nowhere else in the book. LXX ἱλαστήριον: 7 times, in the same four verses. [T]
  • pešaʿ — the word for wilful, rebellious transgression, as distinct from the inadvertent sin the sacrificial system handles — occurs exactly twice in Leviticus, at 16:16 and 16:21, and nowhere else. [T]
  • 16:16 is the only verse in the book containing all three defilement words at once — ṭumʾah (uncleanness, the concern of chs. 11–15), pešaʿ (transgression), and ḥaṭṭaʾt (sin, the concern of chs. 4–5) — and 16:21 adds ʿāwōn (iniquity) to the same list over the live goat. [T]

That is the honest and much stronger argument: chapter 16 is where every category of defilement the book has taught you to name is gathered and dealt with in a single day. Claim the design, not the sum.

Microscript

Now that the holy glory has moved into the camp, the LORD teaches his rescued people how to live with him and not die — by blood that atones for everything they are and have done (1–16), and by a holiness that reaches the table, the body, the bed, the calendar, the field, and the ledger (17–26) — and the system’s own endless repetition quietly admits it is not the last word.

Keynotes: holy (qdš, 144×) and unclean (ṭāmēʾ, 149×) — the two highest-frequency theological roots in the book, and they are not opposites but the poles of a field the priest must divide. Refrains: “you shall be holy, for I am holy” and “I am the LORD.” Structural pulse: atonement makes approach possible → holiness makes the people fit. [T]

Test against the movements: approach (1–7) → inauguration (8–10) → distinguishing (11–15) → atonement (16) → the blood hinge (17) → holiness (18–26) → valuation and redemption (27). Each serves the one question. Chapter 27 is the hardest to fit and is the test case: it fits because it prices what has been devoted to a holy God and provides the way to redeem it back — holiness and redemption applied to property, which is exactly where chapter 25 left off.

The bdl chain — and why it is invisible in translation

bdl (“divide, separate, distinguish”) occurs in seven verses: 1:17; 5:8; 10:10; 11:47; 20:24, 25 (×2), 26. [T]

The first two are the bird offering — the priest tears it open “but shall not sever it completely” — a purely physical sense. They are the same root doing different work and should be flagged, not preached, as part of the chain. Confidence on any thematic link to 1:17 / 5:8: low. [T]

The remaining five make one argument:

  • 10:10 — standing in the wreckage of Nadab and Abihu, the priest’s entire vocation is defined: “to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean.”
  • 11:47 — the food laws close by claiming that same mandate as their purpose: “to make a distinction between the unclean and the clean.”
  • 20:24–26 — the argument turns round: “I am the LORD your God, who has separated you from the peoples. You shall therefore separate the clean beast from the unclean… for I the LORD am holy and have separated you from the peoples, that you should be mine.”

Israel separates because God separated Israel. The ethic is grounded in the election and never the reverse — and bdl is the verb Genesis 1 uses for God dividing light from darkness. [T] for the chain; moderate–high for the Genesis 1 link.

The chain is a Hebrew-only phenomenon. The LXX renders 10:10 and 11:47 with the same verb (διαστεῖλαι), preserving that pair — but then uses διορίζω at 20:24 and ἀφορίζω at 20:26. Three different Greek verbs where the Hebrew has one root, so the chain is broken in Greek and invisible in English. A preacher working from a translation will not see it. [T] — and a genuinely useful thing to know before preaching chapter 11 or 20.

The refrains

  • “I am the LORD” — 52 verses: 11:44–45, then 18:2 through 26:45 only. Twenty-nine in the bare form, twenty-three expanded to “I am the LORD your/their God”. Zero occurrences in chapters 1–10, 12–17. The refrain is planted at the close of the food laws and then becomes the governing punctuation of the holiness block. Chapter 19 alone carries sixteen. [T] (Counting note: this figure is of verses containing the formula in either form. The superseded overview gave 49; the difference is a counting convention, not a disagreement — the distribution, which is the load-bearing claim, is identical.)
  • “You shall be holy, for I am holy” — the full motto occurs five times: 11:44, 11:45, 19:2, 20:7, 20:26. Its first statement is about diet, not about sex or worship — a fact worth preaching. The ground clause “for I am holy” then recurs a sixth time at 21:8, where it is applied not to the people but to the priest whom the people must treat as holy. And the motto escalates: 20:26 alone adds “to me” and the separation verb — “you shall be holy to me… and I have separated you from the peoples, that you should be mine.” [T]
  • “I am the LORD who brought you out of the land of Egypt” — eight times in that first-person participial form (11:45; 19:36; 22:33; 25:38, 42, 55; 26:13, 45), always grounding a demand in a prior rescue; a ninth grounding at 23:43 uses a different construction for the same rescue. [T]
  • “Remember” — twice in the whole book, both in 26:42 and 26:45, where God remembers “my covenant with Jacob… Isaac… Abraham” — the patriarchs in reverse order, reaching back past the failure to the oldest promise. They are the only patriarchs named in Leviticus. [T]

19:18 with 19:34 is the finding most often missed. Leviticus states the love command twice in one chapter, in identical Greek: first of the neighbour, then of the resident foreigner — grounded in “you were strangers in the land of Egypt.” Whoever preaches 19:18 without 19:34 has preached half of it. [T]

Text-critical state, and two divergences that touch preaching

The headline is the absence of a headline. MT and LXX Leviticus agree in chapter division, verse division, and order throughout: twenty-seven chapters and 859 verses in both, with no large-scale plus or minus anywhere in the book, and the Greek following the Hebrew versification at the 5/6 seam where the English does not. A verse-by-verse length comparison across all 853 comparable verses shows a tight distribution around a median Greek-to-Hebrew word ratio of 1.86 with no verse-scale omissions. [T] For anyone who has worked in Jeremiah — where the LXX is roughly an eighth shorter and the oracles against the nations are relocated wholesale — this is the opposite situation, and it is worth saying plainly: in Leviticus the text-critical questions are lexical, not architectural.

Two divergences are worth naming in the pulpit:

  1. 26:11 — MT מִשְׁכָּנִי, “I will set my dwelling among you”; LXX τὴν διαθήκην μου, “my covenant.” The “dwelling among you” reading carries real homiletic weight and feeds forward to John 1:14 and Revelation 21:3, so the divergence should be named rather than quietly assumed. Prefer MT; flag. [T] for both readings; the Vorlage question is open.
  2. 17:11 — MT בַּנֶּפֶשׁ (“by / at the cost of the life”); LXX ἀντὶ τῆς ψυχῆς (“in place of the life”). Not a consonantal variant but a translational decision, and a consequential one: the Greek reads the atonement as substitutionary in so many words. Genuinely useful evidence in the penal-substitution discussion — but it is the translators’ reading of an ambiguous Hebrew preposition, and must be presented as such. [T] / [I]

And one that settles a perennial crux by giving the earliest vote: Azazel (16:8, 10, 26). The LXX does not treat עֲזָאזֵל as a proper name. It renders it as a function — τῷ ἀποπομπαίῳ, “for the one sent away” / εἰς τὴν ἀποπομπήν, “for the sending away” — and at 16:26 describes the animal as “the goat set apart εἰς ἄφεσιν, for release.” [T] That does not close the question of the Hebrew term’s etymology, but it does mean the oldest surviving interpretation read it as destination and function, not as a demon’s name. Worth having in hand before the question comes from the floor.

Intertextual Map

Leviticus does not cite. It is cited. There is effectively no external quotation in the book: it presupposes Genesis and Exodus, runs on internal repetition, and is then quarried by the rest of the canon more heavily than almost any other Old Testament book. [T] Consequence for the sweep: passage digs should lean hard on Move 4 (internal echo) and comparatively little on Move 2. Track Exodus and Genesis as the two confirmed live sources, and read the whole book forward into Hebrews.

Christological Trajectory

  • Typology — substitutionary blood. The hand laid on the head (1:4; 3:2; 4:4 and throughout), the purification and reparation offerings (4–6), the two-bird rite (14), the two goats (16), and the stated principle — “the life of the flesh is in the blood, and I have given it for you on the altar to make atonement for your souls” (17:11) — say one thing: a life for a life, and the blood belongs to God. This runs through an established category, escalates properly (an animal for a household → a man for the world), and has explicit NT warrant in Hebrews 9–10. Confidence: high. [I]
  • Typology — the mercy seat. Every occurrence of kappōret in Leviticus (7) falls in chapter 16, where the blood is sprinkled on it; the LXX renders it ἱλαστήριον, and Paul applies that exact noun to Christ (Rom 3:25). The link between the Day of Atonement and the cross is not an inference from theme but a shared word. Confidence: high. [T] (the words) / [I] (the typology)
  • Typology — the better priest. The priest needs atonement for himself before he can make it for anyone else (9:7; 16:6, 11); he is mortal and can be struck down at the altar (10:1–2); he is barred if blemished (21:17–23); and he enters the Most Holy Place once a year, with blood, “so that he may not die” (16:2, 13). The LXX calls him ὁ ἱερεὺς ὁ χριστὸς ὁ τετελειωμένος — the anointed priest who has been perfected — which is Hebrews’ pair of terms for Jesus (Heb 7:28). The office is built to be outgrown. Confidence: high.
  • Trajectory — the scapegoat and forgiveness. The live goat carries “all their iniquities” (16:22) — and it is the only place in the book where a single individual lays both hands on the animal (elsewhere one hand, or many hands from many people) and the only hand-laying accompanied by spoken confession (16:21). The LXX says it is sent away εἰς ἄφεσιν, “for release” — the word the New Testament uses for the forgiveness of sins. Confidence: high (the wording); moderate–high (the typological weight).
  • Trajectory — the jubilee. “Proclaim liberty throughout the land” (25:10) — dĕrôr in Hebrew, ἄφεσις in Greek. Jesus announces his ministry in Isaiah 61’s jubilee language at Luke 4:18–19, and the Greek word is the same. Confidence: high.
  • Contrast — the system confesses its own insufficiency. Sacrifices repeated without end, a Day of Atonement that must come round every year, a veil that stays shut, and a high priest who must atone for himself first. The design points past itself. Hebrews states the verdict the book implies (Heb 10:1–4). Confidence: high. [I]
  • Trajectory — the uncircumcised heart. Leviticus’s own restoration clause locates the problem where the prophets and Paul will locate it: “if then their uncircumcised heart is humbled” (26:41). The book knows that ritual competence is not the cure. Confidence: high. [T]

Moralism check. Two errors, in opposite directions. The first is reducing Leviticus to “try harder to be holy” — which the book’s own architecture forbids: atonement (1–16) precedes the holiness block, the demand is grounded eight times in “I am the LORD who brought you out of Egypt,” God is repeatedly named as “the LORD who sanctifies you” (20:8; 21:8, 15, 23; 22:9, 16, 32), and the bdl chain makes the logic structural — Israel separates because God separated Israel (20:24–26), never in order to be separated. The second error is skipping the book as sub-Christian, which forfeits the vocabulary the New Testament uses to explain the cross. Preach the God who atones and sanctifies as the actor; the call to holiness then flows out of grace rather than towards it, exactly as 1 Peter 1:15–16 carries the motto forward. [T]

Preaching Traps

  1. Skipping Leviticus as sub-Christian ritual. Sounds like: an apologetic aside about ancient religion before hurrying to the Gospels. Why wrong: it is the grammar of the cross — Hebrews’ quarry and Paul’s, and several links are verbal rather than merely conceptual. Bites at: chapters 1–7 and 11–15. Corrective: preach it forward to Christ our once-for-all sacrifice and permanent priest.

  2. Moralising holiness into self-improvement. Sounds like: “be separate, like Israel was.” Why wrong: holiness rides on prior atonement and on God’s own sanctifying work; 20:24–26 grounds Israel’s separating in God’s. Bites at: chapters 18–20, and any “practical application” landing in 19. Corrective: every command follows a rescue and imitates a character.

  3. Losing reverence — treating access as casual. Sounds like: “we can come boldly now” with nothing of what boldness cost. Why wrong: holiness and danger rise together with proximity, and the book says so over two corpses (10:3). Bites at: chapter 10 and chapter 16. Corrective: recover the awe, then preach the access Christ opened — the second is worthless without the first.

  4. Privatising holiness to the “spiritual” sphere. Bites at: the table (11), the body (12–15), sex and society (18–20), the calendar (23), land and money (25). Why wrong: Leviticus refuses a sacred/secular split; it disciples the kitchen, the courtroom, the bedroom, the farm, and the ledger. Corrective: preach whole-life holiness — and note that the “be holy” motto is stated first about diet.

  5. Over-claiming the architecture. Sounds like: “chapter 16 is the exact centre of the book” or “of the Torah.” Why wrong: the arithmetic does not support it — 456 verses precede chapter 16 and 369 follow, and the midpoint verse falls in chapter 15. Equally, “the Holiness Code begins at chapter 17” is lexically unsupported: chapter 17 has neither qdš nor “I am the LORD.” Bites at: the series-introduction sermon. Corrective: claim the design — chapter 16 as functional pivot on the pešaʿ and kappōret evidence, 17 as hinge, 18 as the block’s real opening. It is a better argument and it survives cross-examination.

  6. Over-claiming the details. The rationale for the food taxonomy, the identification of ṣāraʿat as “leprosy” (it plainly is not Hansen’s disease — the same word covers mould in a wall), the meaning of Azazel, the asymmetry of the childbirth periods. Bites at: 11–15 and 16. Why wrong: several rationales are genuinely contested and the text does not explain itself. Corrective: preach the stated function — separation, holiness, guilt carried away — and flag the debates honestly. Leviticus is unusually willing to give purposes (10:10; 11:47; 17:11; 20:26); preach those.

  7. Preaching 19:18 without 19:34. Sounds like: “love your neighbour” as the summary of Levitical ethics. Why wrong: sixteen verses later the identical command is extended to the resident foreigner and grounded in Israel’s own experience of being outsiders — and 24:22 insists on “the same rule for the sojourner and for the native.” Bites at: chapter 19. Corrective: preach both halves, and let 25:23 land the point — Israel are themselves still “strangers and sojourners with me.”

Pastoral landmine (flag ⭐ HIGH). The contested sexuality texts (18:22; 20:13) require a careful solo study alongside the NT usage, presenting the interpretive options fairly and pastorally. Do not let them arrive in a sermon as a passing illustration of “holiness in the bedroom.”

What This Pass Changes

The superseded book-overview-leviticus.md (v0.2.0) was read only after the text work above was complete. Roughly three-quarters of its spine survives an independent pass, and several of its counted claims were confirmed exactly. Recording both directions honestly:

Confirmed independently (reached here before that document was opened): the presence-crisis premise (Exod 40:35 → Lev 1:1); the atonement-then-holiness shape; chapter 17 as hinge rather than the holiness block’s opening, on the evidence that it contains neither qdš nor “I am the LORD”; qdš absent from chapters 1, 3, 9, 13, 15, 17, 18; kappōret 7× all in chapter 16; kipper 52× with 16 in chapter 16; “Sinai” in four verses, all at seams; 37 speech formulae with none in chapter 26; bĕrît 10× with 8 in chapter 26; gāʾal only in chapters 25 and 27; “remember” only at 26:42, 45 with the patriarchs reversed; and the refusal of the “exact centre” claim.

Corrected on counted evidence.

  1. min, “after its kind”: 9 occurrences, all in chapter 11 — not 16. The higher figure appears to have counted false matches on הַיָּמִין (“the right hand/side”) in chapters 7–9. The true figure is the stronger claim: the word occurs nowhere else in Leviticus.
  2. “Be holy, for I am holy”: the full motto is 5×, but the ground clause recurs a 6th time at 21:8, applied to the priest. Stating both is sharper than either alone.
  3. ἱλαστήριον occurs 7×, matching kappōret exactly — the superseded version’s list (16:2 ×2, 14 ×2, 15) was very nearly right; the full distribution is 16:2 (×2), 13, 14 (×2), 15 (×2).
  4. Greek counts revised: ἐξιλάσκομαι 51 (not 56); ἄμωμος 23 (not 20). Accent-insensitive counting is required or these run low.
  5. The blood claim restated by density. Chapter 17 is the book’s most blood-saturated chapter per verse (13 occurrences in 16 verses); chapter 4 has a comparable raw total across more than twice the length. The density form is the defensible one.
  6. “I am the LORD”: 52 verses here against 49 there — a counting convention, not a disagreement. The distribution, which carries the argument, is identical.

Added. The pešaʿ evidence for chapter 16 (the two-occurrence distribution, and 16:16 as the only verse holding all three defilement words) — a stronger warrant for the pivot than lexical density alone; the identical-clause relation between 9:24 and 10:2; the unique double-handed, confessed hand-laying at 16:21; the 10:3 ↔︎ 22:32 echo; the 19:34 ↔︎ 25:23 reversal; the negation of “pleasing aroma” at 26:31; the two-narrative bracket (ch. 10 and 24:10–23) and its “one rule” resolution at 24:22; the internal speech-seam at 4:1 dividing chapters 1–3 from 4–5; the addressee analysis (four Moses-and-Aaron speeches, all in the purity block; one Aaron-alone speech, at 10:8); the LXX τελειόω / χριστός pair at 4:5 with Hebrews 7:28; ἄφεσις at 16:26 joining the scapegoat to the jubilee and to NT forgiveness; the demonstration that the bdl chain is broken in Greek and invisible in translation; the LXX’s non-personal rendering of Azazel; the Genesis 3:8 “walk among you” echo at 26:12; and the whole-book MT/LXX structural comparison.

Tension with the Torah master overview. book-overview-torah.md describes Leviticus as “atonement (1–16) / holiness (17–27)”. The lexical evidence puts chapter 17 outside the holiness block and chapter 27 outside the 18–26 speech-frame, so the shape is better stated 1–16 / 17 / 18–26 / 27. Flagged rather than silently altered; the master should be revised on its next pass. [T]
