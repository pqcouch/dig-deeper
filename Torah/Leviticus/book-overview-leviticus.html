Book Overview: Leviticus

Translation: ESV (with BHS and Rahlfs LXX supplied and worked directly) Genre: Covenant law and liturgy set within narrative; the central volume of the Torah Mode: Finalise/Upgrade (v0.2.0) — v0.1.0 revised against the supplied Hebrew and Greek texts; built on the completed Leviticus whole-book sweep (fifteen units) and the Torah master overview; Schnittjer Torah Story companion consulted at the checking stage Date: 27 August 2026 Sweep status: Whole-book sweep in hand; ⭐ HIGH units await solo digs before the pulpit

Presenting Situation

Narrator / author. Almost entirely divine speech: the book is a chain of thirty-seven “the LORD spoke to Moses, saying…” formulae, opening at 1:1 and running to 27:1. [T] The book is set at Sinai in the first month of the second year and spoken from the tent of meeting (1:1). [T] Mosaic authorship is the traditional and canonical ascription, and the book presents Moses as the recipient and mediator of every speech — but the book does not name its own author, so the ascription is [I]/[S], not a text-observation.

Audience. Narrative-world: the just-redeemed people at Sinai with the newly-erected tabernacle. Compositional: wilderness Israel learning how to live around the resident glory [I]. Canonical: later Israel and the church, reading the grammar of atonement and holiness that the New Testament — above all Hebrews and 1 Peter — will quarry. Default to the canonical audience. [I]

Occasion. Exodus ended with the glory filling the tent so completely that “Moses was not able to enter” (Exod 40:35). Leviticus opens with the LORD calling Moses and speaking from the tent — God now teaches his redeemed, resident-among people how a sinful nation may approach him and live. [T]

Pressure. How may a sinful people approach, live with, and not be destroyed by the Holy One dwelling in their midst? Proximity to glory increases both privilege and peril (10:3, “among those who are near me I will be sanctified”). [T]

Purpose. To make the resident presence survivable and enjoyable: by atonement (chs. 1–16) that deals with sin so approach is possible, and by holiness (chs. 18–27, hinged at 17) that fits a redeemed people to host a holy God — “You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy” (19:2). [I]

A practical note on versification. The Hebrew and English chapter divisions part company at 5/6: BHS 5:20–26 = ESV 6:1–7, so Hebrew 6:1 = English 6:8. Commentaries working from the Hebrew will cite the priests’ manual as 6:1–7:38, a cleaner seam than the English 6:8. Worth stating in any handout. [T]

Device. Two framed halves — atonement (1–16) and holiness (18–26) — pivoting on the Day of Atonement (16) and hinged by ch. 17, the whole set inside the story of God now dwelling in the camp. The movement runs from how sin is covered to how a covered people lives, radiating holiness outward from altar to table to bed to calendar to field. [T] / [S: Schnittjer confirms the two-part shape and the central place of ch. 16]

Three structural signals, counted. (i) The divine-speech formula occurs 37×, but not once in ch. 26 — chs. 25–26 are a single speech delivered “on Mount Sinai” (25:1). (ii) Sinai is named only 4× in the whole book: once as that speech-heading (25:1) and three times in closing colophons (7:38; 26:46; 27:34), which frame the book’s major seams. (iii) Ch. 27 opens with a fresh speech formula after 26:46 has already sounded like the book’s end — the appendix is deliberate, not accidental. [T]

On “the centre.” Ch. 16 is the pivot by function (everything before leads to it; everything after flows from a cleansed people) and by lexical density (below), not by arithmetic: the book’s 859 verses have their midpoint near 15:7 and their word-midpoint at 14:51. Claim the design, not the sum. [T]

Microscript

Now that the holy glory has come to dwell in the camp, the LORD teaches his redeemed people how a sinful nation may live with him and not die: by substitutionary blood that atones (1–16), climaxing in the Day of Atonement, and by a comprehensive, God-imitating holiness that fits them to host his presence (18–27) — a whole system that, in its endless repetitions and closed veil, quietly confesses it is not yet the end. Keynotes: holy / holiness (the qdš root, 137×); atonement (kipper, 52×); the refrains “be holy, for I am holy” and “I am the LORD.” Structural pulse: atonement makes approach possible → holiness makes the people fit. [T]

Test against the movements: offerings → priesthood → clean/unclean → Day of Atonement → the blood hinge → the holiness block → the devotion appendix. Each serves the one question — how may a sinful people live with the Holy One?

The bdl chain — a seven-fold leading word

bdl (“divide, separate, distinguish”) occurs exactly seven times: 1:17, 5:8, 10:10, 11:47, 20:24, 20:25, 20:26. [T]

The first two are the bird offering — “he shall tear it open by its wings but shall not sever it completely” (1:17; cf. 5:8) — a physical sense, and an honest caveat: they are the same root doing different work, and the thematic chain should not be over-read back into them. [T] (confidence on the link to 1:17/5:8: low — flag, don’t preach)

The remaining five are a single argument:

  • 10:10 — in the wreckage of Nadab and Abihu, the priest’s whole mandate is defined: “to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean.”
  • 11:47 — the food laws close with that mandate as their stated purpose: “to make a distinction between the unclean and the clean.”
  • 20:24–26 — the climax reverses the direction: “I am the LORD your God, who has separated you from the peoples. You shall therefore separate the clean beast from the unclean… for I the LORD am holy and have separated you from the peoples, that you should be mine.”

Israel separates because God separated Israel. The ethic is grounded in the election, not the reverse — and bdl is the Genesis 1 verb for God dividing light from darkness and waters from waters. This upgrades v0.1.0’s vague “separation/division” under Genesis categories to a located, testable chain. [T], Genesis link moderate–high.

The refrain distributions

  • “I am the LORD” — 49 verses: 11:44–45, then 18:2 through 26:45 only. Zero in chs. 1–10, 12–17. The refrain is planted at the close of the food laws and then becomes the governing punctuation of the holiness block. [T]
  • “Be holy, for I am holy” — 5 occurrences: 11:44, 11:45, 19:2, 20:7, 20:26. The motto is stated first about diet, not about sex or worship — a fact worth preaching. [T]
  • “Remember” — 2 occurrences in the entire book, both in 26:42, 45, where God remembers “my covenant with Jacob… Isaac… Abraham” — the patriarchs in reverse order, reaching back past the failure to the oldest promise. [T]

19:18 and 19:34 together are the finding most likely to be missed: Leviticus states the love command twice in one chapter, in identical Greek, first of the neighbour and then of the resident foreigner. Whoever preaches 19:18 without 19:34 has preached half of it. [T]

Two textual variants that touch preaching

  1. 26:11 — MT מִשְׁכָּנִי, “I will set my dwelling among you”; LXX τὴν διαθήκην μου, “my covenant.” The “tabernacle among you” reading carries real homiletic weight (and feeds Rev 21:3), so the divergence should be named rather than assumed. Prefer MT; flag. [T]
  2. 17:11 — MT בַּנֶּפֶשׁ (“by/in the life”); LXX ἀντὶ τῆς ψυχῆς (“in place of the life”). Not a consonantal variant but a translational decision, and a consequential one: the LXX reads the atonement as substitutionary in so many words. Useful evidence in the penal-substitution discussion — but it is the translators’ reading, not the Hebrew’s plain sense, and must be presented as such. [T] / [I]

Intertextual Map

Leviticus is not a citing book — it presupposes and runs on Exodus and Genesis, and is itself quarried by the rest of Scripture more than almost any other OT book. Its argument is carried by internal repetition, not external citation, which means passage digs should lean on Move 4 (internal echo) rather than Move 2. For the digs: track Exodus (the tabernacle, Sinai) and Genesis (creation categories) as confirmed live sources, and read the whole book forward into Hebrews.

Consequence for the sweep. Teaching Leviticus well unlocks the NT’s atonement and holiness vocabulary more than any other OT book. Preach it forward: its repetitions and restrictions are designed to make Christ’s finished work glorious. [T] / [S: reception]

Christological Trajectory

  • Atonement by substitutionary blood. The hand-laid burnt offering (1), the purification and reparation offerings (4–6), the two-bird cleansing (14), the two goats (16), and the stated principle — “the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement by the life” (17:11) — all say: a life for a life, and blood belongs to God. The LXX renders that principle ἀντὶ τῆς ψυχῆς, “in place of the life.” Hebrews reads the whole chain as fulfilled once-for-all in Christ (Heb 9–10). Confidence: high.
  • The mercy seat. All seven occurrences of kappōret in Leviticus fall in ch. 16, where the blood is sprinkled; the LXX renders it ἱλαστήριον — the word Paul applies to Christ in Rom 3:25. The link between the Day of Atonement and the cross is not an inference from theme but a shared word. Confidence: high (upgraded from [S] in v0.1.0).
  • The better priest. The priests need atonement for themselves (9; 16), are mortal and can be struck down (10), and are barred if blemished (21); the high priest enters the Most Holy Place once a year, with blood, “that he may not die” (16). And the LXX calls the anointed priest ὁ ἱερεὺς ὁ χριστός — “the christ priest” (4:5, 16; 6:15). The system reaches for the sinless priest with permanent access (Heb 4–7). Confidence: high.
  • The system confesses its own insufficiency. Sacrifices endlessly repeated, the Day of Atonement recurring every year, the veil staying shut — “it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins” (Heb 10:4). The very design points beyond itself to the torn veil and opened access. Confidence: high. (The sweep’s finding #6, foregrounded here at its own recommendation.)
  • The feasts and Jubilee. The sacred calendar (23) is fulfilled in Christ (Passover, Firstfruits, Pentecost — 1 Cor 5:7; 15:20; Acts 2); the Jubilee’s liberty (25) is ἄφεσις in the Greek, the NT’s word for release and forgiveness, proclaimed by Jesus at Luke 4:18–19. Confidence: high.

Moralism check. Resist reducing Leviticus to “try harder to be holy,” and resist the opposite error of skipping it as sub-Christian. Holiness is the grateful, God-imitating life of the already-redeemed — atonement (1–16) precedes the holiness block (18–27), and the commands are grounded in “I am the LORD who brought you out” (9×) and “I am the LORD who sanctifies you” (20:8). The bdl chain makes the same point structurally: Israel separates because God separated Israel (20:24–26), never in order to be separated. God is the one who atones and sanctifies; the call to holiness flows from his prior grace, and 1 Peter 1:15–16 carries the motto straight into Christian conduct. [T]

Preaching Traps

  1. Skipping Leviticus as sub-Christian ritual. Bites at: the offerings (1–7), purity (11–15). Why wrong: it is the grammar of the cross — Hebrews’ quarry, and Paul’s (Rom 3:25). Corrective: preach it forward to Christ our once-for-all sacrifice and priest.

  2. Moralising holiness into self-improvement. Bites at: the holiness block (18–20). Why wrong: holiness rides on prior atonement and God’s sanctifying work; 20:24–26 grounds Israel’s separating in God’s. Corrective: every command follows redemption and imitates God’s own character.

  3. Losing reverence — treating access as casual. Bites at: Nadab and Abihu (10); the Day of Atonement (16). Why wrong: holiness and danger rise together with proximity (10:3). Corrective: recover awe — the God who invites us near is holy — while preaching the access Christ opened.

  4. Privatising holiness to the “spiritual” sphere. Bites at: the table (11), body (12–15), sex and society (18–20), time (23), land and money (25). Why wrong: Leviticus refuses a sacred/secular split — it disciples the kitchen, courtroom, bedroom, farm, and ledger. Corrective: preach whole-life holiness.

  5. Over-claiming the details (the food-taxonomy rationale; the ṣāraʿat = “leprosy” rendering; Azazel; the childbirth asymmetry). Bites at: 11–15; 16. Why wrong: several rationales are genuinely contested. Corrective: preach the function (holiness, separation, guilt carried away) and flag the debates honestly.

  6. Over-claiming the architecture — new in v0.2.0. “Leviticus 16 is the exact centre of the book / of the Torah” is a favourite pulpit line, and the arithmetic does not support it: the verse midpoint falls near 15:7 and the word midpoint at 14:51. Equally, “the Holiness Code begins at chapter 17” is lexically unsupported — ch. 17 has neither qdš nor “I am the LORD.” Bites at: any series-introduction sermon. Corrective: claim the design (16 as functional pivot, 17 as hinge, 18 as the block’s opening) — it is a better argument than the arithmetic, and it survives cross-examination.

Pastoral landmine (flag HIGH). The contested sexuality texts (18:22; 20:13) demand a careful solo study with the NT usage (Rom 1; 1 Cor 6), presenting options fairly and pastorally rather than a hot take. [S: sweep open questions]
