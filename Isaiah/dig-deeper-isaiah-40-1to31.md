## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each reached by more than one tool:

1. **The comfort rests on a legal declaration, and the declaration uses Leviticus 26's own words for an exile that is over.** `[T]` In the whole Hebrew Bible, iniquity is the subject or object of רָצָה ("to be paid off, to make amends") in only three places. Two are **Lev 26:41 and 43**, where the people in the land of their enemies "make amends for their iniquity" (יִרְצוּ אֶת־עֲוֺנָם) while the land "enjoys its sabbaths". The third is **Isa 40:2, נִרְצָה עֲוֺנָהּ**. The clause beside it, מָלְאָה צְבָאָהּ ("her term of service is full"), uses the word Job 7:1 uses for man's "hard service on earth". **"Comfort" is not a mood. It announces that the term set by the covenant curse has been served** (WLC). Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Linking Words (the three כִּי clauses of v.2), Tool 11 (Move 1 on Lev 26), Original Audience. *High confidence on the verbal datum. Moderate on the sense of רָצָה: the lexica separate "be pleased, accept" from "pay off", and the WLC index does not.* (Every occurrence of רָצָה with עָוֹן is listed under Tool 11.)

2. **The chapter turns on one antithesis: flesh withers, the word stands. Verse 24 then applies the same withering to the rulers who threaten the exiles.** `[T]` Verses 6–8 use חָצִיר ("grass") four times, צִיץ ("flower") three times and יָבֵשׁ ("withers") twice, against a single וּדְבַר־אֱלֹהֵינוּ יָקוּם לְעוֹלָם ("the word of our God stands for ever"). The only other verse in the Hebrew Bible with both חָצִיר and צִיץ is **Ps 103:15**. There, in the next two verses, man's days are grass, the wind passes over, **and the חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness") of the LORD is from everlasting to everlasting**. Isaiah keeps four of those elements and moves the fifth. **The חֶסֶד that fades now belongs to "all flesh" (v.6, וְכָל־חַסְדּוֹ), and what stands for ever is God's word.** The third occurrence of יָבֵשׁ comes at v.24: God "blows on" the rulers "and they wither". All flesh is grass, and that includes Babylon's princes. Surfaced by: Repetition, Parallels, Structure (the *setumah* closing v.8), Tool 11 (Ps 103; 1 Pet 1:24–25), Translations. *High confidence on the counts and on Ps 103 as the nearest verbal partner. Which text is prior is open.*

3. **Israel's complaint in v.27 is answered with the prophet's own words from the darkest point of chapters 6–8.** `[T]` Within a span of five verses, Jacob, the verb סָתַר ("to hide") and the verb קָוָה ("to wait, hope") come together in only two places in the Hebrew Bible. One is **Isa 8:17**: "I will wait for the LORD who is hiding His face from the house of Jacob, I will even look eagerly (וְקִוֵּיתִי) for Him". The other is **Isa 40:27–31**. Jacob complains "my way is hidden from the LORD"; the chapter ends "those who wait (קוֹיֵ) for the LORD will gain new strength". There is a second link of the same kind. The verbs "know", "hear" and "understand" stand together in only two verses of Isaiah: **6:9**, the commission to harden, and **40:21**, "Do you not know? Have you not heard?… Have you not understood?" **The hardening vocabulary and the hidden face of chapters 6–8 are both reversed here, in the chapter's last paragraph.** Surfaced by: Tool 11 (Move 4), Repetition, Context (Positional Necessity), So What? *High confidence on both verbal data; moderate-to-high that the echo is designed.*

4. **The Gospels read 40:3 from the Greek, and all three Synoptics change "our God" to "his". Mark pairs the verse with the one other prophet who uses its rare verb.** `[T]` The piel imperative of פָּנָה ("clear!") occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible, all in Isaiah: **40:3; 57:14; 62:10**. The piel with דֶּרֶךְ ("way") occurs once more, at **Mal 3:1** (וּפִנָּה־דֶרֶךְ לְפָנָי, "and he will clear the way before me"). That is the verse Mark 1:2 sets in front of Isa 40:3. In Matt 3:3, Mark 1:3 and Luke 3:4, Isaiah's τὰς τρίβους **τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν** ("the paths of our God") becomes τὰς τρίβους **αὐτοῦ** ("his paths") (SBLGNT). **The LORD whose road is cleared is the one John goes before.** *Category 3 — the NT's own text.* Surfaced by: Translations, Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Christological Reading. *High confidence.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level** (per the overview, which this run does not re-derive): the book confronts Judah with the holiness of her God, exposes every rival trust, and holds out what survives the levelling — the stump's shoot, the Servant, the seed. `[S: overview]`

**Passage level.** This chapter states the terms on which comfort can be offered to people who have been judged, and then defends them against those people's own despair. It does three things, in order:

- it **declares** the sentence served (vv.1–2);
- it **commissions** voices to announce God's coming (vv.3–11);
- it **argues**, by twenty-odd questions, that the God who comes cannot be measured, counselled, likened or outlasted (vv.12–26), and turns that argument on Jacob's complaint (vv.27–31). `[T]`

The chapter's own grammar shows its purpose. It opens with commands addressed to unnamed plural heralds (נַחֲמוּ, דַּבְּרוּ, קִרְאוּ, פַּנּוּ, יַשְּׁרוּ). It closes with a statement about "those who wait" (v.31). **The comfort is spoken *about* Israel before it is spoken *to* Israel**, and the people themselves speak only once, in v.27, and then to complain. `[T]`

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Chapters 36–39 end with three things:

- Hezekiah has been told that "all that is in your house… shall be carried to Babylon" and that his sons will be eunuchs there (39:6–7) `[T]`;
- he has answered that the word is "good", "for there will be peace and truth in my days" (39:8) `[T]`;
- Leningrad then marks a *petuchah* after 39:8. `[T]` *(Gate e4: this is a feature of the apparatus, in Leningrad as BHS prints it; tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`. The Hezekiah dig flagged the same marker on the same terms.)*

The preceding movement has therefore created a specific need: **a people sentenced to Babylon by a word the king called "good", with no comforter in view.**

**Necessity answer.** Chapter 40 exists here because 39:8 has left the sentence standing and the king indifferent to the generation that will serve it. Three features of the text show the chapter answering that need.

- **It answers a word with a word.** 39:8 has דְּבַר־יְהוָה ("the word of the LORD") called "good" by a king thinking of his own days. 40:8 has דְּבַר־אֱלֹהֵינוּ ("the word of our God") standing "for ever" (לְעוֹלָם). **The horizon moves from "my days" to "for ever".** `[T]` for both phrases; `[I]` for the pairing.
- **It reassigns the address.** Hezekiah heard "your sons" (39:7). The heralds are told to speak to "my people" (עַמִּי, 40:1). `[T]`
- **It reverses the movement of 39:6.** In 39:6 everything is carried *to* Babylon (יִשָּׂא… בָּבֶל, with the same verb נָשָׂא). In 40:11 the shepherd "carries" (יִשָּׂא) the lambs in his bosom. `[T]` for the verb; `[I]` that the link is meant. *Moderate confidence — נָשָׂא is common.*

**Implication.** Preached after 39:8, the chapter's opening is grace arriving where the verdict fell. **And because the comfort is grounded in v.2's three legal clauses, it is credible only after the verdict.** Grace into condemnation is the positional shape. `[I]`

**Revisit after Tool 11 (Move 2).** The book-usage tracking sharpens this. Two of chapter 40's strongest internal links run back past 36–39 to **chapters 6–8**: 6:9 → 40:21 and 8:17 → 40:27–31. Chapter 40 does not only answer chapter 39. It answers the commission to harden and the prophet's own season of waiting. So the hinge at 36–39 turns a door whose frame was set in 6–8. `[I]`

#### General context

- **Before:** 39:1–8, the Babylonian embassy and the sentence. The Hezekiah dig has shown that Isaiah frames 36–39 with poetry on both sides — chapter 35 before (the highway, "your God will come") and 40:1 after. `[S: Hezekiah dig]` Chapter 35 matters to chapter 40 directly (see Tool 11, Move 4).
- **After:** 41:1 picks up 40:31's last-but-three words — **יַחֲלִיפוּ כֹחַ** ("gain new strength") — and gives them to the coastlands and peoples summoned to court. In the whole Hebrew Bible the verb with כֹּחַ ("strength") appears only at Isa 40:31, 41:1 and Hab 1:11. `[T]` **A *petuchah* separates the chapters in Leningrad, and a catchword binds them.** The renewed strength promised to Israel in 40:31 is offered ironically to the nations in 41:1, so that they can come and lose their case. `[I]`
- **Section:** the first unit of 40–48, the overview's "comfort, and the trial of the gods". `[S: overview]` The idol-making of vv.19–20 is the first of the book's idol-manufacture passages (41:6–7; 44:9–20; 46:6–7). The phrase לֹא יִמּוֹט ("that will not totter") ends both 40:20 and 41:7. `[T]`

### 3. Structure

**Leningrad's markers, as a control.** The chapter carries seven *setumot* and one closing *petuchah* (after vv.2, 5, 8, 11, 16, 24, 26; *petuchah* after 31). `[T — in Leningrad]` `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` They divide it into eight paragraphs. The divisions below were derived from the speakers and the grammar first, and then compared with the markers. They coincide at every major seam.

| Verses | Section | Function | Marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1–2** | The decree: "Comfort… speak… call" | Heavenly commission; the verdict served | *setumah* |
| **3–5** | The first voice (ק֣וֹל קוֹרֵא, "a voice calls") | Prepare the road; the glory revealed | *setumah* |
| **6–8** | The second voice (ק֚וֹל אֹמֵר, "a voice says") | Flesh is grass; the word stands | *setumah* |
| **9–11** | The herald (מְבַשֶּׂרֶת, "bearer of good news") | "Here is your God": warrior and shepherd | *setumah* |
| **12–16** | "Who…?" — creation | No one measured or counselled him | *setumah* |
| **17–24** | "To whom will you liken God?" — idols and rulers | Idols manufactured; rulers withered | *setumah* |
| **25–26** | "To whom will you liken me?" — the stars | The Holy One names the host | *setumah* |
| **27–31** | "Why do you say, O Jacob…?" | The complaint answered | ***petuchah*** |

**Two halves, and a hinge of voices.** Verses 1–11 are proclamation: three voices — a voice calling (v.3), a voice saying (v.6), the herald (v.9). Verses 12–31 are disputation. **The first half moves outward from the heavenly council to the cities of Judah. The second moves inward, from the cosmos to Jacob's mouth.** `[T]`

**A frame of speaking.** God "says" (יֹאמַר) at v.1, and the Holy One "says" (יֹאמַר) at v.25. These are the only two occurrences of that form in the chapter. Jacob's "Why do you say (תֹאמַר)…?" (v.27) is the third occurrence of the same verb in the imperfect. **The chapter has God speak twice before Israel speaks at all**, and when Israel does speak, God's speaking answers it. `[T]`

**Bookends.** The chapter opens and closes on its addressee, unnamed then named: "my people" (v.1) and "Jacob… Israel" (v.27). Its first word is a command to comfort; its last clause, וְלֹא יִיעָפוּ ("and not become weary"), is the comfort received. `[I]`

**Chiasm in the last paragraph.** The pair יָעֵף/יָגֵעַ ("weary"/"tired") is ordered so:

- v.28 — God: לֹא יִיעַף וְלֹא יִיגָע ("not weary, not tired")
- v.30 — youths: וְיִעֲפוּ… וְיִגָעוּ ("weary… tired")
- v.31 — those who wait: יָרוּצוּ וְלֹא יִיגָעוּ יֵלְכוּ וְלֹא יִיעָפוּ ("run and not get tired, walk and not become weary")

**v.31 reverses v.28's order.** The waiters end where God began, which is the chapter's point in miniature. `[T]` *High confidence.*

**The connector to hold on to is v.2's triple כִּי.** It makes the consolation an argument from facts. Lose it, and "comfort" becomes encouragement without ground. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

The chapter is poetry throughout, and the parallelism does interpretive work at four points.

- **v.3: בַּמִּדְבָּר ‖ בָּעֲרָבָה** ("in the wilderness ‖ in the desert"), each paired with an imperative: "clear… the way" ‖ "make straight… a highway". **The parallelism attaches "in the wilderness" to the road, not to the voice.** This, more than the accents, is what decides the Hebrew reading of v.3 (see Tool 8 and Book-Overview Tensions). `[T]` *High confidence.*
- **vv.6b–8: a staircase with a broken step.** "All flesh is grass ‖ all its חֶסֶד like the flower of the field". Then "the grass withers, the flower fades" twice — the second time without the explanatory clause. Then the antithesis: "but the word of our God stands for ever". **The parallelism is built so that the reader expects a third withering and meets a word that does not wither.** `[T]`
- **v.10 ‖ v.11: the arm twice.** "His arm rules for him" (וּזְרֹעוֹ מֹשְׁלָה לוֹ) ‖ "in his arm he gathers the lambs" (בִּזְרֹעוֹ יְקַבֵּץ טְלָאִים). The same זְרוֹעַ ("arm") is the warrior's and the shepherd's. **Might and tenderness are one limb, not two attributes in tension.** `[T]` *High confidence.*
- **vv.12–14: synonymous questions, one verb repeated.** תִּכֵּן ("measured, gauged") stands in v.12 of the heavens and in v.13 of the רוּחַ of the LORD. **The one who gauged the heavens cannot himself be gauged.** No English version keeps the verb (Tool 8). `[T]`

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory.**

- *Personal and collective names:* Jerusalem (vv.2, 9), Zion (v.9), Judah (v.9), Lebanon (v.16), Jacob and Israel (v.27).
- *Divine names and titles:* יְהוָה (nine verses), אֲדֹנָי יְהוִה (v.10), אֵל (v.18), קָדוֹשׁ (v.25), אֱלֹהֵי עוֹלָם (v.28).
- *Numbers:* none.
- **Routine scenery:** Judah's "cities" and Lebanon. Lebanon is the paradigm of inexhaustible timber, offered and found insufficient.
- **Candidates for canonical freight:** Jacob/Israel at v.27 (see the 8:17 link), and two titles, treated below.

**Key words** (each in its own script with an English gloss):

- **נַחֲמוּ נַחֲמוּ ("comfort, comfort")** — a doubled piel imperative, plural. The addressees are unnamed: the LXX supplies ἱερεῖς ("priests") at v.2, the Targum prophets `[S — not checked in this run]`. **The verb's distribution in Isaiah is a hinge in itself:** seventeen occurrences in fourteen verses, three of them in chs 1–39 (1:24; 12:1; 22:4) and fourteen from 40:1 on (WLC). `[S: sweep — verified]`
  - The first of the three, 1:24, is the hostile sense: אֶנָּחֵם, "I will ease myself" on my enemies. `[T]`
  - The third, 22:4, is the prophet refusing comfort: "do not try to comfort me (לְנַחֲמֵנִי) over the destruction of the daughter of my people". `[T]` **In 22:4 the prophet will not be comforted; in 40:1 heralds are ordered to comfort.**
- **דַּבְּרוּ עַל־לֵב ("speak to the heart")** — not "kindly" in a general sense. The idiom (verb + עַל + לֵב, "heart") occurs in ten verses of the Hebrew Bible (WLC). Nine are tender address to someone wronged, frightened or estranged — Shechem to Dinah (Gen 34:3), Joseph to his brothers (Gen 50:21), the Levite to his concubine (Judg 19:3), Boaz to Ruth (Ruth 2:13), God to his wife in the wilderness (Hos 2:16). The tenth, 1 Sam 1:13, is Hannah "speaking in her heart", a different sense. **Only Gen 50:21 and Ruth 2:13 join it to נָחַם ("comfort") as Isa 40:1–2 does.** Both are an injured party's reassurance to the one who has reason to fear him. `[T]` *High confidence on the distribution.*
- **צָבָא ("warfare, term of service", v.2)** — the noun of the title "LORD of hosts", and of Job 7:1's "hard service" for man on earth. **The word occurs twice in this chapter and both times means something other than the title.**
  - v.2: Jerusalem's term of service is "full".
  - v.26: the stars' "host" is led out by number.
  - The title יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, counted where the two words stand together, occurs in **47 verses of chs 1–39 and 6 of chs 40–66** (WLC; the fuller forms with אֲדֹנָי or אֱלֹהֵי are not included in that count). It does not occur in chapter 40.
  - *Pattern-break, stated with its pattern:* the book's dominant divine title in 1–39 is absent from the chapter that opens 40–66, while its noun is reassigned to Jerusalem's sentence and the stars. `[T]` for the distribution; `[I]` for the significance. *Moderate — never higher, per the absence rule.*
- **נִרְצָה ("is paid off, accepted", v.2)** — see Headline 1 and Tool 11.
- **כִּפְלַיִם ("double", v.2)** — three occurrences in the Hebrew Bible: Isa 40:2; Job 11:6; Job 41:5 (WLC). In Job 11:6 wisdom is "double" (כִּפְלַיִם לְתוּשִׁיָּה, "two-sided" or "manifold"); in Job 41:5 it is the "double" of the bridle — a folded or paired thing. **The word's two other uses favour "twofold, matching, folded over" over "punishment beyond the deserved".** *Moderate confidence; see Difficult Verses.*
- **חֶסֶד ("lovingkindness, loyalty", v.6)** — "all its חֶסֶד is like the flower of the field". NASB95 "loveliness", ESV "beauty" and NIV84 "glory" all make it an aesthetic word. **In the Hebrew it is the covenant word, attributed for once to "all flesh" and declared to fade.**
  - BHS's apparatus proposes emending it to הֲדָרוֹ ("its splendour") or חֶמְדּוֹ ("its desirability"), and notes that the Greek, Syriac and Vulgate and 1 Pet 1:24 read δόξα ("glory"). `[T — BHS apparatus]`
  - The Ps 103 parallel gives a text-internal reason to keep חַסְדּוֹ: Ps 103:15–17 sets man-as-grass against the LORD's חֶסֶד that lasts for ever. **Isa 40:6 transfers חֶסֶד from God to flesh, and puts the word where Ps 103 had God's חֶסֶד.** The "error" the emendations remove may be the point. `[I]` *Moderate confidence — but it is an argument from the canon, not from a manuscript.*
- **מְבַשֶּׂרֶת ("bearer of good news", feminine, v.9 ×2)** — the participle stands in Isaiah at 40:9; 41:27; 52:7; 60:6; 61:1 (WLC). Here it is feminine, in apposition to Zion and Jerusalem (NASB95: "O Zion, bearer of good news"). **The city that needed comforting becomes the herald.** The LXX makes the herald masculine and someone other than Zion (ὁ εὐαγγελιζόμενος Σιων, "you who bring good news *to* Zion"). The NIV84 follows that construal in its text and gives the Hebrew one in a footnote. `[T]`
- **תֹּהוּ ("emptiness", vv.17, 23)** — the word of Gen 1:2's תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ ("formless and void"). Nations (v.17) and rulers (v.23) are reckoned תֹהוּ before him. **Creation's starting-point is where the empires end.** 45:18 will say God did not create the earth תֹהוּ. `[T]` for the word; `[I]` for the Genesis echo. *Moderate–high.*
- **גִּזְעָם ("their stock", v.24)** — גֶּזַע occurs in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: **Isa 11:1** ("a shoot from the stock of Jesse"), **Isa 40:24**, and **Job 14:8** ("though its stock dies in the dust") (WLC). See Tool 11, Move 4. `[T]`
- **קָדוֹשׁ ("Holy One", v.25)** — the bare title, without "of Israel" and without the article. The book's title קְדוֹשׁ יִשְׂרָאֵל ("Holy One of Israel") is not in chapter 40; the 25-verse distribution in the overview (12 in 1–39, 13 in 40–66) begins again at 41:14. `[S: overview — consistent with the lemma list]`
- **אֱלֹהֵי עוֹלָם ("God of eternity", v.28)** — **the only occurrence of this title in the Hebrew Bible** (WLC, plain phrase pass). The skeletal pass returns three more verses. Each was read before counting, under the amended gate rule:
  - **Gen 21:33** has אֵל עוֹלָם ("El Olam"), Abraham's name for God at Beersheba. That is a *cognate title*, named as such and not counted.
  - **Jer 49:34, 36** have אֶל־עֵילָם ("concerning Elam"). Unrelated words collapsed onto the pattern. Dropped.
  - The Gen 21:33 cognate is worth one line: the God who outlasts the exile bears a title akin to the one Abraham gave him in the land as a resident alien. `[I]` *Moderate.*

**Translation-tradition split (40:3).** The NASB95, ESV and NIV84 agree with one another and with the Hebrew parallelism: "*in the wilderness* clear the way". The LXX (φωνὴ βοῶντος ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ, "a voice of one crying in the wilderness"), the KJV, and all four Gospels attach the wilderness to the voice.

**Classification under the Three-Way Triage.** This is **category 3, not category 2**: the consonants are identical, and the split is a question of construal. The Greek construal is the NT's own text, and stands. Neither reading is an error. The Hebrew places the road in the wilderness. The Gospels find the voice there too, and John the Baptist is in fact in the wilderness. **A preacher can say both without choosing.** *High confidence.*

**The accents — a correction to the overview and the sweep (gate e4).** In Leningrad, קוֹרֵא carries a *zaqef qaton* and בַּמִּדְבָּר a *zaqef gadol*. Both are disjunctive accents. So the accents separate "calling" from "in the wilderness". **They do not, by themselves, attach "in the wilderness" to "clear"**: they set it apart on both sides. The parallelism decides the reading; the accents only fail to support the Greek one. Accentuation is a reading tradition, not authorial, and is tagged `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.

**Ancient versions — other Greek divergences.** Each is classified first.

- **v.5** — LXX adds τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ θεοῦ ("the salvation of God") as the object of "see"; the Hebrew has no object. *Category 3*: Luke 3:6 quotes the Greek (SBLGNT). See Tool 11.
- **v.11** — LXX renders עָלוֹת יְנַהֵל ("he leads the nursing ewes") as ἐν γαστρὶ ἐχούσας **παρακαλέσει** ("he will *comfort* those with young"), and lacks "carry them in his bosom". **The Greek closes vv.1–11 with the verb it opened them with (παρακαλεῖτε, v.1), making an inclusio the Hebrew does not have.** *Category 1 in reverse:* a feature the Greek creates, not one it loses. It is a fact about the Greek reading tradition. `[T — Rahlfs and Swete agree]`
- **v.13** — רוּחַ יְהוָה ("the Spirit of the LORD") → νοῦν κυρίου ("the mind of the Lord"). *Category 3*: Rom 11:34 and 1 Cor 2:16 quote the Greek. See Tool 11.
- **vv.28–31** — the Greek renders יָעֵף ("to be weary") as πεινάω ("to hunger") throughout, and v.29 is loose ("to those not grieving, grief"). *Category 1/translational*; nothing in the NT rests on it.

**Pulpit divergence note:** N/A — no pulpit text declared. For a later sermon: the ESV (at the preacher's own church) differs from the NASB95 at v.6 ("And I said") and keeps "wait for" at v.31. The NIV84 differs at vv.2, 6, 9, 13 and 31, as tabled above.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Four registers, in sequence:**

1. **Heavenly urgency** (vv.1–5) — imperatives piled up and plural, voices offstage, a road being bulldozed through a desert;
2. **Elegy** (vv.6–8) — the flat, repeated "the grass withers, the flower fades", whose second statement is shorter than the first, as if the line itself were fading;
3. **Proclamation** (vv.9–11) — "lift up… lift it up… do not fear… behold… behold… behold";
4. **Interrogation** (vv.12–31) — question after question, satire in the middle (vv.19–20, the poor man shopping for wood that "will not rot" and a craftsman to make a god "that will not totter"), and the last paragraph tender.

**The satire is placed inside the cosmology.** The idol is introduced between the God who weighs mountains (v.12) and the God who sits above the circle of the earth (v.22). The idol is fixed so that it will not move; God's whole activity in the paragraph is motion — stretching, bringing out, blowing, calling. `[T]`

**Soundtrack:** a herald's trumpet over a ground bass of grief, then a long, rising cross-examination, and at the end a single voice speaking quietly to someone exhausted. Don't preach vv.6–8 cheerfully. The grief there is real, and the comfort is that the word outlasts it, not that it was never there.

**Leviticus 26:40–45 → Isa 40:2** *(high confidence — rare verbal collocation, from a live source)* `[T]` for the datum; `[I]` for the intention

*Move 1 — Source context.* Leviticus 26 closes the Holiness Code with blessings and curses. Its last curse is exile: scattering among the nations, and the land left desolate "to enjoy its sabbaths" (26:34–35, 43). Then comes the turn: "if they confess their iniquity… and their uncircumcised heart is humbled, and they then make amends for their iniquity (יִרְצוּ אֶת־עֲוֺנָם)… then I will remember my covenant with Jacob" (26:40–42). And then, unconditionally, God will not reject them, "for I am the LORD their God" (26:44–45). **The verse is the hinge between the last curse and the remembered covenant.**

*The verified collocation.* רָצָה with עָוֹן in one verse: Lev 7:18; Lev 26:41; Lev 26:43; Isa 40:2; Jer 14:10; Hos 8:13 (WLC). Reading each:

- **Lev 7:18; Jer 14:10; Hos 8:13** are the "accept" sense: the offering, or the people, are *not* accepted and the iniquity is remembered.
- **Only Lev 26:41, 43 and Isa 40:2 make the iniquity itself the thing paid off.**

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Leviticus is a live source: the overview lists 53:10 (אָשָׁם, "guilt offering") and 61:1 (דְּרוֹר, "release"). **This adds a third Levitical touchpoint, the earliest of the three, and it is the one that sets the others in order.** The sequence runs:

1. 40:2 — the exile's term is served (Lev 26);
2. 53:10 — the guilt-offering is a person (Lev 5–7);
3. 61:1 — release is proclaimed (Lev 25).

**Isaiah 40–61 follows Leviticus backwards, from the curse of ch. 26 to the Jubilee of ch. 25.** `[I]` *Synthetic claim — moderate confidence until audited; its three constituents are each verified.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Lev 26:34–35 and 43 (the land's sabbaths) are cited at 2 Chr 36:21 to explain the length of the exile. Jeremiah's seventy years stand in the same verse. `[S — the 2 Chr 36:21 citation is from knowledge, not checked in this run]` If so, Lev 26 is already the canon's own key to the exile's term. Isaiah 40:2 declares the term complete, in Leviticus's words.

*What it adds.* "Comfort" has a legal basis that the hearer can test against the Torah. The exiles are not told to feel better; they are told the sentence is served, in the terms the covenant set for its end.

### 12. Genre

**Prophetic poetry.** Two sub-forms:

- **vv.1–11 — a commissioning scene in a divine council.** Plural imperatives to unnamed agents, voices without bodies. Compare 6:1–8, where Isaiah overhears the council and is sent. `[T]` for the form; `[I]` for the 6:8 comparison.
- **vv.12–31 — disputation.** Rhetorical questions, a quoted complaint (v.27), and a refutation.

**Reading rules.**

- The imagery is figurative. Mountains are not literally levelled; "the circle of the earth" is not a cosmological diagram (see Pitfalls).
- The questions are rhetorical, but they are not decorative. The genre's force is that the hearer is made to answer them.
- The council voices are not identified, and **the passage does not identify them**. Do not settle the speakers more precisely than the text does.

### 13. Copycat

**N/A for narrative characters** — there is no narrative. The chapter has one human utterance that it **rebukes** (v.27) and one human posture it **commends** (קוֹיֵ יְהוָה, "those who wait for the LORD", v.31). The posture is prescriptive, but as **reception, not achievement**: v.29 says God "gives" strength, and the waiters "exchange" (יַחֲלִיפוּ) theirs for it. `[T]` The herald's commission (v.9) is directed to Zion; its application to Christian proclamation comes through 1 Pet 1:25 and 52:7 → Rom 10:15, not by direct copying.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Canonical position (Phase 0.55).**

- *Section:* Nevi'im, Latter Prophets; Isaiah stands first, before Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
- *Reading implication:* the canonical reader meets this comfort having read the whole of Kings, including 2 Kgs 25, the fall of Jerusalem. **They know the sentence of 39:6 has been carried out, though Isaiah never narrates it.**
- *Divergences between canonical and compositional order* in this chapter's partners:
  - Lamentations, Ps 103 and Ps 147 all stand later in the Tanak (Ketuvim) than Isaiah.
  - Lamentations is widely dated after 586 `[S]`.
  - **Where Lam 2:13's "what can I liken to you, that I may comfort you?" and Isa 40's "to whom will you liken me?" are related, the direction is open.**

**Where the passage sits.** In the narrative world of the book, after 39:6–7's sentence. It addresses a people for whom the exile is present and its term complete (v.2). `[T]` Whether the chapter was composed in the eighth century or the sixth is a historical question the text does not settle. `[I]/[S]` — not tagged `[T]` either way.

**Where the reader sits.** After the coming the chapter announces. The four Gospels identify the voice as John the Baptist and the coming one as Jesus. Luke 2:25 makes Simeon's "waiting for the consolation of Israel" the posture of 40:31. **The Christian reader is in the waiters' position again**: the glory has been revealed in one sense (John 1:14) and is still to be seen by "all flesh" (Rev 1:7) `[I]`.

**How that reframes the reading.** The chapter's road leads not only to Jerusalem's walls but to the one whose way John prepared. Its "word that stands for ever" is, for 1 Peter, "the word preached as good news to you". The comfort of v.2 is now grounded more deeply than Leviticus 26 could ground it — in the guilt-offering of 53:10. `[I]`

### 15. Who Am I?

| Party | Function |
|---|---|
| **The unnamed heralds of vv.1–2** | God's agents. Not "me" by default. Their commission is the prophetic word's, and its NT successor is the apostolic proclamation (1 Pet 1:25) |
| **The voices (vv.3, 6)** | The Gospels identify the first with John the Baptist. Not a model for the preacher's personality, but a pattern for his message: "prepare the way" for someone else |
| **"I/he" of v.6** | The one who asks "What shall I call out?" — a herald who has to be given his message. The *only* place in the chapter where a human says what he needs |
| **Zion / Jerusalem, herald (v.9)** | The comforted city turned proclaimer — **recipient of the message before she is bearer of it** |
| **Jacob / Israel (v.27)** | **Us, as warning and as recipient.** The complaint is recognisable; the chapter rebukes it and answers it. This is the identification the passage invites |
| **The youths of v.30** | Representatives of human strength at its best. Even they stumble |
| **"Those who wait" (v.31)** | Us, as recipients of a promise — not as athletes of faith |
| **The idol-maker (vv.19–20)** | A foil; a warning |
| **God as warrior-shepherd (vv.10–11)** | Not me. Christ, in the NT reading (John 10:11; Rev 22:12) |

**The commonest misreading** is to make the preacher the "comforter" and the congregation the "people", so that the sermon becomes the preacher's encouragement. The comfort is God's decree, delivered by heralds who did not write it.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** That a judged and exhausted people should **stop saying "my way is hidden from the LORD"** (v.27) and **wait for him** (v.31). The basis is threefold: their sentence is served (v.2), his word outlasts every human thing (v.8), and he is beyond all comparison in power and understanding (vv.12–28).

**Stage 2.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | God's word outlasts every human loyalty, including mine, and every power that threatens me — "all flesh" includes empires (v.24) |
| **Stop** | Complaining that God has lost track of my case (v.27). Building comfort on my own faithfulness, which is grass (v.6). Shopping for securities that "will not totter" (v.20) |
| **Start** | Hearing the comfort as a verdict before treating it as a feeling (v.2). Lifting my eyes from my case to the One who names the stars (v.26). **Waiting** — the active, hopeful expectancy of 8:17, not a resigned pause |
| **Motivation** | Not "try harder to soar". **He gives** strength to the one who has none (v.29). The waiters *exchange* their strength for his (v.31, יַחֲלִיפוּ) |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me:** Do I preach comfort I have not received? Am I the youth of v.30, running on my own strength until I stumble?
- **For a Christian friend:** the one who says "God has forgotten my situation" — hand them v.26 before v.31.
- **For the church:** corporately, we are Jacob. The comfort is addressed to "my people" (plural heralds, collective addressee). We are also Zion-as-herald (v.9): comforted in order to say "here is your God".
- **For an unbeliever:** the chapter's claim is that every human glory is grass, including the ones they trust. The comfort is offered to those whose term is served — and for the NT, to those for whom Christ has served it.

**Prayer.**

- Thanks that our iniquity has been paid for, not merely overlooked.
- Sorry for every "my way is hidden from you".
- Help me wait — and give strength where I have none.
- Mercy on those who think the empires of this age will not wither.

**Grammatical and syntactical features**

- **v.1 — the imperfect יֹאמַר, not the perfect אָמַר.** "Your God *says*" (or "keeps saying") — the speech is ongoing, not the stock prophetic perfect "thus said". The same form returns at v.25. *Moderate confidence on the nuance; high on the form.*
- **v.2 — three כִּי clauses with three different verbs and three subjects:** her term (מָלְאָה, "is full"), her iniquity (נִרְצָה, "is paid off"), she herself (לָקְחָה, "has received"). The perfects present the facts as accomplished. *High.*
- **vv.22–23 — participles of continuous divine activity:** הַיֹּשֵׁב ("who sits"), הַנּוֹטֶה ("who stretches out"), הַנּוֹתֵן ("who gives") — and in v.26 הַמּוֹצִיא ("who brings out"), and v.29 נֹתֵן ("who gives") to the weary. **The same participle of נָתַן reduces rulers to nothing (v.23) and gives strength to the weary (v.29).** `[T]` *High.*

### Textual Variants

*(Category 2 only, per the triage.)*

**40:6 — "he said" or "I said"**

- **The issue:** MT וְאָמַר ("and he said") against 1QIsaᵃ ואומרה (= וָאֹמְרָה, "and I said") and the LXX καὶ εἶπα ("and I said"). BHS's apparatus reads: "𝔔ᵃ ואומרה 𝔊(𝔙) καὶ εἶπα, l וָאֹמַר" — that is, *read* "and I said". `[T — BHS apparatus as exported]`
- **Evidence:** the oldest Hebrew witness and the Greek agree against MT. The difference between the two Hebrew readings is one vowel and (in 1QIsaᵃ) a final *he*.
- **Most likely original:** weighing the external evidence and the first-person question "What shall I call out?" in the same verse, **the first person is likelier**. MT's third person can be read as an unnamed respondent, but it is the harder reading syntactically rather than the better one. *Moderate confidence.*
- **Preaching implication:** the ESV and NIV84 follow the variant ("And I said"); the NASB95 follows MT. If "I said" stands, **this is the only place in chs 40–66 where the prophet's own call-question is heard** — a herald who asks for his message, like 6:8's "Here am I, send me". The preacher can mention it in passing. No doctrine rests on it.

**40:7 — absent from the Greek**

- **The issue:** the whole of the Hebrew 40:7 — "the grass withers, the flower fades, when the breath of the LORD blows upon it; surely the people are grass" — is **not in the LXX** (Rahlfs and Swete agree). The Greek 40:7 corresponds to Hebrew 40:8a. BHS: "v 7 > 𝔊 || b–b prb add" — the apparatus also judges "surely the people are grass" a probable addition. `[T — BHS apparatus]`
- **Evidence:** lines 7a and 8a are identical (יָבֵשׁ חָצִיר נָבֵל צִיץ). **An eye skipping from the first to the second would lose v.7 entirely**, which explains the shorter Greek as an accident without requiring a shorter *Vorlage*. 1QIsaᵃ's reading at this point was **not checked in this run** — BHS cites no Qumran evidence for the verse. Some accounts report a correction there `[S — unverified]`.
- **Most likely original:** the longer Hebrew, with the shorter Greek explained by homoioteleuton. *Moderate confidence.* BHS's suspicion of אָכֵן חָצִיר הָעָם is a separate judgement and is **not** adopted here. The clause applies the image to "the people" — the only time the chapter says it of Israel rather than "all flesh" — and it fits the חַסְדּוֹ reading above.
- **Preaching implication:** 1 Pet 1:24 quotes the shorter Greek (category 3, and it stands). The Hebrew's v.7 adds that **it is God's own breath that withers the grass** — the comfort's God is also the judgement's. Preach v.7 from the Hebrew; there is no need to raise the variant.

**40:2 — ἱερεῖς ("priests") in the Greek**

- The LXX names the addressees of v.2 as priests; the Hebrew does not. *Category 2*, of small weight, probably interpretive rather than a different *Vorlage*. *Moderate–uncertain.* No preaching implication beyond declining to name the heralds.

**40:20 — הַמְסֻכָּן תְּרוּמָה ("he who is too impoverished for an offering")**

- BHS marks הַמְסֻכָּן as absent from the Greek and Syriac, and notes the proposal that it is a kind of wood. **That would make the line "mulberry/sissoo-wood as an offering — a tree that will not rot he chooses".** `[T — BHS apparatus]`; `[S]` for the Akkadian wood-name behind the proposal, from knowledge, not checked.
- The NASB95, ESV and NIV84 all take the "poor man" reading. *Uncertain — consider verifying.* Nothing in the chapter's argument changes either way.

**Other apparatus notes, of no preaching weight:** 40:12, 1QIsaᵃ "waters of the sea"; 40:26, "and strong in power". *(Recorded so the preacher knows they were seen.)*

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting**

- **Time period:** the narrative horizon is set by 39:6–7 — Babylon has taken Jerusalem's treasures and sons. The address presupposes exile as present and its term complete (v.2). `[T]` The date of composition is not stated. `[S]`
- **Location:** the heralds speak *to* Jerusalem (vv.2, 9) and to the cities of Judah, from somewhere outside them. The road runs through the wilderness and desert (ʿărābâ) — the terrain between Babylon and Judah, or the Judean wilderness itself. The text does not say which. `[I]`
- **Political context:** the Babylonian empire; Cyrus is not yet named (he comes at 44:28). `[T]`

**What the original audience knew**

- **Processional roads.** Babylon's great processional way carried the images of its gods into the city at festivals. A highway built "for our God" across the desert answers the gods who were carried along roads. `[S — from knowledge; moderate]`
- **Idol manufacture.** Mesopotamian cult images were made by craftsmen, plated in gold and fixed in place. Rituals then "opened the mouth" of the image so that it could be treated as living. `[S — moderate]` **Verses 19–20 describe the manufacture and stop before the ritual**, leaving the god at the stage where it is visibly a product.
- **Astral religion.** The stars were the gods of the Babylonian pantheon and objects of divination. "Lift up your eyes… who created these?" (v.26) **demotes the gods to an army that answers a roll-call.** `[S — high]`
- **Lebanon** was the source of the best timber in the Levant, for the temples of every power that could reach it. Even all of it is too little for a fitting sacrifice (v.16). `[T]` for the verse; `[S]` for the timber trade.
- **Exile as covenant curse.** A Torah-literate audience knew Lev 26 and Deut 28–30 and their promises of a turn after exile. `[I]`

**What this changes about how we read**

- vv.18–26 are not abstract monotheism. They are a **polemic aimed at the religion of the empire** that held the exiles, its images and its stars.
- "Her warfare is ended" is not comfort for a general weariness. It is the end of a **covenant sentence** the audience could look up.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience**

- **Canonical section:** Nevi'im (Latter Prophets).
- **What the canonical reader knows:** the Torah, including Lev 26's curse and its promised turn. Joshua–Kings, ending with Jerusalem burned (2 Kgs 25). Isaiah 1–39, ending with the sentence of 39:6–7.
- **Canonical register:** the reader has Deuteronomy 32 and Exodus 15 in the ear (the shepherd's נָהַל; the eagle) and the Prophets' own conversation (Mal 3:1 will reuse 40:3).

**The first hearers**

- **Who they were:** exiles, or the descendants of those taken, addressed as "my people", "Jerusalem", "Jacob".
- **Their situation:** their city burned, their king's line diminished, their gods (as the Babylonians saw it) defeated by Marduk. `[S]`
- **What they brought:** the conviction that God had either lost the contest or stopped attending — and v.27 quotes it.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances**

- **Shocking:** "all flesh is grass" — including Israel ("surely the people are grass", v.7) — **in a chapter of comfort.** The comfort does not flatter.
- **Surprising:** the city in need of comfort is made the herald (v.9).
- **Comforting:** the term is served (v.2). God carries the lambs (v.11). Not one star is missing (v.26) — so not one exile is lost.
- **Disturbing:** "Why do you say…?" (v.27). The God who comforts also rebukes the complaint.

**What we bring that they didn't**

- A therapeutic reading of "comfort" as emotional reassurance, where they would hear a verdict and a change of status.
- An individualist reading of v.31 as personal resilience, where they would hear a people's return.
- A scientific frame for v.22's "circle", where they heard a throne above the horizon.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** direct prophecy (the forerunner and the coming of the LORD, vv.3–5, as the NT reads it); trajectory (shepherd-king; the word); contrast (flesh that withers, the Word that stands and became flesh).

**How the passage points to Christ**

- **The coming LORD.** The Synoptics read "the way of the LORD… the paths *of our God*" as "his paths" — the paths of the one John precedes. **This is the NT identifying Jesus with the LORD who comes in person.** Not a type, and not a resemblance. `[T — SBLGNT]` *Four tests (category, NT precedent, escalation, authorial pattern): the identification is not typology but fulfilment of a coming the text announces; NT precedent is explicit in all four Gospels.*
- **The glory revealed to all flesh.** John 1:14 — "we beheld his glory" — and Luke's σωτήριον inclusio (Luke 2:30; 3:6; Acts 28:28) present Jesus as the salvation 40:5 LXX promised all flesh would see.
- **The shepherd's arm.** John 10:11. The Shepherd who gathers lambs in his arm (40:11) is the arm revealed in 53:1 — the Servant — and the arm of 53:1 is a man crushed for others. **The tender arm and the crushed Servant are one.** `[I]` built on the verified 40:5–10 → 53:1 link.
- **The word that stands.** 1 Peter identifies it with the gospel preached. John 1:1, 14 adds that the Word became flesh. **The Word that "stands for ever" took the nature that "is grass"** — the chapter's antithesis is crossed in the incarnation. `[I]` *Canonical reflection — legitimate, not authorial intent in Isa 40.*
- **The comfort of Israel.** Simeon, waiting (προσδεχόμενος) for "the consolation of Israel", takes the child in his arms (Luke 2:25–30). The posture of 40:31 and the comfort of 40:1 meet in one man holding one baby.

**Trajectory**

- **What the passage anticipates:** God's personal return to Zion; his glory seen by all flesh; strength given to the exhausted.
- **What Christ fulfils:** he is the coming one whose way is prepared; the arm revealed; the shepherd; the word preached.
- **What remains:** "all flesh shall see it together" awaits the consummation (Rev 1:7). The waiting of v.31 continues for the church.

**Moralism check**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** be like the eagle; be resilient; "wait" as a technique of spiritual endurance.
- **The gospel grounding:** v.2's iniquity paid off — and, in the canon, paid by the Servant (53:10–12). v.29's strength is given.
- **Christ as hero:** he is the one who comes with might and carries the lambs. The waiters are carried before they soar.

**Confidence:** *high* on the Gospels' identification of the coming LORD; *high* on 1 Peter's identification of the word; *moderate–high* on the Lukan σωτήριον inclusio; *moderate* on the arm-chain as authorial design.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**40:2 — "double for all her sins"**

- **Category:** doctrinal and pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** does God punish beyond what is deserved?
- **Common mishandling:** "God has paid you back twice over in blessing" (no textual basis), or "you suffered double what you deserved" (unfair God).
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the third of three grounds for comfort. Whatever it means, **it must function as consolation, not complaint.**
- **Main options:**
  1. **Double punishment** — the full measure, emphatically served (cf. Jer 16:18's מִשְׁנֵה עֲוֺנָם, a different word);
  2. **The matching equivalent** — what folds over the debt, exactly covering it;
  3. **A double portion of blessing** in exchange (cf. 61:7's מִשְׁנֶה) — which the clause's syntax, "for all her sins", makes harder.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** כִּפְלַיִם is too rare for certainty (three occurrences). Its two uses in Job mean "twofold, folded". The rhetorical function favours **"fully, completely"** — the sentence is served in full — over a claim of excess. *Moderate confidence.*
- **Suggested handling:** acknowledge briefly. Do not preach divine excess, and do not invent a double blessing.

**40:22 — "the circle of the earth"**

- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** sometimes cited as proof that the Bible teaches a spherical earth; sometimes mocked as a flat-disc cosmology.
- **What the text surfaces:** חוּג ("circle, vault, horizon") occurs at Isa 40:22; Job 22:14 ("he walks on the circle of heaven"); Prov 8:27 ("when he drew a circle on the face of the deep"). It is the circle of the horizon or the vault — a description from the viewer's position. **It makes no claim about cosmology either way.**
- **Suggested handling:** do not use it as a science proof-text. Preach it as the throne's height above the grasshoppers.

**40:31 — "they will mount up with wings like eagles"**

- **Category:** commonly misused (personal resilience, sporting or business motivation, the poster on the office wall).
- **Handling:** see Preaching Pitfalls.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: "They shall mount up with wings like eagles" as a resilience text

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on spiritual stamina, or a motivational line on a poster — "believe in yourself, rise above it".
- **Why it's wrong:** the subject of v.31 is "those who wait for the LORD", and the verb is יַחֲלִיפוּ — they *exchange* strength. v.29 says he *gives* it to "him who lacks might" (לְאֵין אוֹנִים). v.30 has just denied the youths any reserve of their own. The waiting is the waiting of 8:17, in a season when God's face is hidden.
- **The corrective:** preach vv.27–31 as one unit, starting with Jacob's complaint. The eagles are a promise to the exhausted, not a challenge to the energetic.

### Pitfall: "Comfort" as emotional reassurance

- **What it looks like:** "God wants you to feel his comfort today." A warm sermon with no reason given.
- **Why it's wrong:** the comfort is three clauses of fact (v.2) in the language of Lev 26 — a sentence served. Comfort here is a change of status that permits a change of feeling, not the feeling itself.
- **The corrective:** preach v.2 before v.1 lands. Then the grounds of the comfort, in the NT, are found in the Servant's guilt-offering (53:10), which is the deeper "paid off".

### Pitfall: Preaching 40:3 either as only John the Baptist or as only the Babylonian return

- **What it looks like:** one sermon makes the chapter a prediction about John with no Isaian road; another dismisses the Gospels' reading because "the Hebrew says the wilderness belongs to the road".
- **Why it's wrong:** the Hebrew places the road in the wilderness; the Gospels read the Greek, which places the voice there, and change "our God" to "his". Both are in the canon, and they do not conflict. The Gospels' identification of the coming LORD is the NT's own reading (category 3).
- **The corrective:** preach the Isaian road first — God coming back with his people — and then let the Gospels say who came.

### Pitfall: "All flesh is grass" as a memento mori only

- **What it looks like:** a funeral sermon on human frailty, ending with "the word of God stands for ever" as general reassurance.
- **Why it's wrong:** the chapter applies the withering to Israel's own חֶסֶד (v.6) and, in v.24, to the rulers of the earth. It is not only a word about death. It is a word about every rival to God's word — including the powers that hold God's people captive.
- **The corrective:** keep v.24 in view. The grass is the empire as well as the mourner.

### Pitfall: "To whom will you liken God?" as abstract philosophy of religion

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on the attributes of God (omnipotence, omniscience), proved from the questions of vv.12–26.
- **Why it's wrong:** the questions are aimed at a specific complaint (v.27) and a specific rival (the manufactured gods of vv.19–20 and the stars of v.26). Their purpose is pastoral, not systematic.
- **The corrective:** arrive at v.27 as the reason vv.12–26 exist.

## Book-Overview Tensions

*Surfaced, not resolved.*

1. **The 40:3 accents — a correction to both the overview and the sweep.** Both say the Masoretic accents settle the Hebrew reading, by a *zaqef qaton* after קוֹרֵא. In Leningrad, בַּמִּדְבָּר also carries a disjunctive (*zaqef gadol*). The accents separate the wilderness from both neighbours; **the parallelism, not the accent, decides the reading.** The Textual Witnesses row for 40:3 should say so, and should carry the e4 tag.

2. **The מְסִלָּה thread — a correction.** The overview's echo-table row for 40:3 lists מְסִלָּה at "11:16; 19:23; 33:8; 40:3; 49:11; 62:10" and pairs 40:3 with 35:8's "Way of Holiness" in the same row. The sweep's banner-and-highway row goes further and puts 35:8 inside the מְסִלָּה sequence.
