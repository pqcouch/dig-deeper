Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools:

  1. The poem’s last line defines wisdom in the words the book used for Job, and settles a question the friends kept asking. 28:28 gives wisdom the terms of Job’s portrait (1:1, 8; 2:3). It also answers Eliphaz, who asked about Job’s “fear” once in each of his three speeches. [T] for the words; [I] for the answer
    • The portrait. “Fearing God and turning away from evil (יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים וְסָר מֵרָע)” (1:1, the narrator; 1:8 and 2:3, the LORD). The poem ends: “the fear (יִרְאַת) of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil (וְסוּר מֵרָע) is understanding” (28:28).
      • In Job, סוּר (“turn aside”) and רַע (“evil”) meet only at these four verses (lemma, WLC). The sweep and the overview found the same. High.
    • The friends’ question. The noun יִרְאָה (“fear”) occurs in Job five times (lemma 3374):
      • “Is not your fear of God your confidence?” (4:6, Eliphaz’s first speech);
      • “he forsakes the fear of the Almighty” (6:14, Job);
      • “you are doing away with the fear of God” (15:4 ESV, Eliphaz’s second);
      • “Is it for your fear of him that he reproves you?” (22:4 ESV, Eliphaz’s third);
      • “the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom” (28:28).
      • Eliphaz raises it in each of his three speeches. The poem settles it, not by saying whether Job has it, but by saying what it is. The narrator and the LORD have already said that he has it. High on the words; moderate on the design — new. v1.1: the one-per-speech series is real but not beyond chance, since a noun of similar frequency falls about once per speech; the tie to 1:1, 8; 2:3 is the strong part [S: audit 4].
    • The Greek keeps both links. Swete renders 28:28 as θεοσέβεια (“godliness”) and τὸ ἀπέχεσθαι ἀπὸ κακῶν (“to keep away from evils”). At 1:1, 1:8 and 2:3 Job is θεοσεβής (“godly”) and ἀπεχόμενος ἀπὸ παντὸς πονηροῦ (or κακοῦ) (“keeping away from every evil”). θεοσεβής occurs in Swete’s Job only at those three verses. In the books of the Hebrew canon the Greek has the noun elsewhere only at Gen 20:11, and the adjective only at Exod 18:21. [T]
    • Proverbs uses the same pair. Outside Job, “fear” (verb, adjective or noun) meets סוּר מֵרָע in one verse only at Prov 3:7 (“Fear the LORD and turn away from evil”), 14:16 (“A wise man is cautious [יָרֵא, ‘fears’] and turns away from evil”) and 16:6 (lemma). (1 Sam 12:20 and Zeph 3:15 match the lemmas in another sense.) Prov 3:7 and 14:16 also put “wise” in the verse. [T] High.
    • Surfaced by: Move 4, Repetition, Vocabulary, Translations (Greek), Tool 11.
  2. The poem answers particular lines in Eliphaz’s offer, Zophar’s challenge and Job’s search, and God’s speech later repeats one of its lines word for word. [T] for the words; [I] for the use. (v1.1: restated; the internal window test is withdrawn as evidence of design [S: audit 4].)
    • The window test, withdrawn. v1.0 argued from a window test: Job 28 shares six rare lemmas (each in 150 verses or fewer, WLC) with each of 22:24–28 (Eliphaz), 26:6–10 (Job) and 38:24–28 (the LORD), against a mean of 1.55 (SD 1.29) across the 878 five-verse windows elsewhere in Job. The numbers reproduce. But when the same test is run for every chapter of Job, 31 of the other 41 have a more prominent top neighbour (Job 28 z = 3.46; Job 26 z = 4.64; Job 38 z = 4.30; claim audit 4, C3, with the main session’s rebuild) [S: audit 4]. The overlap is the vocabulary of the subject, not a design. The relation rests on particular lines.
    • God’s speech. “A course for the thunderbolt (וְדֶרֶךְ לַחֲזִיז קֹלוֹת)” (28:26) is word for word 38:25, and the phrase occurs nowhere else (the sweep’s finding). High. The Old Greek lacked 28:26b (asterisked in Rahlfs), so this is a link in the Hebrew (Textual Variants).
    • Eliphaz’s gold. “Place your gold in the dust, and the gold of Ophir among the stones of the brooks; then the Almighty will be your gold” (22:24–25). אוֹפִיר (“Ophir”) occurs in Job only at 22:24 and 28:16. Eliphaz promised that God would be worth more than gold. The poem says wisdom cannot be bought with the gold of Ophir (28:16). Moderate [S: audit 4].
    • Job’s search for God. “He knows the way I take” (23:10) → “God understands its way, and He knows its place” (28:23). ידע (“know”) + דֶּרֶךְ (“way”) occur together in one verse in Job only at 23:10 and 28:23 (lemma; 47 verses in the Hebrew Bible). Job says God knows his way; the poem says God alone knows wisdom’s way. Moderate [S: audit 4]. (v1.0 also cited ידע + מצא, 23:3 / 28:13, and עִמָּדִי, 23:10 / 28:14; both are too common to bear weight, low.)
    • Job’s hymn. “His mighty thunder, who can understand (יִתְבּוֹנָן)?” (26:14) → “God understands (הֵבִין) its way” (28:23); קָצָה (“end, fringe”) occurs in Job only at 26:14 and 28:24. Low–moderate [S: audit 4]: בין is common in Job (23 tokens), and one shared rare word is near chance. The strong link with Job’s hymn is 26:10 → 28:3 (Headline 4).
    • Zophar’s challenge. Two blind auditors in claim audit 4 found, independently, that Zophar’s 11:6–7 stands behind the poem’s first strophe (Headline 4). Moderate–high [S: audit 4].
    • What it shows. The poem answers Eliphaz’s promise of gold and Zophar’s “secrets of wisdom”, and takes up Job’s own “He knows the way I take”. God’s speech later repeats one of its lines. These are specific echoes; they do not show that the poem was built from these chapters. [I]
    • Surfaced by: Move 4, Positional Necessity, Repetition; tested by claim audit 4.
  3. The ear and the eye. The poem starts a line that ends in Job’s last speech. [T] for the words; [I] for the line
    • A whisper to the ear. “My ear received a whisper (שֵׁמֶץ) of it” (4:12, Eliphaz’s vision). “How faint a word (שֵׁמֶץ) we hear of Him” (26:14). שֵׁמֶץ (“whisper”) occurs only at these two verses (lemma).
    • A rumour to the ear. “Abaddon and Death say, ‘With our ears (בְּאָזְנֵינוּ) we have heard (שָׁמַעְנוּ) a report of it (שִׁמְעָהּ)’” (28:22). Two lines before, wisdom is “hidden from the eyes (מֵעֵינֵי) of all living” (28:21). Two lines after, God “sees (יִרְאֶה) everything under the heavens” (28:24). At 28:27 God “saw it (רָאָהּ)”.
    • The end of the line. “I have heard of You by the hearing of the ear (לְשֵׁמַע־אֹזֶן); but now my eye (עֵינִי) sees You” (42:5).
      • אֹזֶן (“ear”) + שָׁמַע (“hear”) + שֵׁמַע (“report, hearing”) meet in one verse only at Job 28:22, Job 42:5 and Ps 18:45 (lemma). In Job the noun שֵׁמַע occurs only at 28:22 and 42:5.
    • What it shows. Eliphaz claimed a whisper from the spirit world. Job’s hymn admits that we hear only a whisper of God. The poem says even Death has only a rumour of wisdom; only God sees it. Job’s last words move from the ear to the eye. The poem names the gap that only the LORD’s speech closes. Moderate — new. v1.1: hearing-then-seeing is a Job idiom (“Behold, my eye has seen all this, my ear has heard and understood it”, 13:1; “when the ear heard … when the eye saw”, 29:11), and a noun shared by two verses of a 42-chapter book is a modest datum. The words are high; the line is moderate [S: audit 4].

  4. Man the miner does on a small scale what Job’s speeches say God does. Yet he cannot find wisdom. [T] for the pairs; [I] for the design
    • The limit to darkness. “Man puts an end (קֵץ) to darkness, and to the farthest limit (תַּכְלִית) he searches out” (28:3). God “has inscribed a circle on the surface of the waters at the boundary (תַּכְלִית) of light and darkness” (26:10). תַּכְלִית + חֹשֶׁךְ occur together only in these two verses (lemma, WLC).
    • Overturning mountains. “He overturns (הָפַךְ) the mountains at the base” (28:9). God “removes the mountains … when He overturns them (הֲפָכָם) in His anger” (9:5). הפך (“overturn”) + הַר (“mountain”) occur together only in these two verses in the Hebrew Bible (lemma).
    • Darkness brought to light. “What is hidden he brings out to the light (יֹצִא אוֹר)” (28:11). God “brings the deep darkness into light (וַיֹּצֵא לָאוֹר)” (12:22). In Job, יצא (“bring out”) + אוֹר (“light”) occur together only in these two verses (lemma). v1.1: in the whole Hebrew Bible the pair meets in seven verses (also Hos 6:5; Isa 13:10; 51:4; Mic 7:9; Ps 37:6), so it is exclusive only within Job: moderate [S: audit 4]. The Greek translator heard it: ἀνακαλύπτω (“uncover”) with βάθ- (“depth”) occurs only at Dan 2:22, Job 12:22 and 28:11 (Swete) [S: audit 4].
    • Splitting rock for water. “He hews out channels (יְאֹרִים) through the rocks (בַּצּוּרוֹת … בִּקֵּעַ)” (28:10). בקע (“split”) + צוּר (“rock”) occur together only at Job 28:10, Ps 78:15 (“He split the rocks in the wilderness”) and Isa 48:21 (“He split the rock and the water gushed forth”) (lemma). In both of the others the subject is God, giving Israel water in the wilderness. v1.1: the flint line has a closer partner. חַלָּמִישׁ (“flint”) + הפך (“overturn”) occur together only at Job 28:9 and Ps 114:8 (“who turned the rock into a pool of water, the flint into a fountain of water”), found by two blind auditors [S: audit 4].
    • Searching. Man “searches out (חוֹקֵר)” the dark (28:3). The poem’s last act before the coda is God’s: “He established it and also searched it out (חֲקָרָהּ)” (28:27). The verb brackets the poem. Eliphaz had used it of the friends’ research: “We have investigated it (חֲקַרְנוּהָ)” (5:27) — the sweep’s link.
    • Zophar’s limit and hidden things (v1.1). Zophar wished God would show Job “the secrets of wisdom (תַּעֲלֻמוֹת חָכְמָה)” (11:6) and asked, “Can you discover (תִּמְצָא) the depths (חֵקֶר) of God? Can you discover the limits (תַּכְלִית) of the Almighty?” (11:7). תַּעֲלֻמָה (“hidden thing”) occurs only at Job 11:6, 28:11 and Ps 44:22 [Eng 44:21]. 11:7 is the only verse joining חֵקֶר and תַּכְלִית, and 28:3 joins the verb חקר with תַּכְלִית. The miner reaches “every limit” and brings “the hidden thing” to light, yet wisdom is not found (תִּמָּצֵא, 28:12–13). The sweep noted 11:6 → 28:11 and 11:7 → 28:3 separately; claim audit 4’s auditors B and C, blind to each other, read them as one answer to Zophar. Moderate–high [S: audit 4].
    • What it shows. The poem gives man God’s own acts in miniature, from the darkness to the mountains, the hidden things and the water from the rock. It does not do this to despise human skill. It shows how far that skill reaches and where it stops. New as a pattern. v1.1: pairs (i) 26:10, (ii) 9:5 and the rock pair are high; 12:22 is moderate; the design is moderate, because comparable eleven-verse Job passages (in chapters 3, 7, 14 and 24) reach similar counts of exclusive pairs with Job’s hymns, so the cluster is partly the vocabulary of the subject [S: audit 4].

  5. The text has three strophes, a refrain and a run of leading words. It puts no break before the poem. [T]
    • Three strophes. BHS prints a petuḥah after 28:11 and after 28:19, so the strophes are 1–11, 12–19 and 20–28. The second and third open with the refrain: “But where can wisdom be found? And where is the place of understanding?” (28:12) / “Where then does wisdom come from? And where is the place of understanding?” (28:20). They differ in one verb: תִּמָּצֵא (“be found”) / תָּבוֹא (“come”).
    • “Place”. מָקוֹם (“place”) occurs five times: 28:1, 6, 12, 20, 23. The chain starts in chapter 27: the wicked man is swept “out of his place (מִמְּקֹמוֹ)” (27:21) and hissed “from his place (מִמְּקֹמוֹ)” (27:23). Gold has a place (28:1); sapphire has a place (28:6); wisdom’s place is asked twice (28:12, 20); God knows its place (28:23).

    • “Value” (ערך). The noun עֵרֶךְ (“worth”, 28:13) and the verb עָרַךְ (“equal”, 28:17, 19) run through the second strophe. This supports the Hebrew’s “its worth” at 28:13 against the Greek’s “its way” (see Textual Variants).
    • No break before the poem.   7. Who speaks? As the text stands, the poem is inside Job’s discourse but in a voice above the dispute. It is the truth, and it is not yet the resolution. [T] for the markers; [I] for the reading
    • The frame. וַיֹּסֶף אִיּוֹב שְׂאֵת מְשָׁלוֹ (“And Job again took up his discourse”) occurs only at 27:1 and 29:1 (lemma, Hebrew; the Greek formula is identical) [S: audit].
    • The formula needs no second voice. It first follows Job’s own speech (26:1–14), with no one speaking between. So at 29:1 it cannot show that someone other than Job spoke chapter 28. The claim audit read 27–28 and 29–31 as two panels of one closing discourse [S: audit]. This dig agrees. New to the argument: the observation that 27:1 itself follows Job.
    • No break. BHS prints no marker from 27:1 to 28:11 (Headline 5). v1.1: as BHS prints it, the change of speaker at 20:1 (Job → Zophar) also lacks a preceding marker: BHS has none after 19:29, though the WLC transcription has one. So the absence of a marker before 28:1 supports Job’s speaking on but does not decide it (claim audit 4, main-session correction of the auditor’s moderate–high) [S: audit 4].
  • The formula changes (v1.1). Job last “answers” (וַיַּעַן) at 26:1. At 27:1 and 29:1 he “takes up his discourse again”, and “answered” returns for him only after the LORD speaks (40:3; 42:1). The change marks the end of the exchange, not a new speaker [S: audit 4].
    • The voice. The Hebrew poem has no first or second person outside quoted speech: no “I”, no “you”, no address to the friends or to God. Chapter 27 has all of them (27:2–6, 11–12). v1.1: the shift to the third person comes at 27:13, not at 28:1, so it does not separate the poem from what precedes it [S: audit 4]. The Greek of 28:10 reads εἶδέν μου ὁ ὀφθαλμός (“my eye saw every precious thing”) in both Swete and Rahlfs. This is probably עֵינִי (“my eye”) read for עֵינוֹ (“his eye”), but it makes the poem Job’s in the Greek. BHS has no apparatus note at 28:10, so the reading is an inference about the translator, not a recorded variant [S: audit 4]. [T]; [I] for the cause.
    • The coda. 28:28 opens with a narrative verb, וַיֹּאמֶר לָאָדָם (“And to man He said”). It quotes a saying of God addressed to humanity as such (לָאָדָם, “to man”). Elihu reports God’s words to the afflicted and to the snow (36:10; 37:6), but no other line in the dialogue quotes God speaking to mankind. It names God once as אֲדֹנָי (“the Lord”), its only occurrence in Job (as BHS prints it). v1.1: the wayyiqtol continues the creation narrative of 28:25–27 (“when He imparted weight … then He saw it … and He said”), as וָאֹמַר (“and I said”) does at 38:11 after the sea’s bounds are set. It is not a narrator stepping in [S: audit 4].
    • The reading. On the text’s own markers, the poem stands within Job’s discourse. Its register is detached, and Andersen’s view that it is the author’s own coda (TOTC, Job, 14:241; Logos round 2) remains possible [S].
    • For the preacher, the result is the same either way. The poem says what wisdom is in the very terms of Job’s character. Then Job goes on (29–31), still crying for an answer, and God must still speak (38–41). Knowing the right answer about wisdom does not end the suffering of the man who has it. Moderate [S: audit 4].

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

  • Stated purpose. None within the poem; Job states no purpose for the book. [T]
  • What the poem does. It asks one question twice, “Where can wisdom be found?” (28:12, 20). It answers it in three moves:
    • not where man digs (28:1–11);
    • not anywhere in the living world or the deep, and not for any price (28:13–19);
    • God alone knows its way, and he has said what it is for man (28:21–28). [T]
  • The purpose, in the book. The poem stands after the debate has broken down (Headline 2; Context) and before Job’s last defence, Elihu and the LORD. It judges the debate: no party to it has found wisdom by argument. And it prepares for the LORD’s speech: what God knows by ways and places, he will ask Job about by ways and places (28:23 → 38:19, 24). [I]
  • Book-overview note: confirms the overview’s “wisdom poem at the hinge” (movement III). It extends it: the poem is lexically bound to Eliphaz’s last speech (22) and Job’s last hymn (26) as well as to God’s speech (38).

What it links to is 27:13–23, where the wicked man’s silver is heaped like dust and passes to the innocent (27:16–17): “for there is a mine for silver” (28:1). [T] for the connective and the shared words; [I] for the link.
  • וְ (“But”, 28:12, 20). The refrains open with וְ, rendered “But” (NASB95, ESV at 28:12) and “then” (28:20). The turn is from the places that metals have to the place wisdom does not have. [T]
  • כִּי (“For”, 28:24). God knows wisdom’s place because he sees everything. [T]
  • אָז (“Then”, 28:27). “Then He saw it” — at the time of creation (28:25–26 “when He imparted weight … when He set a limit”). [T]
  • וַיֹּאמֶר (“And He said”, 28:28). A narrative verb, the only wayyiqtol of אמר (“say”) in the poem. The poem has described; now it quotes. [T] As at 38:11, the wayyiqtol follows the setting of bounds at creation [S: audit 4].
  • הֵן (“Behold”, 28:28). The same particle opens Job’s 26:14 (“Behold, these are the fringes of His ways”). The BHS apparatus notes that one manuscript and the Syriac lack it here. [T]

5. Parallels

  • 28:12 / 28:20. Identical except תִּמָּצֵא (“be found”) / תָּבוֹא (“come”). Synonymous, with a shift from human finding to wisdom’s origin. [T]
  • 28:14. “The deep says, ‘It is not in me’; and the sea says, ‘It is not with me.’” Personified synonymy. עִמָּדִי (“with me”) is the word Job used at 23:10 (“the way that is with me”). [T]
  • 28:15–19. A list in parallel lines, each a negative. The second lines (15b–19b) are asterisked in the Rahlfs export: the Old Greek had only the first line of each verse (see Ancient Versions). [T]
  • 28:22. Abaddon ‖ Death. Elsewhere Abaddon is paired with Sheol (Job 26:6; Prov 15:11) or the grave (Ps 88:12 [Eng 88:11]). [T]
  • 28:28. Synonymous: “the fear of the Lord” = “wisdom”; “to depart from evil” = “understanding”. חָכְמָה / בִּינָה (“wisdom / understanding”) is the poem’s pair (28:12, 20, 28). The coda’s two lines are the refrain’s two nouns, answered. [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

  • None formally. The narrator speaks only in the frame (1:1–3:1; 32:1–5; 38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1, 7–17) and in the speech headings. [T]
  • But the poem’s voice is close to the narrator’s.
    • It has no first or second person (Headline 7), though the change comes at 27:13, not 28:1 [S: audit 4].
    • Its coda uses the narrator’s terms for Job (1:1).
    • It opens 28:28 with a narrative verb, though that verb continues the creation narrative of 28:25–27 (as at 38:11) [S: audit 4].
    • Whether this makes the poem the narrator’s, or Job speaking in a voice the narrator shares, is the contested judgement. The text does not mark a change of speaker. [T] / [I]

7. Vocabulary

  • חָכְמָה (“wisdom”). 18 verses in Job (lemma 2451). Four are here (28:12, 18, 20, 28). The rest belong to the parties:
    • Eliphaz: 4:21; 15:8 (does Job “limit wisdom to himself”?);
    • Zophar: 11:6 (“the secrets of wisdom”);
    • Job: 12:2 (“with you wisdom will die”), 12:12–13 (“with God are wisdom and might”), 13:5 (“silence … would be your wisdom”), 26:3;
    • Elihu: 32:7, 13; 33:33 (“I will teach you wisdom”);
    • the LORD: 38:36–37; 39:17. [T]
    • Observation. After the poem, Elihu claims to teach wisdom (33:33). The LORD speaks of wisdom only as his to give or withhold (38:36; 39:17). [I]
  • בִּינָה (“understanding”). 9 verses in Job (lemma 998): 20:3; 28:12, 20, 28; 34:16; 38:4, 36; 39:17, 26. The LORD’s first demand uses it: “Tell Me, if you have understanding (בִינָה)” (38:4). [T]

  • מָקוֹם (“place”). 21 verses in Job; five in this poem (Headline 5). [T]
  • תַּעֲלֻמָה (“the hidden thing”, 28:11). Three times in the Hebrew Bible: Job 11:6 (Zophar’s “secrets of wisdom”); 28:11; Ps 44:22 [Eng 44:21] (lemma 8587) [S: audit] (audit 2 verified the chain). Zophar wished God would show Job the hidden things of wisdom. Man brings hidden things out of rock, but not wisdom. Moderate–high (sweep). [T]

The verb is at 28:21 (“hidden from the eyes of all living”) and 42:3: “Who is this that hides (מַעְלִים) counsel without knowledge?” — Job quoting the LORD’s 38:2 with עלם for חשׁך (lemma 5956: 6:16; 28:21; 42:3). [T] The poem’s hidden wisdom and Job’s confession about hidden counsel use the same verb. Moderate.
  • תַּכְלִית (“limit”, 28:3). Five verses in the Hebrew Bible: Job 11:7; 26:10; 28:3; Neh 3:21; Ps 139:22 (lemma 8503). Zophar: “Can you discover the limits (תַּכְלִית) of the Almighty?” (11:7). [T]

  • בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ (“the proud beasts”, 28:8). שַׁחַץ only here and 41:26 [Eng 41:34], where Leviathan is “king over all the sons of pride” (lemma 7830) [S: overview] confirmed. The proud beasts that never trod the miner’s path return as Leviathan’s subjects. [T]
  • אֲבַדּוֹן (“Abaddon”). Five verses: Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Prov 15:11; Ps 88:12 [Eng 88:11] (lemma 11). In Job’s hymn “Abaddon has no covering” before God (26:6); here Abaddon has only a rumour of wisdom (28:22). Everything lies open to God, but wisdom is not open to Abaddon. [T] / [I]

8. Translations

  • 28:1 כִּי. NASB95 and ESV “Surely”; NIV84 omits it. The connective that ties the poem to chapter 27 is lost in NIV84 (Linking Words). Category 2 (connective lost).
  • 28:3 the subject. The Hebrew verb has no expressed subject until הוּא (“he”) in 3b. NASB95 and ESV supply “Man”; NIV84 “Man”. Swete reads τάξιν ἔθετο σκότει (“he set an order for darkness”), which could be read of God. The ambiguity is in the Hebrew, and it is the poem’s point: the miner does what God does (26:10). Category 3 (interpretive supply). Note to the preacher: the ESV’s “Man” is right, but the echo of God’s act in 26:10 should be heard.
  • 28:4. The verse is the poem’s hardest. NASB95 “He sinks a shaft far from habitation, forgotten by the foot”; ESV “He opens shafts in a valley away from where anyone lives; they are forgotten by travelers”; NIV84 “Far from where people dwell he cuts a shaft, in places forgotten by the foot of man”. BHS proposes נְחָלִים עַם for נַחַל מֵעִם־גָּר. Swete reads 4b differently: “those who forget the righteous way grew weak among mortals” (see Ancient Versions). Category 3.
  • 28:11 the first line. NASB95 “He dams up the streams from flowing”; ESV “He dams up the streams so that they do not trickle”; NIV84 “He searches the sources of the rivers”, with the footnote “Septuagint, Aquila and Vulgate; Hebrew He dams up”. The BHS apparatus compares Ugaritic mbk nhrm (“the sources of the rivers”). NIV84 reads חִפֵּשׂ (“search”) for חִבֵּשׁ (“bind”) and takes מִבְּכִי as “sources”. Category 3. The pulpit text (ESV) follows the Hebrew.
  • 28:13 “its worth”. All three render the Hebrew’s עֶרְכָּהּ (“its worth, value”). Swete has ὁδὸν αὐτῆς (“its way”), and BHS proposes דַּרְכָּהּ (see Textual Variants). Category 1.
  • 28:23. NASB95 “God understands its way”; ESV and NIV84 “the way to it”. The Hebrew is דַּרְכָּהּ (“its way”). The ESV’s “the way to it” makes wisdom a destination; the Hebrew lets it be wisdom’s own way, which God understands. Category 2.
  • 28:27. NIV84 “then he looked at wisdom and appraised it; he confirmed it and tested it” supplies the noun “wisdom” for the pronoun. NASB95 and ESV keep “it”. Category 1.
  • 28:28 “depart from evil”. ESV “to turn away from evil” matches its own 1:1 (“turned away from evil”). NIV84 “to shun evil” matches its own 1:1 (“shunned evil”). NASB95 “depart from evil” does not match its own 1:1 (“turning away from evil”): the study text breaks the link the Hebrew makes. Category 2 — and the pulpit text (ESV) carries the link.
  • The יִרְאָה chain (Headline 1). ESV renders the noun “fear (of God)” at 4:6, 6:14, 15:4, 22:4 and 28:28. NASB95 has “fear of God” (4:6), “fear” (6:14), but “reverence” (15:4; 22:4). NIV84 has “piety” (4:6; 15:4; 22:4) and “fear” (6:14; 28:28). Only the ESV lets the congregation hear Eliphaz’s three questions and the poem’s answer in the same word. Category 2.
  • Pulpit divergence note. The ESV carries both of the poem’s main links (1:1 → 28:28; 4:6, 15:4, 22:4 → 28:28) better than the study text. Two smaller points: at 28:23 “the way to it” should be heard as “its way”; at 28:1 “Surely” is a “for”. No divergence works against the findings.

9. Tone and Feel

  • Calm. No lament, no accusation, no address. After the anguish of 3–27, the register drops. [T]
  • Wonder at human skill. Strophe 1 is admiring, not mocking: man goes where no falcon has looked and no lion has walked (28:7–8). [T] / [I]
  • Accumulation. Strophe 2 piles up eleven negatives and a dozen precious things. The effect is a cumulative shutting of doors. [T]
  • Awe. Strophe 3 moves from death’s rumour to God’s sight, and from the weather to the word. [I]
  • Gravity, not sweetness, at the end. The coda is not a reward. It is a command in the form of a definition, said “to man”. [I]
  • Book-overview note: the overview’s “calm tone differs from everything around it” is confirmed.

Prov 1:7; 9:10; 15:33; Ps 111:10 (with Isa 11:2; 33:6) → 28:28 (high)

  • Move 1, source context. Prov 1:7 heads Proverbs: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and instruction.” Prov 9:10 closes its first section: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding (בִּינָה).” Ps 111:10 ends a hymn to God’s works: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom; a good understanding have all those who do His commandments.” [T]
  • The words. יִרְאָה (“fear”) and חָכְמָה (“wisdom”) meet in one verse in seven places: Isa 11:2; 33:6; Job 28:28; Prov 1:7; 9:10; 15:33; Ps 111:10 (lemma) [S: overview] confirmed. Only Job 28:28 names אֲדֹנָי (as BHS prints it). Only Job 28:28 and Prov 9:10 have בִּינָה as well.
  • The syntax. Proverbs and the Psalm say the fear is the “beginning” (רֵאשִׁית / תְּחִלַּת). Job says it is wisdom: יִרְאַת אֲדֹנָי הִיא חָכְמָה (“the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom”). The only other verse with יִרְאַת + divine name + הִיא + predicate is Isa 33:6: “the fear of the LORD is his treasure (יִרְאַת יְהוָה הִיא אוֹצָרוֹ)” (phrase search; Prov 31:30’s “a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised” is a different construction). In Isaiah the fear is the treasure; in Job 28 it is the wisdom no treasure can buy. [T] for the syntax; [I] for the connection. Low–moderate [S: audit 4] — a syntactic companion, not a source. Deut 4:6 shares the identity form with wisdom + understanding + הִיא, and the treasure motif is closer in Prov 15:16 (fear of the LORD with אוֹצָר, “treasure”, only Isa 33:6 and Prov 15:16) and Prov 2:4–5.
  • Move 2, book usage. Proverbs is live in Job: Prov 8:25 at 15:7; Prov 13:9 and 24:20 at 18:5–6; 21:17 [S: overview; 15–21 dig]. Here Proverbs 1–9 is used at three points (1:7 / 9:10; 2:4–6; 3:13–20 / 8:10–29).
  • Move 3, OT-to-OT. Isa 11:2 puts “the spirit of wisdom and understanding … the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD” on the shoot of Jesse. יִרְאָה, חָכְמָה and בִּינָה meet in one verse only at Isa 11:2, Prov 9:10 and Job 28:28 (lemma). [T]
  • What it adds. Proverbs states the motto as a first principle; Job reaches it at the end of a search. In Tanak order the Psalter has already taught it (Ps 34:12, 15; 111:10) before Job tests it and Proverbs sets it out [S: audit 4]. Among the Proverbs sayings, 14:16 echoes Job 1:1 and 16:6 echoes 28:28, which points to a shared formula rather than to borrowing in either direction [S: audit 4]. [I]

12. Genre

  • A wisdom poem with a refrain, inside a book of disputation. Sub-genre features: the “where is X?” question with a negative catalogue (the deep, the sea, Abaddon, Death); a list of precious things valued against wisdom (as Prov 3:14–15; 8:10–11, 19); a creation clause (28:25–27); a closing divine saying. [T]
  • Not a hymn. It praises no one by name until the last line, and it addresses no one. [T]
  • Not a lament. No complaint, no petition. [T]
  • Genre implication. The poem should be read as a meditation within the drama, not as Job’s prayer and not as a friend’s argument. [I]

13. Copycat

  • What the poem warns against copying. The miner’s confidence that skill and effort reach everything (28:1–11). The trader’s assumption that the precious can be bought (28:15–19). [I]
  • What it commends. The fear of the Lord and turning from evil (28:28) — the character of Job in 1:1. Not “be like Job” as a hero, but “this is what God calls wisdom”, which Job had before his suffering and kept through it (Christological Reading guards the moralism). [I]

14. Bible Timeline

  • Setting. Patriarchal in colour (Uz; wealth in livestock; no temple or law cited) [T]; date of composition not stated [S].
  • Mining in the book’s world. Copper and iron mining and smelting (28:2) are attested across the ancient Near East; the overview gives no date, and none is needed for the poem’s sense [S: recalled].
  • Canon timeline. Read in the Ketuvim after Psalms and before Proverbs. [T]

15. Who Am I?

  • The miner — every human being who reaches, digs, trades and achieves. [I]
  • Abaddon and Death — the limit of every human search: even there, only a rumour. [I]
  • Job — the man who has the fear of God and still suffers, who will go on speaking (29–31). [I]
  • The reader — addressed in the person of “man” (אָדָם) in the coda. [T] / [I]

16. So What?

  • Intended response. Stop looking for wisdom where human skill reaches. Receive God’s word about it: fear the Lord and turn from evil. [I]
  • Worldview. Wisdom is not a resource to be extracted or a commodity to be bought. It belongs to God, who made the world by measure and decree, and it is given to man as a relationship — fear — and a way of life — turning from evil. [I]
  • For a sufferer. The poem does not explain suffering. It says where wisdom is, and that the man who has it may still not have answers. It points the sufferer to God, who sees what is hidden from all living (28:21, 24). [I]
  • Candidate FCF (for point-purpose).
    • “We trust that enough effort, skill or money will get us what we most need — and when life breaks, we search everywhere for the explanation except in God himself.”
    • Alternative: “We want answers before we will trust; God gives a way of trust before he gives answers.”
  • Prayer. For the fear of the Lord; for trust where understanding fails.

Original Language Observations

  • 28:1 מוֹצָא (“source, mine”). Literally “a place of going out” (יצא). The same root closes strophe 1: “what is hidden he brings out (יֹצִא) to the light” (28:11). Silver has a place it comes out of; man brings the hidden out. Wisdom has no such outlet (28:12). The LORD’s speech uses מוֹצָא of the “source” of the grass (38:27, in the window that ranks top three); in Job the noun occurs only at 28:1 and 38:27 (lemma). [T]
  • 28:3 the unexpressed subject. קֵץ שָׂם לַחֹשֶׁךְ (“[he] puts an end to darkness”) has no subject until הוּא in 3b, and the last subject named is “the innocent” of 27:17 or God of 27:13 (if 27:13–23 is read on). The reader supplies “man” from the context of mining. The line also says what God does at 26:10. [T] / [I]
  • 28:4 the hardest line. פָּרַץ נַחַל מֵעִם־גָּר — “he breaks open a shaft (נַחַל, usually ‘wadi, torrent’) away from where people sojourn (גָּר)”. הַנִּשְׁכָּחִים מִנִּי־רָגֶל — “the ones forgotten by the foot”. דַּלּוּ מֵאֱנוֹשׁ נָעוּ — “they dangle, far from man they sway”. The picture is of miners on ropes in shafts where no traveller passes. The nouns are uncertain; the picture is not. [T] for the words; [I] for the picture.
  • 28:11 מִבְּכִי נְהָרוֹת חִבֵּשׁ. “From weeping (בְּכִי) he binds (חִבֵּשׁ) the rivers” — so NASB95 and ESV, “he dams the streams from trickling”. The BHS apparatus compares Ugaritic mbk nhrm, “the sources of the rivers”, reading מַבְּכֵי. The same consonantal root is in 38:16, נִבְכֵי־יָם (“the springs of the sea”). NIV84’s “He searches the sources of the rivers” combines the Ugaritic sense with the versions’ verb (Greek, Aquila, Vulgate: חִפֵּשׂ, “search”). [T]
  • 28:13 עֶרְכָּהּ (“its worth”). From ערך, “set in order, set beside, value”. The verb returns twice in the strophe: לֹא־יַעַרְכֶנָּה (“cannot equal it”, 28:17, 19). Elsewhere in Job עָרַךְ is the lawsuit verb: “I have prepared (עָרַכְתִּי) my case” (13:18); “I would present (אֶעֶרְכָה) my case before Him” (23:4). In 28:17, 19 it is the merchant’s “set beside, compare”. The verb that Job used to set out his case before God is used here for what cannot be set beside wisdom. [T] for the words; [I] for the resonance (low–moderate; the senses differ).
  • 28:15 סְגוֹר. Elsewhere “enclosure” (Hos 13:8, “the enclosure of their heart”); here, as 1 Kgs 6:20 זָהָב סָגוּר, “pure gold” — the BHS apparatus glosses it so. [T]
  • 28:18 וּמֶשֶׁךְ חָכְמָה מִפְּנִינִים. מֶשֶׁךְ is “a drawing out, acquisition” (NASB95 “acquisition”; ESV and NIV84 “price”). The Greek reads ἕλκυσον σοφίαν (“draw wisdom up”), taking it as an imperative of the same root, משׁך (“draw”). [T]
  • 28:22 שִׁמְעָהּ (“a report of it”). The noun שֵׁמַע with suffix; in Job only here and 42:5 (Headline 3). [T]
  • 28:23 הֵבִין דַּרְכָּהּ. The hiphil of בין (“understand, discern”). The few manuscripts that read הֵכִין (“established”) would make the line say what 28:27 says (הֱכִינָהּ). The MT keeps the poem’s Leitwort and answers 26:14. [T]
  • 28:27 the four verbs. רָאָהּ (“He saw it”) · וַיְסַפְּרָהּ (“and declared/counted it”) · הֱכִינָהּ (“He established it”) · וְגַם־חֲקָרָהּ (“and also searched it out”). Each takes the 3fs suffix, wisdom. The order runs from seeing to recounting, establishing and testing. The LORD’s speech uses ספר with חָכְמָה (38:37) and כון of the raven’s provision (38:41). [T]
  • 28:28 וַיֹּאמֶר לָאָדָם. A wayyiqtol of אמר (“and He said”) introduces the quotation. אָדָם (“man, mankind”) occurs in this poem only here. Elsewhere the poem calls man אֱנוֹשׁ (“mortal man”, 28:4, 13). The saying is addressed to mankind as such. [T] The verb continues the creation narrative of 28:25–27, as וָאֹמַר (“and I said”) does at 38:11 [S: audit 4].
  • 28:28 הִיא. “The fear of the Lord — that is wisdom”. The pronoun is the copula of identity, as in Isa 33:6. [T]

Historical and Cultural Background

  • Mining. Shaft mining with ropes, the smelting of copper and the refining of gold (28:1–2, 4) were known across the ancient Near East; copper was mined in the Arabah and Sinai [S: recalled]. The poem’s detail is the knowledge of someone who has seen mines. Nothing in the sense depends on a site or a date.
  • Ophir and Cush. Ophir is the source of Solomon’s gold (1 Kgs 9:28; 10:11) [T]; its location is unknown [S]. Cush (“Ethiopia”, 28:19) is south of Egypt [S]. Both stand for the far ends of trade.
  • The gems. The identifications of several stones (שֹׁהַם “onyx”; פִּטְדָה “topaz”; רָאמוֹת “coral”; גָּבִישׁ “crystal”) are uncertain, as the versions’ variety shows [T] (the English differs: NIV84 “jasper” for גָּבִישׁ, “rubies” for פְּנִינִים).
  • Abaddon. “Destruction”, the realm of the dead (NIV84 renders it so, with “Hebrew Abaddon” in its margin) [T].

Christological Reading

  • Category: trajectory, not prophecy. The poem predicts nothing. It places wisdom with God alone, beyond human reach and price, and says God gives a word about it to man. [T] / [I]

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Ending the series at 28:28

  • What it looks like: “Here is the answer to Job: fear God and turn from evil.” The sermon ends on the motto, and the rest of the book is an appendix.
  • Why it’s wrong: Job is already what 28:28 describes (1:1). The poem tells him nothing he lacks. He goes on speaking (29–31), Elihu speaks, and the LORD must still speak (38–41). The poem defines wisdom; it does not answer Job.
  • Corrective: preach 28 as the book’s true word at the hinge, and as the definition that makes 29–42 necessary. Say that the right answer about wisdom does not end the suffering of the man who has it.

Pitfall: “Be wise like Job” (moralism)

  • What it looks like: “Job feared God and turned from evil; so must you, and you will be blessed.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the poem’s subject is God, who alone knows wisdom’s way and place (28:23–27). The fear of the Lord is not the price of wisdom; the poem has just said that wisdom has no price (28:15–19). And blessing as the reward of piety is the friends’ theology, which the book refutes.
  • Corrective: keep God as the actor. The fear of the Lord is the human response to a God who sees what is hidden and has spoken.

Pitfall: Despising human skill

  • What it looks like: a sermon against science, technology or study, using 28:1–11 as an illustration of human folly.
  • Why it’s wrong: strophe 1 admires the miner. He goes where no falcon has looked and no lion has walked (28:7–8). The poem’s point is that his skill, wonderful as it is, stops short of wisdom.
  • Corrective: honour the miner, then show where his lamp goes out.

Pitfall: Preaching the friends

  • What it looks like: treating 28:28 as a friend’s victory (“Eliphaz was right: Job needed the fear of God”).
  • Why it’s wrong: Eliphaz questioned whether Job had the fear (4:6; 15:4; 22:4). The narrator and the LORD said he had it (1:1, 8; 2:3). The poem’s definition sides with the narrator and the LORD, not with Eliphaz’s suspicion.
  • Corrective: read 28:28 back to 1:1, not to 22:4. (The overview’s preaching trap 1, confirmed.)

Pitfall: Turning Proverbs 2:4 against Job 28 — or Job 28 against Proverbs

  • What it looks like: either “Seek wisdom like silver and you will find it” (Prov 2:4) preached as a promise of human achievement, or Job 28 preached as a refutation of Proverbs.
  • Why it’s wrong: both books say “the LORD gives wisdom” (Prov 2:6; Job 28:28). Job 28 removes the illusion that the search earns it; Proverbs never claimed it does.
  • Corrective: preach them as two voices of one canon: seek with all your strength, and receive it as a gift.

Pitfall: Saying the poem was built from chapters 22, 26 and 38 (v1.1)

  • What it looks like: “The poet gathered the words of Eliphaz, Job and God into one poem.”
  • Why it’s wrong: claim audit 4 showed that the overlap of vocabulary is no greater than most Job chapters have with their neighbours [S: audit 4]. What the text supports is a handful of specific echoes.
  • Corrective: name the specific echoes — the gold of Ophir (22:24; 28:16), Zophar’s hidden things and limit (11:6–7), “He knows the way” (23:10; 28:23), and the thunderbolt line that God repeats (28:26; 38:25).

Pitfall: Settling the speaker from the pulpit

  • What it looks like: “Job here reaches serenity” or “The author here steps in” stated as fact.
  • Why it’s wrong: the text frames the poem inside Job’s discourse but does not mark its voice. The Greek’s “my eye” is a translator’s reading.
  • Corrective: say that it stands within Job’s discourse in a voice above the dispute, and that the sermon’s point does not depend on the speaker.
