## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across several tools:

1. **The Accuser's prediction and Job's response turn on one verb, and Job fulfils it in the opposite sense.** `[T]` ברך ("bless") stands four times in the chapter (1:5, 10, 11, 21, lemma). Twice it means "curse" by euphemism: Job fears his sons have "blessed" God in their hearts (1:5), and the Accuser swears Job will "bless You to Your face" (1:11). Twice it means "bless": God "blessed the work of his hands" (1:10), and Job says יְהִי שֵׁם יְהוָה מְבֹרָךְ ("blessed be the name of the LORD", 1:21). The Accuser said Job would "bless" God to his face; he did, and the narrator rules that he did not sin (1:22). — Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Translations, Narrator's Comment, Move 4 (42:12). All three English versions resolve the euphemism, so the congregation cannot hear it. *High.*

2. **Job worships in the posture of the disasters and names God as the taker.** `[T]` / `[I]` נפל ("fall") stands four times: the Sabeans "fell" on the herds (1:15), the fire "fell" from heaven (1:16), the house "fell" on the young people (1:19), and Job "fell to the ground and worshiped" (1:20). לקח ("take") stands three times: the Sabeans "took" (1:15), the Chaldeans "took" (1:17), and "the LORD has taken" (1:21). Job looks through raiders and weather to God, without knowing the Accuser. The narrator does not correct him; he says "Job did not sin" (1:22). — Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Narrator's Comment, Tone. *High on the counts; the reading is `[I]`.*

3. **The test of a man who fears God is told in the words of Abraham's test.** `[T]` (synthetic) Two phrases in Job 1 stand elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible only in Genesis 22. וְהִשְׁכִּים בַּבֹּקֶר ("rising early in the morning") with עֹלָה ("burnt offering") meets in one verse only at Gen 22:3 and Job 1:5. אַל־תִּשְׁלַח יָדְךָ ("do not put forth your hand") occurs only at Gen 22:12 and Job 1:12 (phrase search). Gen 22:12 is where God declares, after the test, "now I know that you fear God (יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים)". And the genealogy that closes the same chapter names עוּץ ("Uz"), Nahor's firstborn (Gen 22:21). In Genesis the limit spares the son; in Job it spares only the father. — Surfaced by: Tool 11 (Move 1–3), Vocabulary (proper-noun check), Positional Necessity. *High on the two phrases; moderate on the design.*

4. **The Accuser describes Job's life in the vocabulary of the Torah's blessings, and asks whether the piety is only the return on them.** `[T]` "You have blessed the work of his hands (מַעֲשֵׂה יָדָיו בֵּרַכְתָּ)" (1:10) is the formula of Deuteronomy's covenant blessing: ברך with מַעֲשֵׂה יָד stands at Deut 2:7; 14:29; 15:10; 16:15; 24:19; 28:12, Isa 19:25 and Job 1:10, and nowhere else. "His possessions have increased (פָּרַץ)" pairs ברך with פרץ as at Gen 28:14 and 30:30 (Jacob). The question הַחִנָּם ("for nothing?", 1:9) is therefore aimed at covenant religion itself: does anyone fear God once the blessings stop? — Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Tool 11 (Move 1–2), Author's Purpose, Linking Words. *High on the words.*

5. **The reader alone is shown heaven, and the narrator frames the chapter with his own verdict.** `[T]` The chapter alternates earth (1:1–5) → heaven (1:6–12) → earth (1:13–22). Its two וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם ("now there was a day") openings (1:6, 13) put the council and the catastrophe on the same footing. Job never learns of the council. The narrator opens with Job's character (1:1) and closes with his ruling (1:22). חטא ("sin") stands only at the two ends: Job fears his sons "have sinned" (1:5); "Job did not sin" (1:22). — Surfaced by: Structure, Narrator's Comment, Repetition, Move 4. *High.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview's purpose is confirmed. It shows a righteous man who, stripped of everything, will neither curse God nor buy relief by confessing sins he did not commit. It exposes the friends' theology as speaking falsely of God (42:7), and brings the sufferer and the reader to a fear of God that wants God, not his gifts. That fear is reached through God's self-disclosure, not through an explanation. `[I]`

**Passage level.** Job 1 does three things, and all three are on the page.

- **It establishes the facts of the case before any human argues it.** Job's character is stated by the narrator (1:1) and by God (1:8) in the same four terms. The reader therefore knows the friends' premise is false before they speak. `[T]`
- **It poses the book's question.** "Does Job fear God for nothing?" (1:9). The question is about God as much as about Job: is God feared for himself, or for the hedge and the blessings? `[T]` / `[I]`
- **It gives the first answer, in deed and word.** Job loses property and children in one day, falls to the ground, worships and blesses the name (1:20–21). The narrator certifies the answer (1:22). `[T]`

**What the author includes and omits.** The narrator accelerates through the disasters (four reports in five verses, each cut off by the next) and slows at Job's response (three verbs of mourning and one of worship before a word is spoken, 1:20). He gives no reason to Job and none to the reader beyond the Accuser's question. He says nothing of the children's guilt: Job's "perhaps" (1:5) is never confirmed. `[T]`

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Job 1 opens the book, so the preceding movement is the canon. In the BHS order the reader comes to Job straight from the Psalter. `[T]` for the order. The Psalter has promised, repeatedly and in these terms, that the man who fears the LORD is blessed:

- "How blessed is the man (אַשְׁרֵי הָאִישׁ) who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked (בַּעֲצַת רְשָׁעִים)" (Ps 1:1). The phrase עֲצַת רְשָׁעִים ("the counsel of the wicked") occurs in the Hebrew Bible only at Ps 1:1 and in Job, at 10:3; 21:16; 22:18. `[T]`
- "How blessed is the man who fears the LORD (יָרֵא אֶת־יְהוָה) … wealth and riches are in his house" (Ps 112:1, 3). `[T]`
- "Behold, for thus shall the man be blessed (יְבֹרַךְ) who fears the LORD (יְרֵא יְהוָה)" (Ps 128:4). The psalm has just described "the work of your hands" and sons "around your table" (128:2–3). `[T]`

Job 1 opens on exactly such a man — fearing God (1:1), his house full (1:3), his sons at table (1:4), "the work of his hands" blessed (1:10). `[T]` for the parallels. The Psalter has set up a promise; Job 1 puts it under test. `[I]`

**Necessity answer.** The prologue must come first because the book depends on a gap in knowledge. `[I]`

- The reader must know from the narrator and from God that Job is innocent (1:1, 8). Otherwise the dialogue would be a trial of Job; with the prologue, it is a trial of a doctrine.
- The reader must also be shown the council that Job never sees (1:6–12). This forbids the reader, from the first page, the friends' later move of claiming to know why a man suffers.
- And the Accuser's question must be asked before any suffering, so that everything that follows is heard as an answer to it.

**Implication for this passage.** Job 1 is not the "background" to the real book. It is the book's governing question, its evidence, and its first answer. The Psalter's promise of blessing to the God-fearer is not being denied. It is being asked whether the fear was ever for the blessing. A sermon on Job 1 has to hold two vantage points at once — heaven's, which the reader is given, and Job's, which he is not. `[I]`

#### Surrounding context

- **Immediately after:** chapter 2 repeats the council almost word for word (2:1–7). The Accuser is refused his first wager and asks for a second: "skin for skin". The chapter ends with the friends' seven silent days (2:11–13). `[T]`
- **Section:** 1:1–3:1 is the prose prologue. In Leningrad, as the WLC transcribes it, it carries prose accents only (no dehi or ole), against the poetic system from 3:2 (overview, verified). `[T]` *as the WLC transcribes it*; a reading tradition, not authorial.
- **Paragraphing:** petuḥot close 1:5 and 1:22. Leningrad therefore divides the chapter into the portrait (1:1–5) and the first test (1:6–22). It puts no break between the council and the disasters. `[T]` for the markers, `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–5 | **The man on earth** | Character (1:1); children and wealth (1:2–3); the feasts and Job's preventive sacrifice (1:4–5). Petuḥah. |
| 1:6–12 | **The council in heaven** | "Now there was a day" (1:6). YHWH commends Job; the Accuser asks "for nothing?"; permission with a limit — רַק ("only", 1:12). |
| 1:13–19 | **The day of four messengers** | "Now there was a day" (1:13). Four reports, each cut off by the next and each ending "I alone have escaped to tell you". |
| 1:20–22 | **The response and the verdict** | Five verbs of action (1:20); five lines of speech (1:21); the narrator's ruling (1:22). Petuḥah. |

**Device: alternation, with a doubled opening.** Earth → heaven → earth. The two וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם ("now there was a day") clauses (1:6, 13) set the council and the catastrophe side by side as two "days". The phrase occurs in the Hebrew Bible at 1 Sam 1:4; 14:1; 2 Kgs 4:8, 11, 18; Job 1:6, 13; 2:1 (phrase search). `[T]`

**The disasters alternate in kind.** A human raid (the Sabeans, 1:15), fire "from heaven" (1:16), a second human raid (the Chaldeans, 1:17), and a wind "from across the wilderness" (1:19). Human, heavenly, human, natural. `[T]`

**The losses run from least to most.** First the working animals (oxen and donkeys, 1:14–15), then the sheep (1:16), then the camels — the most valuable (1:17) — and finally the children (1:18–19). The servants die in every report. The order of loss differs from the order of listing in 1:3 (sheep, camels, oxen, donkeys). The loss ends where the portrait began: "seven sons and three daughters" (1:2). `[T]` for the order; `[I]` that it climaxes.

**The response is paced like a rite.** Five wayyiqtol verbs in one verse — וַיָּקָם ("arose"), וַיִּקְרַע ("tore"), וַיָּגָז ("shaved"), וַיִּפֹּל ("fell"), וַיִּשְׁתָּחוּ ("worshiped", 1:20) — before a word is spoken. Then five lines of speech (1:21) and a two-clause verdict (1:22). The narrator hurries through the losses and slows down for the response. `[T]` for the forms; `[I]` for the effect.

### 4. Linking Words

- **כִּי ("for", 1:5).** Job's sacrifices are grounded in a fear, not a fact: כִּי אָמַר אִיּוֹב אוּלַי חָטְאוּ בָנַי ("for Job said, 'Perhaps my sons have sinned'"). The narrator adds the habitual כָּכָה יַעֲשֶׂה אִיּוֹב כָּל־הַיָּמִים ("thus Job did continually"). Job's piety is regular and anxious, and it looks after others before himself. `[T]`
- **כִּי ("for", 1:8).** God's commendation is grounded: "for there is no one like him on the earth". `[T]`
- **הֲלֹא ("have You not…?", 1:10) → וְאוּלָם ("but", 1:11).** The Accuser's argument has two steps. First, God has fenced and blessed Job; then — "but" — remove the blessing and see. `[T]`
- **אִם־לֹא (1:11).** An oath-formula with its curse unspoken: "if he does not bless (= curse) You to Your face…". The Accuser swears. `[T]`
- **רַק ("only", 1:12).** "Only do not put forth your hand on him." God sets the limit. The same particle then recurs four times in the survivors' refrain: רַק־אֲנִי לְבַדִּי ("I alone", 1:15, 16, 17, 19). רַק stands five times in the chapter (1:12, 15, 16, 17, 19, lemma). The limit God sets is also the limit the disasters keep: in every blow one servant is left "only" to report. `[T]` for the count; `[I]` for the link.
- **עוֹד זֶה מְדַבֵּר וְזֶה בָּא ("while he was still speaking, another also came", 1:16, 17; 1:18 has עַד).** The temporal link that turns four reports into one onslaught. `[T]`
- **בְּכָל־זֹאת ("through all this", 1:22).** It gathers the whole chapter into the verdict. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

The chapter is prose, but two passages are shaped in parallel.

**1:21 — Job's words, in three pairs:**

> עָרֹם יָצָאתִי מִבֶּטֶן אִמִּי / וְעָרֹם אָשׁוּב שָׁמָּה
> ("Naked I came from my mother's womb, / And naked I shall return there.")
> יְהוָה נָתַן / וַיהוָה לָקָח
> ("The LORD gave / and the LORD has taken away.")
> יְהִי שֵׁם יְהוָה מְבֹרָךְ
> ("Blessed be the name of the LORD.")

- The first two pairs are **antithetical** — came / return; gave / taken. The third line is a single line, not a pair, and it resolves both. `[T]`
- The subject of the second pair is named twice, יְהוָה … וַיהוָה ("the LORD … and the LORD"). Hebrew needed no repeated subject; the repetition is emphatic. `[T]`
- The verse carries prose accents in the WLC, like the rest of the prologue. Its shape is poetic; the accent system treats it as prose. `[T]` *as the WLC transcribes it*.

**1:15–19 — the four reports.** The reports are strict parallels: an agent, the loss, the servants struck, and the same closing line. The fourth breaks the pattern. It opens by repeating 1:13 word for word ("Your sons and your daughters were eating and drinking wine in their oldest brother's house"), and its victims are הַנְּעָרִים ("the young people") — the children, not the servants. `[T]`

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Three authorial statements carry the chapter.

- **1:1 — the verdict before the story.** "That man was blameless, upright, fearing God and turning away from evil." The narrator gives his own judgement before any character speaks, and God then repeats it word for word (1:8). `[T]`
- **1:5 — the habit.** כָּכָה יַעֲשֶׂה אִיּוֹב כָּל־הַיָּמִים ("Thus Job did continually"). The narrator tells us Job's piety is a pattern, not a crisis measure. `[T]`
- **1:22 — the ruling.** בְּכָל־זֹאת לֹא־חָטָא אִיּוֹב וְלֹא־נָתַן תִּפְלָה לֵאלֹהִים ("Through all this Job did not sin nor did he blame God").
  - It answers 1:5 directly: חטא ("sin") stands only at 1:5 and 1:22 in the chapter (lemma).
  - It answers 1:11: the Accuser swore Job would "bless" God to his face in the curse sense, and the narrator rules that he did not.
  - It endorses 1:21, including "the LORD has taken". The narrator knows the Accuser's hand was in it (1:12), yet he does not call Job's attribution wrong. `[T]` / `[I]`
  - תִּפְלָה ("unseemliness, folly, wrong") occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible: Jer 23:13; Job 1:22; 24:12 (lemma). *High.*

**What the narrator withholds.** He never tells Job about the council, and he never tells the reader why God accepted the wager beyond the Accuser's question. That silence is also authorial. `[T]` for the absence (the council is never mentioned after 2:7).

### 7. Vocabulary

**תָּם ("blameless", 1:1, 8).** It means complete, whole, of one piece — not sinless. Job holds seven of its fourteen occurrences in the Hebrew Bible (1:1, 8; 2:3; 8:20; 9:20, 21, 22, lemma). Its first bearer is Jacob, אִישׁ תָּם ("a peaceful man", Gen 25:27). The phrase אִישׁ תָּם occurs only at Gen 25:27; Job 1:8; 2:3 (phrase search). The pair תָּם with יָשָׁר ("upright") occurs at Job 1:1, 8; 2:3; Ps 37:37; Prov 29:10 (lemma). From 2:3 the noun תֻּמָּה ("integrity") becomes the thing in dispute: God says Job "still holds fast his integrity" (2:3), and his wife asks, "Do you still hold fast your integrity?" (2:9). *High.* `[T]`

**יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים ("fearing God", 1:1, 8, 9).** The adjective יָרֵא ("fearing") with אֱלֹהִים ("God") first describes a person at Gen 22:12: "now I know that you fear God (יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה)", said of Abraham after the test (lemma co-occurrence; the full list is in Tool 11). The Accuser takes the phrase up as a verb, הַחִנָּם יָרֵא אִיּוֹב אֱלֹהִים ("Does Job fear God for nothing?", 1:9). The fear of God is the book's keynote: it is also the last word of the wisdom poem (28:28). *High.* `[T]`

**הַשָּׂטָן ("Satan", literally "the adversary, the accuser", 1:6, 7, 8, 9, 12).**
- **The count.** שָׂטָן stands **fourteen times in eleven verses** of Job, all within 1:6–2:7, and always with the article: 1:6, 7 (×2), 8, 9, 12 (×2); 2:1, 2 (×2), 3, 4, 6, 7 (lemma). `[T]` The article marks a title — "the adversary" — rather than a personal name. *High.*
- **Elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible** the noun is used of human adversaries (1 Sam 29:4; 2 Sam 19:23; 1 Kgs 5:18; 11:14, 23, 25; Ps 109:6), and of the angel of the LORD sent to stand in Balaam's way: וַיִּתְיַצֵּב מַלְאַךְ יְהוָה בַּדֶּרֶךְ לְשָׂטָן לוֹ ("the angel of the LORD took his stand in the way as an adversary against him", Num 22:22; also 22:32). With the article, as a heavenly figure, it stands only in Job 1–2 and Zech 3:1–2. Without the article it names the one who incited David (1 Chr 21:1). `[T]` (lemma)
- **What that means here.** The Hebrew presents a member of the heavenly court who comes "among" the sons of God (1:6), answers when spoken to, and acts only by permission (1:12). Whether this figure is already the devil of the New Testament is a question the Hebrew text does not settle. The Greek renders him ὁ διάβολος ("the devil") throughout, the New Testament's word (see Tool 8). `[T]` / `[S]` — the identification is contested.

**חִנָּם ("for nothing", 1:9).** An adverb built on חֵן ("favour, grace"), meaning "gratis, for no return" and so "without cause". In Job it stands at 1:9; 2:3; 9:17; 22:6 (lemma), passing from the Accuser to God ("you incited Me against him … without cause", 2:3), to Job, and to Eliphaz. In the Psalter it is the word of the righteous sufferer hated "without cause": Ps 35:7, 19; 69:5 [Eng 69:4]; 109:3; 119:161 (lemma). *High.* `[T]`

**ברך ("bless", 1:5, 10, 11, 21).** The one verb carries both senses in the chapter — "curse" by euphemism at 1:5 and 1:11, "bless" at 1:10 and 1:21. BHS calls 1:5 *correctio vel euphemismus pro* וְקִלְלוּ ("a correction or euphemism for 'and they cursed'") and cross-refers 1:11 to it, *as BHS prints it*. Whether the Hebrew is a scribal substitution or the author's own euphemism, the double sense is now part of the text and the chapter uses it. See Headline Finding 1. *High.* `[T]`

**The Accuser's patrol: שׁוּט and הִתְהַלֵּךְ (1:7).** מִשּׁוּט בָּאָרֶץ וּמֵהִתְהַלֵּךְ בָּהּ ("from roaming about on the earth and walking around on it").
- שׁוּט ("to roam, go to and fro") is the verb of the LORD's own eyes that "range to and fro throughout the earth" (Zech 4:10; 2 Chr 16:9). It is also the search of Jerusalem for one man who does justice (Jer 5:1), and Joab's census, "go about through all the tribes of Israel" (2 Sam 24:2, 8) (lemma).
- הִתְהַלֵּךְ בָּאָרֶץ ("to walk about in the earth") is the phrase of the LORD's patrol in Zechariah's first and last night visions (Zech 1:10, 11; 6:7). It also describes Abram (Gen 13:17) and Joshua's surveyors (Josh 18:4, 8) (phrase search).
- *What it adds.* The Accuser speaks of himself in the vocabulary of the LORD's inspection of the earth. `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the implication. *Moderate–high.* See Tool 11.

**שׂוּךְ ("to hedge, fence", 1:10).** שַׂכְתָּ בַעֲדוֹ ("You have made a hedge about him"). The ketiv writes the pronoun as את (qere אַתָּה, "You"). At 3:23 Job uses the by-form סכך with the same preposition: "whom God has hedged in (וַיָּסֶךְ אֱלוֹהַּ בַּעֲדוֹ)". The Accuser's protective hedge has become Job's prison. `[T]`; the lemma index splits the two verbs (7753 against 5526 a), and the preposition בַּעַד joins them. *Moderate–high.*

**נַעַר ("young man, servant", 1:15, 16, 17, 19).** Four times. In the first three reports it means "servants"; in the fourth, the same word is the children — "it fell on the young people (הַנְּעָרִים) and they died" (1:19) (lemma). The fourth report does not change the noun; it changes the victims. `[T]` *High* on the form; the effect is `[I]`.

**עָרֹם ("naked", 1:21).** "Naked I came … naked I shall return." In Job the word recurs at 22:6 (the naked whom Eliphaz says Job stripped); 24:7, 10 (the poor who go about naked); and 26:6 ("Naked is Sheol before Him") (lemma). It is the word of Gen 2:25 (עֲרוּמִּים, "naked"). `[T]` *Moderate* for the Genesis echo.

**תִּפְלָה ("blame, unseemliness", 1:22)** — see Tool 6.

**The name אִיּוֹב ("Job").** Its etymology is uncertain `[S]`. Its consonants, איוב, are those of אוֹיֵב ("enemy"), אויב, with waw and yod exchanged. Job twice complains that God counts him "as Your enemy (לְאוֹיֵב)" (13:24; Elihu quotes it at 33:10) (lemma 341: 13:24; 27:7; 33:10). By the hard rule for consonant play, the correspondence alone proves nothing. The corroboration here is the thematic one: the man called "Job" accuses God of treating him as "enemy". *Moderate at best*; widely noted `[S]`, and flagged for a lexicon check. For the sound: *ʾiyyôḇ* against *ʾôyēḇ*.

**Proper-noun inventory (Question 0).** Uz; Job; the sons of the east; the Sabeans; the Chaldeans; YHWH; Elohim; the sons of God; the Accuser. Significant numbers: seven and three (1:2); 7,000 and 3,000; 500 and 500 (1:3); three bands (1:17); four corners (1:19); four messengers.

- **עוּץ ("Uz")** — canonical freight. A son of Aram (Gen 10:23); Nahor's firstborn in the genealogy closing Abraham's test (Gen 22:21); a Horite clan of Edom (Gen 36:28); "the kings of the land of Uz" (Jer 25:20); "O daughter of Edom, who dwells in the land of Uz" (Lam 4:21) (lemma). *Load-the-story:* Gen 22:20–24 follows immediately on "now I know that you fear God" (22:12). A land named for the chapter of the God-fearing test is where a God-fearer is tested. `[T]` for the references; `[I]` for the significance. *Moderate.*
- **בְּנֵי־קֶדֶם ("the sons of the east")** — where Jacob went to Laban (Gen 29:1); the raiders of Gideon's day (Judg 6:3, 33; 7:12; 8:10); the peoples whose wisdom Solomon's exceeded (1 Kgs 5:10 [Eng 4:30]); Isa 11:14; Jer 49:28; Ezek 25:4, 10 (phrase search). Job is "the greatest" of them (1:3). *Moderate* on the wisdom association.
- **שְׁבָא ("the Sabeans", 1:15)** — a name from the Table of Nations and Abraham's family (Gen 10:7, 28; 25:3). Its caravans recur at 6:19. Its better-known history is as traders (Ps 72:10, 15; the queen, 1 Kgs 10). Here they are raiders. Scenery, with a patriarchal pedigree. `[T]`
- **כַּשְׂדִּים ("the Chaldeans", 1:17)** — Abraham's "Ur of the Chaldeans" (Gen 11:28, 31; 15:7) and, later, Babylon, which took Jerusalem (2 Kgs 24:2; 25:4–13) (lemma). For a canonical reader of the Writings, "the Chaldeans formed three bands … and took" cannot be innocent of the exile. `[I]` *Moderate.*
- **Numbers.** Seven and three, 7,000 and 3,000, and 500 and 500 are numbers of completeness: ten children, ten thousand head of the larger flocks, a thousand of the working stock. `[T]` for the arithmetic; the symbolism is `[I]`. "Three bands" (שְׁלֹשָׁה רָאשִׁים) is a military formation — Gideon (Judg 7:16), Abimelech (Judg 9:43), Saul (1 Sam 11:11), the Philistine raiders (1 Sam 13:17) (phrase search). No further freight detected.
- **Routine:** the four corners (1:19). The phrase otherwise names the corners of altars and stands (1 Kgs 7:34; Ezek 43:20; 45:19); no freight detected.

- **The euphemism is resolved everywhere** (1:5, 11). All three versions are right to give the sense. But every English reader loses the fact that the word the Accuser swears Job will use against God is the word Job uses for God at 1:21 (Headline Finding 1).
- **NIV84 makes 1:22 one clause.** "Job did not sin by charging God with wrongdoing" makes the second clause the *means* of the first. The Hebrew has two co-ordinate clauses: he did not sin, *and* he did not ascribe folly to God. NASB95 and ESV keep them apart.
- **ESV is the most consistent at חִנָּם.** It has "for no reason" at 1:9 and "without reason" at 2:3, so the link between the Accuser's charge and God's admission can be heard. NASB95 has "for nothing" and "without cause"; NIV84 "for nothing" and "without any reason".
- **ESV and NIV84 lose "there" at 1:21.** The Hebrew שָׁמָּה ("there") is odd: one does not return to the mother's womb. It points to the earth, as in "until you return (שׁוּבְךָ) to the ground … to dust you shall return (תָּשׁוּב)" (Gen 3:19). NASB95 keeps the oddity; the others smooth it (NIV84 only in the margin).

**Ancient versions check.** The modern versions agree on several weighty renderings, so the check runs. Swete, triaged:

- **1:6 οἱ ἄγγελοι τοῦ θεοῦ ("the angels of God") for בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים.** *Category 1* (a rendering, not a variant): the Greek interprets the phrase. NIV84 follows the Greek's sense. **Pulpit note:** a congregation hearing NIV84 hears "angels" and loses the phrase that links 1:6 to 38:7 ("all the sons of God shouted for joy") and to Gen 6:2, 4.
- **1:6 ὁ διάβολος ("the devil") for הַשָּׂטָן.** *Category 3* — the New Testament's own word for the figure. It stands for the Greek reading of Job, not as a correction of the Hebrew.
- **1:9 Μὴ δωρεὰν σέβεται Ἰὼβ τὸν κύριον; ("Does Job worship the Lord for nothing?").** δωρεάν ("freely, for nothing") is the word of John 15:25, "They hated Me without a cause (δωρεάν)", citing the Psalms' חִנָּם (Swete Ps 34:19; 68:5 have δωρεάν). *Category 1* at 2:3, where Swete has διὰ κενῆς ("in vain") and breaks the 1:9 / 2:3 link. See the Christological Reading.
- **1:11 εὐλογήσει ("he will bless").** Swete keeps the word "bless" literally at 1:11. At 1:5, though, it paraphrases: "my sons may have thought evil things against God". *Category 1.* **Translation-tradition split:** the Greek at 1:11 preserves the double sense the modern versions resolve. - **1:22 ἀφροσύνην ("folly")** for תִּפְלָה. It agrees with the root sense. *Category 1.*
- Pluses and minuses of substance — at 1:3, 1:5, 1:16 and 1:21 — are *category 2* and are weighed under Textual Variants.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Three registers in sequence.**

1. **Serene and formal (1:1–12).** A storyteller's cadence: "There was a man". Round numbers, habitual verbs (1:4–5), a court scene conducted in set phrases. The portrait of Job is almost idyllic, and the council reads as ceremony.
2. **A cataract (1:13–19).** Participles of normal life — "eating and drinking wine" (1:13), "plowing … feeding" (1:14) — broken by four messengers. Each is cut off by the next, עוֹד זֶה מְדַבֵּר וְזֶה בָּא ("while he was still speaking, another also came"). Each ends with the same numb line: וָאִמָּלְטָה רַק־אֲנִי לְבַדִּי לְהַגִּיד לָךְ ("and I alone have escaped to tell you"). The repetition is the tone — hammer, hammer, hammer, hammer.
3. **Grief become worship (1:20–22).** Five verbs, no speech, then five lines, the last a blessing. The narrator's verdict closes it flatly and without comment.

**Soundtrack.** A slow chorale, four blows on a drum, silence, then one voice singing the chorale's words back. **For the preacher:** do not hurry 1:20. The rite precedes the words; the torn robe and the shaved head belong to the blessing. `[T]` for the devices; the register is `[I]`.

**Genesis 22 → 1:1, 5, 8, 12** *(high on the phrases; moderate on the design)* `[T]` / `[I]` (synthetic)

*Move 1 — Source context.* Gen 22:1–19: "God tested (נִסָּה) Abraham" (22:1). He is told to offer "your son, your only son, whom you love" as a burnt offering (22:2). "Abraham rose early in the morning (וַיַּשְׁכֵּם … בַּבֹּקֶר)" and split "the wood for the burnt offering (עֹלָה)" (22:3). At the last moment the angel says, "Do not stretch out your hand (אַל־תִּשְׁלַח יָדְךָ) against the lad (הַנַּעַר) … for now I know that you fear God (יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים אַתָּה), since you have not withheld your son" (22:12). The oath of blessing follows (22:16–18), then the genealogy of Nahor, beginning with "Uz his firstborn" (22:21). The register is dread turned to provision; the cited words stand at the chapter's pivot.

*The verbal data.*
- השׁכים בבקר with עֹלָה in one verse: **Gen 22:3 and Job 1:5 only** (lemma co-occurrence). Exod 24:4–5 has both across two verses, and Exod 32:6 (the golden calf) has rising early and burnt offerings with מִמָּחֳרָת ("the next day") instead of בַּבֹּקֶר.
- אַל־תִּשְׁלַח יָדְךָ: **Gen 22:12 and Job 1:12 only** (phrase search).
- יְרֵא אֱלֹהִים of a person: first at Gen 22:12 (lemma).
- עוּץ: Gen 22:21, among five other references (Tool 7).

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Genesis is a live source in Job (overview: Gen 1–3; the patriarchal genealogies of Gen 10; 22; 25; 36; Gen 18:27; 33:19; 35:29). Gen 22 in particular is already in the overview's Presupposes, for Uz and Buz (1:1; 32:2). It has not been read there for its *test*. The book returns to the Abrahamic register at its end: "dust and ashes", Abraham's words when he interceded (Gen 18:27 → Job 42:6), and Job himself interceding (42:8–10). `[T]`

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* The Genesis texts Job 1 draws on are already in conversation with one another. The oath that follows Abraham's test (Gen 22:16–18) is repeated to Isaac "because Abraham obeyed Me" (Gen 26:3–5): וְהִתְבָּרְכוּ בְזַרְעֲךָ כֹּל גּוֹיֵי הָאָרֶץ ("in your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed") occurs only at Gen 22:18 and 26:4 (phrase search). The same chapter then gives Isaac's wealth in the words of Job 1:3 (next entry). And God calls Abraham "My servant" (עַבְדִּי, Gen 26:24), as he calls Job (1:8). The patriarchal chain — Abraham tested, Isaac blessed and great, Jacob blameless and increased — is one conversation before Job enters it. `[T]` for each link; `[I]` for the chain.

*What it adds.* Job 1 tells the test of a God-fearer in Abraham's words, and inverts it. In Genesis God tests; in Job the Accuser proposes and God permits. In Genesis the limit "do not stretch out your hand" spares the son; in Job it spares only the father, and the sons die. In Genesis the test ends in "now I know that you fear God"; in Job that verdict is given *before* the test (1:8), and the test is whether it was true "for nothing". The reader who knows Gen 22 feels the dread of 1:12 before the messengers arrive. `[I]` *Moderate* on the design; the two phrases are *high*.

**The patriarchs' wealth — Gen 26:13–14; 30:30, 43; 28:14 → 1:3, 10** *(moderate–high)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Isaac in Gerar: "the man became rich … until he became very wealthy (גָדַל מְאֹד); for he had possessions (מִקְנֵה) of flocks and possessions of herds and a great household (וַעֲבֻדָּה רַבָּה), so that the Philistines envied him" (Gen 26:13–14). Jacob to Laban: "your little has increased (וַיִּפְרֹץ) to a multitude, and the LORD has blessed (וַיְבָרֶךְ) you" (Gen 30:30). "So the man became exceedingly prosperous (וַיִּפְרֹץ הָאִישׁ), and had large flocks and female and male servants and camels and donkeys" (Gen 30:43). God to Jacob at Bethel: "you will spread out (וּפָרַצְתָּ) … and in you … shall all the families of the earth be blessed (וְנִבְרְכוּ)" (Gen 28:14).

*The verbal data.* עֲבֻדָּה ("household servants") occurs **only at Gen 26:14 and Job 1:3** in the Hebrew Bible, each time with מִקְנֶה, צֹאן and בָּקָר nearby, and with the root גדל ("great") in the adjacent verse or clause (lemma). ברך with פרץ ("bless" with "increase") in one verse: Gen 28:14; 30:30; Job 1:10 (and 2 Sam 13:25, in another sense) (lemma). *High* on the words.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* The patriarchal idiom is live throughout the frame (overview: Gen 25:27 תָּם; 33:19 קְשִׂיטָה; 35:29 "old and full of days"). Job 1 gathers the vocabulary of all three patriarchs in its first ten verses: Jacob's תָּם (Gen 25:27), Isaac's greatness and household (Gen 26:13–14), Jacob's increase under blessing (Gen 30:30, 43), and Abraham's test (Gen 22). `[T]` for each; the gathering is `[I]`.

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* See the Genesis 22 entry: Gen 26 is the chapter in which Isaac inherits Abraham's oath.

*What it adds.* Job is drawn as a patriarch, blessed as the patriarchs were blessed. The Accuser's charge in 1:10 — "You have blessed the work of his hands, and his possessions have increased" — describes the patriarchal promise working as it should, and asks whether the piety was ever more than the return on it. `[I]`

**Deuteronomy's blessing formula — Deut 2:7; 14:29; 15:10; 16:15; 24:19; 28:12 → 1:10** *(high on the words)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* "The LORD your God has blessed you in all the work of your hand (בְּכֹל מַעֲשֵׂה יָדֶךָ)" (Deut 2:7) — of the forty years in the wilderness, "you have not lacked a thing". In Deut 28:12 it is a covenant blessing for obedience: "The LORD will open for you His good storehouse, the heavens … to bless all the work of your hand". The other four uses attach it to generosity: the tithe for the Levite, the stranger, the orphan and the widow (14:29); open-handed lending (15:10); the feast (16:15); the gleanings left for the poor (24:19). The formula is the covenant's promise of blessing on labour, tied again and again to giving to the needy.

*The verbal data.* ברך with מַעֲשֵׂה יָד in one verse: Deut 2:7; 14:29; 15:10; 16:15; 24:19; 28:12; Isa 19:25; Job 1:10 — nowhere else (lemma co-occurrence).

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Deuteronomy is a live source in Job. The covenant curse "severe boils … from the sole of your foot to the crown of your head" (Deut 28:35) stands at 2:7 — the same chapter as 28:12. "I wound and I heal" (Deut 32:39) is taken up by Eliphaz at 5:18, and the widow's pledge (Deut 24:17) by Job at 24:3 (sweep, verified). **The book uses Deut 28 at both ends of the covenant: its blessing (28:12) in the Accuser's charge, and its curse (28:35) on Job's body a chapter later.** `[T]` for both links; the pairing is `[I]`. *Moderate–high.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Deut 28:12 and 28:35 are in the same covenant list, the blessing and the curse, already set against each other by Deuteronomy itself (28:1–14 and 28:15–68).

*What it adds.* The Accuser speaks Deuteronomy. His question — fear "for nothing"? — is aimed at the covenant logic itself: blessing for obedience, curse for disobedience. Job will suffer the curse's words without the curse's cause. The book does not reject Deuteronomy's order. It asks whether the fear of God can outlast the removal of its blessings, and it asks the question in Deuteronomy's own words. And Job's own record (29:12–17; 31:16–22) is the generosity to which Deuteronomy attached the formula. `[I]`

**Psalm 113:2 → 1:21** *(high on the phrase; moderate on the design)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Ps 113 opens the Hallel: "Praise, O servants of the LORD (עַבְדֵי יְהוָה), praise the name of the LORD. Blessed be the name of the LORD (יְהִי שֵׁם יְהוָה מְבֹרָךְ) from this time forth and forever" (113:1–2). It praises the God who "raises the poor from the dust (מֵעָפָר) and lifts the needy from the ash heap (מֵאַשְׁפֹּת)" (113:7), and "makes the barren woman abide in the house as a joyful mother of children" (113:9). A psalm of reversal: from dust and ash-heap to princes, from barrenness to sons.

*The verbal data.* יְהִי שֵׁם יְהוָה מְבֹרָךְ occurs **only at Ps 113:2 and Job 1:21** (phrase search). Direction of dependence: open.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* The Psalter is a live source and Job's canonical neighbour (Ps 8 at 7:17–18; Ps 107:40 at 12:21, 24; Ps 88 at 19:13, all in the overview). Ps 113 is not in the overview's map. *Addition.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Ps 113:7 is word for word Hannah's song: "He raises the poor from the dust, He lifts the needy from the ash heap" (1 Sam 2:8). The pair דַּל ("poor") with אַשְׁפֹּת ("ash heap") occurs only at 1 Sam 2:8 and Ps 113:7 (lemma). Hannah's song also says "The LORD kills and makes alive … the LORD makes poor and rich; He brings low, He also exalts" (1 Sam 2:6–7). So Job 1:21 stands at the junction of two texts already in conversation. It has Ps 113's blessing formula, and the "gives / takes" of Hannah's song. `[T]` for the links; `[I]` for the junction. *Moderate.*

*What it adds.* Job blesses the name in the words of the Psalter's great psalm of reversal — but at the bottom of the reversal, not at the top. The psalm praises the God who lifts the poor from dust and ash heap; Job blesses the same name as he goes down into them (2:8). The reader who knows the psalm knows its ending, which Job does not yet know. And the epilogue will enact it (42:10–17). `[I]`

**Zechariah 1–6; 2 Samuel 24 / 1 Chronicles 21 → 1:6–12** *(high on the words; moderate on the design)* `[T]` (synthetic)

*Move 1 — Source context.*
- **Zechariah's night visions.** Horsemen report to the angel of the LORD, "We have patrolled the earth (הִתְהַלַּכְנוּ בָאָרֶץ)" (Zech 1:11; also 1:10). The Accuser stands at the high priest Joshua's right hand "to accuse him (לְשִׂטְנוֹ)", and "the LORD rebuke you, Satan!" (3:1–2). "The eyes of the LORD … range to and fro (מְשׁוֹטְטִים) throughout the earth" (4:10). "These are the four spirits (winds) of heaven, going forth after standing before (מֵהִתְיַצֵּב עַל) the Lord of all the earth" (6:5), and they "patrol the earth" (6:7). The frame is the heavenly court overseeing the earth after the exile.
- **2 Samuel 24.** "The anger of the LORD burned against Israel, and it incited (וַיָּסֶת) David against them" (24:1). Joab is sent to "go about (שׁוּט) through all the tribes" (24:2), and they "went about (וַיָּשֻׁטוּ) through the whole land" (24:8).
- **1 Chr 21:1** retells it: "Then Satan (שָׂטָן) stood up against Israel and moved (וַיָּסֶת) David".

*The verbal data* (all in the WLC).
- לְהִתְיַצֵּב עַל ("to present oneself before") occurs at Job 1:6; 2:1; Zech 6:5; and Num 23:3, in another sense (phrase search).
- הִתְהַלֵּךְ בָּאָרֶץ: Zech 1:10, 11; 6:7; Job 1:7; 2:2; also Gen 13:17; Josh 18:4, 8 (phrase search).
- שׁוּט: Zech 4:10; 2 Chr 16:9; Jer 5:1; 2 Sam 24:2, 8; Job 1:7; 2:2; and others (lemma).
- סות: Job 2:3 (next unit); 2 Sam 24:1; 1 Chr 21:1.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* No earlier use — this is the book's first scene. Planted for 2:1–7, where the same phrases return and God says, "you incited Me (וַתְּסִיתֵנִי)" (2:3).

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* The three sources are already in canonical conversation. 1 Chr 21:1 rewrites 2 Sam 24:1, putting שָׂטָן where Samuel had the LORD's anger — the canon's own witness that "who moves whom" is a question it holds open. Zechariah sets the patrol, the eyes and the Accuser in one sequence of visions. Job 1 gathers the patrol (Zech 1; 6), the eyes' verb (Zech 4:10; 2 Sam 24) and the incitement (2 Sam 24; 1 Chr 21) into one council. `[I]`

*What it adds.* The Accuser describes his work in the vocabulary of the LORD's own oversight. He presents himself among the "sons of God" in the manner of Zechariah's patrolling spirits, and by 2:3 he has done what 2 Sam 24 says the LORD did and 1 Chr 21 says Satan did. **The text holds both sides and so must the preacher:** the adversary acts, and God permits, limits and owns it (1:12; 2:3, 6). `[I]` *Moderate.*

**2 Kings 1:10–14 → 1:16** *(moderate, Move 1 only)* `[T]`

"If I am a man of God, let fire come down from heaven and consume you." Then "the fire of God (אֵשׁ אֱלֹהִים) came down from heaven and consumed him and his fifty" (2 Kgs 1:12). | 1:22 תִּפְלָה | 24:12 "God does not pay attention to folly (תִּפְלָה)" | Job would not ascribe folly to God; later he complains that God ignores the folly done on earth | Moderate |

*Addressee-differentiation.* Chapter 1 names five parties in heaven and on earth: YHWH, the sons of God, the Accuser, Job, and Job's children and servants. The sons of God return, rejoicing, at 38:7. The Accuser is not rebuked, restored or named after 2:7. The children and servants die and are not replaced like for like: the children are not doubled (42:13). Job is answered by God himself. `[T]`

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew prose narrative — the opening of a prose frame around a poetic disputation.** `[T]` The prose accentuation of 1:1–3:1 in the WLC agrees (see Tool 2).

- **Stylised, not artless.** The chapter uses round numbers, formulaic repetition, symmetrical councils and a four-part messenger sequence. That is a storyteller's patterning, not evidence that the events are fable. The book presents Job as a man "in the land of Uz", and Ezekiel and James name him as a real exemplar (Ezek 14:14, 20; Jas 5:11). `[T]` for the references. Whether the prologue reports history or is a composed frame for a wisdom disputation is `[S]` and does not change the reading rules.
- **Reading rules.**
  - Attach every line to its speaker: the Accuser speaks true facts in a false frame (1:10).
  - The narrator's statements (1:1, 5, 22) are authoritative, and the messengers' are not ("the fire of God", 1:16, is their reading).
  - The heavenly scene is a disclosure to the reader, not an explanation given to the sufferer.
- **Embedded forms:** a messenger-report sequence (1:14–19); mourning rites (1:20); a short poetic blessing in a prose setting (1:21).

**Where the story sits.** A patriarchal world outside Israel: family priesthood, wealth in flocks and herds, and Uz in the orbit of Aram and Edom. There is no Sinai, Temple or Israel on the page. `[T]` The setting is not a date of composition, which is `[S]` (the overview gives the range).

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross. The Accuser who presents himself before the LORD (1:6) is the "accuser of our brethren", who "has been thrown down, he who accuses them before our God day and night" (Rev 12:10). ### 15. Who Am I?

| Character | Function |
|---|---|
| **Job** | Primarily a **righteous sufferer** whose pattern runs to Christ (see the Christological Reading). Secondarily an **example**, and the New Testament authorises it: "You have heard of the endurance of Job" (Jas 5:11). The example is endurance *and* lament addressed to God, not stoicism |
| **The reader** | Placed in heaven's gallery. We are given what Job never is. The identification the text forces is the reader's: *do I, who know there is a scene I cannot see, refrain from explaining another's suffering?* |
| **The Accuser** | Foil, and a warning: his cynicism about disinterested piety is the voice every believer hears within |
| **The children and servants** | Victims. Not examples of anything; the text does not moralise their deaths |
| **The messengers** | Not us — though their first theological reading ("fire of God") is ours by instinct |

**The "Job-is-me" trap.** The preacher's temptation is to make Job the hearer and the heavenly council a comfort: "God is proving you". The chapter gives the reader, not Job, that knowledge. The friends will make this very move and be condemned (42:7). `[I]`

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** To face the Accuser's question: *do we fear God for nothing?* The chapter shows a man who, stripped of the blessings, still worships and blesses. It shows the reader a God who is worth that. `[T]` / `[I]`

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | Loss can come under heaven's permission without fault in the sufferer, and without the sufferer being told why. God is sovereign over it — he limits, permits and owns it (1:12; 2:3) — and is not its moral author in the Accuser's sense. The world is not a transaction: blessings are gifts, not wages (1:21 "The LORD gave"). |
| **Stop** | Treating blessing as proof of favour and loss as proof of guilt. Explaining others' suffering. Worship that is a contract. |
| **Start** | Grieving honestly *and* blessing God in the same act (1:20–21). Holding loosely what God gave. |
| **Motivation** | Not "be like Job" but "God is worth it": the name is blessed because of who the LORD is, not because of what he has left us. The gospel ground is the Son, who was hated "for nothing" (δωρεάν, John 15:25) and who, when the Accuser offered him "all these things" in return for worship (Matt 4:9), refused. |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me:** Would I still bless God with the hedge gone? Where is my faith quietly a contract?
- **For a Christian friend:** Sit with the grieving before speaking. The torn robe comes before the blessing, and the friends' best moment is their seven days' silence (2:13).
- **For the church:** Our worship services cannot be built on the assumption that God is praised for results. Lament belongs in our gathering.
- **For an unbeliever:** The Accuser's charge is the sceptic's too — that religion is self-interest. The chapter answers with a life: a man who worshipped with nothing left. The gospel answers with a greater one.

**Prayer.**

- Thanks that every good thing is the LORD's gift.
- Confession of love for God that is really love for his gifts.
- A request for the grace to grieve without cursing and to bless when he takes away.
- Mercy for the bereaved, and restraint from explaining their loss.

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**

- **The opening is not a narrative sequence.** אִישׁ הָיָה בְאֶרֶץ־עוּץ ("There was a man in the land of Uz") puts the noun first and uses a plain perfect, where Israel's stories usually begin וַיְהִי ("and it came to pass"; e.g. 1 Sam 1:1 וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד). The book does not present itself as the next episode of Israel's history. It opens as a tale set apart. The closest parallel in form is Esth 2:5 אִישׁ יְהוּדִי הָיָה בְּשׁוּשַׁן … וּשְׁמוֹ מָרְדֳּכַי ("There was a Jew in Susa … whose name was Mordecai"), also a man introduced outside the land. `[T]` for the forms; `[I]` for the effect. *Moderate.*
- **Habitual verbs (1:4–5).** וְהָלְכוּ … וְעָשׂוּ … וְשָׁלְחוּ ("used to go … hold … send") and יַעֲשֶׂה ("used to do") mark custom, not a single event. The disaster then breaks a routine. `[T]`
- **Participles of ordinary life (1:13–14).** אֹכְלִים וְשֹׁתִים ("eating and drinking"), חֹרְשׁוֹת ("plowing"), רֹעוֹת ("feeding"), and מְדַבֵּר … בָּא ("speaking … came", 1:16–18). The catastrophe arrives in the middle of continuous action. `[T]`
- **1:21's perfects and jussive.** נָתַן … לָקָח ("gave … has taken") are completed facts. יְהִי ("let it be", jussive) turns fact into a wish of blessing. `[T]`
- **The oath (1:11).** אִם־לֹא with the imprecation suppressed: an oath-formula the Accuser swears. `[T]`

**Wordplay and sound.**

- The double sense of ברך (semantic, not phonetic) — Headline 1.

### Textual Variants

**Category 2 — Hebrew and Greek.** Swete (Vaticanus) is the observation layer; the Old Greek's shape needs a Rahlfs check, since BHS says "about a fifth of this book is lacking in the Greek".

- **1:16 — "the fire of God".**
  - *The issue:* Swete has "Fire fell from heaven", without "of God". BHS: *16ᵃ > G* ("lacking in the Greek").
  - *Evidence:* The Greek of Job paraphrases freely and often softens anthropomorphic or harsh statements about God. `[S]`
  - *Most likely original:* the Hebrew אֵשׁ אֱלֹהִים; the Greek omission reads as the translator's reserve rather than a different *Vorlage*.
  - *Preaching implication:* none for an English pulpit; all three versions have "the fire of God".
  - *Confidence:* moderate.
- **1:5 — the sacrifice.**
  - *The issue:* Swete adds "and one calf for sin concerning their souls" (μόσχον ἕνα περὶ ἁμαρτίας).
  - *Most likely original:* the Hebrew. The plus assimilates Job's patriarchal burnt offerings to Levitical sin-offering practice.
  - *Confidence:* moderate–high.
- **1:21 — Job's blessing.**
  - *The issue:* Swete adds "as it seemed good to the Lord, so it came to pass".
  - *Most likely original:* the Hebrew. The plus smooths Job's words into resignation.
  - *Confidence:* moderate–high.
- **1:3; 1:15; 1:17.** Swete adds "and great works on the earth" (1:3). It renders Sheba as "the raiders" (οἱ αἰχμαλωτεύοντες, 1:15) and the Chaldeans as "the horsemen" (οἱ ἱππεῖς, 1:17). These are interpretive renderings with no bearing on a finding. *Category 1–2*; *low weight.*

**Variation inside the Hebrew** (apparatus and reading-tradition features — witness named; spread unchecked).

- **1:18 עַד ("until") for עוֹד ("while still").** BHS: *l c mlt Mss עוֹד cf 16.17* ("read with many manuscripts עוֹד, compare vv.16–17"), *as BHS prints it*. The English versions all translate "while he was still speaking", in effect following the many manuscripts. No finding depends on it.
- **1:8 עַל ("on") for אֶל ("to").** BHS: *mlt Mss אֶל cf 2,3* — many manuscripts read אֶל, as at 2:3. No finding depends on it.
- **1:5 — BHS *correctio vel euphemismus*.** The apparatus raises the possibility that "bless" is a scribal substitution for "curse". Either way, the double sense of ברך is part of the transmitted text, and Headline 1 rests on the text as transmitted. `[T]` *as BHS prints it*.
- **Ketiv (as the WLC prints it; the qere is from the Masorah, as BHS prints it):**
  - 1:4 אחיתיהם ("their sisters"; qere אַחְיוֹתֵיהֶם)
  - 1:10 את (qere אַתָּה, "You"; BHS also records a few manuscripts reading סכת)
  - 1:21 יצתי ("I came out"; qere יָצָאתִי; BHS: some manuscripts write it as the qere)
  - None changes the sense. All three were confirmed by lemma, since a surface search would skip them.
- **1:21 שָׁמָּה ("there").** BHS: *sic L, mlt Mss Edd* ("so Leningrad, many manuscripts and editions") — noting a variation in the spelling. No bearing on the sense.
- **Paragraphing.** Petuḥot after 1:5 and 1:22 in Leningrad, as the WLC transcribes it. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.**
- **Time period (of the story):** a patriarchal age; no Israelite institutions appear. The date of composition is `[S]`.
- **Location:** "the land of Uz", associated with Aram and Edom (Gen 10:23; 36:28; Lam 4:21), east of the Jordan. The Sabeans and Chaldeans raid from south and north-east respectively `[S]`, and the great wind comes "from across the wilderness" (1:19).
- **Political context:** no state appears. Raiding parties strike a wealthy householder: a world of clans and caravans.

**What the original audience knew.**

1. **Wealth measured in herds, and in camels.** 3,000 camels (1:3) is a trading magnate's fortune. The list's order and pairing (sheep and camels; oxen and donkeys) reflect pastoral and ploughing wealth. `[I]`
2. **The head of house as priest.** Before Sinai, the head of the family offered sacrifice himself: Noah's burnt offerings (Gen 8:20), Abraham's (Gen 22), Jacob's sacrifice at Mizpah (Gen 31:54). Job belongs to that world. `[T]` for the references.
3. **Mourning rites.** Tearing the garment and cutting the hair were standard expressions of grief. For the hair, see Jer 7:29 גָּזִּי נִזְרֵךְ ("cut off your hair") and Mic 1:16 קָרְחִי וָגֹזִּי ("make yourself bald and cut off your hair") (lemma גזז). Falling to the ground is prostration before a superior — here in worship (וַיִּשְׁתָּחוּ). `[T]`
4. **A heavenly court.** The picture of God enthroned among "sons of God" who present themselves and report was familiar from Israel's own Scriptures (1 Kgs 22:19–22; Zech 1–6). It was also a feature of the wider ancient Near East `[S]`. In Job the court is wholly subordinate: the Accuser can do nothing without permission (1:12).
5. **Raiders.** The Sabeans (of Sheba) and the Chaldeans were known to the canonical reader as traders and as Babylon respectively. Here both appear as raiding bands. `[I]`
6. **Messenger conventions.** "I alone have escaped to tell you" is the voice of a battle survivor. Compare the messenger from the battle at Aphek who brings Eli the news (1 Sam 4:12–17). `[I]`, conceptual.

**What this changes about how we read.**

- Job's worship at 1:20 is not stoic composure. It is performed inside the full rites of grief. The blessing comes *with* the torn robe, not instead of it.
- For a canonical reader who knows the exile, "the Chaldeans … took them" (1:17) carries a charge the patriarchal setting cannot quite contain. The book lets Israel's loss be heard inside a Gentile's. `[I]`, moderate.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.**

- **Canonical section:** Ketuvim. The reader has the Torah and the Prophets behind them, including the exile and the unfinished restoration that closes the Nevi'im.
- **What the canonical reader knows:** the patriarchs' blessings; Deuteronomy's blessings and curses; the Psalter's promises to the God-fearer (Pss 1; 112; 128); the fall of Jerusalem to the Chaldeans; Ezekiel's naming of Job (Ezek 14:14, 20), which stands before Job in the canon.
- **Canonical register:** Psalms and Proverbs as neighbours; "the sons of God" and the Accuser from Zechariah's visions.

**The first hearers (canonical default).** A covenant people who have suffered loss on a national scale and who know the Deuteronomic logic by which loss means guilt (Deut 28). `[I]`

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** God boasts of Job to the Accuser and then allows the ruin (1:8, 12). The children die for no fault named. `[T]`
- **Surprising:** the Accuser's challenge is couched in the covenant's own words of blessing (1:10; Deut 28:12). And the God-fearer is a Gentile from Uz, not an Israelite. `[T]`
- **Comforting:** the Accuser's power is limited (רַק, 1:12). A man can bless God in the ruin, and heaven sees it (1:22). `[T]`
- **Disturbing:** if loss need not mean guilt, the neat Deuteronomic reading of Israel's own history is not the whole truth. And Job is never told why. `[I]`

**What we bring that they didn't.** A therapeutic instinct that wants an explanation. A prosperity instinct that hears blessing as a wage. A dualist instinct that credits Satan and exonerates God — the text refuses that (1:12, 21, 22; 2:3). A modern discomfort with the heavenly court as "mythology". `[I]`

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**(1) The Adversary under God's sovereignty**

- **Where this passage sits:** the fullest Old Testament scene of the Accuser in the heavenly court, and the first in canonical order within the Writings.
- **What comes before:** the serpent of Gen 3 (a different figure and word, but the same work of calling God's goodness into question: "Has God said?"); the angel as "adversary" to Balaam (Num 22:22); the incitement of 2 Sam 24:1.
- **What comes after:** 1 Chr 21:1; Zech 3:1–2 ("the LORD rebuke you"); Christ tempted in the wilderness, where the devil offers "all these things" for worship (Matt 4:8–10); "Satan has demanded permission to sift you" (Luke 22:31); "the accuser … has been thrown down" (Rev 12:9–10).
- **What this contributes:** the Adversary acts only by permission and within limits (1:12). Suffering under his hand is not outside God's rule, and accusation is not the last word.
- **Confidence:** high on the trajectory; the identity of the Job figure with the devil is canonical reflection, not a claim of the Hebrew.

**(2) Blessing and the fear of God**

- **Where this passage sits:** the test of the covenant's blessing-logic.
- **What comes before:** the patriarchal blessings (Gen 22; 26; 30); Deuteronomy's blessing on "the work of your hands" (Deut 28:12); the Psalter's "blessed is the man who fears the LORD" (Pss 112:1; 128:1–4).
- **What comes after:** the fear of the LORD as wisdom (Job 28:28; Prov 1:7); the Beatitudes' blessing on the poor and mourning (Matt 5:3–4); Paul's "I have learned to be content" (Phil 4:11–13); "God is able to make all grace abound" (2 Cor 9:8).
- **What this contributes:** blessing is God's gift, and the fear of God is not its price. Job 1 separates the two so that the fear can be seen for itself.
- **Confidence:** moderate–high.

**Relation.** *Mutual illumination.* The Adversary's accusation (theme 1) is precisely an accusation about blessing (theme 2): that the fear of God is the product of the hedge. The chapter answers theme 2 in order to refute theme 1. `[I]`

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** trajectory, a servant typology through an established category, and contrast.

**How the passage points to Christ.**

- **"My servant" (1:8).** God names Job עַבְדִּי ("My servant") twice in the prologue and four times in the epilogue. He also names Abraham so in the chapter that continues Gen 22 (Gen 26:24). The category is Isaiah's servant. *Tests:* category — passes (servant); NT precedent — weak for Job specifically, strong for the category; escalation — passes (Job suffers under permission; the Servant bears the sin of many); authorial pattern — passes (the title frames the book). *Moderate.*
- **"For nothing" (1:9).** The Accuser's charge supposes that no one serves God without pay. Swete's δωρεάν ("for nothing") is the word by which Christ says, "they hated Me without a cause (δωρεάν)" (John 15:25), citing the Psalms' righteous sufferer (Ps 35:19; 69:5 — חִנָּם, Swete δωρεάν). The righteous man who served God for nothing and the Righteous One hated for nothing stand at the two ends of the same word. *Category 3*, a canonical-reading link through the Greek; *moderate*. And δωρεάν is the adverb of grace: "justified as a gift (δωρεάν) by His grace" (Rom 3:24). The God the Accuser says is loved only for his gifts gives the greatest gift "for nothing". `[I]`
- **The Accuser's offer answered (1:20).** Swete's Job πεσὼν χαμαὶ προσεκύνησεν ("falling to the ground, worshipped"); the devil to Christ, "All these things I will give You, if You fall down and worship me (πεσὼν προσκυνήσῃς)" (Matt 4:9, SBLGNT). Job, having lost all things, fell and worshipped God. Christ, offered all things, refused to fall and worship the devil. The participle-and-verb idiom is common in the New Testament, so the link is conceptual, not an allusion. *Contrast; moderate.*
- **Intercession planted (1:5 → 42:8).** Job offers for his children at the beginning and prays for his accusers at the end. Christ "always lives to make intercession" (Heb 7:25). *Type with escalation — moderate.*

**Trajectory.**

- **What the passage longs for:** a God-fearer whose integrity is proved "for nothing", and a verdict on the Accuser's charge.
- **What Christ fulfils:** the Son, tested by the Accuser in the wilderness, holds to the Father with nothing given, and at the cross is stripped of everything. The accuser is "thrown down" (Rev 12:10).
- **What still remains:** the Accuser's final destruction (Rev 20:10), and the believer's endurance in the meantime (Jas 5:11; 1 Pet 1:6–7).

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Be patient like Job; bless God in trouble." It is true as far as it goes (Jas 5:11), but alone it becomes a demand to perform.
- **The gospel grounding:** God is worth fearing for nothing because he gives himself — ultimately, in the Son, and in justification given "as a gift" (δωρεάν, Rom 3:24; cf. 8:32).
- **Christ as hero:** the Son is the true Servant, who answered the Accuser's question perfectly. Job's blessing in loss is a shadow of that obedience, and it is also given to those in Christ to share (1 Pet 4:13).

**Confidence.** *Moderate–high* on the servant and intercession trajectory; *moderate* on the δωρεάν and Matt 4:9 lines.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**1:6–12 — the heavenly wager.**
- **Category:** ethical and apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** God appears to allow a righteous man's children to die to settle an argument.
- **Common mishandlings:** "God and Satan made a bet" (flippant); or "Satan did it, not God" (dualist).
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the scene discloses to the reader that the suffering has a cause Job cannot see, so that the friends' explanation can be judged false. It is not given as a theodicy.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text does not soften it. God initiates the conversation (1:8) and permits and limits the harm (1:12). In 2:3 God says, "you incited Me against him to ruin him without cause". Job attributes the loss to the LORD (1:21), and the narrator approves (1:22). The book never explains the permission to Job, and God's speeches (38–41) do not mention it.
- **Handling:** acknowledge the difficulty; do not explain it away; preach the limit (רַק) and the verdict (1:22) alongside it.

**1:18–19 — the children's deaths.**
- **Category:** pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** ten children die, and the book neither mourns them at length nor replaces them like for like (42:13).
- **Handling:** a landmine for bereaved hearers. Do not call them "replaced" in 42:13. Do not suggest their feasting was sinful — the text never says so, and Job's "perhaps" (1:5) is unconfirmed.

**1:21 — at funerals.**
- **Category:** weaponised / pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** "The LORD gave and the LORD has taken away" is quoted as fatalism, or as a demand for composure.
- **Honest exegesis:** it follows the torn robe and the shaved head (1:20), and chapter 3 follows it. The line is worship in grief, not a substitute for grief.
- **Handling:** keep 1:20 with 1:21.

**1:6 — the Accuser in heaven.**
- **Category:** doctrinal / apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** Satan appears among the sons of God and speaks with God.
- **Options:** (a) the heavenly court's official prosecutor, not yet the fallen devil of later revelation `[S]`; (b) the devil himself, permitted access (Rev 12:10 "accuses them before our God day and night") `[S]`.
- **Honest exegesis:** the Hebrew shows a figure with a title, access, malice (1:11) and no independent power. It does not decide between the options.
- **Handling:** state what the text shows and let the canonical trajectory (Tool 14) carry the rest.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: "The patience of Job" from 1:21 alone

- **What it looks like:** Job as the model of calm acceptance — "the LORD gave, the LORD has taken away" as the whole of godly suffering.
- **Why it's wrong:** 1:21 follows five verbs of grief and worship (1:20), and chapter 3 follows 1:21. The book's verdict — Job "spoke what is right" (42:7) — covers his laments as well. James commends ὑπομονή ("endurance", Jas 5:11), not stoicism.
- **The corrective:** keep the torn robe with the blessing, and preach 1:20–22 as worship *in* grief.

### Pitfall: Dualism — "Satan did it, not God"

- **What it looks like:** "God didn't do this to Job; the devil did. God only allowed it."
- **Why it's wrong:** the text refuses the neat separation. God raises Job's name (1:8), grants the power and sets its limit (1:12). Job says "the LORD has taken away" (1:21), and the narrator says Job did not sin in saying it (1:22). In 2:3 God says, "you incited Me against him".
- **The corrective:** hold what the text holds: the Adversary acts with malice; God permits, limits and owns the event; and God is not the Accuser. Leave the mystery where the book leaves it.

### Pitfall: "God is proving you, as he proved Job"

- **What it looks like:** a pastoral application that tells a sufferer which heavenly scene lies behind their grief.
- **Why it's wrong:** the council is disclosed to the reader, never to Job. The book gives the reader that knowledge so that the reader does not presume on it, and the friends are condemned for explaining (42:7).
- **The corrective:** preach the privilege of the reader as a restraint. We know there is a scene we cannot see; therefore we do not narrate it for others.

### Pitfall: Blaming the children

- **What it looks like:** "The sons were partying; Job was right to worry; the house fell on them."
- **Why it's wrong:** the narrator never says the feasts were sinful. Job's "perhaps" (אוּלַי, 1:5) is never confirmed, and the chapter's only verdict on sin concerns Job, and it is "did not sin" (1:22).
- **The corrective:** let the deaths be innocent. That is the book's whole point.

### Pitfall: Prosperity — the hedge as the normal Christian's portion

- **What it looks like:** "God puts a hedge around those who fear him" — with 1:10 quoted as a promise.
- **Why it's wrong:** 1:10 is spoken by the Accuser, and its point is cynical. The hedge is real (God does not deny it), but it is the thing about to be removed.
- **The corrective:** attach the line to its speaker. The blessing is gift, and the fear of God must be shown to be more than its wage.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

**Open.**

1. **The identity of הַשָּׂטָן** — a heavenly prosecutor or the devil of later revelation? The Hebrew shows a title, access, malice and no independent power; it does not decide. A commentary survey is needed (Clines; Hartley; Longman). `[S]` routed.
