Point & Purpose: Job 3:1–26

Sermon: 2 of the nine unit sermons in the Job series — the third sermon preached, after the introductory sermon on the whole book and Sermon 1 (1:1–3:1) Exegesis: Job/dig-deeper-job-3-1to26 (the solo dig, which is also the unit report, 30 September 2026), with book-overview-job (v0.1.1), the structure claim audit, and the two backbones already made (point-purpose-job-book, point-purpose-job-1-1to3-1) Study text: NASB95 · Pulpit text: ESV (declared 28 September 2026), checked against the Logos export for every note below Date: 30 September 2026

Warrant tags: [T] derivable from the text · [I] a reasonable inference from it · [S] supplied by a secondary source.

Scope

Single sermon on 3:1–26. The unit is set by the text. The Leningrad codex, as BHS prints it, has a petuḥah after 3:1 and after 3:26 and none inside the poem. The poem has three stanzas of 8, 9 and 7 verses (3:3–10, 11–19, 20–26), and the questions mark the turns. [T] Twenty-six verses of poetry can be read whole and preached whole. The chapter should not be split: its meaning lies in the descent from wish, to question, to present turmoil, and in the last verse’s reversal of the rest-words of the middle stanza. [T] for the structure; [I] for the judgement.

3:1 belongs to both sermons. It closes Sermon 1, which ended on the one real curse in the prologue. Read it again here to open. The overlap is deliberate: Sermon 1 heard 3:1 as the end of the silence; Sermon 2 hears it as the start of the speech. [I]

Series context. The congregation has already heard: - the book’s shape, the question “for no reason?” (1:9), and God’s verdict on Job’s speech (42:7), in the introductory sermon; - the councils, the tests and Job’s worship, in Sermon 1, which ended with the friends silent for seven days and Job opening his mouth.

So this sermon need not introduce the book or retell the prologue. It can take one thing for granted: the reader knows that Job’s suffering came “without reason” (2:3, ESV), and Job does not. It spends its time on the thing only chapter 3 can do: letting the congregation hear a righteous man speak his darkest words, and hear that they do not end his faith.

Where the series question stands. “Do you love God — or only what he gives you?” In chapter 3 it is answered by what Job does not say. He has nothing left, and he curses everything he can name — his day, his night, his conception, the light — except God (3:1). He even names God twice, as the one who should not seek that day (3:4) and as the one who “has hedged in” the man (3:23). The Accuser said that a man stripped of God’s gifts would curse God. Job, stripped of them, curses the day he was born and still does not let go of God. [T] for the object of the curse; [I] for the answer to the series question.

Reading. Read the whole chapter aloud, 3:1–26. It takes about three minutes. Read it slowly and do not soften it. The ESV’s three paragraphs (3:3–10, 11–19, 20–26) match the three stanzas, so the structure can be heard as it is read. If a reader other than the preacher reads, brief them on the tone beforehand: this is lament, not recitation.

Fallen Condition Focus

When suffering makes us wish we had never been born, we either bury the wish and fall silent before God, or fear that saying it will sever us from him — and those around us, like Job’s friends, rush to correct what they should first hear.

Where it comes from. It is the unit report’s candidate FCF, “Anchored”, kept word for word. - What the first hearers felt. The collapse of the wish to live under unbearable, unexplained suffering (3:11, 20–22), and the fear that such speech is itself unfaithfulness. [T] for the pattern. - The rush to correct. It is in the text, one verse later: “If one ventures a word with you, will you be impatient? Yet who can keep from speaking?” (4:2, ESV). Eliphaz hears Job’s “roaring” (3:24) as a lion’s, and says it will be broken (4:10). [T] - What they shared with us. Readers of the Writings came to Job 3 straight from the Psalter, with Ps 22 and Ps 88 in their ears, and knew Jeremiah’s cursed birthday (Jer 20:14–18). They knew such speech was spoken by the righteous. We bring two instincts they did not: a therapeutic one that treats the speech as a symptom, and a pious one that treats it as sin. [I] - What differs. The Son has spoken the lament of the forsaken (Mark 15:34) and risen, and he offers rest to the weary (Matt 11:28). [T] for the texts.

Teaching Point

Faith’s darkest speech is not faith’s end: Job, with nothing left, curses the day he was born but not God; he speaks creation backwards, longs for the rest of the dead, and ends in turmoil — and the God he names but does not yet address hears it, will answer it in its own words, and in the Son has cried the forsaken “why” himself and been answered; so the lament of the suffering is safe to speak, and is meant to become prayer.

  • Subject: What becomes of faith when a believer wishes he had never been born?
  • Complement: It is not ended. The curse falls on the day, not on God (3:1). The words are the words of the suffering righteous, prophet and psalmist (Jer 20; Ps 22). God hears them and answers them from the storm in their own vocabulary (38:8, 12, 17). And Christ has spoken the forsaken cry and been answered by resurrection.

A shorter form a hearer could carry home: “You can say the worst thing to God, and not lose him.”

Built from the unit report’s convergent findings: - Headline 1: the curse falls on the day, not God — and Job’s first wish, יְהִי חֹשֶׁךְ (“let there be darkness”, 3:4), turns God’s first word backwards (Gen 1:3). - Headline 2: the lament speaks to no one, names God twice, and becomes prayer at 10:18. - Headline 3: from curse to “why?” to present turmoil; the dead have what Job lacks. - Headline 4: God’s first speech answers the chapter in its own words. - Headline 5: the company of the righteous — Jeremiah 20, Psalm 22.

Why the TP makes God the subject. - The sermon could easily make Job the subject (“Job was honest; be honest”). The text does not allow it. God frames the poem: he is named at 3:4 and 3:23, as the one who should not seek the day and as the one who has hedged in the man. [T] - The book gives God the last word on this speech twice: in the storm, answering its images (38:8 gathers four of its words into one verse), and in the verdict on the man who spoke it (42:7). [T] - The good news is not that lament is healthy. It is that God receives it, answers it, and has himself, in the Son, borne the forsakenness it names. [I]

Purpose Statement

To give the suffering permission to speak their darkest words — and to speak them towards God — because God does not cast off the righteous who curse their day, and has answered their cry in Christ; and to teach the church to listen before it answers. Differentiated by hearer:

  • The one who has wished they had never been born: you are not the first, and you are not faithless. A prophet said it (Jer 20:14) and remained God’s prophet. A righteous man said it (Job 3:3) and God said he spoke rightly (42:7). Say it — and say it to God, as Job learns to (10:18). If the wish has become more than a wish, tell someone today (see Keep Honest 3).
  • The one who hides it: the believer who keeps his darkness from God, and so from prayer. The Psalter and this chapter are God’s own permission and vocabulary. Bring the “why” to God.
  • The listeners — most of us: the friends’ best moment was their seven days of silence (2:13). Their worst began the moment Job spoke and Eliphaz answered (4:2). When someone near you says “I wish I’d never been born”, your first calling is to stay and hear it, not to correct it — and to make sure they are safe.
  • The sceptic, who thinks faith must pretend: the Bible does not hide from despair. It gives despair a voice, then gives God’s answer from the storm, and finally from the cross.

The motive throughout is God’s patience with his servant, and the Son’s cry in our place, not Job’s honesty as a technique. [I]

Candidate Big Question

“Can you say the worst thing to God — and not lose him?”

A longer form for the introduction: “When the pain makes you wish you had never been born, is that the end of your faith — or can you say it, and still be held by God?”

A form for the application to the church: “When someone says it to you, will you answer — or will you listen?”

Candidate. The preacher settles the final wording. It should stand beside the series question as its application to this text: the series asks whether God is loved for himself (1:9); this sermon asks whether that love can survive the cry of a man who has nothing left. The answer, from 3:1, is yes.

Proposed Structure

Three movements that follow the three stanzas, and a short landing. The stanza divisions are the poem’s own, marked by the questions at 3:11 and 3:20 and matched by the ESV’s paragraphs. [T] The descent is the sermon’s shape: wish (3:3–10), question (3:11–19), present (3:20–26). The first three movements should take about three-quarters of the sermon. The landing names the answer; it does not hurry to it.

1. “Let the day perish” (3:1–10)

After seven days of silence, Job opens his mouth — and curses his day, not God. - The narrator’s frame (3:1). “Job opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth.” The verb is קלל (“curse”), the plain word, used here for the first time in the book. Its object is יוֹמוֹ (“his day”). The Accuser said Job would “curse you to your face” (1:11; 2:5). The curse has come, and it has missed its target. [T] - Sixteen wishes, and no one addressed (3:3–9). Let the day perish; let the night that said “A man is conceived” be barren. Every creation-word in these verses — אוֹר (“light”), חֹשֶׁךְ (“darkness”), יוֹם (“day”), לַיְלָה (“night”), שָׁנָה (“year”) — is a word of Genesis 1. [T] - The first wish (3:4). In Hebrew, two words: יְהִי חֹשֶׁךְ (“let there be darkness”). God’s first word was יְהִי אוֹר (“let there be light”, Gen 1:3). Job tries to speak the first day of creation backwards over his own first day. [T] High. - The reason (3:10). “Because it did not shut the doors of my mother’s womb, nor hide trouble from my eyes” (ESV). The curse is a grievance against the day for letting him live. [T]

Hinge: 3:1 against 1:11 and 2:5. The man who blessed the name of the LORD (1:21) now curses the day of his birth, and does both without cursing God. Hold 1:21 and 3:3 together: both are what faith sounds like in the dark. [I]

Colour, if used, as colour: the only other “let there be darkness” in the Hebrew Bible is the ninth plague on Egypt (Exod 10:21). The man God called “my servant” calls down on his birth the darkness God sent on Pharaoh. The link is verified in its words; the design is a pattern held for audit (Keep Honest 5).

Function: raise the FCF. Let the congregation hear the worst words a believer can say about his own life — and show them where the curse falls.

2. “Why did I not die at birth?” (3:11–19)

The curse becomes a question, and the question becomes a picture of the dead at rest. - The questions (3:11–12). “Why did I not die at birth, come out from the womb and expire? Why did the knees receive me? Or why the breasts, that I should nurse?” (ESV). The voice of a child who wishes he had never been fed. [T] - The company Job keeps. Jeremiah, beaten and put in the stocks, cursed his birthday in the same words: “Cursed be the day on which I was born … Why did I come out from the womb?” (Jer 20:14, 18, ESV). מות (“die”) with מֵרֶחֶם (“from the womb”) occurs only at Num 12:12, Jer 20:17 and Job 3:11. The curse on one’s birth is the speech of a faithful prophet in the depths. [T] High for the link; direction open. - The rest of the dead (3:13–19). “For then I would have lain down and been quiet; I would have slept; then I would have been at rest” (3:13, ESV). Kings among their ruins, princes with their gold, the stillborn child, the prisoner who no longer hears the taskmaster, the slave free from his master. Death levels the whole scale: “the small and the great are there” (3:19). [T] - The rest-words. נוח (“rest”, 3:13, 17), שׁקט (“be quiet”, 3:13), and רֹגֶז (“turmoil”), which the wicked “cease from” there (3:17). Hold them: they return in the last verse. [T]

Hinge: the dead have what Job lacks. The stanza is almost tender, and the preacher should let it be — this is how a sufferer imagines the grave. But it is poetry, not doctrine. The same place is abandonment in Ps 88:5 (“like one set loose among the dead”, ESV). Job does not teach that the dead are blessed; he says death would be better than this. [I]

Function: stay with the longing honestly, and show that it has a place in Scripture’s own mouth. The righteous — prophet and psalmist — have said this.

3. “Why is light given?” (3:20–26)

The question widens to every sufferer, narrows to “a man”, and ends with “me”. - Every sufferer (3:20–22). “Why is light given to him who is in misery, and life to the bitter in soul, who long for death, but it comes not, and dig for it more than for hidden treasures?” (ESV). Name what these verses are: a question, addressed to no one, not a plan. Job longs for death; he does not seek to end his life. [T] / [I] — see Keep Honest 3. - A man (3:23). “Why is light given to a man whose way is hidden, whom God has hedged in?” (ESV). גֶּבֶר (“man”) occurs in the chapter only here and at 3:3: the man announced at conception is the man hedged in. And the hedge has turned. The Accuser complained that God had “put a hedge around him” to protect him (1:10); now Job feels God’s hedge as confinement. [T] Moderate–high. - Me (3:24–25). “For my sighing comes instead of my bread, and my groanings are poured out like water. For the thing that I fear comes upon me, and what I dread befalls me” (ESV). Job’s word for “groanings”, שַׁאֲגֹתָי (literally “roarings”), is the word of Ps 22:1 [Heb 22:2], “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? … the words of my groaning” (ESV). [T] - The last line (3:26). “I am not at ease, nor am I quiet; I have no rest, but trouble comes” (ESV). Every rest-word of the middle stanza comes back, negated: נוח, שׁקט, and רֹגֶז, which the wicked ceased from (3:17) and which now comes to Job. Three denials and one arrival. The poem began with a man trying to cancel a day; it ends with a man who cannot stop the days coming. [T]

Hinge: 3:26 against 3:13 and 3:17. The ESV lets the congregation hear it (see the pulpit-text note). Read 3:17 and 3:26 side by side.

Function: stay in the dark long enough to be believed. The chapter ends on “trouble comes”, and what follows it is Eliphaz, not God. Let the sermon feel that before it moves.

4. Landing: the cry God hears (beyond the chapter)

Job does not yet speak to God. But God is listening, and so, at last, is the Son. - The cry becomes prayer. Seven chapters later Job asks the same question again — but now to God: “Why did you bring me out from the womb?” (10:18, ESV). לָמָּה (“why”) + רֶחֶם (“womb”) + יצא (“come out”) in one verse occurs only at Jer 20:18, Job 3:11 and Job 10:18. The lament addressed to no one becomes a lament addressed to God. Name it; Sermon 3 will preach it. [T] High. - God answers in the chapter’s own words. When God speaks from the storm, he does not argue with the curse. He takes Job back to the creation he tried to undo: “Or who shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb?” (38:8, ESV) — the doors and the womb of 3:10–11. “Have you commanded the morning since your days began, and caused the dawn to know its place?” (38:12). “Have you seen the gates of deep darkness?” (38:17). Job himself will confess that God “brings deep darkness to light” (12:22). Name it; Sermon 8 will expound it. [T] High on the words; moderate–high on the design. - The Christ turn. The “why” of the forsaken righteous has been spoken by the Righteous One. “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Mark 15:34, ESV, praying Ps 22:1). He said, “My soul is very sorrowful, even to death” (Matt 26:38). He “offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard” (Heb 5:7). The Father answered him by raising him. And the rest Job looked for in the grave he offers to the weary now: “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matt 11:28). [I] — the typology is moderate (unit report, Christological Reading).

So the lament is safe to speak. Not because lament is healthy, but because the one who hears it has borne the forsakenness it names, and been answered. And the church may sit with the suffering, as the friends did for seven days, without needing to explain.

Function: press the PS. Permission to lament; the turn from lament about God to lament to God; the church taught to listen. End with the permission, not with a tidy resolution.

Keep Honest

  1. Do not preach chapter 3 as Job’s fall from faith.
    • The object of the curse is the day, not God (3:1). The Accuser’s wager is lost here, not won.
    • The narrator gives no verdict at 3:1 (contrast 1:22; 2:10). God’s rebuke in 38:2 is of words “without knowledge”, not of lament. God’s final verdict is that Job spoke rightly (42:7), and this is the man who spoke chapter 3.
    • Do not pronounce the chapter exemplary either. Job’s curse is descriptive, not a technique. Its later form, addressed to God (10:18), is the pattern the book commends.
  2. Do not hurry to the answer, and do not preach it cheerfully.
    • The book makes the reader wait thirty-five chapters for God to speak. Chapter 3 ends on “trouble comes”.
    • Keep the landing short. No “Job teaches us to be honest with our feelings!”
    • Leviathan (3:8) gets a sentence at most. Do not identify him with Satan; the text does not.
  3. Handle the death-wish (3:20–22) with care.
    • Some who hear this sermon will know this longing. Do not skip the verses; do not rebuke the longing as sin; do not speak of death as rest in a way that sounds like comfort to someone who is suicidal.
    • Name the difference between longing for death and acting on it, gently. Commend speaking the darkness to God, as Job does (10:18), and to trusted people.
    • Know where help is before the service — pastoral care, a GP, a helpline — and say plainly that anyone carrying this should tell someone today, without promising what that help will involve. Be aware of who is in the room.
  4. Do not let 3:25 be turned into a law.
    • “What I fear comes upon me” is used in positive-confession teaching: fear attracts calamity. The text says the calamity came “without reason” (2:3, ESV), by permission in heaven.
    • The verse reports Job’s experience; its doubled verb (פַחַד פָּחַדְתִּי, “a dread I dreaded”) intensifies the dread and describes no mechanism. Do not link it to Job’s sacrifices at 1:5 — the words differ.
    • If the congregation is likely to have met the teaching, name it and refute it from 2:3.
  5. Do not answer the lament as Eliphaz did, and do not preach the synthetic links as proven.
    • The sermon must not move at once to what Job should have said, or to reasons for his suffering. That is 4:1ff., and God condemns it (42:7).
    • Exodus 10 at 3:4 (with Exodus 9 at 2:12), גאל (“claim, redeem”) at 3:5 beside 19:25 (“my Redeemer”), and Isa 53:7 (“he opened not his mouth”) against 3:1 are verified in their words but open in their design. Use them as colour if at all, never as the sermon’s hinge.
    • Genesis 1, Jeremiah 20, 10:18 and 38:8 are firm and may bear weight.

Christ-centred check

  1. “Be like Job”, or “trust the Christ whom Job points to”?
    • The TP’s subject is what becomes of faith, and its complement makes God the actor: he hears, answers, and in the Son bears the cry.
    • Job is not held up as a model of honesty to copy, nor as a warning to avoid. His speech is the evidence that God does not cast off the righteous who lament.
    • The one command-shaped application (bring your darkness to God; listen before you answer) flows from God’s patience and the Son’s cry, not from a standard to meet. Pass.
  2. Is Christ the subject or merely an example?
    • Movement 4 presents Christ as the one whose forsakenness makes our lament safe to speak, and whose resurrection answers it — not as a better lamenter to imitate. Pass.
  3. Is the good news grounded in Christ’s saving work?
    • Yes: the forsaken cry borne on the cross (Mark 15:34), the prayer heard and answered by resurrection (Heb 5:7), and rest given to the weary (Matt 11:28). Pass.
    • Caution: no New Testament text cites Job 3. The link runs through Psalm 22, which Job 3:24 shares a rare word with, and through the category of the righteous sufferer. Present it as the canon’s answer to Job’s cry, not as Job’s meaning.

Pulpit-text note (ESV)

Checked against the ESV export for Job 1–4, 10, 12, 14, 38, 39 and 41, and for the parallels (Gen 1:3; Exod 10:21; Ps 22:1, 10; Ps 88:5; Jer 20:14–18; Matt 11:28; 26:38; Mark 15:34; Heb 5:7).

Where the ESV helps the plan. For this chapter the ESV carries more of the findings than the NASB95 — unusually for this project. - 3:26 against 3:13, 17, 18 — movement 3’s hinge. “Been quiet … at rest” (3:13); “cease from troubling … at rest” (3:17); “at ease” (3:18); then “I am not at ease, nor am I quiet; I have no rest, but trouble comes” (3:26). The whole reversal is audible. Note that “at ease” at 3:18 and 3:26 renders two different Hebrew verbs (שׁאן at 3:18, שׁלו at 3:26; WLC lemma): the ESV makes the echo fuller than the Hebrew’s lemma count. Preach it as the English stands, but do not claim a repeated Hebrew word for “ease”. - 3:21–26 — “comes”. “Who long for death, but it comes not … my sighing comes … the thing that I fear comes upon me … trouble comes.” The congregation will hear death refused and everything else arriving. At 3:21 “it comes not” is the ESV’s rendering of “there is none” (NASB95); the Hebrew’s five uses of בוא are at 3:6, 7, 24, 25 and 26. The English chain carries the point; do not attribute the word at 3:21 to the Hebrew. - 3:1 against 1:11 and 2:5 — movement 1’s hinge. The ESV has “he will curse you to your face” (1:11; 2:5) and “cursed the day of his birth” (3:1). In Hebrew the words differ (ברך, the Accuser’s euphemism; קלל, the plain curse), but for this sermon’s point the English contrast is exactly right: the curse came, and it fell on the day. - 3:3 and 3:23 — “a man”. Both are גֶּבֶר; the NASB95 has “a boy” at 3:3. The bookend is in the pew Bible. - 3:23 against 1:10 — “hedged in” / “put a hedge around him”. The turned hedge is audible. - 3:10 against 38:8 — “shut the doors of my mother’s womb” / “shut in the sea with doors when it burst out from the womb”. “Shut”, “doors” and “womb” are all heard again. The NASB95 has “opening” and “enclosed”, which hide it. The landing’s key link is in the pew Bible. - 3:11 against 10:18 and Jer 20:18 — “come out from the womb” / “bring me out from the womb” / “Why did I come out from the womb”. All three audible. - 3:3 against Jer 20:14 — “the day on which I was born”. Word for word. - 3:5 against 12:22 and 38:17 — “deep darkness”. Audible at all three. - 3:9 against 41:18 — “the eyelids of the morning” / “the eyelids of the dawn”. Close; the NASB95’s “the breaking dawn” loses it. - 3:24 against Ps 22:1 — “my groanings” / “the words of my groaning”. The Psalm 22 link is audible in English, and Mark 15:34 (“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”) matches Ps 22:1 word for word. - 3:25 against 4:14 — “what I dread” / “dread came upon me”. Eliphaz’s pickup is audible, if movement 4 mentions him.

Where the congregation will not hear what the plan rests on. - 3:4 — movement 1’s first wish. The ESV has “Let that day be darkness!” (NASB95: “May that day be darkness”). No English version lets the congregation hear יְהִי חֹשֶׁךְ as “let there be darkness”, the reverse of Gen 1:3. Supply it: “Job’s first wish, in Hebrew, is two words: ‘Let there be darkness.’ The first words God spoke over the world were ‘Let there be light.’ Job tries to say the first day of creation backwards over the day he was born.” The ESV’s Exod 10:21 (“that there may be darkness over the land of Egypt”) is not audibly the same either; it is colour only (Keep Honest 5). - 3:1 — “the day of his birth”. The Hebrew has only “his day”, echoing 1:4 (“each one on his day”, which the ESV keeps). Not needed for the plan. - 3:24 — “groanings”. The Hebrew “roarings” is lost, and with it Eliphaz’s “roar of the lion” (4:10, ESV). Optional; mention only if movement 4 uses Eliphaz’s reply. - 3:5 — “claim”. It hides גאל, the redeemer’s verb (19:25). The sense at 3:5 is disputed; optional colour at most (Keep Honest 5). - 3:8 against 41:10 — “rouse up Leviathan” / “dares to stir him up”. The same Hebrew verb; not needed for the plan. - 3:18 against 39:7 — “the voice of the taskmaster” / “the shouts of the driver”. One Hebrew word (נֹגֵשׂ); the ESV keeps “hear not” in both, so the echo is half-audible. Not needed for the plan.

Minor. 3:16 “a hidden stillborn child” (NASB95 “a miscarriage which is discarded”) joins 3:21 “hidden treasures” in English only. 3:6 “rejoice” follows the Masoretic vowels. Neither bears on the plan.

For the NIV84, if the sermon is preached elsewhere. It has “A boy is born!” (3:3), “life” rather than “light” at 3:23 (which blunts the repeated question), “the first rays of dawn” (3:9, losing the eyelids), “the doors of the womb” (3:10, keeping the doors), and “turmoil” at both 3:17 and 3:26 (keeping that pair). Its “be included” (3:6) follows the BHS proposal, not the Masoretic vowels.

Hand-off

How it links to its neighbours. - Back to Sermon 1, which ended on 3:1. Open by re-reading it. - Forward to Sermon 3 (4:1–14:22), which begins with Eliphaz’s reply (4:2) and contains 10:18, where the question of 3:11 is turned to God. Movement 4 names both without preaching them. - Forward to Sermon 8 (38:1–42:6), where God answers chapter 3 in its own words. Movement 4 names 38:8, 12 and 17; Sermon 8 will expound them. - To the introductory sermon’s “two ways of speaking”. Chapter 3 is speech about God, not yet to him — the raw material of prayer. The book backbone promised that Sermon 2 would show “a lament addressed to no one that becomes a lament to God”; this plan shows the first half and points to the second.
