## Scope

**Single sermon on 4:1–14:22: the first round of the dialogue.** The series has already fixed one sermon for this unit. It is far longer than the usual limit for a single sermon (273 verses, eleven chapters), so the decision needs a reason.

**Why one sermon is the right decision.**
- **The round's meaning is in the exchange, not in the six speeches taken one by one.** The speakers take up one another's words throughout. Bildad's first line answers Job's last image (6:26 → 8:2). Zophar quotes Job's hymn back at him (9:11–12 → 11:10). Job's tree rewrites Bildad's plant (8:16–17 → 14:7–8). `[T]` (unit report, Headline 3)
- **Two threads run the length of the round.** One is hope, which brackets it from 4:6 to 14:19. The other is call and answer, which runs from 5:1 to 14:15. `[T]`
- **Splitting the round would cut both threads.** It would also invite the commonest failure with these chapters, a tour of the speeches (Keep Honest 1). `[I]`

**The text marks the unit.** BHS sets a petuḥah only at the ends of speeches, six in all. The round closes at 14:22, where Eliphaz begins again (15:1). `[T]`

**If the series is ever re-planned.** The fallback is three sermons, each a friend's speech with Job's reply: 4–7, 8–10 and 11–14. Even then the hope bracket would have to be named in each. `[I]`

**Series context.** By this point the congregation has heard:
- the book's shape, the question "for no reason?" (1:9, ESV), God's verdict "you have not spoken of me what is right" (42:7, ESV) and the "two ways of speaking", in the introductory sermon;
- the councils, the tests and the friends' seven silent days (2:13), in Sermon 1;
- Job's curse on his day, and the promise that its question would be turned to God (10:18), in Sermon 2.

So this sermon need not introduce the friends or retell the prologue. Its own task is to let the congregation hear the friends' answer, and Job's refusal of it. It should also keep a promise Sermon 2 made, that "the lament addressed to no one becomes a lament addressed to God" (10:18). `[I]`

**Where the series question stands.** *"Do you love God — or only what he gives you?"* In this round it is pressed from an unexpected side: by the friends.
- **What they offer.** Each friend offers Job his life back if he will seek God rightly.
  - Bildad: "If you will seek God … surely then he will rouse himself for you and restore your rightful habitation … your latter days will be very great" (8:5–7, ESV).
  - Zophar: "You will forget your misery" (11:16, ESV).
- **What that would mean.** Their counsel amounts to seeking God so as to have the gifts back. That is the very piety the Accuser said Job had (1:9–11). `[I]` *Moderate.* This reading is the backbone's own, and it is not one of the report's Headlines.
- **What Job wants instead.** Job refuses to confess what he has not done (6:29–30; 13:18). He asks for God himself: "But I would speak to the Almighty" (13:3, ESV); "Oh that you would … remember me!" (14:13, ESV). `[T]` for the words.
- **The irony.** Bildad's "beginning" and "latter days" return at the end of the book, in the narrator's voice: "the LORD blessed the latter days of Job more than his beginning" (42:12, ESV). The promise comes true, but not by the route Bildad laid down. רֵאשִׁית ("beginning") with אַחֲרִית ("latter end") occurs in Job only at 8:7 and 42:12 (WLC, lemma). `[T]` for the pair. The book overview already carries this in its Echo Table ("ironically fulfilled", *high*); the lemma check here confirms it.

**Reading.** Do not try to read the round. Read two passages, about four minutes in all, and read other verses within the movements.
- **4:1–9: Eliphaz's opening.** It includes "who can keep from speaking?" (4:2), "your hope" (4:6) and "who that was innocent ever perished?" (4:7).
- **13:20–14:22: Job's closing prayer.** It runs from "Then call, and I will answer" (13:22) to "so you destroy the hope of man" (14:19).

The two readings put the hope bracket in the congregation's ears before the sermon begins. If a second reader is used, give Eliphaz's lines a reasonable, pastoral tone, not a pompous one (Keep Honest 2).

## Fallen Condition Focus

**When suffering comes, we either reach for an explanation that blames the sufferer, or we lose hope that God will ever answer — and we are tempted to stop speaking to him.**

**Where it comes from.** This is the unit report's candidate FCF (Original Audience Reception), kept word for word. The report rates it "anchored in the text": the call-and-answer and hope chains anchor it, and the "we" is inferred.

**What the first hearers felt.** Their own Bible's answer did not fit the sufferer in front of them.
- **The friends speak the Bible's own words.** Eliphaz speaks the Song of Moses: "For he wounds, but he binds up" (5:18, ESV; Deut 32:39). He speaks Proverbs' saying on discipline (5:17; Prov 3:11–12) and the Psalter's comfort: "So the poor have hope, and injustice shuts her mouth" (5:16, ESV; Ps 107:42). `[S: audit]` for the ratings.
- **They apply them as a verdict.** "If your children have sinned against him, he has delivered them into the hand of their transgression" (8:4, ESV). The reader knows otherwise (1:5; 2:3). `[T]`
- **The sufferer's prayers seem to go unheard.** "If I summoned him and he answered me, I would not believe that he was listening to my voice" (9:16, ESV). "Why do you hide your face?" (13:24, ESV). `[T]`

**What they shared with us.** Both reflexes.
- **The friends' reflex** is the habit of explaining suffering, and our own way of quoting single verses at it.
- **Job's despair** is the fear that God will not answer, which ends in silence towards him. `[I]`

**What differs.** We know that God answered Job: "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (38:1, ESV). We know that God "has spoken to us by his Son" (Heb 1:2), and that "all who are in the tombs will hear his voice" (John 5:28). `[T]` for the texts.

**How the round answers it.** The FCF names two temptations, and the round shows a third way.
- **The friends,** who explain and never pray. `[T]`
- **Silence,** which Job never chooses. `[T]`
- **Job's way:** he goes on speaking to the God who seems to destroy his hope. `[T]` for the address (Headline 2)

## Teaching Point

**Hope in suffering does not rest on a formula spoken about God — "the integrity of your ways your hope" (4:6) — but on God himself, the God who calls and answers. Job refuses his friends' explanation. He goes on speaking to the God who seems to destroy his hope, and longs for the day when "you would call, and I would answer you" (14:15). That God answered him from the whirlwind, and in his Son calls the dead by name. So the sufferer may keep speaking to God, and wait for his call.**

- **Subject:** Where does a sufferer's hope come from?
- **Complement:** It does not come from a formula that ties hope to the sufferer's record and speaks for God to him (4:6–7; 8:5–7; 13:7). It comes from God's call: Job longs for it (14:15), God answers (38:1), and in Christ God speaks the word that raises the dead (John 5:25).

A shorter form a hearer could carry home: *"Hope doesn't come from having the explanation. It comes from the God who calls."*

**Built from** the unit report's convergent findings.
- **Headline 1:** hope is the round's Leitwort. It brackets the round from Eliphaz's "your hope" (4:6) to Job's "you destroy the hope of man" (14:19).
- **Headline 2:** call and answer, from "Call now; is there anyone who will answer you?" (5:1) to "You would call, and I would answer you" (14:15). No friend ever speaks to God, and every speech of Job's ends addressed to God.
- **Headline 3:** the round is built out of one another's words. The friends hand Job's lines back as accusations, and Job hands theirs back as protest.
- **Headline 4:** the lawsuit, "Can mortal man be in the right before God?" (4:17, ESV), ending with "I know that I shall be in the right" (13:18, ESV) and "Will you speak falsely for God?" (13:7, ESV).
- **Headline 5:** Scripture on both sides. The friends' texts are true, and their verdict is false.

**Why the TP makes God the subject.**
- **The easy versions put a human at the centre.** "Don't be like the friends" makes the sermon a lecture on bad counsel, and "Be honest like Job" makes Job a technique. Both have a human subject. `[I]`
- **The round's own question is about God.** It asks whether God will answer: 5:1; 9:16; 13:22; 14:15. `[T]`
- **The book answers it with God's action.** "Then the LORD answered Job" (38:1, ESV). `[T]`
- **The good news is not that lament is healthy or that the friends were wrong.** It is that the God who seemed to destroy hope is the God who calls, and has called in Christ. `[I]`

## Purpose Statement

**To move hearers from explaining suffering, their own or other people's, and from falling silent before God, to speaking to God in hope of his call — because God answers, and in Christ has called the dead to life; and to teach the church to comfort as Job asked to be comforted, not as the friends did.**

Differentiated by hearer:

- **The sufferer who has stopped praying:** Job's prayers were not answered within the round, and he went on praying them. Speak to God as Job did, about the hope you feel God has taken: "Remember that my life is a breath" (7:7, ESV). Your prayer is not answered yet, but it is not unheard. God answered Job (38:1), and he has spoken his last word in his Son.
- **The one who has been told it is their fault:** the bereaved, or the person told that their illness or loss reveals hidden sin. Bildad's 8:4 and Zophar's 11:6 are in the Bible as the error God condemns (42:7). Hear them as that, and hear God call Job "my servant" (42:8).
- **The comforter — most of us:** the friends' best moment was silence (2:13). Their failure began when they explained. Job asks for kindness, not theology: "He who withholds kindness from a friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty" (6:14, ESV). Do not speak for God to the sufferer. Speak to God with him, and pray for him, as Job will pray for his friends (42:8, 10).
- **The sceptic,** who thinks believers must have neat answers: here the Bible lets a man accuse God for chapters on end, and condemns the men with the answers. The answer it gives at last is a Person who speaks, not a formula.

The motive throughout is **God's faithfulness to call and answer, sealed in the Son**. It is not the moral that the friends were unkind, nor that Job was brave. `[I]`

## Candidate Big Question

**"When God seems to have taken your hope, will you explain it — or keep speaking to him?"**

A longer form for the introduction: *"Where does hope come from when the explanations don't fit and God doesn't answer?"*

A form for the application to the church: *"When someone is suffering, will you speak to them for God — or to God with them?"*

*Candidate.* The preacher settles the final wording.
- **How it applies the series question.** The series asks whether God is loved for himself (1:9). This sermon asks whether a man can go on speaking to God when God has taken even hope. The friends offer hope on condition of restoration; Job asks for God.
- **The answer from the round:** Job keeps speaking.
- **The answer from the book:** God answers.

## Proposed Structure

The structure has four movements and a landing. It follows the round's own movement, not the order of its six speeches.
- It begins with the friends' answer (1).
- It moves through Job's turn to God (2) to the two ways of speaking face to face (3).
- It ends at Job's last prayer, where hope and its destruction stand side by side (4).

Each movement draws on several speeches, so the exchange can be heard (Keep Honest 1). Movements 1 and 2 overlap in time: the friends and Job alternate throughout the round. The order is the order of the argument. The first four movements should take about four-fifths of the sermon.

### 1. "Is not … the integrity of your ways your hope?" — the friends' answer (4:1–5:27, with 8:1–22 and 11:1–20)

*The friends speak true words about God, and turn them into a verdict on Job.*
- **Eliphaz begins gently (4:2–6).** "If one ventures a word with you, will you be impatient? Yet who can keep from speaking?" (4:2, ESV). He reminds Job that he once comforted others (4:3–4). Then he gives Job the formula: "Is not your fear of God your confidence, and the integrity of your ways your hope?" (4:6, ESV). Hope rests on Job's record. `[T]`
- **The doctrine (4:7–8).** "Remember: who that was innocent ever perished? Or where were the upright cut off? As I have seen, those who plow iniquity and sow trouble reap the same" (ESV). This is true as a proverb (Prov 22:8), and false as a verdict on this man (1:8; 2:3). `[T]`; `[S: audit]` for Prov 22:8.
- **The taunt (5:1).** "Call now; is there anyone who will answer you? To which of the holy ones will you turn?" (ESV). Eliphaz assumes that no one will answer Job's cry. Hold the line: the whole round will turn on it. `[T]`
- **True Scripture, wrongly applied (5:17–18).** "Behold, blessed is the one whom God reproves … For he wounds, but he binds up" (ESV). These are the words of Prov 3:11–12 and Deut 32:39, spoken accurately. Let the friends sound wise (Keep Honest 2). `[S: audit]` for the ratings.
- **Bildad's conditions (8:4–7).** "If your children have sinned against him … If you will seek God … if you are pure and upright, surely then he will rouse himself for you … your latter days will be very great" (ESV). The first condition is the cruellest line in the round, and the reader knows it is false. The words of Job's fear for his sons at 1:5 return in it: חטא ("sin") with בֵּן ("son") occurs in Job only at 1:5 and 8:4 (lemma). `[T]`
- **Zophar's verdict (11:6, 13–20).** "Know then that God exacts of you less than your guilt deserves" (11:6, ESV). Then comes the promise: "And you will feel secure, because there is hope" (11:18, ESV). `[T]`
- **Every word is to Job, none to God.** Every second-person form in the friends' three speeches is singular and addressed to Job: Eliphaz 40, Bildad 17, Zophar 33 (WLC morphology). The friends speak about God to Job; they never speak to God. `[T]`

*Hinge:* 4:6 against 5:1. Hope tied to the sufferer's record, and a God who will not answer the sufferer's call. Those two lines are the friends' whole theology, and the rest of the round answers both.

*Colour, if used:* the friends tell Job to remember, and Job asks God to. Eliphaz says "Remember: who that was innocent ever perished?" (4:7, ESV), and Zophar says "you will remember it as waters that have passed away" (11:16, ESV). Job turns the word to God: "Remember that my life is a breath" (7:7); "Remember that you have made me like clay" (10:9); "and remember me!" (14:13, all ESV). זכר ("remember") occurs in the round only at these five verses, in five of the six speeches (WLC, lemma). `[T]` The English carries every one.

*Function:* raise the first half of the FCF, the explanation that blames the sufferer. The congregation should recognise itself in the friends before it sees what is wrong with them.

### 2. "My days … come to their end without hope" — Job turns to God (6:1–7:21; 9:1–10:22)

*Job refuses the friends' answer and, for the first time, speaks to God.*
- **Hope inverted (6:8–9).** "Oh that I might have my request, and that God would fulfill my hope, that it would please God to crush me" (ESV). Job's only hope is to die. `[T]`
- **What he asks of his friends (6:14, 26).** "He who withholds kindness from a friend forsakes the fear of the Almighty" (6:14, ESV). This uses Eliphaz's word, "fear of God" (4:6). Then: "Do you think that you can reprove words, when the speech of a despairing man is wind?" (6:26, ESV). Bildad's first line throws the image back: "the words of your mouth [are] a great wind" (8:2, ESV). אֵמֶר ("word") with רוּחַ ("wind") occurs only at Job 6:26, 8:2 and Prov 17:27 (lemma). `[T]`
- **Without hope (7:6).** "My days are swifter than a weaver's shuttle and come to their end without hope" (ESV). Zophar's last line turns it on the wicked: "their hope is to breathe their last" (11:20, ESV). כלה ("come to an end, fail") with תִּקְוָה ("hope") occurs only at Job 7:6 and 11:20 in the Hebrew Bible (lemma). What Job said of his own days, Zophar says of the godless. `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the intent.
- **The first words to God (7:7).** "Remember that my life is a breath; my eye will never again see good" (ESV). From here to the end of chapter 7 Job speaks to God, ending "you will seek me, but I shall not be" (7:21, ESV). Bildad picks up the verb: "If you will seek God" (8:5, ESV). `[T]`
- **The lawsuit he cannot win (9:2, 16, 33).**
  - Eliphaz's vision asked, "Can mortal man be in the right before God?" (4:17, ESV). Job makes it his own question: "how can a man be in the right before God?" (9:2, ESV).
  - He cannot get a hearing: "If I summoned him and he answered me, I would not believe that he was listening to my voice" (9:16, ESV).
  - He has no one to stand between: "There is no arbiter between us, who might lay his hand on us both" (9:33, ESV).
  - Read 9:33 as the longing it is under either reading (see the pulpit-text note). The Masoretic text has לֹא ("there is no"). Some manuscripts, the Greek and the Syriac read לוּ ("would that"), as BHS records. Swete has εἴθε ἦν ὁ μεσίτης ἡμῶν ("would that there were our mediator"). `[T]` for the readings.
  - `[T]`
- **The question of chapter 3, now asked of God (10:18).** "Why did you bring me out from the womb? Would that I had died before any eye had seen me" (ESV). In Sermon 2 the "why" of 3:11 was addressed to no one. Here it is addressed to "you". לָמָּה ("why") with רֶחֶם ("womb") and יצא ("come out") in one verse occurs only at Jer 20:18, Job 3:11 and Job 10:18 (Job 3 dig). `[T]` *High.*

*Hinge:* 7:7 and 10:18. Job's lament is now spoken to God. The friends' doctrine has driven him not away from God but towards him, with complaint. This is what the book will call speaking "what is right" (42:7). `[T]` for the address; `[I]` for the link to 42:7.

*Function:* raise the second half of the FCF, the loss of hope that God will answer, and show that Job does not choose the silence that loss invites. Keep the Sermon 2 promise.

### 3. "Will you speak falsely for God?" — the two ways of speaking face to face (12:1–13:19)

*Job sets the two ways side by side: speaking for God to the sufferer, or speaking to God about the suffering.*
- **The friends' laughter turned (12:4).** Bildad promised, "He will yet fill your mouth with laughter" (8:21, ESV). Job answers: "I am a laughingstock to my friends; I, who called to God and he answered me, a just and blameless man, am a laughingstock" (12:4, ESV). שְׂחוֹק ("laughter", "laughingstock") occurs in Job only at 8:21 and 12:4 (lemma). `[T]`
- **The friends' Scripture answered (12:7–25).** Bildad said, "inquire, please, of bygone ages" (8:8, ESV). Job: "But ask the beasts, and they will teach you" (12:7, ESV). Job's hymn of God's power (12:13–25) takes up Eliphaz's hymn (5:9–16) and turns it. Eliphaz's God "gives rain on the earth"; Job's God "sends them out, and they overwhelm the land" (5:10; 12:15, ESV). Eliphaz's God makes the crafty "grope at noonday"; Job's God makes the leaders of the earth "grope in the dark without light" (5:14; 12:25, ESV). Job's God makes fools of the wise, the friends included. `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the turn. *Moderate–high.*
- **The two ways (13:3–8).**
  - **To God:** "But I would speak to the Almighty, and I desire to argue my case with God" (13:3, ESV).
  - **About the friends' speech:** "As for you, you whitewash with lies; worthless physicians are you all. Oh that you would keep silent, and it would be your wisdom!" (13:4–5, ESV).
  - **For God:** "Will you speak falsely for God and speak deceitfully for him? Will you show partiality toward him? Will you plead the case for God?" (13:7–8, ESV).
  - `[T]`
- **Job's warning, fulfilled at the end (13:10).** "He will surely rebuke you if in secret you show partiality" (ESV). God does: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7, ESV). `[T]`
- **Hope, and the case (13:15–19).** "Though he slay me, I will hope in him; yet I will argue my ways to his face" (13:15, ESV). "Behold, I have prepared my case; I know that I shall be in the right" (13:18, ESV). The vision's question, "in the right before God" (4:17), ends in Job's mouth as a confidence. Then comes the Servant's question: "Who is there who will contend with me?" (13:19, ESV; Isa 50:8). `[T]`; `[S: audit]` for Isa 50.

*Hinge:* 13:3 against 13:7, "to the Almighty" against "for God". The ESV carries both prepositions. This is the book backbone's hinge for movement 2, and this is the text it comes from. `[T]`

*Function:* state the TP's central contrast. Speech for God, which explains, is condemned; speech to God, which complains, is vindicated. The point is not that complaint is good. It is that God is the one to be spoken to.

### 4. "You would call, and I would answer you" — hope at the edge of the grave (13:20–14:22)

*Job's last prayer in the round: the longing for God's call, and the charge that God destroys hope, both spoken to God.*
- **The call he asks for (13:22–24).** "Then call, and I will answer; or let me speak, and you reply to me" (13:22, ESV). Then the lament of Psalm 44: "Why do you hide your face and count me as your enemy?" (13:24, ESV; Ps 44:24). `[T]`
- **Man and the tree (14:1–12).** "Man who is born of a woman is few of days and full of trouble" (14:1, ESV). "For there is hope for a tree, if it be cut down, that it will sprout again" (14:7, ESV). Bildad had pictured the godless as a plant torn up while "out of the soil others will spring" (8:16–19, ESV). Job's tree, cut down, sprouts again; man does not: "so a man lies down and rises not again" (14:12, ESV). יוֹנֶקֶת ("shoots") with שֹׁרֶשׁ ("root") occurs in Job only at 8:16–17 and 14:7–8 (lemma). `[T]`
- **The longing (14:13–15).** "Oh that you would hide me in Sheol, that you would conceal me until your wrath be past, that you would appoint me a set time, and remember me! If a man dies, shall he live again? All the days of my service I would wait, till my renewal should come. You would call, and I would answer you; you would long for the work of your hands" (ESV). `[T]`
  - The death-wish of 6:9 ("crush me") has become a wish to be kept and remembered. `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the movement.
  - Eliphaz's taunt, "Call now; is there anyone who will answer you?" (5:1), is answered by a longing: the one who would call is God. `[I]`
- **The fall (14:18–22).** "The waters wear away the stones; the torrents wash away the soil of the earth; so you destroy the hope of man" (14:19, ESV). The round ends in pain and mourning (14:22). `[T]`
  - The last תִּקְוָה ("hope") of the round is in Job's mouth, and it is God who destroys it (WLC, lemma 8615 b). The first was in Eliphaz's mouth, and it rested on Job's integrity (4:6).

*Hinge:* 14:15 against 14:19. The longing and the accusation stand four verses apart, and both are spoken to God. The round does not resolve them. The sermon should not either, until the landing. `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the hinge.

*Function:* let the question stand: "If a man dies, shall he live again?" Show that the only hope Job can name is God's call, which only God can give.

### Landing: the God who calls (beyond the round)

*Eliphaz said no one would answer. God did.*
- **The answer from the whirlwind.** "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (38:1, ESV). It is the very thing the friends said would not happen (5:1). God reverses the order Job proposed: "I will question you, and you make it known to me" (38:3, ESV). Name it; Sermon 8 will expound it. `[T]`
- **The verdict.** God calls the friends wrong and Job "my servant". The friends go to Job, and "my servant Job shall pray for you" (42:7–8, ESV). The man who spoke to God now speaks to God for the men who spoke for God. "And the LORD restored the fortunes of Job, when he had prayed for his friends" (42:10, ESV). Bildad's "latter days" come, from God's hand, after Job has prayed (42:12). `[T]` for the words; `[I]` for the irony.
- **The Christ turn.** The call Job longed for has been spoken.
  - **The call:** "an hour is coming, and is now here, when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live" (John 5:25). At a tomb, Jesus "cried out with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come out'" (John 11:43).
  - **The arbiter Job lacked (9:33):** "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5).
  - **The courtroom Job feared:** the Servant's "Who will contend with me?" (Isa 50:8) becomes the church's "Who shall bring any charge against God's elect? It is God who justifies" (Rom 8:33).
  - **The hope that God made:** the hope Job said God destroys (14:19) is the hope God has made, "a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" (1 Pet 1:3).
  - `[I]`: the trajectory is *moderate–high* (call; mediator) and the Servant type *moderate* (unit report, Christological Reading).

So the sufferer's hope does not rest on the integrity of their ways. Nor does it rest on finding the explanation. It rests on the God who calls, and who has called in Christ. Keep speaking to him: his call will come.

*Function:* press the PS. End with the call and the invitation to keep praying, not with a lecture on comforters. The church's application, to comfort by praying with the sufferer, flows from the gospel and does not replace it.

## Keep Honest

1. **One sermon, not six summaries, and not the whole round read aloud.**
   - The meaning of the round is in the exchange (Headlines 1–3). A tour of "what Eliphaz said, what Bildad said, what Zophar said" loses it.
   - Preach the two threads, and show the speeches answering one another along them.
   - Choose the verses each movement needs. The plan above has more than any one sermon can use. The hinges are 4:6 with 5:1, then 7:7 with 10:18, then 13:3 with 13:7, then 14:15 with 14:19.
2. **Quote the friends with their speaker, and let them sound wise.**
   - Never offer the friends' lines as promises. "He will yet fill your mouth with laughter" (8:21) and "because there is hope" (11:18) are verdicts on Job by men God rebukes (42:7), and Job answers both in the round (12:4; 14:19).
   - Do not caricature the friends either. They speak the Song of Moses, Proverbs and the Psalter accurately. Their error is the application. A congregation that laughs at them will not see itself in them.
3. **Handle 8:4 and 11:6, and the death-wish, with care.**
   - **8:4 and 11:6.** Any hint that suffering or bereavement reveals hidden sin is the book's central warning (1:5; 2:3; 42:7). Name 8:4 as the error the book exposes, never as Scripture's word to the bereaved. Be aware of who is in the room.
   - **The death-wish (6:8–9; 7:15–16; 10:18).** Treat it as Sermon 2 did. Do not skip it and do not rebuke it as sin. Show that Job speaks it to God, and that the round moves from "crush me" (6:9) to "remember me" (14:13). Say plainly that anyone carrying such thoughts should tell someone today. Do not promise what that help will involve.
4. **"Job was patient" — or "Job lost his faith": neither.**
   - Job's faith is shown in the address, not in calm. He speaks to God at the end of every speech. God will call that speech "right" (42:7).
   - Preach 14:19 ("you destroy the hope of man") as lament, not as doctrine, and do not soften it. The honest reading holds both 38:2 ("words without knowledge") and 42:7 ("what is right"). The content is limited; the address is right.
   - James's "steadfastness of Job" (Jas 5:11) is holding on, not serenity. The Greek of 14:19 has ὑπομονή ("steadfastness") for the hope God destroys. It is colour at most: James does not quote the verse.
5. **Do not answer 14:14 too fast, and do not make 14:13–15 a resurrection creed.**
   - The round ends on hope destroyed (14:19) and on pain (14:22). Let the question stand until the landing.
   - 14:13 asks to be hidden in Sheol "until your wrath be past". It is a longing within the old horizon, not a doctrine of resurrection.
   - The ESV's "You would call, and I would answer you" (14:15) is a wish. The NASB95 makes it a confidence ("You will call"). The Hebrew allows both. Preach it as longing. The canon supplies the certainty (John 5:25), and Job does not yet have it.
6. **Hold each thread at the strength it has been measured at.**
   - **Hope (תִּקְוָה) may bear the sermon's weight.** A chance check (WLC; the 1,070 verses of Job; a sliding window of 273 verses, the size of the round) tested every lemma that occurs in 8 to 20 verses of Job, 148 lemmas in all.
     - Only 8 put three-quarters of their verses into some window.
     - Only 4 occur in at least five of the six speeches.
     - **Hope is the only lemma that does both.** The design claim is above chance: *moderate–high* (queue #52).
   - **Call and answer (קרא + ענה) is the round's question, not a hidden structure.** 5 of its 6 verses in Job fall in the round. That is noticeable, but common: about one pair in six of similar frequency shows as much. Its strength is what it does: Eliphaz's taunt (5:1) is fulfilled at 38:1. Preach it as the question the round asks, not as a statistical spine. *Moderate.*
   - **Colour, not hinges.** The Servant's courtroom (Isa 50:8–9 at 13:19, 28), the contest over Deut 32:39 and Ps 107, and Jas 5:11's ὑπομονή are verified in their words but open in their design. Use them as colour, never as the hinge of a movement.

## Christ-centred check

1. **"Be like Job", or "trust the Christ whom Job points to"?**
   - The TP's subject is the source of hope, and its complement makes God the actor: he calls, answers and raises.
   - Job is not held up as a model of persistence to copy. His speech to God shows where hope must look.
   - The friends are not held up only as a warning. Their failure shows a formula in God's place.
   - The applications (keep speaking to God; comfort by praying with the sufferer) flow from God's call in Christ, not from a standard to meet. **Pass.**
2. **Is Christ the subject or merely an example?**
   - The landing presents Christ as the voice that calls the dead, the mediator Job lacked, and the one whose resurrection is the living hope. He is not a better sufferer to imitate. **Pass.**
3. **Is the good news grounded in Christ's saving work?**
   - Yes. The mediator's work (1 Tim 2:5), the justified believer's acquittal (Rom 8:33–34) and the hope made by resurrection (1 Pet 1:3) are its ground. **Pass.**
   - *Caution:* no New Testament text cites Job 4–14. The links run through the categories the round raises: the call (14:15), the arbiter (9:33), the courtroom (13:19) and hope (14:19). Present them as the canon's answer to Job's longing, not as the meaning of his words.

## Pulpit-text note (ESV)

Checked against the ESV export for Job 1–2, 4–14, 38 and 42, and for the parallels (Deut 32:39; Ps 39:13; Ps 44:24; Ps 107:40, 42; Isa 50:8–9). The New Testament quotations are checked against the ESV New Testament exports (Heb 1:2; John 5:25, 28; 11:43; Rom 8:33; 1 Tim 2:5; 1 Pet 1:3; Jas 5:11).

**Where the ESV helps the plan.** For this unit the ESV carries the main thread better than the NASB95.
- **The hope chain — the TP's subject.** All nine verses with תִּקְוָה ("hope") have "hope" in the ESV: 4:6; 5:16; 6:8; 7:6; 8:13; 11:18; 11:20; 14:7; 14:19. The NASB95 has "longing" at 6:8, which hides Job's "God would fulfill my hope" (ESV). **The bracket 4:6 → 14:19 can be heard from the pew Bible.**
  - The ESV also has "hope" at 6:19, a verb of the same root, and at 13:15, a different verb (יחל, "wait, hope"), read with the qere (Keep Honest 4). So the congregation will hear eleven "hopes", all in the right places. Do not claim the Hebrew word at 6:19 or 13:15.
- **"In the right" — the lawsuit.** "Can mortal man be in the right before God?" (4:17); "how can a man be in the right before God?" (9:2); "a man full of talk be judged right" (11:2); "I know that I shall be in the right" (13:18). **The whole צדק chain is audible.** The NASB95 has "just", "right", "acquitted" and "vindicated", which hides it.
- **13:3 against 13:7 — movement 3's hinge.** "But I would speak to the Almighty" against "Will you speak falsely for God?" Both prepositions are audible.
- **The "remember" chain.** "Remember" at 4:7; 7:7; 10:9; "you will remember it" at 11:16; "and remember me!" at 14:13. All five are audible.
- **6:26 against 8:2.** "The speech of a despairing man is wind" and "the words of your mouth be a great wind". Audible.
- **7:6 against 11:20.** "Without hope" and "their hope is to breathe their last". Audible.
- **9:12 against 11:10.** "Who can turn him back?" in both verses, word for word. If Zophar's quotation of Job is used, it can be heard.
- **8:16 against 14:7.** "His shoots spread over his garden" and "its shoots will not cease". Bildad's plant and Job's tree share the word in English as in Hebrew.
- **8:21 against 12:4.** "Fill your mouth with laughter" and "a laughingstock". Audible.
- **8:7 against 42:12.** "Your beginning … your latter days" and "the latter days of Job more than his beginning". Audible.
- **7:1 against 14:14.** "Hard service" and "the days of my service"; "a hired hand" at 7:1 and 14:6. The bracket of Job's speeches is half audible. 10:17's "fresh troops" hides the middle link (צָבָא).
- **The parallels.**
  - 13:24 against Ps 44:24: "Why do you hide your face?", word for word.
  - 5:18 and 10:7 against Deut 32:39: "he wounds, but he binds up" and "I wound and I heal"; "there is none to deliver out of your hand" and "there is none that can deliver out of my hand". Both halves of the contested verse are audible.
  - 13:19 against Isa 50:8: "Who … will contend with me?", word for word.

**Where the congregation will not hear what the plan rests on.**
- **14:15 — movement 4's hinge.** The ESV has "You would call, and I would answer you; you would long for the work of your hands": a wish, continuing the "Oh that" of 14:13. The NASB95 has "You will call, and I will answer You", a statement.
  - The Hebrew verbs allow either, and the ESV's reading is defensible.
  - The plan already preaches 14:15 as longing (Keep Honest 5), so the ESV fits it. But do not say "Job is sure that God will call".
- **9:16 — "summoned".** The ESV has "If I summoned him and he answered me". The NASB95's "If I called" keeps the call-and-answer chain, and the ESV breaks it at its darkest point.
  - **Supply it** if the chain is traced: "The word for 'summoned' is the same word as 'call' in 5:1 and 13:22."
- **12:4 — "I, who called to God and he answered me".** The ESV makes it first person; the Hebrew has the third person ("the one who called on God", NASB95). The Greek lacks the line.
  - The congregation will hear Job testify that God used to answer him. That is a fair reading of the speaker's intent, but do not build on the first person.
- **6:14 — kindness.** The ESV makes the friend who withholds kindness the one who "forsakes the fear of the Almighty". The NASB95 reads the Hebrew of the despairing man, who needs kindness "so that he does not forsake the fear of the Almighty". The Hebrew allows both.
  - Movement 2's point stands either way: Job asks for kindness. But do not say the verse rebukes the friends in so many words.
- **10:15 → 11:15 → 13:8, 10 → 42:8 — lifting the face.** The ESV has "lift up my head", "lift up your face", "show partiality" and "accept his prayer". The idiom נשׂא פָּנִים ("lift the face") that links Job's warning to God's acceptance of Job is lost.
  - Optional colour. If used, say it: "The words God uses — 'I will accept him' — are literally 'I will lift his face', the very phrase Job used when he warned the friends against showing God partiality."
- **8:8 against 12:7 — "inquire, please" and "ask".** The Hebrew's שְׁאַל־נָא ("please ask") is the same at both verses. The ESV and NASB95 both lose the parody. Not needed for the plan.

**Minor.**
- 7:20 follows the *tiqqun sopherim* and the Greek ("a burden to you"); the Masoretic text has "to myself". Mention only if the verse is read.
- 6:21 follows the ketiv ("you have now become nothing").
- 13:15 follows the qere ("I will hope in him"); the ketiv reads "I have no hope". Preach the whole verse.
- None bears on the plan.

**9:33 — "There is no arbiter" or "Would that there were an arbiter".**
- **The readings.** The ESV and NASB95 follow the Masoretic לֹא ("there is no"). BHS records לוּ ("would that") in some manuscripts, in the Greek and in the Syriac, with a cross-reference to 16:4. Swete has εἴθε ἦν ὁ μεσίτης ἡμῶν ("would that there were our mediator"). The NIV84 follows לוּ: "If only there were someone to arbitrate between us".
- **What still needs checking.** The project plan asked for a Logos check on this verse before this backbone: the manuscript spread, and a commentary. The BHS apparatus above answers the first half. A commentary has not yet been consulted.
- **Why the plan does not wait on it.** Movement 2 and the landing use 9:33 as Job's longing for someone to stand between him and God. That holds under either reading. Do not make anything turn on "there is no" as a denial. If the sermon is preached from the NIV84, the longing is explicit.

**For the NIV84, if the sermon is preached elsewhere.**
- **It carries the hope chain fully.** All nine verses have "hope", with three more at 6:11, 6:19 and 13:15.
- **It breaks call and answer worse than the ESV.** It has "summoned" at 9:16 and "summon me" at 13:22. At 5:1 it has "Call if you will, but who will answer you?". At 14:15 it has "You will call and I will answer you", a statement, like the NASB95.
- **It turns 4:17 into "Can a mortal be more righteous than God?"** That loses the link to 9:2 ("how can a mortal be righteous before God?").
- **It has "speak wickedly on God's behalf" (13:7) against "speak to the Almighty" (13:3).** The two ways of speaking stay audible.
- **At 7:20 it follows "a burden to you"**, with a footnote.

## Hand-off

Ready for the manuscript stage. The candidate Big Question, the four movements and the landing are the inputs. The four hinges (4:6/5:1; 7:7/10:18; 13:3/13:7; 14:15/14:19) are the skeleton, and everything else is optional.

**How it links to its neighbours.**
- **Back to Sermon 2,** which promised that 10:18 would turn the "why" of 3:11 to God. Movement 2 keeps that promise.
- **Forward to Sermon 4 (16:1–17:16; 19:1–29).** The hope thread runs on: "where then is my hope?" (17:15, ESV); "my hope has he pulled up like a tree" (19:10, ESV), which rewrites the tree of 14:7. The longing for a mediator grows from "There is no arbiter" (9:33) to "my witness is in heaven" (16:19) and "I know that my Redeemer lives" (19:25). Movement 4 should leave the congregation ready for that.
- **Forward to Sermon 8 (38:1–42:6),** where the LORD answers. The landing names 38:1 and 38:3; Sermon 8 will expound them.
- **Forward to Sermon 9 (42:7–17),** where the verdict, Job's prayer for his friends and the "latter days" (42:12) are preached at full length. The landing names them without preaching them.
- **To the introductory sermon's "two ways of speaking".** That sermon took its hinge from 13:3 and 13:7. This one preaches the text it came from, and shows the friends' way in full: speech for God, which never once becomes prayer.
- **The prologue audit stays deferred.** This plan's links to the prologue (1:5 → 8:4; 2:3 → 11:6; 1:9–11 → the friends' offer) are verified in their words and do not wait on it. The audit may add to them before the series is preached.
