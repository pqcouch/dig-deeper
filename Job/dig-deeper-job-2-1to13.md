## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across several tools:

1. **God himself admits the cause, and answers the Accuser's question with the Accuser's word.** `[T]` "He still holds fast his integrity, although you incited Me (וַתְּסִיתֵנִי) against him to ruin him (לְבַלְּעוֹ) without cause (חִנָּם)" (2:3).
   - חִנָּם is the word of 1:9 ("for nothing?"). God concedes that Job's piety was not bought, and that his ruin had no cause in Job.
   - סות ("incite") is the verb of 2 Sam 24:1 (the LORD) and 1 Chr 21:1 (Satan).
   - Job will later use both of God's words without knowing he is quoting: "He … multiplies my wounds without cause (חִנָּם)" (9:17), and "You … would swallow me up (וַתְּבַלְּעֵנִי)" (10:8) — בלע, the verb of 2:3 (lemma).
   - The Greek removes the admission: "you said to destroy his possessions in vain" (Swete).
   - — Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Narrator's structure, Tool 11, Move 4, Translations. *High.*

2. **The wife repeats God's words exactly and draws the Accuser's conclusion.** `[T]` "Do you still hold fast your integrity? (עֹדְךָ מַחֲזִיק בְּתֻמָּתֶךָ)" (2:9) is God's sentence of 2:3 (עֹדֶנּוּ מַחֲזִיק בְּתֻמָּתוֹ) turned into a question. חזק ("hold fast") with תֻּמָּה ("integrity") occurs only in these two verses (lemma). Her "bless (= curse) God" (בָּרֵךְ) is the Accuser's prediction (1:11; 2:5). She stands on earth between heaven's two voices and does not know it. Job calls her speech the speech of הַנְּבָלוֹת ("the foolish women", 2:10) — from the root God later uses for the friends' folly (נְבָלָה, 42:8). — Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Structure, Move 4. *High.*

3. **The blow is the covenant curse, and the friends' mourning gesture is the gesture that brought the plague of boils.** `[T]` (synthetic)
   - "Sore boils from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head" (2:7) is Deut 28:35. שְׁחִין ("boils") with קָדְקֹד ("crown of the head") occurs only there and at Job 2:7 (lemma). The Greek of both reads ἐν ἕλκει πονηρῷ ("with an evil sore").
   - The friends "threw dust over their heads toward the sky" (וַיִּזְרְקוּ … הַשָּׁמָיְמָה, 2:12). זרק ("throw") with הַשָּׁמַיְמָה ("toward heaven") occurs only at Exod 9:8, 10 and Job 2:12 (lemma). In Exodus it is Moses throwing soot toward heaven, which "became boils (שְׁחִין)" on Egypt (9:9–10).
   - Job bears Egypt's plague and Israel's curse without being Egypt or a covenant-breaker. Only the NASB95 lets both links be heard.
   - — Surfaced by: Tool 11 (Move 1–3), Vocabulary, Translations, Original Audience. *High on the words; moderate on the design.*

4. **The man who turns from evil receives evil from God, and does not sin with his lips.** `[T]` רַע ("evil") runs through the chapter at four points:
   - 2:3 — Job "turning away from evil" (מֵרָע)
   - 2:7 — "sore (evil) boils" (שְׁחִין רָע)
   - 2:10 — "shall we not accept evil (הָרָע)?"
   - 2:11 — "all this evil (הָרָעָה) that had come upon him"

   The lemma index splits these across three homograph entries (7451 a, b, c), and each was checked. Job's reply makes God the giver of both good and evil (2:10), and the narrator certifies it (2:10b). The ESV alone keeps "evil" at 2:10 and 2:11; NASB95 has "adversity", NIV84 "trouble(s)". — Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Translations, Narrator's Comment. *High.*

5. **The friends' best moment is their silence.** `[T]` "They sat down on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights with no one speaking a word to him, for they saw that his pain was very great" (2:13).
   - "Seven days and seven nights" occurs only here (phrase search). Seven days is the period of mourning for the dead (Gen 50:10; 1 Sam 31:13) and of Ezekiel's appalled silence among the exiles (Ezek 3:15).
   - Job later tells them, "O that you would be completely silent, and that it would become your wisdom!" (13:5).
   - "Very great" (גָדַל … מְאֹד) is the verb-and-adverb pair of the patriarchs' greatness (Gen 24:35 Abraham; 26:13 Isaac, lemma). Job, "the greatest" in 1:3, is now great only in pain.
   - — Surfaced by: Structure, Tone, Move 4, Vocabulary. *High on silence; moderate on the patriarchal echo.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview's purpose is confirmed (see the Job 1 dig). `[I]`

**Passage level.** Job 2 carries the test to its limit and sets the stage for the dialogue. It does four things.

- **It escalates the test to the self.** The Accuser lost the first wager; he now claims a man will give everything "for his life" (2:4). God grants the body and keeps the life (2:6). `[T]`
- **It gives the reader God's verdict on causality before anyone argues about it.** "Without cause" (2:3) settles for the reader the question the friends will spend twenty-nine chapters answering wrongly. `[T]`
- **It carries the Accuser's voice to earth.** The wife speaks his prediction as advice (2:9), and Job refuses it (2:10). `[T]`
- **It brings the friends on stage and silences them.** They come to comfort, and their right response is silence (2:11–13). The silence gives way to Job's curse on his day (3:1). `[T]`

**What is included and omitted.** The Accuser is given no reply to God's charge of 2:3; he moves straight to "skin for skin". After 2:7 he is never mentioned again. The wife is not named. After her question she has three words in the Hebrew — בָּרֵךְ אֱלֹהִים וָמֻת ("curse God and die") — and she is never heard from again. `[T]`

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Chapter 1 ended with the Accuser's first wager lost: Job lost "all that he has" and blessed the name, and "did not sin" (1:21–22). But God's limit of 1:12 had left one thing untouched — "only do not put forth your hand on him". The Accuser's theory survives if the self is the last hedge. `[T]` / `[I]`

**Necessity answer.** Job 2 must come here, and not later, for four reasons. `[I]`

- **The test is incomplete until it reaches the body.** "Skin for skin" (2:4) is the objection chapter 1 left open.
- **God's admission (2:3) must be heard before the friends speak,** so that their doctrine — suffering proves guilt — is refuted in the reader's hearing before it is asserted.
- **The human world must now echo heaven's contest.** The wife voices the Accuser's line on earth, and Job must refuse it with his own mouth — not only the narrator for him.
- **The dialogue needs its participants and its occasion.** Three friends come, and their seven silent days give Job the space in which to curse his day (3:1). Leningrad's paragraphing makes the point: the petuḥah after 2:10 closes the test, and the next falls after 3:1. The friends' arrival and Job's first words are one unit. `[T]` for the markers, `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`.

**Implication for this passage.** Job 2 is the hinge from the prose test to the poetic argument. It must be preached with both ends in view: the verdict of heaven ("without cause") and the silence that will break into lament. A sermon that ends at 2:10 has preached the triumph of the test and missed the chapter's handoff to grief. `[I]`

#### Surrounding context

- **Immediately before:** 1:6–12 — the first council, which 2:1–6 reproduces almost verbatim, so that its differences carry the weight (Tool 10). `[T]`
- **Immediately after:** 3:1 — "Afterward Job opened his mouth and cursed his day", then the poetic lament. The accent system changes at 3:2 (overview, WLC). `[T]` *as the WLC transcribes it*.
- **Section:** 1:1–3:1, the prose prologue.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 2:1–6 | **The second council (heaven)** | Repeats 1:6–12 with additions. God's admission "without cause" (2:3). "Skin for skin". The body granted, the life kept (אַךְ, "only", 2:6). |
| 2:7–8 | **The blow (earth)** | The Accuser's exit and the boils "from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head". Job takes a potsherd and sits among the ashes. |
| 2:9–10 | **The wife and the verdict** | Her question and command; Job's reply; "Job did not sin with his lips". Petuḥah. |
| 2:11–13 | **The friends arrive** | Three come to "sympathize … and comfort". They do not recognise him; they weep, tear their robes and throw dust toward heaven; seven days of silence. (Continues into 3:1.) |

**Device: the same alternation as chapter 1, compressed.** Heaven (2:1–6) → earth (2:7–13). The second council is **a near-repetition whose differences are the message** (Tool 10). `[T]`

**Two voices meet one silence.** The Accuser speaks in heaven, and the wife repeats his conclusion on earth. Job answers, and the friends are silent for seven days. `[T]` for the sequence; `[I]` for the design.

**Narrative economy.** One verse for the blow (2:7) against three for the council's changed wording (2:3–5). The narrator's weight falls on the reasons and the responses, not on the suffering itself. `[T]` / `[I]`

### 4. Linking Words

- **וְעֹדֶנּוּ ("and he still…", 2:3) → עֹדְךָ ("do you still…?", 2:9).** The same particle, first as God's boast and then as the wife's reproach. `[T]`
- **וַתְּסִיתֵנִי ("although you incited Me", 2:3).** The waw joins God's praise of Job to God's admission about himself. NASB95, ESV and NIV84 all render it concessively ("although" / "though"), rightly. `[T]`
- **אוּלָם ("however", 2:5).** The Accuser's pivot from the proverb (2:4) to the demand. It matches וְאוּלָם at 1:11. `[T]`
- **אִם־לֹא (2:5).** The oath with its curse suppressed, as at 1:11. `[T]`
- **אַךְ ("only", 2:6).** The limit changes particle: רַק at 1:12, אַךְ here. And it changes object: "him" in 1:12, "his life" (נַפְשׁוֹ) here. `[T]`
- **גַּם … וְ… ("shall we indeed accept good … and not accept evil?", 2:10).** A rhetorical question in two balanced halves. `[T]`
- **כִּי … כִּי ("for they saw that…", 2:13).** The reason for the silence: they saw the greatness of his pain. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

The chapter is prose, but it contains three balanced sayings.

**2:4 — the Accuser's proverb.**

> עוֹר בְּעַד עוֹר / וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר לָאִישׁ יִתֵּן בְּעַד נַפְשׁוֹ
> ("Skin for skin! / Yes, all that a man has he will give for his life.")

The second line explains the first: "skin for skin" means a man will trade any outer thing to save his own skin. The exact sense of the proverb is debated `[S]` — barter of skins, or one's own skin for another's. The second line fixes its application. בְּעַד ("for", "on behalf of") occurs twice here. It is the preposition of the hedge God set "about" Job (1:10, בַּעֲדוֹ, three times). It returns at 3:23, "whom God has hedged in (וַיָּסֶךְ … בַּעֲדוֹ)" (lemma). `[T]` The Accuser's two speeches turn on one preposition: God fenced round him; a man will give everything to fence round himself.

**2:10 — Job's question, antithetical.**

> גַּם אֶת־הַטּוֹב נְקַבֵּל מֵאֵת הָאֱלֹהִים / וְאֶת־הָרָע לֹא נְקַבֵּל
> ("Shall we indeed accept good from God / and not accept adversity [evil]?")

Good and evil are paired as gifts from one source; the verb קבל ("accept, receive") is repeated in both halves. `[T]`

**2:13 — the silence, doubled.** שִׁבְעַת יָמִים וְשִׁבְעַת לֵילוֹת ("seven days and seven nights") and וְאֵין־דֹּבֵר אֵלָיו דָּבָר ("with no one speaking a word to him", verb and noun from one root). `[T]`

### 6. Narrator's Comment

- **2:3 (in God's mouth, but the narrator's verdict).** God's repetition of the four-term portrait (1:1, 8), with the addition "he still holds fast his integrity", is the reader's continued assurance of Job's innocence. `[T]`
- **2:10b — the ruling, narrowed.** בְּכָל־זֹאת לֹא־חָטָא אִיּוֹב בִּשְׂפָתָיו ("In all this Job did not sin with his lips"). Set against 1:22:
  - it adds בִּשְׂפָתָיו ("with his lips");
  - it omits "nor did he ascribe folly (תִּפְלָה) to God".
  - The narrator's verdict is still unqualified praise. But the precision invites a question the text does not answer. In 1:5 Job feared that his sons had sinned "in their hearts" (בִּלְבָבָם); in 2:10 he is cleared of sinning "with his lips". Jewish tradition read the difference as a hint that Job sinned in his heart (b. B. Bat. 16a) `[S]`.
  - The narrower wording is `[T]`. Any inference about the heart is `[I]` at most, and the next chapter (3:1) — where Job "opened his mouth" — is where the lips are next tested. *Moderate.*
- **2:11 — the friends' purpose, stated by the narrator.** לָבוֹא לָנוּד־לוֹ וּלְנַחֲמוֹ ("to come to sympathize with him and comfort him"). The narrator grants their good intention before their speeches betray it. `[T]`
- **2:13 — their reason for silence.** כִּי רָאוּ כִּי־גָדַל הַכְּאֵב מְאֹד ("for they saw that his pain was very great"). An inside view of the friends, and a sympathetic one. `[T]`

### 7. Vocabulary

**סות ("to incite, entice", 2:3).**
- It occurs in the Hebrew Bible at: Deut 13:7; Josh 15:18; Judg 1:14; 1 Sam 26:19; 2 Sam 24:1; 1 Kgs 21:25; 2 Kgs 18:32; 1 Chr 21:1; 2 Chr 18:2, 31; 32:11, 15; Isa 36:18; Jer 38:22; 43:3; Job 2:3; 36:16, 18 (lemma).
- Its most telling uses are two. It is the LORD's incitement of David (2 Sam 24:1), which the Chronicler assigns to Satan (1 Chr 21:1). And it is the enticement of a man by the one closest to him: "the wife of your bosom" who entices to other gods (Deut 13:7); Achsah urging her husband (Josh 15:18; Judg 1:14); Jezebel who "incited" Ahab (1 Kgs 21:25).
- In Job the one incited is God, by the Accuser — and it is God who says so. `[T]` for the data; `[I]` for the pattern. *High* on the data.

**בלע piel ("to swallow up, ruin", 2:3).** לְבַלְּעוֹ ("to ruin him"). The verb returns in Job's mouth at 10:8: "Your hands fashioned and made me altogether, and would You destroy me (וַתְּבַלְּעֵנִי)?" (lemma). Job, who never hears 2:3, accuses God in the very word God used of himself. `[T]` *High.*

**חִנָּם ("without cause", 2:3).** See Headline 1. It answers 1:9 and is picked up by Job at 9:17 ("multiplies my wounds without cause") (lemma). `[T]`

**תֻּמָּה ("integrity", 2:3, 9).** The noun occurs in the Hebrew Bible only at Job 2:3, 9; 27:5; 31:6; Prov 11:3 (lemma). חזק ("hold fast") with תֻּמָּה occurs only at 2:3, 9. Job's own oath will say, "Till I die I will not put away my integrity from me. I hold fast (הֶחֱזַקְתִּי) my righteousness" (27:5–6) — the verb of 2:3, 9 in the first person. `[T]` *High.*

**עוֹר / עֶצֶם / בָּשָׂר ("skin / bone / flesh", 2:4–5).**
- The Accuser's targets become Job's lament vocabulary:
  - "My flesh is clothed with worms … my skin hardens and runs" (7:5)
  - "clothe me with skin and flesh, and knit me together with bones" (10:11)
  - "My bone clings to my skin and my flesh" (19:20)
  - "after my skin is destroyed … from my flesh I shall see God" (19:26)
  - "My skin turns black" (30:30)
  - Elihu: "his flesh wastes away … his bones … stick out" (33:21)
  - All by lemma co-occurrence in Job.
- "Bone and flesh" (עֶצֶם וּבָשָׂר) is also the kinship formula — "my bone and my flesh" (Gen 29:14; Judg 9:2; 2 Sam 5:1; 19:13–14), beginning with "bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh" (Gen 2:23). The next voice after the Accuser demands Job's "bone and flesh" is his wife's. `[T]` for the formula; `[I]` for any intended irony. *Uncertain.*

**נֶפֶשׁ ("life", 2:4, 6).** The Accuser: a man "will give all … for his life (נַפְשׁוֹ)". God: "only spare (שְׁמֹר) his life (נַפְשׁוֹ)". God takes the Accuser's own word and makes it the limit. שׁמר ("keep, guard") puts the Accuser under orders to preserve what he has just said a man will sell everything to save. `[T]` / `[I]` *High* on the words.

**שְׁחִין רָע ("sore boils", 2:7).**
- שְׁחִין ("boil, inflamed skin") occurs at Exod 9:9, 10, 11 (the sixth plague); Lev 13:18, 19, 20, 23 (a boil assessed for skin disease); Deut 28:27, 35 (the covenant curse, "the boils of Egypt" at 28:27); 2 Kgs 20:7 and Isa 38:21 (Hezekiah); Job 2:7 (lemma).
- The word carries Egypt's plague, the Levitical test of uncleanness, and Deuteronomy's curse. `[T]` *High.*

**חֶרֶשׂ ("potsherd", 2:8).** Job scrapes himself with a broken piece of pottery. The same word is the Psalter's image of desolation: "My strength is dried up like a potsherd (כַּחֶרֶשׂ) … and You lay me in the dust (עָפָר) of death" (Ps 22:16 [Eng 22:15]) (lemma). לְהִתְגָּרֵד ("to scrape himself") occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible (lemma). `[T]`

**אֵפֶר ("ashes", 2:8).** "He was sitting among the ashes." ישׁב ("sit") with אֵפֶר occurs only at Job 2:8 and Jonah 3:6, where the king of Nineveh "sat on the ashes" in repentance (lemma). In Job it is the posture of mourning, not repentance for sin. אֵפֶר returns at 30:19 and 42:6, in the pair עָפָר וָאֵפֶר ("dust and ashes") — Abraham's words (Gen 18:27). In this chapter the pair is split: Job sits in ashes (2:8), and the friends throw dust (עָפָר, 2:12). `[T]` (synthetic) *Moderate–high.*

**נָבָל, plural נְבָלוֹת ("fool / foolish women", 2:10).** The נָבָל is morally and religiously senseless, not merely unintelligent: "The fool (נָבָל) has said in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Ps 14:1; 53:2). The construction כְּאַחַד הַנְּבָלִים ("like one of the fools") is Tamar's warning to Amnon (2 Sam 13:13), close to Job's כְּדַבֵּר אַחַת הַנְּבָלוֹת ("as one of the foolish women speaks"). NIV84's margin notes that the word denotes moral deficiency. God will apply the cognate נְבָלָה ("folly") to the friends (42:8). `[T]` *High* for the sense.

**קבל piel ("to accept, receive", 2:10).** In the sense "receive" it occurs at 1 Chr 12:19; 21:11; 2 Chr 29:16, 22; Ezra 8:30; Esth 4:4; 9:23, 27; Prov 19:20; Job 2:10 (lemma; Exod 26:5; 36:12 are a different stem and sense). The distribution leans to books usually dated late, and the verb is common in Aramaic. That is sometimes used in dating arguments `[S]`; this report draws no date from it. `[T]` for the distribution.

**יעד niphal ("to meet by appointment", 2:11).** וַיִּוָּעֲדוּ יַחְדָּו ("they made an appointment together"). The niphal is used of those who meet by agreement: "Do two walk together unless they have made an appointment (נוֹעָדוּ)?" (Amos 3:3). It is also used of rulers who band together (Josh 11:5; Ps 48:5) and of Nehemiah's enemies inviting him to a meeting (Neh 6:2, 10) (lemma). Job later uses the same root in the lawsuit: "if it is a matter of justice, who can summon Me (יוֹעִידֵנִי)?" (9:19). `[T]` for the data; any hint of conspiracy is *uncertain*.

**נוד + נחם ("sympathize … comfort", 2:11).** The pair occurs at Isa 51:19; Nah 3:7; Ps 69:21 [Eng 69:20]; Job 2:11; 42:11 (lemma). In each of the other three, the comforters are absent: "who will console you?" (Isa 51:19); "where shall I seek comforters for you?" (Nah 3:7); "I looked for sympathy, but there was none, and for comforters, but I found none" (Ps 69:21). The friends come with exactly the right verbs. The book's second use (42:11) goes to others. `[T]` *High.*

**כְּאֵב ("pain", 2:13).** It occurs at Job 2:13; 16:6 ("my pain is not lessened"); Ps 39:3; Isa 17:11; 65:14; Jer 15:18 (lemma). `[T]`

**Proper-noun inventory (Question 0).**
- YHWH; the sons of God; the Accuser; Job; his wife (unnamed).
- Eliphaz the Temanite; Bildad the Shuhite; Zophar the Naamathite.
- Numbers: three friends; seven days and seven nights.
- **Eliphaz / Teman** — Esau's son and grandson (Gen 36:11, 15). Teman is proverbial for wisdom: "Is there no longer any wisdom in Teman?" (Jer 49:7) (overview, verified). *Moderate.*
- **Bildad the Shuhite** — Shuah, son of Abraham and Keturah (Gen 25:2) (overview). *Moderate* on the gentilic.
- **Zophar the Naamathite** — no genealogy in the Hebrew Bible (overview). The Greek makes all three kings (Tool 8).
- **Seven days** — see Headline 5.

### 9. Tone and Feel

- **2:1–6: ceremonial, and chilling.** The council repeats itself almost word for word, which makes the new sentences land: God's admission (2:3) and the Accuser's cynical proverb (2:4). There is no hint of Job's side of the argument, only a transaction over his body.
- **2:7–8: brutal brevity.** One verse for the disease from sole to crown, one for the potsherd and the ashes. The participle וְהוּא יֹשֵׁב ("while he was sitting") freezes Job in the ash-heap.
- **2:9–10: domestic, sharp, then still.** The only dialogue between husband and wife in the book: a three-word question, a three-word command, and a firm reply ending in a question. The narrator closes it with the verdict.
- **2:11–13: tender, then silence.** Travel, recognition failed, loud weeping, torn robes, dust thrown heavenward — then seven days in which "no one [was] speaking a word".
- **Soundtrack.** A low repeated figure (the council); a single blow; two voices, one shrill and one steady; then a long rest. **For the preacher:** let the seven days' silence be felt before chapter 3's scream. `[T]` for the devices; the register is `[I]`.

**Systematic sweep:** every distinctive word or phrase in 2:1–13 was checked across the canon by lemma and phrase (run28–29).

---

**Deuteronomy 28:35 (with 28:27) → 2:7** *(high)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Deut 28:15–68 lists the curses on a covenant people who "do not obey the LORD your God" (28:15). Among them: "The LORD will smite you with the boils of Egypt (בִּשְׁחִין מִצְרַיִם)" (28:27). And: "The LORD will strike you on the knees and legs with sore boils (בִּשְׁחִין רָע), from which you cannot be healed, from the sole of your foot to the crown of your head (מִכַּף רַגְלְךָ וְעַד קָדְקֳדֶךָ)" (28:35). The register is judgement for apostasy, and the curse turns Egypt's plague on Israel.

*The verbal data.* שְׁחִין with קָדְקֹד: **Deut 28:35 and Job 2:7 only**. "From the sole of the foot to the crown / head" also at 2 Sam 14:25 (Absalom, flawless) and Isa 1:6 (Israel, wounded from sole to head) (lemma and phrase search). Swete: ἐν ἕλκει πονηρῷ (Deut 28:35) and ἕλκει πονηρῷ (Job 2:7).

*Move 2 — Book usage.* The Job 1 dig found the Accuser speaking Deuteronomy's *blessing* formula, "You have blessed the work of his hands" (1:10; Deut 28:12). Here, one chapter on, Job's body bears the same chapter's *curse* (28:35). **The book uses Deuteronomy 28 at both ends, in the Accuser's charge and in the Accuser's blow.** Deut 24:17 (at 24:3) and 32:39 (at 5:18) confirm that Deuteronomy is live. `[T]` for each; `[I]` for the pairing. *Moderate–high.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Deut 28:27 already names the boils "of Egypt", so Deuteronomy is itself quoting Exodus 9. Job 2:7 activates a curse that was itself an allusion. The next entry shows that 2:12 reaches back to Exodus 9 directly.

*What it adds.* The canonical reader recognises the covenant curse on Job's skin. By Deuteronomy's logic Job is a covenant-breaker, and that is the friends' logic. The reader has heard God say "without cause" (2:3). The allusion does not deny the curse-order; it shows a man suffering its words without its cause. `[I]`

---

**Exodus 9:8–11 → 2:7, 12** *(high on the words; moderate on the design)* `[T]` (synthetic)

*Move 1 — Source context.* The sixth plague. "Take for yourselves handfuls of soot from a kiln, and let Moses throw it toward the sky (וּזְרָקוֹ מֹשֶׁה הַשָּׁמַיְמָה) in the sight of Pharaoh. It will become fine dust (אָבָק) over all the land of Egypt, and will become boils (שְׁחִין) breaking out with sores on man and beast" (Exod 9:8–9). "Moses threw it toward the sky, and it became boils" (9:10). The magicians could not stand before Moses "because of the boils" (9:11). A judgement on Egypt's hardness.

*The verbal data.* זרק with הַשָּׁמַיְמָה ("toward heaven"): **Exod 9:8, 10 and Job 2:12 only** (lemma co-occurrence and phrase search). שְׁחִין: Exod 9:9, 10, 11 and Job 2:7 (with Lev 13; Deut 28; 2 Kgs 20:7; Isa 38:21).

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Exodus is not in the overview's live-source list. This is the book's first Exodus link that is verbal rather than conceptual. *Addition.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Deut 28:27 ("the boils of Egypt") has already joined Exod 9 to the covenant curses, so the two sources were in conversation before Job.

*What it adds.* The friends perform, in mourning, the gesture that in Exodus brought boils on a hardened enemy of God. In Job the boils come first (2:7) and the gesture second (2:12). Whether the friends' dust "toward heaven" is meant as lament, as appeal, or as an unconscious re-enactment of judgement, the text does not say. `[I]` The canonical reader may hear the friends' later theology in their first act: they treat Job's boils as heaven's judgement. *Moderate.*

---

**Lamentations 4:8, 21 → 2:4–5, 12** *(moderate)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Lam 4 laments Jerusalem's nobles after the siege. "Their appearance is blacker than soot, they are not recognized (לֹא נִכְּרוּ) in the streets; their skin (עוֹרָם) is shrivelled on their bones (עַל־עַצְמָם)" (4:8). The chapter ends: "Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, who dwells in the land of Uz (בְּאֶרֶץ עוּץ); but the cup will come round to you as well" (4:21).

*The verbal data.* נכר ("recognise") with עוֹר ("skin") and עֶצֶם ("bone") in one verse: **Lam 4:8 only**. Job 2 has the three spread over 2:4, 5 and 12 (skin, bone, "did not recognize him") (lemma). ### 12. Genre

**Hebrew prose narrative — the second half of the prologue — with a scene of dialogue and a transition to poetry.** `[T]`

- **Reading rules.**
  - The narrator's words (2:10b, 11, 13) are authoritative. The Accuser's proverb (2:4) is a claim to be tested, not wisdom. The wife's words are advice the narrator frames as folly through Job's reply.
  - The council scene is disclosure to the reader.
  - The seven days of silence belong to the frame. They are narrated time, not an interlude to skip.
- **Embedded forms:** a proverb (2:4); a rhetorical question used as a verdict (2:10); a mourning scene with type-scene features — travel, failed recognition, weeping, torn garments, dust, sitting on the ground, silence (2:11–13).

### 13. Copycat

| Character / action | Category | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Job's refusal (2:10) | **Normative pattern, commended** | The narrator certifies it. Receiving good and evil from God is a posture Scripture commends elsewhere (Jas 5:10–11; Phil 4:11–13) |
| Job's sitting in the ashes (2:8) | **Descriptive** | Mourning, not penance; there is no confession of sin |
| The wife's counsel (2:9) | **Negative** | Job calls it folly. But the narrator gives no further verdict on her, and the Hebrew leaves her grief unsaid (the Greek fills it in). Condemn the counsel, not the grieving woman |
| The friends' coming, weeping and silence (2:11–13) | **Normative pattern** | Coming to the sufferer, entering his mourning, and saying nothing for seven days is the book's model of comfort. Their speeches are its anti-model |
| The friends' dust thrown "toward heaven" (2:12) | **Descriptive** | A mourning rite. Its Exodus echo is for the reader (Tool 11), not a pattern to copy |
| The Accuser's proverb (2:4) | **Negative** | The cynical theory of human nature the book refutes |

**Common copycat misreading.** "Always say something positive to the suffering; Job's wife said the wrong thing." Job's rebuke is right, but the chapter's model for comforters is silence, not better sentences. `[I]`

### 15. Who Am I?

| Character | Function |
|---|---|
| **Job** | Righteous sufferer; a type through the servant category (Christological Reading). An example of receiving good and evil from God without sinning with the lips (Jas 5:11) |
| **The wife** | Not a villain to despise. She is a bereaved mother whose ten children are dead (the Greek makes it explicit), speaking the Accuser's conclusion without knowing it. We are often she |
| **The friends** | Here, the model we should copy — present, grieving, silent. Later, the warning |
| **The reader** | Again given heaven's view: we know "without cause" (2:3). That knowledge forbids us the friends' later explanations |
| **The Accuser** | Foil; the voice of cynicism about human faithfulness ("skin for skin") |

**The trap.** Identifying with Job's heroism and not with the wife's despair or the friends' helplessness. The chapter offers three human responses to suffering and commends the silence of the friends as much as the words of Job. `[I]`

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** To see faith hold when the loss reaches the body, and to learn to sit silently with the suffering before speaking. `[I]`

**Stage 2.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | God is the giver of good and of evil (2:10), and he is not the Accuser. He can say of his own permission, "without cause" (2:3), and the suffering is not evidence of guilt. Visible suffering is not a verdict, even when it looks like the Bible's own curse. |
| **Stop** | Answering the suffering with explanations. Treating the sick as punished. Treating despairing words (2:9) as the final truth about a grieving person. |
| **Start** | Going to the sufferer "by appointment", weeping aloud, sitting on the ground, and saying nothing until the sufferer speaks (2:11–13; 3:1). Receiving evil from God's hand without cursing. |
| **Motivation** | Not bravado. God keeps the sufferer's life (2:6) and speaks of him as "My servant" (2:3). And the Son bore the curse and the boils' full meaning without cause, for us (Gal 3:13). |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me:** When the loss reaches my body, will I still hold fast? Where do I secretly agree with "skin for skin"?
- **For a Christian friend:** Go, weep, sit, and be silent. The friends' seven days are the best pastoral care in the book.
- **For the church:** Care for the chronically ill without a theology of cause; make room for silence and lament.
- **For an unbeliever:** The chapter refuses the cheap answer to suffering (it is punishment) and the cynical one (everyone has a price). It points to a God who can say "without cause", and to a greater Sufferer.

**Prayer.**

- For grace to receive good and evil from God without cursing.
- For silence and presence when others suffer.
- For mercy on the chronically ill.
- For the bereaved who, like Job's wife, want to give up.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** As in chapter 1: a patriarchal household in Uz. The friends travel from Teman (Edom), from Shuah (Arabia, by the genealogy) and from Naamah (unlocated) `[S]`.

**What the original audience knew.**

1. **Skin disease and exclusion.** A boil (שְׁחִין) was a priestly matter (Lev 13:18–23). Visible skin disease set a person apart from the community (Lev 13:45–46). Job's sitting in the ashes may place him outside the ordinary life of the town; the Greek says "outside the city" explicitly. `[T]` for Leviticus; `[I]` for Job's location.
2. **The ash-heap.** Ancient towns had a refuse heap, and the Psalter and Hannah's song speak of God lifting the poor "from the ash heap" (Ps 113:7; 1 Sam 2:8). `[T]` for the texts; the practice is `[S]`.
3. **Mourning rites.** Weeping aloud, tearing the robe and putting dust on the head were the ordinary gestures of grief. עָפָר ("dust") on the head occurs at Josh 7:6; Lam 2:10; Ezek 27:30; Job 2:12, and אֲדָמָה ("earth") at 1 Sam 4:12; 2 Sam 1:2 (lemma). Sitting on the ground for seven days was a mourning for the dead (Gen 50:10; 1 Sam 31:13). `[T]` for the references.
4. **Friendship and comfort visits.** Coming by agreement to "sympathize and comfort" (לָנוּד … וּלְנַחֵם) was the expected duty of friends. Its failure is a stock lament (Ps 69:21). `[T]`
5. **A wife's place.** The Hebrew wife has no name and one line. The Greek gives her a lament and a history. `[T]`

**What this changes about how we read.**

- The friends mourn Job as though for the dead — seven days on the ground, dust on their heads. The man they came to comfort already looks to them like a man under death and curse. That is the first step toward their theology. `[I]`
- Job's ash-heap may be a social exclusion as well as a grief. `[I]`

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Readers of the Writings who know Exodus 9 and Deuteronomy 28. For them, boils "from the sole of the foot to the crown of the head" is a curse formula. They know the laments over absent comforters (Ps 69; Isa 51:19), and Lam 4's disfigured nobles and "the land of Uz". `[I]`

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** God says he was incited to ruin Job "without cause" (2:3). The righteous man bears the covenant curse (2:7). `[T]`
- **Surprising:** Job's wife, not an enemy, speaks the Accuser's line. The friends' first act is silence. `[T]`
- **Comforting:** God keeps Job's life (2:6), and the narrator says Job "did not sin with his lips" (2:10). `[T]`
- **Disturbing:** if the covenant curse can fall on a righteous man "without cause", then reading suffering as judgement — Israel's own habit after the exile — cannot be the whole truth. `[I]`

**What we bring that they didn't.** A medical view of disease that strips it of moral meaning. For them it was the opposite, and that is what makes 2:3 so bold. A discomfort with silence at a bedside. A tendency to treat Job's wife as a moral cautionary tale rather than a bereaved mother. `[I]`

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**(1) Covenant curse borne without cause**

- **Where this passage sits:** a righteous man bearing Deuteronomy's curse (28:35) and Egypt's plague (Exod 9) without their cause.
- **What comes before:** Exod 9 (the plague on Egypt's hardness); Deut 28:27, 35 (the curse on Israel's disobedience); Isa 1:6 (Israel wounded "from the sole of the foot even to the head").
- **What comes after:** the Servant "stricken, smitten of God" and yet "pierced through for our transgressions" (Isa 53:4–5); Christ who "redeemed us from the curse of the Law, having become a curse for us" (Gal 3:13); the bowls of Revelation, where the "evil sore" (ἕλκος … πονηρόν) falls again on the worshippers of the beast (Rev 16:2).
- **What this contributes:** Job's boils show that the curse's form can fall where its cause is absent. The New Testament shows one who bore it deliberately, for others.
- **Confidence:** moderate–high (the Job-to-Christ step is trajectory, not citation).

**(2) Comfort and the comforters**

- **Where this passage sits:** comforters who come, and whose best comfort is silence.
- **What comes before:** Ps 69:21 (no comforters); Isa 51:19 ("who will comfort you?"); Lam 1 (the refrain "there is no one to comfort her").
- **What comes after:** Isa 40:1 ("Comfort, O comfort My people"); the Father giving "another Helper (παράκλητον)" at Christ's request (John 14:16); "the God of all comfort, who comforts us in all our affliction so that we will be able to comfort those who are in any affliction" (2 Cor 1:3–4).
- **What this contributes:** the book's frame sets comfort as the test of theology. The friends' coming is right; their later speech is wrong; and true comfort comes, at the end, from family (42:11) and from God.
- **Confidence:** moderate.

**Relation.** *Mutual illumination.* The friends read Job's curse-marked body (theme 1) as proof, and so fail to comfort (theme 2). Their silence in 2:13 is the one moment their comfort is true, because it offers no explanation. `[I]`

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** servant typology (category), trajectory, and contrast.

**How the passage points to Christ.**

- **"My servant … without cause" (2:3).** The servant category again. The Righteous One was hated "without cause" (John 15:25), citing Ps 69:5, the psalm of the absent comforters (69:21, with 2:11). *Tests:* category — passes; NT precedent — strong for the category, none for Job specifically; escalation — passes (Job's life spared; Christ's not); authorial pattern — passes. *Moderate.*
- **"Only spare his life" (2:6) — contrast.** God limits the Accuser at Job's life. In the gospel, God "did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him over for us all (παρέδωκεν αὐτόν)" (Rom 8:32). The Greek of Job 2:6 uses the same verb (παραδίδωμί σοι αὐτόν) — but keeps his life. **Contrast with escalation.** `[I]` *Moderate.*
- **The curse on the body (2:7).** Job bears Deuteronomy's curse without cause. Christ became a curse for us (Gal 3:13), deliberately and as a substitute. **Trajectory with escalation.** *Moderate–high.*
- **The potsherd and the dust (2:8, 12).** Ps 22:16's "dried up like a potsherd … the dust of death" belongs to the psalm Christ prayed on the cross (Mark 15:34, quoting Ps 22:2). Job has the potsherd and the dust around him. *Uncertain* — shared words, not an allusion.
- **Outside the city (Swete 2:8).** "Jesus … suffered outside the gate (ἔξω τῆς πύλης)" (Heb 13:12). The Greek of Job places him "outside the city (ἔξω τῆς πόλεως)". *Uncertain*; Greek plus only.

**Trajectory.**

- **What the passage longs for:** a vindication of the servant God called righteous, and a comforter who can do more than sit.
- **What Christ fulfils:** the servant who bore the curse without cause, and the God of all comfort (2 Cor 1:3–4).
- **What still remains:** the believer's suffering in the body and the waiting for "the redemption of our body" (Rom 8:23).

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Don't be like Job's wife; be like Job." Or "be like the friends in 2:13".
- **The gospel grounding:** God keeps his servant's life, and the Son was not kept, for us.
- **Christ as hero:** the one who, handed over, did not curse God, but "entrusted Himself to Him who judges righteously" (1 Pet 2:23).

**Confidence.** *Moderate–high* on the curse and servant trajectory; *moderate* on the Rom 8:32 contrast; *uncertain* on Ps 22 and Heb 13:12.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**2:3 — "you incited Me against him".**
- **Category:** doctrinal and apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** can God be incited? Did God cause Job's suffering?
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the admission vindicates Job before the reader, and makes God, not chance, the one responsible. It is not a confession of weakness in God.
- **Options:** (a) anthropopathic language for God's permission; (b) a deliberately bold statement that God owns what he permits.
- **Honest exegesis:** the Hebrew does say "you incited Me"; the Greek softens it. God is not coerced — he sets the limit (2:6) — and he calls the ruin "without cause".
- **Handling:** do not soften it as the Greek did. Name it and preach its purpose: the reader knows the suffering is not punishment.

**2:9 — Job's wife.**
- **Category:** pastoral, and weaponised.
- **The difficulty:** she has been called "the devil's helper" (Augustine) and "a fool" `[S]`.
- **Honest exegesis:** Job rebukes her counsel, not her person, and his "we" ("shall *we* … accept") includes her. She has lost ten children too.
- **Handling:** do not make her a caricature. Let her be the grieving voice of despair that many hearers know from the inside.

**2:10 — "shall we not accept evil from God?"**
- **Category:** doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** does God send evil?
- **Honest exegesis:** רַע here is harm and calamity, not moral evil. Compare Isa 45:7, "causing well-being and creating calamity (רָע)". The narrator approves Job's words, and 2:3 has already said that God permitted the harm.
- **Handling:** distinguish calamity from sin, and keep God's sovereignty.

**2:13 — seven days of silence.**
- **Category:** pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** in practice the silence is often skipped for the speeches.
- **Handling:** make it an application, not a transition.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: Villainising Job's wife

- **What it looks like:** "Satan took everything except the one thing that would do his work for him — Job's wife."
- **Why it's wrong:** the text gives her one line and no narrator's verdict. She has lost the same ten children. Job rebukes her words, and includes her in his "we" (2:10).
- **The corrective:** preach her counsel as the voice of despair many hearers know, and Job's reply as faith speaking to despair — not as a husband defeating a villain.

### Pitfall: Softening 2:3

- **What it looks like:** "God didn't do anything to Job; he only stepped back." Or silence about the verse altogether.
- **Why it's wrong:** God says, "you incited Me against him to ruin him without cause". The Greek translators softened it; preachers should not.
- **The corrective:** preach the admission's purpose. It tells the reader, before the friends speak, that the suffering has no cause in Job.

### Pitfall: Skipping 2:11–13 as a bridge to the speeches

- **What it looks like:** the sermon ends at 2:10 with Job's triumph, or treats the friends' arrival as a setup.
- **Why it's wrong:** the narrator gives the friends good motives (2:11) and a sympathetic inside view (2:13). Their silence is the book's one moment of true comfort, and Job says so (13:5).
- **The corrective:** give the silence its place. It is the chapter's application for the church.

### Pitfall: Reading the boils as judgement, or ignoring what they signify

- **What it looks like:** either "sickness can be a sign of hidden sin" (the friends' reading), or no mention that 2:7 quotes the covenant curse.
- **Why it's wrong:** the canonical reader would hear Deut 28:35, and the text has already said "without cause" (2:3). Both omissions miss the tension the book builds.
- **The corrective:** name the curse-language, and let 2:3 overrule the inference the curse-language invites.

### Pitfall: "Job did not sin — therefore say only positive things when suffering"

- **What it looks like:** 2:10 as a rule of composure.
- **Why it's wrong:** "with his lips" is precise, and 3:1 follows immediately: Job opens his mouth to curse his day. God later says Job spoke rightly (42:7).
- **The corrective:** 2:10 is faith refusing to curse God. It is not a ban on lament.
