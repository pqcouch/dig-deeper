## What this document is

This is the whole-book planning layer for Job. It exists to make the passage digs sharper, not to settle them in advance.

Job poses two particular problems for an overview, and they should be stated at the top.

**First, most of the book is spoken by people God later says were wrong.** The friends' speeches fill 211 verses; God tells Eliphaz that he and his two friends "have not spoken of Me what is right as My servant Job has" (42:7). Job himself is rebuked for speaking "words without knowledge" (38:2). Elihu's six chapters (165 verses, 160 of them speech) are neither rebuked nor commended. So every sentence in the dialogue has to be read with its speaker attached, and an overview that quotes the book as though every line were the book's own voice would mislead every dig downstream. `[T]`

**Second, the reader knows what no character ever learns.** The heavenly council of 1:6–2:7 is never mentioned again by anyone in the book. הַשָּׂטָן ("Satan", literally "the Accuser") occurs fourteen times in eleven verses, all within 1:6–2:7, and never afterwards (WLC, lemma count). Job argues, and God answers, without the prologue ever being disclosed. The book's whole rhetoric depends on that gap. `[T]`

So this document separates three kinds of claim more sharply than usual:

- **What the page shows** — the vocabulary, the frame, the formulas, the counts, and who says what. These are firm, and they are verified.
- **What the page suggests** — the movements, the microscript, and the function of chapter 28 and of Elihu. These are provisional.
- **What interpreters have argued** — date, composition and the major cruxes (19:25–27; 42:6), set side by side rather than decided.

## Presenting Situation

### Narrator and author

**The narrator is anonymous and omniscient.** He reports the heavenly council (1:6–12; 2:1–7), gives God's verdict on Job before any human speaks (1:8; 2:3), and closes the book with a notice of Job's death (42:17). The book never names its author. `[T]`

**Authorship and date are not stated and are widely disputed.** Jewish tradition attributes the book to Moses (b. B. Bat. 15a). `[S]` Modern proposals run from the Solomonic period to the post-exilic, and most commentators date the poem later than its patriarchal setting. `[S]` Nothing in this overview depends on a date, and the digs should not import one.

### The world of the story

**The setting is deliberately patriarchal and deliberately outside Israel.** Every one of the following is on the page `[T]`:

- Job lives "in the land of Uz" (1:1). Uz is a son of Aram (Gen 10:23), a son of Nahor (Gen 22:21) and a Horite clan in Edom (Gen 36:28). Lamentations places "the land of Uz" with "the daughter of Edom" (Lam 4:21).
- Eliphaz "the Temanite" bears the name of Esau's son, and Teman is Eliphaz's son (Gen 36:11, 15). Bildad "the Shuhite" carries the name of Shuah, Abraham's son by Keturah (Gen 25:2). Elihu "the Buzite" carries the name of Buz, Uz's brother (Gen 22:21). Zophar "the Naamathite" has no genealogy in the Hebrew Bible.
- The raiders are from שְׁבָא ("the Sabeans", 1:15) and the caravans from תֵּימָא ("Tema", 6:19). Both are names from Abraham's wider family (Gen 25:3, 15).
- Job is **his own family's priest**: he rises early and offers עֹלוֹת ("burnt offerings") for his children (1:5). There is no altar-site, no Levite and no Temple.
- His wealth is counted in flocks and herds (1:3; 42:12). The 140 years he lives after the restoration, and the formula "old and full of days" (42:16–17) belong to Genesis: the formula is Isaac's exactly (Gen 35:29).
- The currency is the קְשִׂיטָה ("piece of money", 42:11), which elsewhere appears only in the Jacob and Joshua narratives (Gen 33:19; Josh 24:32).

**And what is absent is as striking.** The names יִשְׂרָאֵל ("Israel"), מֹשֶׁה ("Moses"), יְרוּשָׁלַםִ ("Jerusalem") and צִיּוֹן ("Zion") occur **0×** in Job (WLC, lemma counts). בְּרִית ("covenant") occurs three times, and none of them concerns a covenant between God and a people: a covenant with the stones of the field (5:23), with Job's eyes (31:1), and the question whether Leviathan will make one with Job (41:4 [Heb 40:28]). תּוֹרָה ("instruction") occurs once, in Eliphaz's mouth (22:22). `[T]`

### The names of God

**The book's distribution of divine names is one of its firmest structural facts.** `[T]`, WLC lemma counts:

| Name | In Job | In the whole Hebrew Bible | Where in Job |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| יְהוָה ("the LORD") | 32× in 23 verses | 6,521× | the prose frame; the storm speeches' headings (38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1); **and once in the dialogue, at 12:9** |
| שַׁדַּי ("the Almighty") | 31× | 48× | the poetry only — the dialogue, Elihu, and once in God's speech (40:2) |
| אֱלוֹהַּ ("God") | 41× | 60× | the poetry only — from Job's lament (3:4, 23) to God's speeches (39:17; 40:2) |
| אֲדֹנָי ("the Lord") | 1× | 445× | 28:28 only |

**The narrator and God use the covenant name; in the poetry the human disputants do not.** (In the prose, Job himself uses it three times in one verse, 1:21.) They speak as what the story makes them — men of the East who know God as אֵל ("God"), אֱלוֹהַּ ("God") and שַׁדַּי ("the Almighty"). The one exception is 12:9, where Job's line "the hand of the LORD has done this" is word for word Isa 41:20 (see the Intertextual Map). At 28:28, BHS notes that many manuscripts read יהוה ("the LORD") for אֲדֹנָי ("the Lord"). `[T]` for the counts and the apparatus note, *as BHS prints it*.

### Audiences

- **Narrative world:** Job, his wife, the four speakers and the family of 42:11 — none of whom ever learns of the council in heaven. `[T]`
- **Compositional:** unknown. Proposals that the book addresses exiled Israel rest on real verbal features — the restoration idiom שָׁב אֶת־שְׁבוּת ("restored the fortunes", 42:10), and the shared vocabulary with Lam 3 — but a feature is not an occasion. `[I]` at best; confidence low.
- **Canonical:** Israel reading the Writings, who has the Psalter behind it and Proverbs ahead. **Default to this audience for interpretation.** It is the audience that, like the reader of the prologue, knows what Job does not. `[I]`

### Pressure

The book answers **two pressures, and the prologue states the first one in three words.** `[T]`

1. **Is piety disinterested?** The Accuser asks, הַחִנָּם יָרֵא אִיּוֹב אֱלֹהִים ("Does Job fear God for nothing?", 1:9). If he is stripped, "he will surely curse You to Your face" (1:11; 2:5). This is a question about **God's worth**, as much as about Job's virtue: is God feared for himself, or only for what he gives?
2. **Does suffering prove guilt?** The friends answer yes. "Remember now, who ever perished being innocent?" (4:7); "If you are pure and upright, surely now He would rouse Himself for you" (8:6); "Is not your wickedness great?" (22:5). The reader knows from 1:8 and 2:3 that the premise is false in this case. So the dialogue is a test of a **doctrine**, conducted in front of a reader who already has the answer to the particular case but not the general one.

### Purpose

*To show a righteous man who, stripped of everything, will neither curse God nor buy relief by confessing sins he did not commit; to expose the friends' tidy theology as speaking falsely of God (42:7); and to bring the sufferer, and the reader, to the fear of God that wants God himself rather than his gifts — reached not through an explanation but through God's self-disclosure as Creator (38–41).* `[I]`

## The Text and Its Versions

This section is supplied because this project works from the Hebrew as substrate with the Greek alongside. It corresponds to dig-deeper's original-languages and textual-variant extensions. Every feature below belongs to a named witness, and several are reading traditions rather than authorial.

### The Masoretic frame

**The accentuation marks the book as prose–poetry–prose**, with the seams at 3:1/3:2 and 42:6/42:7. `[T]`, *as the WLC transcribes Leningrad*:

| Section | Verses | Prose signs (pashta, zaqef) | Poetic signs (dehi, ole) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1:1–3:1 | 36 | 42 + 52 | 0 + 0 |
| 3:2–42:6 | 1,023 | 0 + 0 | 610 + 37 |
| 42:7–17 | 11 | 9 + 15 | 0 + 0 |

**One detail matters for the arc map.** Elihu's prose introduction (32:1–5) is accented with the **poetic** system (dehi 4×, ole 2×; pashta and zaqef 0×). The Masoretic tradition therefore treats 3:2–42:6 as a single unbroken poetic block, Elihu's narrative preface included. This is a reading tradition, and it tells us how the book was read aloud; it does not tell us how it was composed. `[T]` / `[I]`

**The paragraphing.** The WLC carries 39 paragraph markers in Job. Every speech from chapter 3 to 42:6 closes with a petuḥah (פ) or setumah (ס) **except one: 27:23 runs straight into chapter 28 with no marker**, and the next marker falls at 28:28. Two internal placements are also worth noting: **28:11 and 28:19** divide the wisdom poem into three strophes, each of the second and third opening with the same question (28:12 = 28:20); and **40:2 and 40:5** bracket the first exchange between YHWH and Job. `[T]`, *as the WLC transcribes Leningrad*.

### The Greek Job

**The Old Greek of Job is substantially shorter than the Hebrew.** BHS says at the head of its apparatus that "about a fifth of this book is lacking in the Greek" (*1/5 fere huius libri deest in 𝔊*). The Rahlfs text exported from Logos marks the material supplied later, chiefly from Theodotion, with Origen's asterisk: **212 asterisks and 135 metobeli** in the export. **Swete prints Codex Vaticanus, in which that supplementary material stands without any marks.** So the corpus's Greek layer looks full-length. The Old Greek's shortness is visible only in the Rahlfs signs. `[T]`, *as BHS and the Rahlfs export print it*.

Five features of the Greek matter for the digs `[T]`, Swete unless stated:

1. **הַשָּׂטָן ("the Accuser") becomes ὁ διάβολος ("the devil")** throughout 1:6–2:7. It is the word the New Testament will use.
2. **Job's wife is given a speech** (2:9, with five additional clauses). Her cry becomes a lament for her children and her own wanderings.
3. **19:25–26 reads resurrection into the text**: "on the earth he will raise up (ἀναστῆσαι) my skin". And **42:17a** adds, "it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up".
4. **42:17b–e identify Job with Jobab**, a king of Edom (Gen 36:33, checked). They make him "fifth from Abraham" and turn the three friends into kings. The Greek thus supplies the genealogy the Hebrew withholds — an early reading of the book, not evidence about it.
5. **The Greek gives different wording at points the New Testament uses.** Phil 1:19 reproduces Swete's 13:16 exactly. But 1 Cor 3:19 (from 5:13) and Rom 11:35 (from 41:11 [Heb 41:3]) are **nearer the Hebrew than the Greek**. See the Intertextual Map.

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **1:1–2:13** | **I. The prologue: the question asked in heaven** (prose) | Establishes Job's integrity by the narrator's word and by God's (1:1, 8; 2:3). Poses the Accuser's question (1:9). Two tests strip Job of property, children and health, and he does not "sin with his lips" (2:10). Three friends come to "sympathize with him and comfort him" (2:11) and sit silent for seven days. |
| **3:1–27:23** | **II. The human debate** | Opens with Job's curse on his birth (3). Three rounds follow, each Eliphaz → Job → Bildad → Job → Zophar → Job (4–14; 15–21; 22–27). The friends defend retribution; Job moves from wishing he had never been born to demanding a hearing with God. The third round **breaks down**: Bildad's last speech is six verses (25:1–6), and Zophar has none. |
| **28:1–28** | **III. The wisdom poem: where is wisdom found?** | A calm poem at the book's hinge. Humans can mine silver and gold (28:1–11), but wisdom's place is known to God alone (28:12–27). It ends: "the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil is understanding" (28:28). |
| **29:1–42:6** | **IV. Three discourses and a meeting** | Job's closing speech — then and now, and an oath of innocence ending in a signed demand for God to answer (29–31; "The words of Job are ended", 31:40). Elihu's four speeches (32–37), which end in a storm. YHWH answers "out of the whirlwind" (38:1–40:2; 40:6–41:34 [Heb 41:26]), and Job replies twice (40:3–5; 42:1–6). |
| **42:7–17** | **V. The epilogue: the verdict given on earth** (prose) | God's verdict on the friends (42:7–8). Job's intercession and restoration (42:9–10). The family's comfort (42:11), a double portion (42:12–15) and a patriarch's death (42:16–17). |

**Device: a prose frame around a poetic core, with the poetry arranged symmetrically.** The frame is marked three ways: by genre, by the Masoretic accentuation (see above), and by the divine names — YHWH in the frame, El/Eloah/Shaddai in the debate. Inside the core, Job's opening soliloquy (3) and his closing soliloquy (29–31) bracket the dialogue proper. Chapter 28 stands between them at the hinge. After it the human voices give way — Job's to Elihu's, Elihu's to God's. `[T]` for the markers; `[I]` for the symmetry.

**How the movements relate.** I asks the question; II fails to answer it; III says where the answer lies; IV brings the answer's owner onto the stage; V gives the verdict. The movement is from **heaven, unseen** (I), to **earth, argued** (II), to **heaven, speaking** (IV), to **earth, restored** (V). `[I]`

**Three structural judgements are genuinely contested, and the digs should test them rather than inherit them:**

1. **The third cycle (22–27).** 24:18–25, 26:5–14 and 27:13–23 sound like the friends' doctrine in Job's mouth. And 27:13 is almost word for word Zophar's 20:29 — "This is the portion of a wicked man from God" — checked: זֶה חֵלֶק־אָדָם רָשָׁע occurs at exactly these two verses. **Option A:** the text is disordered, and parts of 24–27 belong to Bildad and Zophar. `[S]` **Option B:** the breakdown is the point, and the friends have run out of words (32:3, 5 says as much). Job then takes over their lines, either to parody them or to concede their doctrine while denying its application. `[I]` Confidence in either: moderate. **The Masoretic text as it stands attributes 26–31 to Job** (26:1; 27:1; 29:1).
2. **Who speaks chapter 28?** Syntactically it continues Job's discourse: 27:1 introduces a speech, 29:1 resumes with the same formula, and the Masoretic paragraphing puts **no break between 27:23 and 28:1** — every other speech in the book ends with one. Its calm tone differs from everything around it. **Option A:** Job, reaching the insight the friends could not. **Option B:** the narrator's own interlude, placed at the hinge. `[S]` both. The formula that brackets it, וַיֹּסֶף אִיּוֹב שְׂאֵת מְשָׁלוֹ ("Job continued his discourse", 27:1; 29:1), occurs **only** at these two places. It is the verb-and-noun pair of Balaam's oracles, וַיִּשָּׂא מְשָׁלוֹ ("he took up his discourse", seven times in Num 23–24). `[T]`
3. **Elihu (32–37).** Is he a fourth failed friend, a genuine advance, or an interruption? `[S]` The text gives three facts. He is **angry four times** in five verses (32:2–5). He anticipates God's charge that Job speaks "without knowledge" (34:35; 35:16 → 38:2). And **he is never mentioned again** — God's verdict names Eliphaz "and your two friends" (42:7). `[T]` The storm he describes (36:27–37:24) is the storm God speaks from. `[I]`

## Microscript

*A blameless man is stripped of everything to test whether anyone fears God חִנָּם ("for nothing", 1:9). He will neither curse God nor accept his friends' verdict that suffering proves guilt, and he demands that God answer him. YHWH answers from the storm — not with the reason, but with himself, the Creator who shuts in the sea and rules Leviathan — and the man who has now seen him (42:5) is declared to have spoken "what is right" (42:7), prays for the friends who did not, and is restored.*

**Keynote:** the fear of God. It is Job's character in the narrator's first sentence — וִירֵא אֱלֹהִים וְסָר מֵרָע ("fearing God and turning away from evil", 1:1). It is the Accuser's question (1:9). And it is the poem's last word — יִרְאַת אֲדֹנָי הִיא חָכְמָה וְסוּר מֵרָע בִּינָה ("the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil is understanding", 28:28). סוּר מֵרָע ("turning from evil") is at 1:1, 1:8, 2:3 and 28:28 in Job, and nowhere else in the book (WLC, phrase search). `[T]`

**Leitwort of the argument:** the language of the lawsuit. יכח ("to argue, reprove, arbitrate") occurs **17 times in 15 verses** of Job — 17 of its 59 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible. צדק ("to be righteous, be in the right", verb) occurs **17 times** — 17 of 41 in the whole Hebrew Bible. מִשְׁפָּט ("justice, one's case") occurs 23 times, רִיב ("to contend", verb and noun) 12 times. All counts WLC lemma. `[T]`

**Test against the movements.** I poses the "for nothing" question. II is the refusal: Job will not curse God, and he will not confess to guilt he does not have. III locates wisdom in the fear of God, beyond human search. IV brings the demanded answer. Elihu presses the charge and prepares the storm, and he is the movement the microscript serves least — which is itself a finding about his ambiguous status. V gives the verdict and the restoration. **The microscript deliberately omits a reason for Job's suffering**, because the book never gives Job one.

## Intertextual Map

**Profile: dense allusion, no formula citations — and above all, a book that quotes itself.** Job never says "as it is written". Its links outward run mainly to the Psalter, the wisdom tradition, the prophetic laments (Jeremiah, Lamentations, Isaiah) and the patriarchal narratives. For almost all of them **the direction of dependence cannot be settled from the text**, so the map reports the overlap and what it does in Job, not who borrowed from whom. Inside the book, the speakers quote one another, God, and themselves (Table B). **Consequence for the sweep: Move 4 (internal echo) will carry more weight than Move 2.** Move 2 will be heavily loaded only at chapters 3, 7, 12, 28 and 38–41. `[T]` / `[I]`

## Christological Trajectory

**Job contains no direct messianic prophecy, and the overview should not create one.** Its Christological weight lies in three places: a **trajectory** (Job's longing for a mediator), a **type** reached through an established category (the innocent servant who suffers and intercedes), and a **contrast** (Job is not the sinless one). Each is flagged for confidence.

### 1. Trajectory — the mediator Job cannot find

Job's lawsuit produces a sequence of longed-for figures. `[T]` for the wording; `[I]` for the sequence.

- **9:33** — "There is no umpire between us, who may lay his hand upon us both." The umpire is a מוֹכִיחַ ("arbiter"); Swete has μεσίτης ("mediator"). *Text note:* BHS records the reading לוּ ("would that") in some manuscripts and versions, which NIV84 follows.
- **16:19–21** — "Even now, behold, my witness (עֵד) is in heaven, and my advocate (שָׂהֵד) is on high." Someone to argue "for a man with God, as a man (בֶּן־אָדָם) with his neighbor" (16:21).
- **19:25–27** — "I know that my Redeemer (גֹּאֵל) lives, and at the last He will take His stand on the earth … from my flesh I shall see God."
- **33:23–24** — Elihu's picture of "an angel as mediator (מַלְאָךְ מֵלִיץ) … one out of a thousand" who says "I have found a ransom (כֹּפֶר)".

**The sequence moves from denial, to hope, to certainty, and then — in Elihu — to ransom.** The New Testament names the figure: "one mediator (μεσίτης) also between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave Himself as a ransom (ἀντίλυτρον)" (1 Tim 2:5–6). He is the "Advocate with the Father" (1 John 2:1) and the Redeemer who stands on the earth. *Confidence: high for the trajectory.*

**Keep honest:** the identity of the גֹּאֵל ("Redeemer") in 19:25 is contested. Is it God himself, against God as accuser, or a third party? `[S]` NASB95's capital R is an interpretive choice, and so is NIV84's margin "defender". Job's words are a longing within his lawsuit, not a prediction he understood. *Confidence: low for any claim that Job foresaw Christ.* The trajectory is canonical, not psychological.

### 2. Type — the innocent servant who suffers "without cause" and intercedes

The category is the **servant**, established in Isaiah. `[I]`

- God calls Job עַבְדִּי ("My servant") twice in the prologue and **four times in the epilogue** (1:8; 2:3; 42:7, 8 [×3]; WLC). `[T]`
- God admits Job was ruined חִנָּם ("without cause", 2:3). `[T]`
- Job is struck on the cheek (16:10) and spat upon (30:10) — with the vocabulary of the servant who gave his cheek and did not hide his face from spitting (Isa 50:6: לְחִי, "cheek"; רֹק, "spitting"). `[T]` for the lemmas.
- **At the end, the servant prays for those who wronged him, and on the strength of that prayer God does not deal with them "according to your folly".** The fortunes are restored "when he prayed for his friends" (42:8–10). The pattern of Isa 53:12 — the servant who "interceded for the transgressors" and divides the spoil — is close. `[I]`

*Confidence: moderate.* The type runs through the servant category and passes the escalation test. Job's prayer spares three friends; Christ's intercession saves a people. Isaiah is not quoted.

**A further thread.** The Accuser stands in God's presence to accuse (1:6–11). Christ prays for Peter against Satan's sifting (Luke 22:31–32). "Who will bring a charge against God's elect? … Christ Jesus is he who … intercedes for us" (Rom 8:33–34). *Confidence: moderate (conceptual).*

### 3. Contrast — Job is not the sinless sufferer

God rebukes Job's words (38:2) and asks, "Will you condemn Me that you may be justified?" (40:8). Job lays his hand on his mouth (40:4) and retracts (42:6). **Job is righteous, not sinless**, and his righteousness includes accusing God. Christ is the sufferer who is without sin and "while being reviled, did not revile in return, … but kept entrusting Himself to Him who judges righteously" (1 Pet 2:23). `[I]` *Confidence: high.*

### 4. Further lines, lower confidence

- **Wisdom hidden and found (28).** Wisdom's place is known to God alone (28:23). The New Testament says that all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ (Col 2:3), who is "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24). *Moderate.*
- **Walking on the sea (9:8, 11).** What Job says only God does, Jesus does (Mark 6:48). *Moderate.*
- **Resurrection.** "If a man dies, will he live again?" (14:14). The Greek already reads 19:26 and 42:17a as resurrection. `[T]` for the Greek; the Hebrew of 19:26 is disputed. `[S]` *Moderate.*

### Moralism check

**The besetting trap is "be patient like Job".** It usually rests on Jas 5:11 read through older English ("the patience of Job"). But the Job of the dialogue is not patient in the stoic sense. He asks, "Why should I not be impatient?" (21:4, לֹא־תִקְצַר רוּחִי), curses his birth (3) and demands a court date with God (13:3; 23:3–7; 31:35). What James commends is ὑπομονή ("endurance"): Job held on to God and would not let him go. **The gospel ground is God's free grace.** The Accuser's question was whether anyone fears God "for nothing". The book shows a man who does, and a God who is worth it. The gospel then supplies what Job lacked: the mediator, the ransom, and a verdict of "right" (42:7) given to the ungodly as a gift. Preach Job as the man who clings to God in the dark and the servant who intercedes — and Christ as the Redeemer Job longed for. Then the call to endure flows from grace, not towards it. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **Preaching the friends.** *How it sounds:* "As Eliphaz wisely said, 'those who plow iniquity … harvest it' (4:8)"; "happy is the man whom God reproves" (5:17). *Why it is wrong:* God says the friends "have not spoken of Me what is right" (42:7). They are wrong not because every sentence is false — Paul cites Eliphaz as Scripture (1 Cor 3:19) — but because they apply true general sentences as a verdict on Job's particular case. *Bites at:* 4–5; 8; 11; 15; 18; 20; 22; 25, and **Elihu (32–37)**, whose status the book leaves open. *Corrective:* quote every line of the dialogue with its speaker, and ask what the line does in that mouth.

2. **"The patience of Job".** *How it sounds:* the sufferer who never complains, with 1:21 and 2:10 as the whole book. *Why it is wrong:* chapters 3–31 are the book's bulk, and God says it was Job, not the friends, who spoke rightly. That verdict follows twenty-nine chapters of lament and protest addressed to God. *Bites at:* 1:20–22; 2:10; and in the omission of chapters 3, 9–10, 16, 23 and 31. *Corrective:* lament addressed **to** God is vindicated. Orthodoxy spoken **about** God against the sufferer is condemned.

3. **Supplying the explanation.** *How it sounds:* "God is proving you, as he proved Job"; "Satan has asked for you". *Why it is wrong:* Job is never told the prologue, and God's answer does not tell him. The book gives the reader the heavenly scene precisely so that the reader does **not** claim to know which scene lies behind another person's suffering. *Bites at:* 1:6–12; 2:1–7; 38–41; 42:11. **A pastoral landmine at the bedside.** *Corrective:* the prologue forbids the friends' error; it does not license a new one. Also avoid dualism: the Accuser acts only under God's permission (1:12; 2:6), and the narrator says the evils were those "the LORD had brought on him" (42:11).

4. **Hearing the storm speeches as a put-down.** *How it sounds:* "Who are you to question God? Be quiet." *Why it is wrong:* God answers — the thing Job asked for (13:22; 31:35) and the friends said would not happen. His questions answer chapter 3 point by point: the doors of the womb, the dawn, Leviathan (see the Echo Table). He goes on to call Job "My servant" four times. *Bites at:* 38:1–42:6. *Corrective:* preach the content of the creation tour — the bounded sea, the fed raven, the wild creatures no one tames — as the answer, not only the fact that God spoke.

5. **The ending as a reward, or as proof the friends were right after all.** *How it sounds:* "Be faithful and God will double it"; or, "See — righteousness is rewarded, as Bildad said." *Why it is wrong:* the restoration comes **after** the verdict, and "when he prayed for his friends" (42:10). It is God's free act, the thing the Accuser said God was buying. The livestock is exactly doubled (1:3 → 42:12), **but the children are not**: seven sons and three daughters, as before (42:13). *Keep honest:* the form שִׁבְעָנָה ("seven") at 42:13 is anomalous, and some ancient readers took it as "fourteen". `[S]` The WLC text as it stands gives the same number as 1:2. *Bites at:* 42:10–17. *Corrective:* the epilogue is grace after vindication, not payment for endurance. Bildad's "your end will increase greatly" (8:7) is fulfilled ironically, on terms Bildad never imagined.

6. **Building on the cruxes as though they were plain.** *How it sounds:* confident doctrine from 13:15, 19:25–27 or 42:6. *Why it is wrong:* 13:15 rests on a qere (see Text and Versions). 19:26 is disputed. 42:6 has a verb with no object, אֶמְאַס ("I reject/retract/despise"), and וְנִחַמְתִּי ("and I am comforted / repent"). NASB95: "I retract, and I repent in dust and ashes." ESV and NIV84: "I despise myself and repent in dust and ashes." Swete: "I count myself earth and ashes." And נחם is the verb of the comforters (2:11; 16:2; 21:34; 42:11). *Bites at:* 13:15; 19:25–27; 42:1–6. *Corrective:* say what is certain and flag the rest. **Where the pulpit text and NASB95 diverge, tell the congregation what they are hearing.** At 9:33 NIV84 says the opposite of NASB95 and ESV. At 42:6 "I despise myself" is heard very differently from "I retract".

Notes for series planning:

- **The versification is not the seam in two places.** 3:1 is prose and belongs with the prologue's narrative, though it rightly heads the lament. 32:1–5 is prose narrative, though the Masoretic tradition accents it as poetry. Either way, it introduces Elihu rather than closing Job's speech.
- **A six-sermon series** that keeps the book's shape: 1–2; 3; 19 (with 9:33 and 16:19 as the build); 28; 38:1–42:6; 42:7–17. It loses the friends' doctrine as a sustained foil, so one sermon must name it.
- **A single sermon** on the whole book is best built on 1:9 and 42:7–8: the question "for nothing?" and the verdict "My servant Job … has spoken what is right".
- **Do not preach the friends' speeches as standalone texts.** If a friend's chapter is the unit, the sermon must hold it against 42:7.
