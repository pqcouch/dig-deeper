# Logos questions — Job, audit 6

**Raised:** 16 November 2026, by claim audit 6 (Sermons 8–9 batch, Job 38:1–42:17).
**Why these are here.** Each was queued because **the primary texts cannot settle it.** Four are questions about how widely a reading is attested, or about a conjecture — and the corpus holds **one** Hebrew manuscript tradition, so it can verify wording and can never report spread. Three are questions about what interpreters or cultures have held, which no search of the text can answer. Audit 6 therefore records no verdict on any of them.

**Two rounds, because they need different shelves.** Round 1 wants the textual notes and the apparatus volumes; round 2 wants the commentaries and the dictionaries.

**What the library has, for targeting.** Owned and relevant: **Keil & Delitzsch**; **Lange**; **Andersen** (TOTC Job); **Konkel–Longman** (Cornerstone); **Holman Old Testament Commentary, Job**; the **Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible**; the **NET Bible notes**; the **Lexham Bible Dictionary**; **Freeman's *Manners and Customs of the Bible***; the **BHS apparatus** and the **Swete apparatus** (plus the Apparatus Alternative volume); **Jones, *Old Testament Quotations and Allusions in the New Testament***; **BDAG** as a PDF outside Logos. **Not owned, and relevant:** **BHQ** (the Job fascicle), the **Göttingen *Iob***, **Metzger's *Textual Commentary***, the **Peshitta**, any **Vulgate besides the Clementine**. **So two of the four spread questions below cannot be fully answered from the library either** — and where that is so, the question asks for the next best thing: what the owned textual notes report about the spread, rather than a count of manuscripts.

## Round 1 — textual

### Q1 (#188). The preposition at 42:7 and 42:8 — the one variant in Job with a doctrinal consequence

**The data, already established.** The BHS apparatus records at 42:7 and again at 42:8 that **many manuscripts read בְּעַבְדִּי where the printed text has כְּעַבְדִּי**. On the printed text God says the friends have not spoken rightly **as** His servant Job has — commending Job's speech. On the variant He says they have not spoken rightly **concerning** His servant Job — saying nothing about Job's own speech. **Swete agrees with the variant at 42:8**: οὐ γὰρ ἐλαλήσατε κατὰ τοῦ θεράποντός μου Ἰὼβ ἀληθές, "you have not spoken what is true **against** my servant Job."

**Ask:**
1. **How widely is בְּ attested, and in what kind of witnesses?** BHS's "many manuscripts" is a claim about spread that cannot be tested from the corpus. Is there anything in the **Lexham Textual Notes** or the **NET notes** at Job 42:7–8 that quantifies or characterises it?
2. **Does any commentator adopt בְּ?** Specifically Keil & Delitzsch, Andersen, Konkel–Longman, Lange, Holman.
3. **Does any English version adopt it?** And does any commentator note that the variant dissolves the tension with 38:2?
4. **Does any of them treat כְּ as the harder reading** — i.e. argue for it on the grounds that it is the one a scribe or translator would be tempted to smooth?

**Why it matters.** The audit prefers the printed text at *moderate* on internal grounds alone. **A sermon on 42:7 that presents "as my servant Job" as unanimous is overstating the evidence**, and the gap is large enough that the preacher should know it.

### Q2 (#181). 41:15 — the word the pulpit text does not have

**The data.** The printed Hebrew at 41:15 [Hebrew 41:7] reads **גַּאֲוָה ("pride")**. BHS proposes **גֵּוֹה ("back")** with the Greek, Aquila and the Vulgate — **and the ESV has adopted the conjecture.** A dig Headline counts גַּאֲוָה as one of five pride-words in the second divine speech, three of which occur nowhere else in Job.

**Ask:** Do the commentators read "pride" or "back"? **Is the ESV's choice usual or unusual among modern versions?** Does any commentator note that the pride reading gives the speech a five-word field culminating in "king over all the sons of pride" at 41:34?

**Why it matters.** If "back" is right, the Headline counts four pride-words, not five. **And either way an ESV congregation is one word short of the pattern the sermon would be tracing.**

### Q3 (#169). The suspended letters at 38:13 and 38:15

**The data.** The BHS apparatus marks a **suspended ʿayin** in רְשָׁעִים at 38:13, and notes the same at 38:15 — the first speech's only two mentions of the wicked.

**Ask:** Is this pair part of the traditional list of suspended letters (with Judg 18:30, Ps 80:14)? **What have the Masoretes or later tradition taken it to signify?** And is there any reason beyond coincidence that it falls on both occurrences of one word in one speech?

**Why it matters.** This is **reception, not composition**, and the audit has it capped at *moderate* and barred from the overview on exactly those grounds. The question is whether it is worth a footnote at all.

### Q4 (#170). The retroversion at 38:34

**The data.** Swete has ὑπακούσεταί σου ("it will obey you") where the printed Hebrew has **תְּכַסֶּךָּ ("it will cover you")**. BHS asks, **with its own question-mark**, whether the Greek reflects **תַּעֲנֶךָּ ("it will answer you")**.

**Ask:** Does any commentator or textual note accept the retroversion? **Is ὑπακούω elsewhere a rendering of ענה, or of כסה?**

**Why it matters, and it is sharper than it looks.** A dig Headline observes that **ענה ("answer") in 38:1–40:5 occurs only in the speech's frame** — God answers, Job answers, and the verb never appears inside the tour. **If the retroversion is right, that Headline is a feature of the Masoretic text only**, because 38:34 would contain an "answer" inside the tour. The audit has the Headline disclosed on that basis.

## Round 2 — interpretive and background

### Q5 (#176, and the audit's own reframing). How is the construction at 42:6 construed?

**This is the highest-priority question in the book.** The verse is the climax, the English versions differ sharply, and **the BHS apparatus records no variant at all** — so the whole difficulty is construal.

**The data, as the audit left them — two of the four were corrected in the auditing, so this is not the form the dig put them in.** The verse reads עַל־כֵּן אֶמְאַס וְנִחַמְתִּי עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר.

- **אֶמְאַס has no object**, and absolute מָאַס is normal in Job — at **7:16, 36:5 and also 34:33**, which the dig missed. *Datum confirmed and broadened.*
- **The exact form וְנִחַמְתִּי stands in four verses — Jer 18:8; 18:10; 26:3; Job 42:6.** *The count is confirmed; the dig's description of the continuations is not.* **Only two of the three Jeremiah verses have עַל, and one of those two has "the good" as its object** (Jer 18:10, עַל־הַטּוֹבָה), while **Jer 26:3 has אֶל, not עַל.** The substance holds — all three mean "relent concerning X" with a preposition and object — but only one of the three is עַל + "the evil".
- **The ordered phrase עַל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר stands in exactly four verses — Exod 32:14; Jer 18:8; Ezek 14:22; Jonah 3:10 — and every one is governed by נָחַם**, on both the plain and the skeletal pass. *The cleanest datum in the audit.*
- **The dig's fourth datum is withdrawn as an argument.** Job 2:8 does read בְּתוֹךְ־הָאֵפֶר, but **עַל is a perfectly normal ash-idiom in Hebrew — Jonah 3:6 reads וַיֵּשֶׁב עַל־הָאֵפֶר, "he sat on the ashes."** So the datum shows only what this book chose in that verse, and nothing should rest on it.
- Swete reads the last clause as a **predicate**: ἥγημαι δὲ ἐγὼ ἐμαυτὸν γῆν καὶ σποδόν, "I count myself earth and ashes" — **and it reads וְנִחַמְתִּי as ἐτάκην, "I melted away", which is neither of the options the dig was weighing.**

**But the audit's own spot checks changed the picture, and the question has to be put in the new form.** Two findings:
- **נָחַם followed by עַל occurs 39 times in the Hebrew Bible, and the object of עַל is always either a decreed action or outcome — the evil (Exod 32:14; Jer 18:8; Ezek 14:22; Jonah 3:10), the good (Jer 18:10) — or else a person or persons mourned** (2 Sam 13:39, Amnon; Jer 31:15, her children; Ezek 32:31, his multitude; Deut 32:36; Ps 90:13; 135:14, His servants). **It is never a substance or a place.** So **neither** English rendering has a clean parallel: "repent **in** dust and ashes" needs עַל to be locative with this verb, which no other verse supports; and "relent **concerning** dust and ashes" needs the object to be an action or a person, which dust and ashes is not — **unless the phrase is a self-designation**, which is exactly what Gen 18:27 ("and I am dust and ashes"), Job 30:19 ("I have become like dust and ashes") and Swete's predicate at **both** verses all take it to be.
- **And the audit strengthened that last point.** The conventional mourning idiom — ashes on the head, sitting in ashes (2 Sam 13:19; Isa 61:3; Jer 6:26; Jonah 3:6; Esth 4:1, 4:3; Dan 9:3) — uses אֵפֶר **without** עָפָר and describes **a gesture**. The **bound** phrase עָפָר וָאֵפֶר, which stands in only Gen 18:27 and Job 42:6, describes **what the speaker is**. *The two idioms are distinct, and 42:6 uses the self-description one.*
- **First-person נִחַמְתִּי does mean "I repented" at Jer 31:19** ("for after I turned away, I repented"), with no עַל. **So the verb's sense alone cannot settle the verse**, and the dig's Headline should not be read as foreclosing it.

**Ask, in this order:**
1. **How does each commentator construe עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר** — locative ("in dust and ashes"), or as the object of נחם ("concerning…")?
2. **Does any of them notice that the object-type is otherwise unattested**, and if so what do they do with it?
3. **Does any read the clause as a predicate self-designation, with Swete** — "I am consoled concerning myself, who am dust and ashes", or similar?
4. **What do they make of the objectless אֶמְאַס?** Is "I retract" (NASB95) or "I despise myself" (ESV) the majority reading, and on what grounds does either supply its object?
5. **Does any of them cite Gen 18:27 here?**

**Why it matters.** The ESV supplies "myself" where the Hebrew has no object, so **the congregation will hear a self-loathing Job and then hear, one verse later, that he "spoke what is right" about God.** That is the most consequential pulpit gap in the series.

### Q6 (#189). The tension between 42:7 and 38:2

**The difficulty.** At 38:2 God says Job "darkens counsel by words without knowledge" and Job agrees at 42:3. At 42:7 God says Job spoke **נְכוֹנָה** and the friends did not. Either the book contradicts itself or the two verdicts are about different things.

**The four resolutions the audit set out**, with the first preferred at *moderate–high* on internal grounds: (1) **different objects** — God faults Job's understanding and commends his direction, the preposition **אֵלַי** ("**to** me") doing the work, so that the friends spoke *about* God *to a sufferer* while Job spoke *to* God *about his suffering*; (2) **different standards** — נְכוֹנָה is "reliable" rather than "accurate"; (3) **the variant** of Q1; (4) **sequence** — Job's last word before the verdict was a retraction.

**Ask:** Which does each commentator take? **Does any of them rest on the preposition אֵלַי, as resolution (1) does?** Does any treat the two verdicts as genuinely unresolved? And does any use the variant as the solution?

**Why it matters.** This is the central difficulty of the book and **the Sermon 9 backbone turns on it.** The audit will not take a preacher past *moderate–high* on an internal argument alone.

### Q7 (#179). Royal-hunt background to 41:1–8

**The claim, recalled and not verified.** That hippopotamus- and crocodile-hunting were royal motifs in Egypt, so that the catalogue of failed capture-methods at 41:1–8 deflates precisely the royal boast God has just invited at 40:10–14.

**Ask:** Is the royal-hunt motif attested, and for these animals? **Freeman's *Manners and Customs*** and the **Lexham Bible Dictionary** are the likely shelves; a commentary on 41 may carry it.

**Why it matters.** It would strengthen a Headline about the royal regalia offered at 40:10. **Nothing rests on it yet, and nothing should until it is sourced.**

### Q8 (#192). Daughters inheriting alongside sons

**The data.** At 42:15 Job gives his daughters **נַחֲלָה ("inheritance") among their brothers** — and he has seven sons, so the law's provision for daughters (Num 27:1–11, with Num 36 and Josh 17:3–6) does not apply. **The narrator draws no conclusion and no later text appeals to it.**

**Ask:** Is this remarkable in the book's own cultural setting, or ordinary? **Is there documentary evidence of daughters inheriting alongside sons in the ancient Near East?** And do the commentators treat it as a mark of restored generosity, as a legal oddity, or pass over it?

**Why it matters.** It carries a preaching pitfall in both directions. **The audit's position is that the verse is striking and is not a norm**, and a background answer would let the preacher say which.

### Q9 (#191). The three names at 42:14

**The data.** **קְצִיעָה** is "cassia", a spice — the plural stands at Ps 45:9. **פּוּךְ** is eye-paint, standing in four verses: 2 Kgs 9:30 (Jezebel painting her eyes), Isa 54:11, Jer 4:30 (Jerusalem painting hers), 1 Chr 29:2. So **קֶרֶן הַפּוּךְ is "Horn of Eye-Paint", a cosmetic jar** — named in the one verse of Job that uses a word for beauty. **יְמִימָה** is less clear; it is written like the adverb in the "from year to year" formula.

**Ask:** What do the lexica and commentators give for **יְמִימָה** — "dove", "day/bright", or something else? **Do any of them read the cosmetic names as a wry note, or purely as a father's delight?** Is there any ancient tradition about the three daughters?

**Why it matters.** It carries a preaching pitfall about treating the daughters as decoration. **The audit has the words at high and either reading at low–moderate, and will not choose between them from the text.**

### Q10 (new — raised by audit 6 itself, #175 and #193). Rom 11:33–35 and the Greek Job

**Why this is new.** The dig claimed that **Rom 11:35 follows the Masoretic Hebrew of Job 41:11 against the Old Greek**, since Swete's Job 41:2 reads "who will withstand me and endure?" with nothing about giving and repaying. **The audit broke that.**

**What the audit found.** **Swete Job 21:31 ends τίς ἀνταποδώσει αὐτῷ, "who will repay him?" — and τίς together with any form of ἀνταποδίδωμι occurs in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Old Testament, that one.** It shares with Rom 11:35 the interrogative, the verb and the dative, differing only in voice — **matching Paul better than Job 41:3 matches his person and voice**, since the Hebrew has God speaking in the first person active and Paul has an impersonal future passive. Separately, **ἀνεξιχνίαστος at Rom 11:33 occurs in Swete at Job 5:9, 9:10 and 34:24 and nowhere else** in the Greek Old Testament but Ode 8:6. And Rom 11:34 is **Isa 40:13** almost verbatim. **So Rom 11:33–35 looks like a Joban frame around an Isaianic quotation — a stronger claim about Job than the one it replaces, and one that drops the Hebrew-against-Greek argument.**

**Ask — and *Jones, Old Testament Quotations and Allusions in the New Testament*, which is owned, is the first place to look:**
1. **Which Joban verse do the authorities give as the source of Rom 11:35** — 41:11, 21:31, 9:4, or none?
2. **Does any of them note the Swete Job 21:31 wording?**
3. **Does any commentator on Romans treat 11:33 as Joban** on the strength of ἀνεξιχνίαστος?
4. **Does Paul's text at 11:35 agree with any known Greek Job recension** — Theodotion in particular, given the Greek Job's recensional history?

**Why it matters.** The claim is the load-bearing citation of the 40:6–42:6 dig's Christological Reading, and the overview carries it in two places, **both rated high**. The audit's reframing is already applied; what is unresolved is whether the authorities see it that way. **Caution recorded by the auditor: Swete is a diplomatic text of Codex Vaticanus, so a finding about "the Old Greek" that rests on Swete alone is a finding about one manuscript.** Rahlfs 1979 is in the library and should be checked at Job 21:31 and 41:2 before this is settled.

## How to use this file

Run **round 1 first**: Q1 bears on Q6, and Q4 bears on a Headline that is already disclosed as text-dependent. **Q5 is the single most valuable question in the set** and is worth putting to several commentaries rather than one.

For each answer, record the work, the edition and the page or location, so the finding can be cited as directly consulted rather than recalled. **Where a commentator is silent, that is an answer too** — the audit's "not assessable from the corpus" scores become "not addressed in the owned literature", which is itself worth knowing before a sermon leans on a reading.
