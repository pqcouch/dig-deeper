## A note on versification, before anything else

**The Hebrew and the English divide this unit differently, and every reference below says which it is using.** `[T]`

| | Hebrew (WLC, BHS) | English (NASB95, ESV) |
|---|---|---|
| Chapter 40 | 32 verses | 24 verses |
| Chapter 41 | 26 verses | 34 verses |
| Leviathan begins at | **40:25** | **41:1** |
| Leviathan ends at | **41:26** | **41:34** |

So **English 40:6–42:6 is Hebrew 40:6–41:26 plus 42:1–6 — fifty-nine verses in all.** This report gives **English references as its primary citation**, because that is what the pulpit and the study text use, with the Hebrew in brackets wherever the two diverge: *41:34 [Heb 41:26]*. Where a count is made, it is made on the Hebrew text and says so.

**The Greek diverges again, and differently.** Swete prints what the Hebrew numbers 40:6–41:26 as **40:1–41:25**, so "Job 41:11" means three different verses in the three traditions. The first half of this unit found the same trap at the other end of the speech (Swete 39:31–35 for Hebrew 40:1–5). `[T]` Anyone comparing editions on these two chapters needs the table above in front of them.

**Extent.** The unit runs from the second speech's heading at 40:6 to Job's second reply at 42:6. 40:6 is a fresh start, not a continuation: BHS's own apparatus notes *"6.7 = 38,1.3"*, and the WLC prints a *petuchah* after 40:5 and another after 42:6, with the next prose narrative beginning at 42:7. The epilogue (42:7–17) is a separate preaching unit in the overview and is not worked here. `[T]`

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across several tools:

1. **Job's own self-investiture at 29:14 is taken up in the second speech's opening three verses.**

> **v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), rulings A1 and B2: narrowed.**
**The counts stand exactly** — מִשְׁפָּטִי in those five verses with the possessive flipping to God at 40:8 (and 23:7 correctly excluded as a different word); הוֹד + הָדָר + לָבַשׁ in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible. **Two narrowings.** The uniqueness claim needs the qualifier *"with the verb לָבַשׁ"*, because **Ps 45:4 commands a human in the same noun pair** with a different clothing verb; and **לָבַשׁ with גֵּאוּת stands in exactly one verse, Ps 93:1 — which shares both the verb and the גאה root of Job 40:10's own גָּאוֹן.** So **40:10 reaches for the royal-divine robing motif, of which Ps 104:1 and Ps 93:1 are two instances, rather than quoting one psalm**; and the contact does not survive into Greek, where the verbs and both nouns differ. **The chapter-29 design is discarded:** chapter 29 ranks **12th to 25th of 39** Job chapters as a partner for this speech at the four rarity ceilings, **100 % of same-length blocks score higher, and the speech's dominant partner at every ceiling is chapter 38 — the other divine speech.** The לָבַשׁ chain is also broken by **39:19, inside the first speech**. *Retained as a thematic correspondence; the lexical design discarded.*

1. **(original wording)** Job's own self-investiture at 29:14 is answered word for word in the second speech's opening three verses — and the clothing he is offered is God's.** `[T]` At 29:14 Job said צֶדֶק לָבַשְׁתִּי … וְצָנִיף מִשְׁפָּטִי — "I **clothed** myself with **righteousness** … **my justice** was my robe and turban." The second speech takes up all three words at once:
   - **מִשְׁפָּטִי**, the identical suffixed form, stands in Job at **27:2, 29:14, 34:5, 34:6 and 40:8** (WLC). In the first four it is **Job's** justice — "God has taken away *my* right" (27:2), "*my* justice was my turban" (29:14), with Elihu quoting him twice. At **40:8 the possessive flips to God**: הַאַף תָּפֵר מִשְׁפָּטִי, "will you annul **My** justice?"
   - **צדק**: Job wore the noun צֶדֶק (29:14); God asks לְמַעַן תִּצְדָּק, "that you may be in the right" (40:8) — the same root, filed as a separate verb entry in the index.
   - **לבשׁ**: Job's verb at 29:14 is God's imperative at 40:10, תִּלְבָּשׁ.
   - And what Job is told to put on is not his own regalia but God's: **הוֹד + הָדָר + לבשׁ co-occur in exactly two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible — Ps 104:1 and Job 40:10.** Ps 104:1 reads הוֹד וְהָדָר לָבָשְׁתָּ, "You have **clothed Yourself** with **majesty and splendour**"; Job 40:10 reads וְהוֹד וְהָדָר תִּלְבָּשׁ, "**clothe yourself** with **majesty and splendour**."

   Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Move 4, Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Linking Words, Tone and Feel. *High for every distribution; the design — that 40:8–10 is aimed at 29:14 — is* `[I]`, *high, because three words of one verse are taken up in three consecutive verses.*

2. **The three lawsuit words absent from the first speech arrive together in 40:8 — and in the whole Hebrew Bible only Deut 25:1 does the same.**

> **v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling B5: reframed, and replaced by two stronger statements.**
**The data are confirmed and widened**: not one member of the justice field occurs in 38:1–40:5, and the field is wider than this report said — **the צדק root has four entries, not three (the report's "17 verses" names the verb alone), and צַדִּיק adds seven verses.** **But the reading fails.** The absence is not a property of the block: **the longest justice-free run in Job is 87 verses, at 19:8–22:2 — longer than the 79-verse run containing this speech** — and 77 verses of the prologue and opening cycle are nearly as long again. **And רָשָׁע, "the wicked", stands twice inside the supposedly empty block, at 38:13 and 38:15.** **Two better statements replace it.** First: **Job 40:8 is the only verse carrying מִשְׁפָּט or any צדק word in the last 147 verses of the book** — one word of justice between Elihu's last words and the final verse. Second: **מִשְׁפָּט with the hiphil of רָשַׁע and with צָדַק stands in exactly two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible — Deut 25:1 and Job 40:8** — so **God's challenge is phrased in the exact three-verb vocabulary of the Deuteronomic courtroom rule ("justify the righteous and condemn the wicked") and inverts it.** *That is the form this Headline should take.*

2. **(original wording)** The three lawsuit words the first speech withheld arrive together in 40:8 — the only verse in Job where they co-occur.** `[T]` The companion dig established that מִשְׁפָּט (23× in Job) and צדק (17×) occur **nowhere** in 38:1–40:5, with positive controls, and that both arrive at 40:8. `[S: prior dig]` This run completes the finding from the other side: **מִשְׁפָּט, the verb רָשַׁע and the verb צָדַק co-occur in exactly one verse of the entire book — 40:8** (WLC co-occurrence by lemma). *So the second speech exists in order to say what the first speech deliberately did not.* The epistemological charge of 38:2 ("words without knowledge") becomes the juridical charge of 40:8 ("will you condemn Me that you may be justified?"), and that shift is the reason the book has two divine speeches rather than one.
   Surfaced by: the Positional Necessity Check, Repetition, Author's Purpose, Structure, Move 4. *High.*

3. **The pride vocabulary is the second speech's spine, it is found almost nowhere else in Job, and Leviathan is crowned king of it.** `[T]` Five words, all verified by lemma against the WLC:
   - גָּאוֹן ("majesty, pride") — 4× in Job: 35:12; 37:4; **38:11** (the sea's proud waves); **40:10**;
   - גֵּאֶה ("proud") — **2× in Job, both here: 40:11, 40:12**;
   - גַּאֲוָה ("pride") — **1× in Job: 41:15 [Heb 41:7]**, of Leviathan's armour;
   - גָּבֹהַּ ("high") — **1× in Job: 41:34 [Heb 41:26]**;
   - שַׁחַץ ("pride") — **2× in the whole Hebrew Bible: Job 28:8 and Job 41:34 [Heb 41:26]**, and the bound phrase בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ stands at those two verses and nowhere else (ordered phrase search, plain and skeletal).

   **God tells Job to put on גָּאוֹן and bring low every גֵּאֶה (40:10–12) — and then shows him a creature whose armour is גַּאֲוָה, who surveys all that is גָּבֹהַּ, and who is מֶלֶךְ over the בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ.** The post Job was invited to fill is already held, by something he cannot get a hook into.
   Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Structure, Move 4, Tone and Feel. *High on every distribution; the design is* `[I]`, *moderate–high.*

4. **Both monsters wear a title that belongs to somebody else, and neither is an enemy.** `[T]`
   - **Behemoth is given Wisdom's own designation.** רֵאשִׁית and דֶּרֶךְ co-occur in exactly **two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible: Prov 8:22 and Job 40:19**. Proverbs' Wisdom says the LORD possessed her רֵאשִׁית דַּרְכּוֹ, "as the beginning of His way"; Job 40:19 says of a grass-eating river-beast הוּא רֵאשִׁית דַּרְכֵי־אֵל, "he is the first of the ways of God."
   - **Leviathan is what God plays with.** לִוְיָתָן and שׂחק co-occur in exactly **one verse of the whole Hebrew Bible: Ps 104:26** — לִוְיָתָן זֶה־יָצַרְתָּ לְשַׂחֶק־בּוֹ, "Leviathan, which You formed to play with him." At 41:5 [Heb 40:29] God asks Job הַתְשַׂחֶק־בּוֹ, "will you **play with him**?" — the same verb and the same preposition-plus-suffix בּוֹ.
   - And neither is defeated. Behemoth was made עִמָּךְ, "along with you" (40:15); Leviathan is הֶעָשׂוּ, "the made one" (41:33 [Heb 41:25]); only his **Maker** may bring a sword near him (40:19). *The chaos-combat tradition's imagery is present and its violence is absent* — which discharges the question the companion dig left open at 38:8–11. `[S: prior dig]`
   Surfaced by: Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Vocabulary, the canonical-history check, Biblical Theology. *High on both exclusive pairs; moderate–high on the reading.*

5. **At 42:6 the construction points away from "I repent in dust and ashes" — and toward a third reading neither English version carries.**

> **v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling A11: reframed. The direction is strengthened; the gloss is not.**
**Datum (iii) is the cleanest result in the audit** — the ordered phrase עַל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר stands in exactly four verses and **every one is governed by נָחַם**. Datum (i) is broadened: **absolute מָאַס also stands at 34:33.** **But datum (ii) is wrong as written** — of the three Jeremiah verses, **only two have עַל, one of those two has "the good" as its object (18:10), and 26:3 has אֶל** — and **datum (iv) falls as an argument, because עַל is a normal ash-idiom in Hebrew: Jonah 3:6 reads וַיֵּשֶׁב עַל־הָאֵפֶר.** **And the decisive finding is new: נָחַם followed by עַל occurs in 39 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the object of עַל is always a decreed action or outcome, or a person mourned — never a substance or a place.** So *neither* English rendering has a clean parallel. **"Relent concerning dust and ashes" is the weakest of the three available readings and is withdrawn.** What the construction supports is the **self-designation** sense — *Job is consoled concerning himself, who is dust and ashes* — which is exactly how **Gen 18:27, Job 30:19 and Swete at both verses** take the phrase. **Note also that first-person נִחַמְתִּי means "I repented" at Jer 31:19, so the verb's sense alone cannot settle the verse.** Held at *moderate* pending the Logos round. *The pulpit note on the ESV's supplied "myself" is untouched and stands.*

> **v1.2 — Logos round, 16 Nov 2026 `[revised: logos-research]`.** **Upgraded to *moderate–high*.** The "concerning" reading is attested in the literature — **Muenchow** (JBL 1989) allows that עַל may function referentially within the idiom, "with dust and ashes identifying the object of the action" — and **the audit's own decisive argument turns out to be Boda's**: he gives the four uses of נחם + עַל and observes that they "emphasize actions, persons, and emotional states — **not substances**", concluding that the phrase "can mean only that Job is changing his mind concerning his mortality or is comforted concerning it". **Pohl** adds an argument this report did not have: **the Masoretic accentuation itself marks עַל as the object for both verbs.** **One plank weakens, though: Muenchow notes the Old Greek may render a different Hebrew *Vorlage*, so Swete may be translating another text rather than construing this one** — the self-designation gloss stays available but is no longer carried by the Greek. *Sources: Muenchow, JBL 108 (1989), 597–98; Pohl, LHBOTS 698 (2020), 242–43; Boda, NSBT 35 (2015), 112–13.*

5. **(original wording)** At 42:6 the syntax points away from "I repent in dust and ashes", and the pulpit text supplies a word the Hebrew does not have.** `[T]` Three verified data, and together they reopen the book's most contested verse:
   - **אֶמְאַס has no object, and that is normal Joban usage.** מָאַס stands 12× in Job, and it is used absolutely at **7:16** (מָאַסְתִּי, "I waste away / I reject") and **36:5** (וְלֹא יִמְאָס, "and He does not reject"). So the absence of an object at 42:6 is the book's own idiom, not an ellipsis needing to be filled. **The ESV fills it — "I despise myself" — and the NASB95 does not.**
   - **וְנִחַמְתִּי followed by עַל is a known construction with a settled sense.** The exact form וְנִחַמְתִּי stands in **four verses of the Hebrew Bible — Jer 18:8; Jer 18:10; Jer 26:3; Job 42:6** (ordered phrase, plain pass). In all three Jeremiah verses it is followed immediately by עַל + object and means "**I will relent concerning** [the disaster]". With a human subject the same construction means "**be comforted concerning**" (2 Sam 13:39, נִחַם עַל־אַמְנוֹן; Ezek 32:31). **Nowhere does נחם + עַל mean "repent in a place."**
   - **The Old Greek read the clause as a predicate, not a location:** ἥγημαι δὲ ἐγὼ ἐμαυτὸν γῆν καὶ σποδόν, "I count myself earth and ashes" — which is how Gen 18:27's עָפָר וָאֵפֶר works, and the ordered phrase stands unprefixed at **Gen 18:27 and Job 42:6 only**.
   Surfaced by: Original Languages, Vocabulary, Tool 8, Textual Variants, Difficult Verses. *High on the three data; the preferred reading is held at* **moderate** *and routed to Open Questions, because the history of interpretation runs the other way and has not been checked.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** As the overview states it, and as the companion dig confirmed from the text rather than inheriting: the book brings sufferer and reader to the fear of God *through God's self-disclosure as Creator*, not through an explanation. `[S: overview]`

**Passage level, and it is not the same as the first speech's.** The second speech has a stated purpose that the first did not, and it is juridical. 40:8 names it: הַאַף תָּפֵר מִשְׁפָּטִי תַּרְשִׁיעֵנִי לְמַעַן תִּצְדָּק — "will you annul **My** justice? will you condemn Me that you may be in the right?" `[T]`

That is a charge the first speech never brought. The first asked Job what he knew (38:2, "words without knowledge"); the second asks what he was **doing** — namely, securing his own acquittal at the cost of God's. The move is from epistemology to jurisprudence, and it is why the book needs two speeches. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**And the passage pursues that purpose by one sustained method: it offers Job the job.** 40:10–14 is an investiture — adorn, clothe, pour out your anger, look on every proud man and bring him low, tread down the wicked, bury them, **and then I will concede that your own right hand can save you** (40:14). The conditional is the whole argument: *govern the world, and I will grant your case.* Then follow forty-four verses on two animals, the second of which cannot be hooked, hired, leashed or sold. **The offer is withdrawn not by refusal but by demonstration.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**A second purpose-level observation the overview does not make.** The speech ends with a creature who is מֶלֶךְ (41:34 [Heb 41:26]). **מֶלֶךְ stands 8× in Job — 3:14; 12:18; 15:24; 18:14; 29:25; 34:18; 36:7; 41:34 — and 41:34 is the only occurrence in either divine speech.** `[T]` The one before it is 29:25, where **Job** says he dwelt כְּמֶלֶךְ, "as a king", among his troops. So the book's last king is not Job and not God but Leviathan — which is a deliberately unsettling place to end a speech about who governs. `[I]` *Moderate.*

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Four things stand immediately behind 40:6, and the fourth is the one that makes the second speech necessary.

1. **The first speech has toured creation and withheld the verdict.** Seventy-six verses, no מִשְׁפָּט, no צדק, the wicked mentioned twice in a subordinate clause. `[S: prior dig]`
2. **Job has conceded standing but not the case.** 40:4–5: הֵן קַלֹּתִי, "I am slight"; יָדִי שַׂמְתִּי לְמוֹ־פִי, "I lay my hand on my mouth"; and then a promise of silence in the form of a staircase numeral that delivers cessation rather than escalation. He withdrew the suit; he did not confess. `[S: prior dig]`
3. **Job's oath of clearance (ch. 31) and his demand for a hearing (31:35) are still formally on the record.** "The words of Job are ended" (31:40) closed his case but did not retract it.
4. **And at 29:14 Job described himself in the vocabulary of a magistrate** — clothed in righteousness, justice as his robe and turban — a self-portrait the first speech never touched. `[T]`

**Necessity answer.** 40:6 exists *here* because a silence is not a settlement.

Job's 40:4–5 was a withdrawal of the lawsuit on the grounds that he is outmatched. That leaves the substance untouched: a man may fall silent before superior power and still believe himself wronged. **So the second speech does not repeat the first; it tests whether the silence was a judgement or only a calculation** — and it tests it by the one method that can tell the difference, which is to **offer Job the office he has implicitly claimed**. A man who has merely been overawed will decline the robes. A man who still thinks he could do the job better will reach for them.

That is why 40:8–14 comes before Behemoth and Leviathan rather than after them. The animals are not a fresh argument; **they are the job description**, handed over after the post has been offered. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**Why not earlier, and why not later.** Not earlier, because the charge of 40:8 would have been unanswerable before the creation tour established who was speaking — a juridical accusation from an unidentified voice is just another friend. Not later, because after 42:6 Job has nothing left to concede and the narrative turns to the friends. **The one place a job offer can function as an argument is after the applicant has admitted he is outmatched and before he has admitted he was wrong.** `[I]` *Moderate.*

**Revisited after Tool 11.** Move 4 below adds a reason the Phase 0.6 pass did not reach: the speech is positioned to answer **chapter 29** specifically, not only chapters 31 and 40. Three words of 29:14 are taken up in 40:8–10 (Headline 1), and 29:25's כְּמֶלֶךְ is answered by 41:34's מֶלֶךְ. **So the first speech reached back over the dialogue to chapter 3, and the second reaches back to chapters 28–29** — the wisdom poem and Job's portrait of his magistracy. The two speeches between them address the book's two self-descriptions: the man who cursed his birth, and the man who sat as a king. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

#### Immediate context

**Before:** Job's first reply (40:3–5), closed by a *petuchah*. **After:** 42:7, the narrator's resumption and God's verdict on the friends — the next preaching unit. **Section:** the close of Movement IV in the overview's Arc Map. `[S: overview]`

**Is this passage read out of context?** Yes, in a particular and damaging way: **41:34's "king over all the sons of pride" is quoted as a description of Satan**, and the Leviathan passage as a whole is read as a demonology. The text makes Leviathan a creature God made (41:33 [Heb 41:25]) and places him in a speech about who can govern, not about who the Adversary is. The book's own Adversary left at 2:7 and is never mentioned again. `[T]` See Preaching Pitfalls.

| **40:7** | 40:7 | The summons, repeated | Near-verbatim 38:3 |
| **40:8–9** | 40:8–9 | **The charge** | My justice; your arm; your voice |
| **40:10–14** | 40:10–14 | **The investiture** | Put on the regalia; govern; and I will concede |

**Device: a charge, an offer, and a demonstration in two unequal panels — with the weight deliberately on the second animal.** `[T]` for the panels and the proportions; `[I]` for the architecture.

**The proportions are the argument.** Behemoth gets **ten verses** (40:15–24). Leviathan gets **thirty-four** (41:1–34 [Heb 40:25–41:26]). `[T]` For comparison, the longest single subject in the whole of the first speech was the warhorse, at seven verses. **So the second speech spends more than half its length on one creature, and it is the creature Job's own curse tried to rouse (3:8).**

**The hinge is 41:9–11 [Heb 41:1–3], and it is where the speech turns from zoology to theology.** Up to 41:8 the questions are about handling Leviathan. At 41:10 the question changes person: וּמִי הוּא לְפָנַי יִתְיַצָּב — "**who then is he that can stand before Me?**" The subject is no longer the animal. From 41:12 to the end there are **no second-person questions at all** — the speech stops asking Job anything and simply describes. `[T]` *High.*

**Masoretic paragraphing agrees with the major seams.** The WLC prints a *setumah* after 41:34 [Heb 41:26] and a *petuchah* after 42:6, and nothing between 40:6 and 41:34. `[T]` *as the WLC transcribes Leningrad*; a reading tradition, not authorial, and its spread is unchecked — see the Declaration.

**Two near-verbatim repetitions mark the structure, and neither is exact.** See Tool 10; both bear on a claim the companion dig made and this run corrects.

### 4. Linking Words

| Verse | Hebrew | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 40:8 | הַאַף | Interrogative + emphatic particle: "**will you really**…?" The sharpest opening in either speech |
| 40:8 | לְמַעַן | **Purpose** — and it is the key connector of the unit: "condemn Me **in order that** you may be in the right". It attributes a motive, not merely an error |
| 40:9 | וְאִם | Conditional, opening the investiture's premise |
| 40:14 | וְגַם … כִּי | **Result + content**: "**then** I also will concede to you **that** your own right hand can save you". The protasis is 40:10–13; this is the apodosis |
| 40:15 | אֲשֶׁר | Relative: "which I made **along with** you" (עִמָּךְ) |
| 40:20 | כִּי | ←*for*← grounding Behemoth's ease: "**for** the mountains bring him food" |
| 40:23 | הֵן … כִּי | Concessive-conditional: "**if** a river rages… **though** Jordan rushes to his mouth" |
| 41:8 [40:32] | אַל | **Prohibition** — the only negative imperative in either divine speech: "**do not** do it again" |
| 41:10 [41:2] | כִּי | ←*for*← : "no one is fierce **that** he would rouse him" |
| 41:11 [41:3] | — | Asyndetic: the two clauses stand side by side with no connector, which is why the verse reads as a maxim and why Paul could lift it |
| 41:33 [41:25] | — | Asyndetic climax |
| 42:3 | לָכֵן | →*therefore*→ : "**therefore** I have declared what I did not understand" |
| 42:6 | עַל־כֵּן | →*therefore*→ : "**therefore** I…" — the inference the whole book turns on |

**Three observations the trace produces.**

**(a) The second speech contains inferential connectors where the first contained almost none.** The companion dig found no →*therefore*→ anywhere in 38:1–40:5. `[S: prior dig]` Here there are two explicit purpose/result constructions in the charge (40:8 לְמַעַן; 40:14 וְגַם … כִּי) and two in Job's reply (42:3 לָכֵן; 42:6 עַל־כֵּן). **The first speech accumulated; the second argues, and Job answers in kind.** `[T]` *High on the data; the contrast is* `[I]`, *moderate–high.*

**(b) לְמַעַן at 40:8 is the most consequential small word in the unit.** It converts a mistake into a motive: God does not say Job *was* wrong but that he condemned God **in order to** be justified himself. That is a charge about intent, and it is the only such charge God brings in the whole book. Note what it does *not* say — not that Job sinned, not that his suffering was deserved. `[T]` *High.*

**(c) Job's two →*therefore*→s are not the same.** 42:3's לָכֵן draws a conclusion about his *speech* ("therefore I have declared what I did not understand"); 42:6's עַל־כֵּן draws a conclusion about *himself*. The second is the crux (see Difficult Verses), and the connector is the reason it cannot be evaded: whatever אֶמְאַס וְנִחַמְתִּי means, it is presented as the **consequence** of 42:5's "now my eye sees You." `[T]` *High.*

**Dropped by the versions?** Both the NASB95 and the ESV carry לְמַעַן at 40:8 ("that you may be justified" / "that you may be in the right") and both carry 42:6's "therefore". 41:8's prohibition is kept in both.

### 5. Parallels

**40:8 — the charge, in a chiastic-leaning bicolon whose halves are not equivalent:**
> הַאַף תָּפֵר מִשְׁפָּטִי / תַּרְשִׁיעֵנִי לְמַעַן תִּצְדָּק
> ("Will you really annul My justice? / Will you condemn Me that you may be in the right?")

The second half does not restate the first; it **supplies the mechanism and the motive**. Annulling God's justice is done *by* condemning him, and condemning him is done *for the sake of* one's own vindication. **Three terms, and the parallelism arranges them as means and end.** Reading the halves as synonymous ("God accuses Job of impiety twice") loses the argument. `[T]` *High.*

**40:10–11 — synonymous, and the pairing is what identifies the regalia:**
> "Adorn yourself with **majesty** (גָּאוֹן) and **height** (גֹּבַהּ) / and **honour** (הוֹד) and **splendour** (הָדָר) clothe yourself."

Four nouns in two cola. The second pair is the fixed divine word-pair of Ps 104:1 (Headline 1); the first pair is the pride vocabulary the speech will later hand to Leviathan (Headline 3). **So the bicolon's two halves point in opposite directions — one to God's wardrobe, one to the monster's** — and the verse works only when both are heard. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**41:33–34 [Heb 41:25–26] — the climax, as an emblematic pair:**
> "There is nothing **on the dust** (עַל־עָפָר) like him / the one made without fear.
> He looks on all that is **high** (גָּבֹהַּ) / he is **king** over all the sons of pride."

The first colon locates him (on the earth, unequalled); the second elevates him (surveying the high, ruling the proud). **The movement within two verses is from ground to throne**, and it is the last thing God says. `[T]`

**The deliberate break in parallelism — and it is at the most important verse in the unit.** 42:6 is **tricolon-shaped but bicolon-length**, and its second half has no partner:
> עַל־כֵּן אֶמְאַס וְנִחַמְתִּי / עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר
> ("Therefore I … and I am comforted / upon dust and ashes.")

**אֶמְאַס has no object and no parallel term.** In a book of relentless bicola, Job's last clause is defective — and the defect is the exegetical problem. `[T]` *High.* Whether that is deliberate aposiopesis (he cannot finish), textual, or simply the book's absolute use of מאס (7:16; 36:5) is the crux; the parallelism cannot settle it, and saying so is the honest result.

**One further pair worth flagging as *not* a parallel.** 40:15's חָצִיר כַּבָּקָר יֹאכֵל ("he eats grass like an ox") is not elevated diction and not parallel to anything. It is bathos, and deliberate: **the first of God's ways eats grass.** `[I]` *Moderate.*

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Fifty-nine verses with **two** narrative verses: 40:6 and 42:1. As in the first half, the narrator says only who spoke, and three features repay attention.

**(a) 40:6 is not identical to 38:1, and the difference is the article.** `[T]`
> 38:1 — מִן **הַ**סְּעָרָה ("out of **the** whirlwind")
> 40:6 — מִן סְעָרָה ("out of **a** whirlwind / out of storm")

BHS's apparatus says *"6.7 = 38,1.3"*, and that shorthand is approximately right rather than exactly right. **The first heading is definite and the second is not.** Whether anything turns on it is genuinely uncertain — the *petuchah* after 40:5 may make the second storm a new one, or the indefinite form may be stylistic variation. Reported because it is a fact about the text that a reader comparing the two verses in Hebrew will see, and because it qualifies a claim the companion dig made (Tool 10). *Confidence: high on the datum; uncertain on its significance.*

**(b) The covenant name, again only in the narrator's mouth.** 40:6 and 42:1 both use יְהוָה. Inside the speech God names himself nowhere in this unit; inside his reply Job names him nowhere either, addressing him only in the second person. `[T]` The pattern the overview identified holds without exception through both speeches. `[S: overview]`

**(c) The narrator still evaluates nothing — and that is now a sustained seventy-one-verse silence across the two replies.** No "and Job wept", no "and he was afraid", no verdict on 42:6. The first evaluation in the book since 2:10 arrives at **42:7**, in God's mouth, in the next unit. `[T]` *High.* **This is the single most important structural fact for preaching 42:6**: the verse the whole book turns on is left uninterpreted by the narrator, and the interpretation that does come says Job **spoke rightly**.

### 7. Vocabulary

#### Proper-noun inventory (Question 0)

**Personal names:** אִיּוֹב ("Job", 40:6; 42:1). **Divine names:** יְהוָה (40:6; 42:1), אֵל (40:9, 19). **Creature names:** **בְהֵמוֹת** (40:15), **לִוְיָתָן** (41:1 [Heb 40:25]). **Place names: one** — **יַרְדֵּן** ("Jordan", 40:23). **Gentilics: one** — כְּנַעֲנִים ("Canaanites/merchants", 41:6 [Heb 40:30]). **Numbers:** none.

*Inventory result and triage.* The companion dig found that the first speech names **no place at all**. `[S: prior dig]` **This one names the Jordan** — the only place-name in either divine speech, and the only geographical reference in the book outside the prologue's raiders and caravans. `[T]` That earns the full check (below). Behemoth and Leviathan earn the full check. כְּנַעֲנִים is here a trade-term ("merchants"), as both English versions render it, and carries no canonical freight in context; one line, no claim.

#### The Jordan (40:23) — name-hyperlink check

**First canonical occurrence:** Gen 13:10–11, the plain of the Jordan that Lot chose. **Major event association:** the crossing of Josh 3–4, where the river is heaped up (נֵד) so Israel enters the land — the event that makes the Jordan *the* boundary-water of the canon.

**Load-the-story rule applied** — Joshua 3 was re-read rather than recalled. There the Jordan is at flood stage (Josh 3:15), and the waters stand in a heap so that the people pass over on dry ground; the twelve stones memorialise it; 4:23 makes the Red Sea comparison explicit.

**What the hyperlink adds, and the honest limit.** Job 40:23 says Behemoth is unmoved **though the Jordan rushes into his mouth** — i.e. the river whose stopping was Israel's founding miracle is, to this animal, a drink. *That* is a striking juxtaposition, and it is available to any canonical reader. **But the warrant is thin and must be marked so:** there is no shared vocabulary with Josh 3–4 (no נֵד, no עָבַר, no יָבָּשָׁה), the name may simply be the largest river a Levantine audience knew, and Job's setting is deliberately outside Israel, which cuts against a conquest allusion. **Weight: *uncertain*, reported as a resonance for the canonical reader and not as a designed allusion.** `[I]` Routed to Open Questions and queued rather than claimed.

#### Behemoth and Leviathan — and a lemma distinction that matters

**בְהֵמוֹת (40:15).** The WLC index files this under a **separate entry from בְּהֵמָה ("beast")**, and under that entry it is a **hapax legomenon — Job 40:15 is its only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible.** `[T]` *High.*

This matters for a claim in the overview's Echo Table, which pairs 12:7 ("ask the **beasts** (בְהֵמוֹת) and they will teach you") with 40:15 and says "the same word, used as a name". `[S: overview]` **The overview is right about the written form and should be read as a claim about the form, not the lemma:** 12:7's בְהֵמוֹת is the ordinary plural of בְּהֵמָה (3× in Job: 12:7; 18:3; 35:11), while 40:15's is indexed as a distinct entry. **So 12:7 → 40:15 is a form-link, not a lemma-chain** — and the form is identical, consonants and pointing alike, which is precisely what makes the wordplay work. Job told the friends to ask the בְהֵמוֹת; God shows him *the* בְהֵמוֹת. *Confidence: high on the identity of the form; moderate on the design, which is the overview's rating.*

**לִוְיָתָן.** 6 occurrences in 5 verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isa 27:1; Ps 74:14; Ps 104:26; Job 3:8; Job 41:1 [Heb 40:25]. **Only two in Job, and they are 3:8 and this passage.** `[T]` *High.* Job's curse wanted him roused (3:8, הָעֲתִידִים עֹרֵר לִוְיָתָן); God says no one dares rouse him (41:10 [Heb 41:2], כִּי יְעוּרֶנּוּ) — **the same verb עור in both.** The overview carries this at *high*; independently reproduced here.

**Referent.** Whether Behemoth and Leviathan are the hippopotamus and the crocodile, mythic chaos-beasts, or deliberately both, is contested and this report does not settle it. `[S]` What the **text** says is not contested and is what the exegesis rests on: God made them (40:15; 41:33 [Heb 41:25]), they are beyond human handling, and they are not his enemies.

#### The words that carry the passage

**מִשְׁפָּטִי — 40:8** `[T]`
Headline 1. The suffixed form stands in Job at **27:2, 29:14, 34:5, 34:6, 40:8**; Job's in the first four, God's at the fifth. (A consonantal search also returns 23:7, which is מִשֹּׁפְטִי, "from my judge" — a different word with identical consonants, and a reminder that the plain pass cannot tell them apart.) *High.*

**גָּאוֹן · גֵּאֶה · גַּאֲוָה · גָּבֹהַּ · שַׁחַץ — the pride field** `[T]`

**הוֹד and הָדָר — 40:10** `[T]`
הוֹד stands 3× in Job (37:22; 39:20; 40:10); **הָדָר stands once — 40:10.** The pair co-occurs canon-wide in 8 verses: Ps 21:5; 45:3; 96:6; 104:1; 111:3; 145:5; 1 Chr 16:27; Job 40:10. **Seven of the eight predicate it of God or of the king; Job 40:10 is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where a human being is told to put it on.** And with לבשׁ added the pair is exclusive to Ps 104:1 and Job 40:10. *High.*

**עדה — 40:10, and a homograph not to be over-read** `[T]`
40:10's עֲדֵה ("adorn yourself") and 28:8's עָדָה ("passed over") share three consonants and are filed by the WLC index as **two entries of one homograph**. 28:8 is already demonstrably the source of 41:34's בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ, so the two verses are genuinely in contact. **But applying the hard rule on sound-based claims: a consonantal coincidence is never sufficient, and here the senses are unrelated and the index separates them.** Reported as an observation at ***uncertain***, not as a finding, and listed under Recommended verification. The honest value of the note is as a worked instance of the homograph trap the corpus README warns about.

**יצב — 41:10 [Heb 41:2]** `[T]`
The hithpael of יצב stands in Job at **1:6; 2:1; 33:5; 38:14; 41:10** — six occurrences in five verses. **1:6 and 2:1 are the heavenly council**: בְּנֵי הָאֱלֹהִים came לְהִתְיַצֵּב עַל־יְהוָה, "to **present themselves** before the LORD", with the Accuser among them. 41:10 asks וּמִי הוּא לְפָנַי יִתְיַצָּב, "who is he that can **stand** before Me?" **The verb of the Accuser's access is the verb of God's challenge.** *High on the distribution.* **One honest qualification:** the prologue uses עַל and 41:10 uses לְפָנַי, so the prepositional idiom differs; the link is carried by the verb and the hithpael stem, not by the whole phrase. *Moderate–high on the reading.*

**עֶבֶד and בְּרִית — 41:4 [Heb 40:28]** `[T]`
עֶבֶד stands 12× in 10 verses of Job: 1:8; 2:3; 3:19; 4:18; 7:2; 19:16; 31:13; **40:28**; 42:7; 42:8. **41:4 is the only occurrence in either divine speech**, and the four that frame the book are "**My servant** Job" (1:8; 2:3; 42:7; 42:8). **בְּרִית stands 3× in Job — 5:23; 31:1; 41:4 — and none of the three is a covenant between God and a people.** `[S: overview]`, reproduced. **So: Leviathan will neither covenant with Job nor be his servant; Job is God's servant, and is about to be called so four times.** *High on the distributions; moderate–high on the contrast.*

**שׂחק — 40:20; 41:5, 29 [Heb 40:20, 29; 41:21]** `[T]`

**יָסַף — 41:8 [Heb 40:32]** `[T]`

**One verb sets the sea's limit, accepts Job's silence, forbids a second attempt on Leviathan, and then doubles Job's estate.** *High on the chain; moderate–high on the reading.* This upgrades the companion dig's two-member note. `[S: prior dig]`

**ישע — 40:14** `[T]`
4× in Job: 5:15; 22:29; 26:2; **40:14**. The two Eliphaz occurrences are about God saving; 26:2 is Job's sarcasm to Bildad; **40:14 is the only salvation-word in either divine speech, and it is ironic**: כִּי־תוֹשִׁעַ לְךָ יְמִינֶךָ, "that your own right hand can **save** you." *High on the distribution; the irony is* `[I]`, *high, given the conditional at 40:10–13.*

**מְזִמָּה — 42:2** `[T]`
**2× in Job: 21:27 and 42:2.** At 21:27 Job says to the friends הֵן יָדַעְתִּי מַחְשְׁבוֹתֵיכֶם **וּמְזִמּוֹת** עָלַי תַּחְמֹסוּ — "I know your thoughts, and **the schemes** by which you would wrong me." At 42:2 he says וְלֹא־יִבָּצֵר מִמְּךָ **מְזִמָּה** — "no **purpose** of Yours can be thwarted." **The word Job used for his friends' malice is the word he now uses for God's unstoppable design, and the book has only these two.** *High.* Note both verses also open with הֵן / ידע.

**שֵׁמַע — 42:5** `[T]`
**2× in Job: 28:22 and 42:5.** 28:22 — אֲבַדּוֹן וָמָוֶת אָמְרוּ בְּאָזְנֵינוּ שָׁמַעְנוּ **שִׁמְעָהּ**, "Abaddon and Death say, 'with our ears we have heard a **report** of it'" — of wisdom. 42:5 — **לְשֵׁמַע**־אֹזֶן שְׁמַעְתִּיךָ, "by the **hearing** of the ear I had heard of You." **The noun for the rumour of wisdom that only Abaddon and Death had caught is the noun for Job's second-hand knowledge of God — and it is now replaced by sight.** *High.* Claim audit 4 rated the words *high* and the hearing-then-seeing line *moderate*, since 13:1 and 29:11 also pair ear and eye. `[S: audit 4]` This run confirms by co-occurrence: אֹזֶן + רָאָה stand together in Job at **13:1, 29:11, 42:5**.

**מָאַס and נחם — 42:6** `[T]`

**עָפָר and אֵפֶר — 41:33; 42:6** `[T]`

The NASB95 is the study text, the ESV the declared pulpit text. **This unit produces the sharpest divergence in the whole Job series, and it falls on the book's most important verse.** Five divergences bear on findings; three go the study text's way, one the pulpit text's, and one is a textual decision rather than a rendering.

**Pulpit divergence note.** The pulpit text is the **ESV**. Two of this report's five Headline Findings are materially affected.

At **42:6** the ESV's "I despise myself" closes an interpretive question the Hebrew leaves open, and closes it in the direction the Hebrew supports least (Headline 5). **The preacher should say out loud what the Hebrew does and does not have.** The honest form of words is short: *the verb has no object; "myself" is the translators' supply, and the book uses this verb without an object twice elsewhere.*

At **41:15** the ESV has followed an emendation away from the MT's "pride", so the word that crowns the speech's spine is missing from the congregation's Bible at that point. Supply it.

Elsewhere the two agree closely enough that nothing in this report turns on the difference — including at 40:8, where both carry "annul my justice" and "that you may be in the right", so Headline 2 is fully audible in either version, and at 41:34, where both have "king over all the sons of pride".

#### Ancient versions check

**Trigger met.** The NASB95 and ESV substantively agree at 40:8, at 41:11 [Heb 41:3], at 41:34 [Heb 41:26] and at 42:2–5, and each carries exegetical weight. Classified first under the Three-Way Triage.

**Category 2 — substantive variant. The Greek of this unit is a materially different text, and it is the most divergent stretch in either speech.**

*Observation from Swete (Vaticanus-based); a reading of record would come from Rahlfs 1979 in `logos-exports/`. The whole-book measure of the Old Greek's shortness was rebuilt in claim audit 5* `[S: audit 5]`.

1. **The versification diverges across the whole unit** (see the table at the head of this report). Swete prints Hebrew 40:6–41:26 as 40:1–41:25.
2. **Swete has a phrase twice that the Hebrew has nowhere.** At **Swete 40:14** (Heb 40:19, Behemoth) and again at **Swete 41:24** (Heb 41:25, Leviathan) the Greek reads πεποιημένον ἐνκαταπαίζεσθαι ὑπὸ τῶν ἀγγέλων αὐτοῦ / μου — "**made to be mocked by his / my angels**." `[T]` The Hebrew at 40:19 has "his Maker brings near his sword" and at 41:25 has הֶעָשׂוּ לִבְלִי־חָת, "the one made without fear." **So the Greek tradition has turned both monsters into God's playthings explicitly, by a formula repeated at both panels, and the Hebrew does neither.** *Position: the Hebrew is the text; the Greek formula is interpretative and probably harmonising.* **Confidence: moderate.** Note the irony worth recording: the Greek says in so many words what Headline 4 argues the Hebrew implies through Ps 104:26 — so the Old Greek is an early witness to the *reading*, even though it is not the text.
3. **Swete loses the climax.** Hebrew 41:26 — מֶלֶךְ עַל־כָּל־בְּנֵי־שָׁחַץ, "king over all the sons of pride" — appears at Swete 41:25 as **βασιλεὺς πάντων τῶν ἐν τοῖς ὕδασιν**, "king of all that are **in the waters**." `[T]` **The Greek domesticates Leviathan into a sea-creature and the pride-climax disappears.** *Position: the Hebrew. Confidence: moderate–high* — שַׁחַץ is a rare word (two verses in the canon) and a translator unsure of it reaching for "waters" is a more economical explanation than a different *Vorlage*. **Consequence to state plainly: Headline 3's climax is a feature of the Hebrew, and the Greek cannot carry it.**
4. **Swete does not have the verse Paul quotes.** Hebrew 41:3's מִי הִקְדִּימַנִי וַאֲשַׁלֵּם has no counterpart in Swete 41:2, which reads τίς γάρ ἐστιν ὁ ἐμοὶ ἀντιστάς; ἢ τίς ἀντιστήσεταί μοι καὶ ὑπομενεῖ — "who has withstood me? or who will withstand me and endure?" `[T]` This is a **category-2 divergence with a New Testament consequence**, and it is treated under Tool 11.
5. **Swete 42:6 reads the last clause as a predicate, not a location:** διὸ ἐφαύλισα ἐμαυτὸν καὶ ἐτάκην· **ἥγημαι δὲ ἐγὼ ἐμαυτὸν γῆν καὶ σποδόν** — "therefore I despised myself and wasted away; and **I count myself earth and ashes**." `[T]` Three departures from the MT at once: an object supplied for מאס, an extra verb (ἐτάκην), and עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר construed as a predicate complement. *Position: the Hebrew is the text; but the Greek is first-rate evidence that **an ancient reader did not take עַל locally**, and it construes the clause exactly as Gen 18:27 does.* **Confidence: moderate–high on the observation; it bears directly on Headline 5.**
6. **Swete 42:2 follows the qere.** Οἶδα ὅτι πάντα δύνασαι, "**I** know" — against the ketiv's "**you** know". BHS: *"Cp 42, 2 ᵃ l c Q Vrs תִּי—, K תָּ—"*, i.e. read with the Qere **and the versions**. `[T]` See Textual Variants.

**Category 1 — translation loss, not variant.** Most of this report falls here and it is worth saying so rather than filing it as textual. **No translation into any language can carry**: the מִשְׁפָּטִי possessive-flip from 29:14 to 40:8 (English must choose "my justice" twice and cannot show the suffix changing owner); the five-word pride field, since English has no single family covering גָּאוֹן, גֵּאֶה, גַּאֲוָה, גָּבֹהַּ and שַׁחַץ; the יָסַף chain across 38:11, 40:5, 41:8 and 42:10; the identity of בְהֵמוֹת at 12:7 and 40:15; and the שֵׁמַע link from 28:22 to 42:5. **The Greek has not failed, and neither have the ESV and NASB95 — translation breaks root-chains.** Reported under Tools 7 and 10, and **not** as a `**Translation-tradition split:**`.

**Category 3 — the Greek as the NT's own text.** > **v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling A4: the claim that follows is WITHDRAWN.** **Swete Job 21:31 ends τίς ἀνταποδώσει αὐτῷ — and τίς together with any form of ἀνταποδίδωμι occurs in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Old Testament, that one.** It matches Rom 11:35's interrogative, verb and dative better than Job 41:3 matches its person and voice (the Hebrew has God in the first person active; Paul has an impersonal future passive). **So there is a Greek Joban antecedent and the inverted-triage claim cannot stand.** What replaces it is stronger: **ἀνεξιχνίαστος at Rom 11:33 stands in Swete at Job 5:9, 9:10 and 34:24 and nowhere else in the Greek Old Testament, and Rom 11:34 is Isa 40:13 almost verbatim — so Rom 11:33–35 is a Joban frame around an Isaianic quotation, drawing on Job in both languages.** **The Logos round partly reverses this correction** `[revised: logos-research]`: **Middendorf** (Concordia Commentary, *Romans 9–16*, 1141) gives Rom 11:35 as **"Paul's adaptation of the Hebrew of Job 41:3 (ET 41:11)"**, and **no source in the library proposes Job 21:31 or Job 9:4**. *So the scholarly view is the one this report originally stated, and it is restored at moderate–high.* **What stays withdrawn is the triage claim**, because the Greek antecedent exists whether or not Paul used it, and no category-3 statement should rest on an absence shown not to be absolute. **The Joban-frame finding is confirmed and has a monograph behind it — Naselli, *From Typology to Doxology: Paul's Use of Isaiah and Job in Romans 11:34–35*** — with **Janzen** (*Reading Job Intertextually*, 248) confirming that ἀνεξιχνίαστος occurs in the LXX only in Job. The original claim read: **No instance in this unit, and the reverse obtains:** at Rom 11:35 the New Testament follows the **Hebrew** against the Old Greek. That is a Tool 11 finding and is set out there. Flagged here so the triage is complete rather than silently empty.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The second speech does not sound like the first, and the shift is structural rather than incidental.**

The companion dig found the first speech to be awe laced with irony and shot through with tenderness — a swaddled sea, fed lion cubs, a watched birth. `[S: prior dig]` **None of that register survives into the second speech.** There is no tenderness in these fifty-nine verses. What replaces it is three distinct tones in sequence.

**(a) Forensic, then sarcastic (40:6–14).** הַאַף — "will you *really*…?" — is the sharpest particle in either speech. Then five imperatives in four verses, every one of them impossible: adorn, clothe, pour out, look, tread down, hide. **The sarcasm is sustained and it is cold.** 40:14's "then I also will concede that your own right hand can save you" is the coldest line God speaks in the book. `[T]` *High.*

**(b) Bathetic, then grand (40:15–24).** The first of God's ways **eats grass** (40:15). His bones are bronze tubes; he lies under lotus plants in the marsh; the Jordan runs into his mouth and he does not notice. **The register slides between the mock-heroic and the genuinely monumental**, and the comedy is at the expense of anyone who thought the catalogue was going to be about dignity. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**(c) Sublime terror (41:1–34).** This is the tonal centre of the unit and the only place in Job that reads like apocalyptic. Fire from the mouth; smoke from the nostrils; a heart hard as a lower millstone; the deep boiling like a pot; a wake that leaves the abyss white-haired; weapons counted as straw; the mighty beside themselves. **Thirty-four verses with no comfort in them and no question to Job after v.11.** `[T]`

**(d) Then five quiet verses (42:1–6)**, four of which are Job repeating other people's words.

**Devices.**
*The impossible imperative* (40:10–13) — six in four verses, functioning as the opposite of commands.
*Accumulated negation* in the Leviathan panel — "cannot avail", "cannot make him flee", "counted as stubble", "nothing like him", "without fear". The weapons list of 41:26, 28–29 exists to be dismissed.
*Interrogative withdrawal.* מִי appears at **41:10, 11, 13, 14 [Heb 41:2, 3, 5, 6]** and then stops. `[T]` After 41:14 the speech asks nothing. **The first speech ended still asking; this one ends declaring** — and the last declaration is that something else is king.
*Hyperbole as theology.* "He makes the deep boil like a pot" is not zoology and is not meant to be.

**Tonal shift at 42:1, and it is the book's largest.** The orchestra stops. Job's five verses contain **no image, no simile and no creature** — the only stretch of poetry in forty chapters with none. `[I]` *Moderate–high.* After the loudest passage in the book, the quietest.

**Soundtrack.** For 40:8–14, a single cold brass line. For Behemoth, something lumbering and faintly comic — bassoons. For Leviathan, full percussion and low brass; this is the organ on full stops. For 42:1–6, one unaccompanied voice, very quiet, almost spoken.

**Note for the preacher.** The emotional architecture is **not** a crescendo into comfort. It runs *accusation → mockery → terror → silence*, and the comfort — if 42:6 contains any — is a single word at the end of the last line. A sermon that warms the Leviathan passage up will be preaching against its text; a sermon that preaches 42:6 loudly will be preaching against its dynamics. `[I]`

#### Idea-level: the inventory of what cannot be done to Leviathan

Eleven failed actions in eight verses (41:1–8 [Heb 40:25–32]), each a verb of human mastery: draw out, press down, put a rope, pierce, make a covenant, take as a servant, play with, leash, bargain, divide, fill with harpoons. Then eight failed weapons (41:26–29). **Nineteen negations, and the only positive imperative in the panel is "lay your hand on him; remember the battle; do not do it again."** `[T]` *High.* The accumulation *is* the argument: the catalogue of human capability is exhausted by one animal.

#### Near-repetition — and two corrections to the companion dig

**(a) 40:7 is *not* verbatim 38:3.** `[T]`
> 38:3 — אֱזָר־נָא כְגֶבֶר חֲלָצֶיךָ **וְ**אֶשְׁאָלְךָ וְהוֹדִיעֵנִי
> 40:7 — אֱזָר־נָא כְגֶבֶר חֲלָצֶיךָ **אֶשְׁאָלְךָ** וְהוֹדִיעֵנִי

**The difference is one waw.** The companion dig wrote "**38:3 is repeated verbatim at 40:7**, and BHS says so in its own apparatus." `[S: prior dig]` **That is wrong in the word "verbatim", and this run corrects it.** BHS's *"6.7 = 38,1.3"* is an editor's shorthand for near-identity, not a claim of exact repetition. And the correction has a positive result: **40:7 and 42:4 *are* exactly identical in the clause** (both אֶשְׁאָלְךָ וְהוֹדִיעֵנִי, without the waw), so **Job's quotation at 42:4 reproduces the *second* summons, not the first.** `[T]` *High.* That is a sharper finding than the one it replaces.

**(b) 40:6 is not verbatim 38:1 either** — 38:1 has the article (מִן **הַ**סְּעָרָה), 40:6 does not (מִן סְעָרָה). `[T]` See Tool 6. Significance uncertain; the datum is not.

**Both corrections come from running the ordered-phrase check rather than trusting the apparatus's equals-sign**, which is the discipline gate item (e3) exists to enforce: *identical*, *verbatim* and *the same phrase* are claims about shape that a lemma check cannot verify.

**Ps 104:1 → Job 40:10** *(high confidence — exclusive canon-wide verbal pair)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Psalm 104 is the canon's great creation-providence psalm. It opens by addressing God directly: "Bless the LORD, O my soul! O LORD my God, You are very great; **You have clothed Yourself with majesty and splendour**" (104:1). What follows is a tour — light as a garment, the heavens stretched out, waters bounded so they do not return to cover the earth (104:9), springs, grass, the beasts that seek their food from God, the young lions, the sea with its creatures, **and Leviathan, formed to play in it (104:26)**. The psalm's own movement is from God's regalia to God's management of a world full of animals that do not work for humans. **The functional resemblance to Job 38–41 is not vague; it is the same itinerary.**

*Move 2 — Book usage.* The Psalter is a confirmed live source in Job (Ps 8:4 at 7:17 and 15:14; Ps 107:40 split across 12:21, 24; Ps 88 at 19:13–14). `[S: overview]` **Ps 104 specifically was queued but untested** after the first half of the unit, where the resemblance to 38:26–27, 39–41 was real at the level of genre but produced no exclusive lexical pair. `[S: prior dig]` **This run settles it in the affirmative, from the other half of the speech:** הוֹד + הָדָר + לבשׁ stand together in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Ps 104:1 and Job 40:10; and לִוְיָתָן + שׂחק in exactly one, Ps 104:26, whose verb and preposition-plus-suffix Job 41:5 reproduces. **Two independent contacts with one psalm, at both ends of this unit.** *So Ps 104 is a live source for the second divine speech;* the genre resemblance the first half could not verify turns out to be anchored in the second.

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Ps 104 is itself in conversation with Genesis 1 (its order follows the creation days) and with the sea-boundary tradition (104:9 ≈ Prov 8:29; Jer 5:22; Job 38:10–11). **So when Job 38–41 engages Ps 104 it is engaging a text that has already digested Genesis 1** — which is why the first speech's Gen 1 relation (established in the companion dig) and this speech's Ps 104 relation are not two separate findings but two points on one line. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

*What it adds.* Decisively: **Job is invited to put on God's own clothing, in God's own words, and is then shown God's own pet.** The sarcasm of 40:10 is not generic ("act like a god"); it is specific and quotational. And the Leviathan panel's rhetorical question at 41:5 has a canonical answer the reader can supply and Job cannot: *yes, someone does play with him* — God, according to Ps 104:26. **The speech's cruellest question is the one whose answer is in the Psalter.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

*Direction of dependence:* **open.** Both books stand in the Ketuvim, Psalms immediately before Job in the BHS order, and nothing in either text marks priority. Reported both ways and resolved neither. `[T]`

**Prov 8:22 → Job 40:19** *(high confidence on the verbal datum; moderate on the use)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* Proverbs 8 is Wisdom's self-presentation. She calls from the gates, claims to have been with the LORD before the world, and at 8:22 says יְהוָה קָנָנִי **רֵאשִׁית דַּרְכּוֹ**, "the LORD possessed me **as the beginning of His way**", before the deeps, before the mountains, when he inscribed the circle on the face of the waters and set the sea's limit (8:27–29). **The claim is a claim to priority in the order of creation**, and it is the ground of her authority: because she was first, she may be trusted.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Proverbs 8 is live in Job and has been used twice before, both times **against** Job. The overview records 15:7 (Eliphaz's taunt, "were you brought forth before the hills?" — Prov 8:25's verb) and 40:19. `[S: overview]` **So the pattern of Job's engagement with Prov 8 is polemical**: Eliphaz uses Wisdom's birth-claim to mock Job's pretensions, and God then transfers it to an animal. *This is a genuine Move 2 result with a direction: the book twice denies Wisdom's priority to a human claimant, and the second time awards it to a hippopotamus.* `[I]` *Moderate.*

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Prov 8:27–29 and Ps 104:9 are already in conversation about the sea's decreed limit, and Job 38:8–11 stands on that junction. `[S: prior dig]` So Prov 8 and Ps 104 — this unit's two external sources — were in dialogue before Job reached either.

*What it adds.* רֵאשִׁית and דֶּרֶךְ co-occur in **two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible**. Wisdom's unique title is reused once in the canon, and it is reused of Behemoth. Three readings are available and the text does not choose between them: (i) *Behemoth is the pre-eminent among created things*, a straight superlative; (ii) *the title is deflationary* — the grandest thing in creation eats grass, so Wisdom's priority-claim is being gently punctured; (iii) *the title is honorific and serious* — this creature stands at the head of God's works and is therefore not a monster to be defeated but a masterpiece. **(iii) fits the panel's own second colon best**, since 40:19b says only his **Maker** may bring a sword near him. *Confidence: high on the pair; moderate on the reading, and the options are named rather than resolved.*

**Job 41:11 [Heb 41:3] → Romans 11:35** *(high confidence; and the triage's mirror-image)* `[T]`

*The data.* Hebrew 41:3 reads **מִי הִקְדִּימַנִי וַאֲשַׁלֵּם** — "who has **come before me** / given to me first, that I should **repay**?" Paul writes ἢ **τίς προέδωκεν** αὐτῷ, καὶ **ἀνταποδοθήσεται** αὐτῷ (Rom 11:35). **προ-δίδωμι renders the hiphil of קדם and ἀντ-αποδίδωμι renders the piel of שׁלם, morpheme for morpheme.** And **Swete has nothing corresponding**: its 41:2 reads "who has withstood me? or who will withstand me and endure?" `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.* In Job the verse is the second half of the hinge (41:10–11 [Heb 41:2–3]). Its function there is to move from Leviathan to God: *nobody dares rouse him; so who can stand before me? who has ever put me in his debt? everything under heaven is mine.* **The maxim is a statement of unindebtedness**, and it is placed as the theological pivot of the whole speech.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* This is the only place in Job where God makes a general claim about creditor and debtor. The theme has been live in the dialogue in the opposite direction — Job has argued that God owes him a hearing (13:22; 31:35) and the friends that Job owes God a confession. **God's one word on obligation is that nobody has a claim.**

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT and the NT setting.* Rom 11:33–36 is a catena: 11:34 cites **Isa 40:13** ("who has known the mind of the Lord?") and 11:35 cites **Job 41:11**. Isa 40 and Job 38–41 are already in canonical conversation — Isa 40:12–14's weighing and measuring of creation is the nearest prophetic analogue to the first divine speech, and claim audit 4 rated Isa 40:12–13 at Job 28:25 *moderate*. `[S: audit 4]` **So Paul has paired the two great "who can instruct God?" texts of the Hebrew canon**, and the pairing is not his invention.

*What it adds, and the triage point.* The Three-Way Triage's category 3 covers cases where the NT **argues from the Greek**. **This is the mirror image: the New Testament's wording follows the Masoretic Hebrew against the Old Greek we possess.** That is worth stating precisely rather than filing as an oddity:
- It is **not** evidence that the Greek is corrupt. The Old Greek of Job is a free and abbreviating translation throughout this unit (Tool 8).
- It **is** evidence that a form of Job 41:3 very close to the MT was available and authoritative in the first century, which the triage's "no default in favour of the MT" rule should not obscure in either direction.
- And it is a **Christological datum**, treated below: Paul's doxology to the unsearchable God ends on words God spoke out of the whirlwind.
*Confidence: high* on the verbal correspondence, which is checkable in both texts; *moderate–high* that Paul is citing Job rather than a shared proverb, on the strength of the Isa 40 pairing in the adjacent verse.

**The sea-monster tradition → the Leviathan panel** *(moderate; and the finding is what is missing)* `[T]`

Isa 27:1; 51:9; Ps 74:13–14; 89:10–11 present Leviathan, Rahab and the dragon as powers God pierced, crushed and broke. Job has invoked them three times in the dialogue, twice as evidence of hostile divine power (3:8; 7:12; 9:13; 26:12–13). `[S: overview]`

He describes him, declares him unequalled, and calls him king. `[T]` The only violence in the panel is **hypothetical and human** — the hooks, harpoons, swords, spears, arrows and slingstones that **fail** (41:1–8, 26–29).

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew poetry: a divine disputation speech containing two extended animal encomia, followed by a five-verse confession.** `[T]`

Reading rules in force: parallelism is operative; the imagery is figurative and must not be pressed — Leviathan's fire-breathing (41:19–21) is not herpetology, and "he makes the deep boil like a pot" is not oceanography; the rhetorical questions expect "no" and do not require Job to answer.

**Three genre points bear on preaching this unit.**

**(a) The encomium is a recognisable form, and recognising it protects against two errors.** 41:12–34 is a praise-catalogue of a creature: body part by body part, then effect on others, then a climactic superlative. The form is shared with the warhorse portrait (39:19–25) and, outside Job, with the descriptions of the beloved in Song of Songs. *Uncertain — consider verifying* the ANE comparanda; this is recalled background, not corpus data. **What it guards against:** reading the panel as *threat* (it is praise, and the praise is God's), and reading it as *description* in the modern sense (it is laudatory, so its hyperbole is generic convention, not a claim about reptile physiology).

**(b) The passage is not apocalyptic, and the distinction matters pastorally.** Leviathan sounds apocalyptic — fire, smoke, the deep, a king of the proud — and is routinely read through Rev 12–13 and Isa 27:1. **But the governing genre marker is in the frame: this creature is addressed inside a disputation in which God is answering a lawsuit, and the text twice says God made him** (41:33 [Heb 41:25]; cf. 40:15). Apocalyptic beasts are enemies awaiting destruction; **this one is exhibit B.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.* See Preaching Pitfalls.

**(c) 42:1–6 is a sub-genre embedded in the host genre, and it is the only one of its kind in the book: a confession built almost entirely of quotation.** Of its five content verses, **42:3a and 42:4 are quotations of God** (38:2; 40:7), and both English versions mark them with quotation marks — an editorial decision, not a feature of the Hebrew, and worth knowing. `[T]` **Job's last speech is mostly other people's words.** Whether that is submission, irony, or the only speech available to him is the crux. `[I]`

Brief by design; the genre of the frame is not contested, and the one contested question (is the Leviathan panel mythological?) is a question about referent, treated under Tool 7, not about reading rules.

### 13. Copycat

**Largely N/A for the divine speech — there are no human characters acting in 40:6–41:34, and the two creatures are exhibits, not examples.** The imperatives at 40:10–13 are addressed to Job and are not prescriptive for anyone, since they are ironic and impossible by design (Tool 9).

Two items do belong here.

**Job's response at 42:1–6 — prescriptive or descriptive?** This is the one imitable act in the unit, and it is the most commonly preached as a template: *be like Job at 42:6*. Three cautions from this run:
1. **The content of the act is contested** (Difficult Verses). An exhortation to imitate 42:6 must first decide what 42:6 is, and the Hebrew does not decide it.
2. **The narrator does not commend it.** He evaluates nothing between 2:10 and 42:7 (Tool 6), and when evaluation comes it commends Job's **speech** — including, on any natural reading, the speech of chapters 3–31. `[T]`
3. **The act is partly quotation** (Tool 12), so "do what Job did" would mean, in part, "repeat God's words back to him."
**Label: descriptive, with the evaluation deliberately withheld and then given in a direction that cuts against moralistic use.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**The manner of the divine act — what the shape reveals about posture.** The tool file asks this of any passage containing divine initiative, and here the shape is unusual: **God's act in this unit is almost entirely *speech about third parties*.** He does not heal Job, restore him, explain to him or forgive him; he describes two animals. **The posture that shape implies is not petition and not effort but attention** — and specifically attention to things that have nothing to do with the hearer's case. That is a legitimate application posture and it is not a prescription to imitate. `[I]` *Moderate–high.* Hand-off note for `/point-purpose`: this pairs with the first half's "receive what is given unasked, and cry out when you can" (38:41), and the two together are the unit's application spine.

### 14. Bible Timeline

#### Canonical position

**Section: Ketuvim, the second book in the BHS order** (Psalms, Job, Proverbs). `[T]` for the corpus order; English Bibles place Job after Esther, following the Greek and Latin tradition `[S]`. The overview's five-field block stands and is not re-derived. `[S: overview]`

**What this unit adds to the canonical-position reading, and it is substantial.** The companion dig noted that the reader who meets Proverbs next has watched God decline to explain. `[S: prior dig]` **This half sharpens it into a verbal fact: the two external sources of the second divine speech are Psalm 104 and Proverbs 8 — the book immediately before Job in the Ketuvim and the book immediately after.** `[T]`
- From **Psalms** (before) Job takes God's regalia (104:1 → 40:10) and God's playmate (104:26 → 41:5).
- To **Proverbs** (after) Job hands back Wisdom's own title, now worn by Behemoth (8:22 → 40:19).
**So the second speech is positioned, lexically, exactly where the canon puts it** — drawing on its predecessor and answering its successor. `[I]` *Moderate–high.* **Direction of dependence is open in both cases** and this report does not settle either; what is not open is that all three books stand in one canonical neighbourhood and share this vocabulary.

**The poetic accentuation** runs through 42:6 and stops: the WLC has the poetic system for 3:2–42:6 and the prose system from 42:7. `[S: overview]` **So this unit ends exactly where the accentuation ends** — one more witness to 42:6 as the seam. `[T]` *as the WLC transcribes Leningrad*; a reading tradition, not authorial, spread unchecked.

#### The timeline proper

**Where the passage sits.** Outside Israel's story, as the whole book is. The unit's single place-name is the Jordan (40:23) and its single gentilic is a trade term. No covenant with a people, no law, no temple, no king of Israel — and the one king named is an animal. `[T]`

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross and resurrection, awaiting consummation.

**What has happened between that materially changes the reading.** Three things.

1. **The question of 41:11 has been answered in a direction the verse did not anticipate.** "Who has given to me first, that I should repay him? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine." Paul quotes exactly this to close a doxology whose next line is "for from him and through him and to him are all things" (Rom 11:36) — **and the chapter before it has said that God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32).** The one who owes nothing gave. **The verse is not contradicted; it is the premise that makes the gift a gift rather than a repayment.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.* This is the unit's most important timeline change and it is also its Christological centre.
2. **The monsters have been reclassified.** In Job, Leviathan is God's unequalled creature and is not destroyed. In the prophets and Revelation the sea-dragon *is* destroyed (Isa 27:1; Rev 20:2, 10), and "the sea was no more" (Rev 21:1). **So the canon does eventually do to the dragon what Job 41 conspicuously does not** — and the difference is eschatological, not contradictory. Job's reader is told that the thing he cannot tame is on a leash; Revelation's reader is told when the leash ends. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
3. **"Now my eye sees You" (42:5) has been both relativised and surpassed.** Relativised: "no one has ever seen God" (John 1:18; 1 John 4:12), so whatever Job means it is not sight of the divine essence. Surpassed: "we beheld his glory" (John 1:14), "whoever has seen me has seen the Father" (John 14:9), and "we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). **Job's seeing is real, partial, and now namable.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**Where the tool is partly not needed.** 41:11's claim about God's unindebtedness is as true now as then, and so is 40:8's question. The timeline is needed at the three points above and nowhere else in the unit.

### 15. Who Am I?

| Character / group | Function |
|---|---|
| **YHWH** | The speaker. Not a figure to identify with — and the specific temptation here is to borrow his rhetoric (see below). |
| **Job** | **Recipient, and representative.** The questions at 40:8–14 are addressed to one man by name, but every reader who has thought the world misgoverned is in their scope. The identification is direct and legitimate. At **42:1–6 Job becomes the model of a response**, which is where Copycat's cautions apply. `[I]` *High.* |
| **Behemoth** | **Exhibit, and a foil for human pretension.** Made alongside Job (40:15), fed by the mountains, unbothered by rivers. His function is to be the grandest thing in creation and still eat grass. Not a figure to identify with; not a symbol of evil. |
| **Leviathan** | **Exhibit, and the speech's answer to the justice question.** He is a creature (41:33), beyond human handling, king over the proud, and God's. **He is not Satan** (see Pitfalls) and not a type of anything. |
| **"Everyone who is proud" (40:11–12)** | **The group Job is ironically invited to judge — and the group the reader should fear being in.** They are the only human beings in the divine speech apart from Job, and they appear as objects of a judgement Job cannot execute. |
| **The "mighty" who fear (41:25)** | Witnesses, establishing scale. אֵלִים is textually unsettled (Tool 8). |

**Where this passage is commonly misidentified, and it is the sharpest case in the book.**

**(a) "I am the one asking 'have you an arm like God?'"** The speech's rhetoric is more borrowable than any other passage in Scripture, and 40:8–14 in a preacher's mouth, aimed at a congregation, inverts the text completely. **God is answering a summons Job issued; he is the defendant taking the stand.** `[T]` No preacher occupies that position, and 42:7 shows what becomes of those who speak *about* God against a sufferer. This is the first half's warning intensified, because the second speech is harsher.

**(b) "Leviathan is the devil, and I am to resist him."** This reading turns an exhibit into an adversary and a creature into a rival, and it requires ignoring 41:33's הֶעָשׂוּ and the absence of שָׂטָן since 2:7. **Whatever Leviathan is, the passage's point is that he belongs to God, not that he opposes him.** `[T]` *High.*

**(c) "I am Job at 42:6, and the application is self-contempt."** Available only on the ESV's supplied "myself", and in tension with 42:7. See Difficult Verses.

**Christ in relation to the characters.** Not through any character in the unit — there is no mediator figure here, no suffering righteous man in the speech, and no human but Job. **The Christological route runs through the speaker and through 41:11's maxim** (Christological Reading).

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.**

From 40:8's charge, from the structure of the offer at 40:10–14, and from Job's reply: **the author seeks a man who stops needing to be in the right at God's expense.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

That is narrower and harder than "stop asking questions". The thing 40:8 names is not doubt, not lament, not even accusation, but a specific exchange: תַּרְשִׁיעֵנִי לְמַעַן תִּצְדָּק — **condemning God in order to be justified oneself.** The book has already vindicated lament (42:7 will say Job spoke rightly) and has never condemned his questions. **What the second speech goes after is the zero-sum assumption underneath the lawsuit: that God's being just and Job's being innocent cannot both be true.** The investiture exposes it — *if you must be the one in the right, then take the job* — and the two animals close it.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

| Domain | Content |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | The world contains things that are magnificent, untameable, frightening and **not evil**. Behemoth and Leviathan are the canon's strongest statement that *power outside human control is not therefore hostile to God* — he made them, feeds one and plays with the other. **A world I cannot manage is not thereby a world nobody manages.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.* |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Stop requiring that God be convicted for me to be acquitted (40:8). Stop reading what I cannot control as what God has lost control of. Stop borrowing 40:8–14 to use on other people. |
| **Behaviour — start** | Look at what has nothing to do with me, and let it be magnificent. And **say less** — the one thing Job does that the text leaves standing is 42:3b, "I have declared what I did not understand." |
| **Motivation — and the guard against moralism** | Not "God is bigger, so submit". The motive is 41:11 read forward: **the God who owes nothing to anyone gave his Son** (Rom 8:32; 11:35–36). Submission to an unindebted God is terror; **receiving from an unindebted God is grace.** A sermon that preaches 41:11 without Rom 11:36 will produce the former. `[I]` |

**Four audiences.**

| Audience | What this passage means |
|---|---|
| **For me** | The honest question is not "do I doubt God?" but "do I need him to be wrong?" 40:8 locates the fault precisely, and it is a fault that can coexist with orthodoxy, church attendance and real suffering. |
| **For a Christian friend** | For someone whose faith is being held together by the conviction that their suffering is unjust: this passage does not take the injustice back (42:7 will confirm they may be right), but it refuses the trade. And the thing to carry is the leash — the monster is real, and it is on one. |
| **For the church together** | A congregation contains people managing things they cannot control — illness, a child, an addiction in the family, a decline. **This is the text that says the untameable is not therefore godless.** Preaching Leviathan as a demon squanders exactly that comfort. |
| **For an unbeliever** | 40:8 is an unusually precise diagnosis of a common and reasonable position: the suspicion that if there is a God he has some explaining to do. The passage does not call that stupid. It asks whether the person making the charge would take the job — and then shows them the job. **That is an argument a thoughtful non-Christian can feel the force of**, and it does not require them to pretend the world looks well-run. |

**Prayer in response.**

- Thanks that the things I cannot tame are yours, and on a leash.
- Thanks that you owe nothing, and gave.
- Sorry for needing you to be in the wrong so that I could be in the right.
- Where I have spoken of what I do not understand, teach me to say so — and let the saying be the beginning of seeing.

**Hand-off:** this feeds the Purpose Statement in `/point-purpose`. The candidate Fallen Condition Focus is at the end of Original Audience Reception. **Note for the backbone:** if Sermon 8 is preached as one sermon on 38:1–42:6, the two halves' So-What sections are not interchangeable — the first speech's is about attention and crying out; this one's is about the zero-sum trade. The unit's single teaching point will have to choose or hold both.

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **40:8's three verbs are second-person and the possessive is first.** תָּפֵר ("you annul"), תַּרְשִׁיעֵנִי ("you condemn **me**"), תִּצְדָּק ("you be in the right") — with מִשְׁפָּטִי, "**my** justice". **The grammar puts Job in the dock by making him the subject of every verb and God the object of the central one.** `[T]` *High.*
- **40:10–13 is a run of six imperatives with no indicative in sight**: עֲדֵה, תִּלְבָּשׁ (imperfect with imperatival force), הָפֵץ, רְאֵה, הַשְׁפִּילֵהוּ, רְאֵה, הַכְנִיעֵהוּ, הֲדֹךְ, טָמְנֵם, חֲבֹשׁ. **Ten imperative forms in four verses** — the densest imperative run in the book. `[T]` Their force is the opposite of command: a protasis whose apodosis is 40:14.
- **41:8's אַל־תּוֹסַף is the only negative imperative in either divine speech.** `[T]` *High.*
- **The hinge at 41:10–11 changes grammatical person twice** — from third-person Leviathan (יְעוּרֶנּוּ) to first-person God (לְפָנַי) to the maxim's impersonal מִי. The pivot is grammatical before it is thematic.
- **42:2's verb is a ketiv.** The WLC prints ידעת unpointed; the ketiv is יָדַעְתָּ ("**you** know") and the qere יָדַעְתִּי ("**I** know"), with BHS recommending the qere *with the versions*. **The head of Job's confession is textually ambiguous between "I know that You can do all things" and "You know that You can do all things."** `[T]` See Textual Variants.
- **42:6's syntax is the crux and is worth laying out plainly**: עַל־כֵּן ("therefore") + אֶמְאַס (qal imperfect 1cs, **no object**) + וְנִחַמְתִּי (niphal perfect 1cs with waw) + עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר (prepositional phrase). **Two finite verbs, one preposition, and a question about which verb the preposition serves.** If it serves וְנִחַמְתִּי, the construction is the Jeremiah one and means "concerning". If it is adverbial to the whole, it locates. **Hebrew allows both; the construction's distribution favours the first.**

#### Wordplay, repetition and sound-features

- **40:19's two cola turn on a near-rhyme of maker and made**: הוּא רֵאשִׁית דַּרְכֵי־אֵל / **הָעֹשׂוֹ** יַגֵּשׁ חַרְבּוֹ — *hûʾ rēʾšît darkê-ʾēl, hāʿōśô yaggēš ḥarbô.* Transliteration kept because the assonance of עֹשׂוֹ / יַגֵּשׁ is the sound of the line.
- **41:8's three clauses are monosyllabic and clipped**: שִׂים־עָלָיו כַּפֶּךָ / זְכֹר מִלְחָמָה / אַל־תּוֹסַף — *śîm-ʿālāyw kappekā, zəkōr milḥāmâ, ʾal-tôsap.* **Nine words, three commands, and the last is "don't."** The rhythm is the joke.
- **42:6 is four words and a preposition** — *ʿal-kēn ʾemʾas wəniḥamtî ʿal-ʿāpār wāʾēper.* **The ʿal … ʿal frame is audible**, and the two instances are doing different work (the first inferential, the second prepositional). Whether that is deliberate is uncertain; that it is the last line Job speaks in the book is not.
- **The עדה homograph at 40:10 / 28:8** — reported under Tool 7 at *uncertain*, with the hard rule applied.

#### Recommended verification

1. **The history of interpretation of 42:6** — the single most important item in this report. Headline 5 argues from the construction's distribution toward "I am comforted / I relent concerning dust and ashes", which is a **minority** reading. **Delitzsch, Konkel–Longman, Andersen and Lange are all in the library** `[S: methods note]`, and a Logos round should establish who has argued the "concerning" sense and on what grounds before this reading goes near a pulpit.
2. **HALOT or BDB on שַׁחַץ** — a two-verse word carrying a headline's climax. Does the lexicon support "pride" at both 28:8 and 41:34, and what is the basis?
3. **A commentary on 41:15 [Heb 41:7]** — whether גַּאֲוָה or the conjectured גֵּוֹה should stand. The ESV has chosen; the MT reads גַּאֲוָה; Headline 3 counts it.
4. **HALOT on חָת (41:33)** — a hapax carrying the Leviathan panel's last clause.
5. **A lexicon on אֵלִים (41:25)** — "gods" or "mighty ones"; and whether BHS's אֵילִים is adopted by anyone.
6. **Behemoth and Leviathan's referents** in two or three commentaries, stated as a range rather than resolved.

### Textual Variants

**Category 2 of the Three-Way Triage only.** Category 1 is reported under Tools 7, 8 and 10; category 3 has no instance and the reverse obtains (Tool 11).

#### 42:2 — the ketiv at the head of the confession

- **The issue.** The WLC prints ידעת unpointed. BHS: *"Cp 42, 2 ᵃ l c Q Vrs תִּי—, K תָּ—"* — read with the Qere and the versions יָדַעְתִּי ("I know"); the ketiv is יָדַעְתָּ ("you know").
- **Manuscript and version evidence.** The qere is supported by **the versions**, and Swete has Οἶδα, "I know". `[T]`
- **Most likely original.** **The qere.** "You know that You can do all things" is possible Hebrew but makes little sense as the opening of a confession, and the versions are unanimous. *Confidence: high.*
- **Preaching implication.** None, since both English versions follow the qere and the sense is secure. **Worth knowing only because it means the first word of Job's final speech is one the Masoretes corrected** — a small reminder that this chapter's text is not everywhere smooth.
- *as BHS prints Leningrad*; a reading tradition, not authorial; spread `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` and capped at moderate.

#### 41:12 [Heb 41:4] — a ketiv that flips a negative

- **The issue.** The WLC prints לא unpointed at Heb 41:4. BHS: *"4 ᵃ Q לו, K לא"* — the ketiv is לֹא ("not"), the qere לוֹ ("to him"). BHS further proposes reading בְּדַיּוֹ for בַּדָּיו and marks the colon *inc* (uncertain).
- **Most likely original.** Undecided. Both English versions follow the **ketiv**: "I will **not** keep silence concerning his limbs." *Confidence: uncertain.*
- **Preaching implication.** Do not build on this verse. Nothing in this report does.

#### 41:15 [Heb 41:7] — "pride" or "back"

- **The issue.** MT reads גַּאֲוָה, "pride". BHS: *"7 ᵃ prp גֵּוֹה cf 𝔊 α´ 𝔙"* — proposes "back", with the Greek, Aquila and the Vulgate.
- **Manuscript evidence.** This is a **conjecture supported by versional renderings**, not by a Hebrew witness in view.
- **Most likely original.** **The MT.** A rare and emphatic word standing in a passage saturated with pride vocabulary (Headline 3) is better explained as original than as a corruption of a commoner one, and the versions may simply have found גַּאֲוָה odd. *Confidence: moderate.*
- **Preaching implication — and it is real.** **The ESV has adopted the conjecture**, so a congregation reading the ESV will not hear "pride" at this verse. If the sermon runs the pride spine, say that the Hebrew reads "pride" here and that their Bible has followed the Greek and Latin. **This is the one place in the unit where the pulpit text has departed from the MT on a word a finding depends on.**

#### 40:6 — the ketiv at the heading, and the missing article

- **The issue.** Two things at once. BHS: *"6 ᵃ 6.7 = 38,1.3 || ᵇ Q מִן"* — the ketiv is the unpointed מנ with qere מִן, exactly as at 38:1. **And the noun lacks the article here where 38:1 has it.** `[T]`
- **Most likely original.** The text as it stands; nothing turns on the preposition.
- **Preaching implication.** None for the ketiv. The article is a datum for anyone reading both headings in Hebrew (Tool 6); significance *uncertain*.

#### 40:17 — a ketiv in the Behemoth panel

- פחדו stands unpointed (Heb 40:17). Rendered "his thighs" by both versions. Nothing rests on it.

#### Lesser notes, recorded and not developed

40:12 (some manuscripts add waw); 40:20 marked *inc*; 40:23 (BHS proposes יִפְשַׁע); 40:25–26 [Heb] orthographic; 41:1–2 [Heb] (manuscript variation in person — see Tool 8 on English 41:9); 41:5, 8, 12 [Heb] (conjectures); 41:17 [Heb] (אֵלִים / אֵילִים); 41:18 [Heb] marked *inc*; 42:3 (a manuscript adds בְמִלִּים, with BHS cross-referring 38:2 — which independently supports the 38:2 → 42:3 quotation).

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time period.** The patriarchal world of the book's own staging. `[S: overview]` No date is imported.
- **Location.** The unit names the Jordan (40:23) and nothing else. **The river is the only piece of real geography in either divine speech**, and the animals are placed in marsh, reed-bed and sea rather than in a named country. `[T]`
- **Political context.** Absent, except as the vocabulary of 40:10–14 presupposes a court (regalia, enthronement, the humbling of the proud) and 41:6 [Heb 40:30] presupposes a market.

#### What the original audience knew

1. **That הוֹד וְהָדָר was royal and divine regalia, not a general compliment.** The pair stands in eight verses of the Hebrew Bible and in seven it belongs to God or the king (Tool 7). **A hearer who knew the Psalter would recognise 40:10 as a quotation of a doxology, and would hear the sarcasm immediately.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
2. **That "treading down the wicked where they stand" and "hiding their faces" was what a king was *for*.** 40:11–13 is not a description of divine omnipotence in the abstract; it is the job description of ANE kingship — subdue the proud, bury the rebel, establish order. **Job is being offered a throne, and the audience knew what the duties were.** `[I]` *Moderate.*
3. **That a crocodile and a hippopotamus were Egyptian, and both were royal game.** Egyptian kings are depicted hunting both; the hippopotamus hunt in particular is a royal motif. *Uncertain — consider verifying;* this is recalled background and the specific iconography would need a background commentary. **If it holds, 41:1–8's catalogue of failed capture-methods lands as a deflation of exactly the royal boast 40:10–14 has just invited Job to make** — which would be a precise and powerful background point, and it is flagged as unverified for that reason.
4. **That hooks, cords, nose-ropes and leashes were how captives and draught animals were handled.** אַגְמוֹן in the nose (41:2 [Heb 40:26]) is the ring used on a captive or a working beast, and Assyrian reliefs show prisoners led by nose-rings. *Moderate.* **So 41:1–8 asks whether Job can reduce Leviathan to a prisoner, a servant, a pet or a commodity — four categories of human dominance, all refused.**
5. **That 41:6's "merchants" dividing him up is the fishing trade.** The audience would hear a market-stall question: *could you even sell him?* The bathos is deliberate.
6. **That ashes were where the bereaved and the penitent sat.** 2:8 put Job בְּתוֹךְ הָאֵפֶר, "among the ashes". `[T]` **So the audience arrives at 42:6 with an established picture of Job sitting in ash** — which is exactly why the locative reading of 42:6 feels natural, and exactly why the preposition matters: **the book's own ash-heap idiom is בְּתוֹךְ (2:8), not עַל.** `[T]` See Difficult Verses.

#### What this changes about how we read

- **40:10–14 is a coronation offer, not a taunt about strength.** Recovering the royal vocabulary turns it from "you're not as strong as me" into "here is the crown; now govern" — which is a far sharper and more answerable challenge, and it is the one Job declines.
- **41:1–8 is about ownership, not danger.** The verbs are commercial and domestic — covenant, servant, play, leash, bargain, divide. **The question is not "can you survive him?" but "can you own him?"** That is why the panel ends with God's own ownership claim: "whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" (41:11).

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section: Ketuvim.** The reader arrives post-exilic, with Torah and the full Prophets behind, living with promises unresolved. `[S]` for the sequence's logic.
- **What the canonical reader knows that bears on this unit specifically.** **Psalm 104 by heart** — which makes 40:10 a recognisable quotation and 41:5's question answerable. **Proverbs 8** — which makes 40:19 an audible transfer of title. **The prologue** — which makes 41:10's "who can stand before me?" an echo they can place, since they have watched the Accuser do exactly that. `[T]`
- **Canonical register.** Psalms before, Proverbs after; and this unit draws on both (Tool 14).

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were.** Israel reading the Writings, who know what Job does not.
- **Their situation.** Holding a doctrine of retribution that the prologue has already falsified in this case and that thirty-four chapters have failed to defend.
- **What they brought.** An expectation, built by the entire dialogue and only sharpened by the first speech's refusal, that God would **adjudicate**. And a settled canonical picture of the sea-dragon as God's defeated enemy.

#### Where the passage fits their world

| What they expected | What the passage delivers |
|---|---|
| A verdict on Job, after the first speech withheld one | **A charge against Job** (40:8) — and still no verdict. The verdict comes at 42:7 and goes against the friends. |
| The justice question dropped | **The justice question named explicitly**, in the speech's first substantive line, with all three of the book's lawsuit words in it |
| Leviathan crushed, as in Isa 27:1 and Ps 74:14 | **Leviathan praised, and called king** |
| The monster as God's enemy | The monster as God's **creature** (41:33) and God's **plaything** (Ps 104:26) |
| Job broken | Job **offered the throne** (40:10–14) |
| A confession in Job's own words | A confession largely composed of **God's words quoted back** (42:3–4) |

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking:** that God's second word to a bereaved man is an accusation of motive (40:8). It is the harshest thing God says in the book, and it comes after Job has already fallen silent.
- **Shocking, second:** the coronation offer. **To a monotheistic audience, 40:10's invitation to put on God's own regalia is close to blasphemous** — which is the point: Job's position, carried to its conclusion, requires it.
- **Surprising:** that the sea-dragon is not killed. For an audience holding Isa 27:1 and Ps 74:14, thirty-four verses of praise for Leviathan with no combat would be the single most unexpected thing in the chapter. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Surprising, second:** that Behemoth has Wisdom's title.
- **Comforting:** that the untameable is on a leash — God plays with it. And that 41:11's "whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" includes, by implication, everything that has happened to Job.
- **Disturbing:** that the last word of the speech gives a crown to something other than God or man (41:34), and the book does not explain it.
- **Funny:** yes, twice. "He eats grass like an ox" (40:15) after "his bones are tubes of bronze". And 41:5's "will you leash him for your girls?"

#### What we bring that they didn't

- **A demonology.** We arrive with Rev 12–13 and read Leviathan as Satan. **The first hearers had the chaos-dragon tradition but not the identification**, and in any case the text calls him a creature. Our overlay is the single largest distortion we bring to this passage.
- **Zoology.** We ask what animal it is. They asked whose it is.
- **A therapeutic expectation of confession.** We hear 42:6 as a breakthrough in self-understanding. **The Hebrew may be saying something far more modest** (Headline 5).
- **An assumption that power implies threat.** The passage assumes the opposite: that magnificence outside human control is a reason for confidence about its owner.

#### Candidate Fallen Condition Focus

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **What they felt** | The need to be in the right — and the fear that being in the right required God to be in the wrong. `[T]` for the charge (40:8); `[I]` for the felt quality, drawn from the dialogue's shape and from Job's oath of clearance (ch. 31). |
| **Candidate FCF (shared concern)** | **We cannot see how God can be just and we be innocent at the same time — so we quietly choose one, and it is usually ourselves.** |
| **Shared / differs** | *Shared:* the zero-sum instinct is exactly as live now as then, and it operates in the pious as readily as in the sceptical. *Differs:* we stand after the cross, where the problem is answered in the one place the book could not reach — at Rom 3:26, God is shown to be "just **and** the justifier". **The FCF's resolution is Christological in a way Job's horizon could not supply.** |
| **Confidence** | **Anchored** — derived from 40:8's לְמַעַן and from the reception work above, not from contemporary cultural analysis. |

#### Implication for the sermon

Recovering the reception does two things. It **restores the coronation offer**, which modern readers skip past as rhetoric and which the first hearers would have found scandalous — and the offer is the hinge of the sermon. And it **removes the demonology**, which is not a minor correction: Leviathan read as Satan makes the chapter a warning, and Leviathan read as God's creature makes it a comfort. **The text's own classification of him (41:33, "the one made") is the decisive word, and it should be quoted.**

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, selected on the three criteria, with the relation between them named.

#### 1. The sea, the dragon, and the limits of chaos

- **Where this passage sits in the trajectory.** At the canon's most unexpected station: the point where the chaos-monster is named, described at length, and **not destroyed**.
- **Textual signal (criterion 1).** Very strong: לִוְיָתָן named (41:1), the thirty-four-verse panel, the explicit הֶעָשׂוּ ("the made one", 41:33), the exclusive Ps 104:26 contact, and the absence of every combat verb.
- **What comes before.** Gen 1:21's תַּנִּינִם created on the fifth day and pronounced good — **the Torah's own demythologising move, and the real precedent for Job 41**. Then the poetic tradition: the waters bounded (Gen 1:9; Ps 104:9; Prov 8:29; Job 38:8–11), Rahab pierced (Isa 51:9), Leviathan's heads crushed (Ps 74:14), and Leviathan given to play in the sea (Ps 104:26). **The tradition already contains both a defeated dragon and a domesticated one, and Job 41 stands squarely with the second.** `[T]`
- **What comes after.** Isa 27:1 — "in that day the LORD will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent"; the beast from the sea (Rev 13:1); "the dragon, that ancient serpent" bound and then destroyed (Rev 20:2, 10); and "the sea was no more" (Rev 21:1). **The canon does eventually kill the dragon — eschatologically.** `[I]`
- **What this contributes.** The theme's shape is *created good → bounded → still untamed → finally removed*, and **Job 41 is the station where the middle term is held without either of the comforting resolutions.** The monster is neither a demon to be feared nor a problem already solved. He is a creature, on a leash, whose leash the reader cannot see. **For a sufferer, that is the exact shape of the pastoral problem and the exact shape of the answer**: what is loose in your life is not loose in the universe. *Confidence: moderate–high.*

#### 2. Justice — God's and ours

- **Where this passage sits.** At the point where the Old Testament states the problem of theodicy in its sharpest form and declines to resolve it.
- **Textual signal.** 40:8, the only verse in Job with all three lawsuit words; the possessive flip from 29:14's מִשְׁפָּטִי to 40:8's; and the investiture's conditional (40:14).
- **What comes before.** Abraham's "shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Gen 18:25) — **and Gen 18:27, the only other verse in the canon with Job 42:6's phrase, is from that same conversation.** `[T]` Then Moses' intercessions, the covenant lawsuit of the prophets, the imprecatory psalms, and Habakkuk's "why do you make me see iniquity?" (Hab 1:3).
- **What comes after.** The question is not answered in the Old Testament. **Rom 3:25–26 answers it in the only terms that can: God is shown to be δίκαιον καὶ δικαιοῦντα — "just *and* the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus."** The zero-sum trade that 40:8 names as Job's fault is dissolved, not refuted, by a cross.
- **What this contributes.** 40:8 identifies the fault with a precision no other verse in Scripture matches: not doubt, not complaint, but **condemning God in order to be justified**. Naming it is the passage's permanent contribution, and the fact that the book leaves it named and unresolved is what makes Job a book the New Testament needed. *Confidence: moderate–high.*

#### Confluence

The relation is **(b) mutual illumination**, and it is worth stating because the two themes look unrelated. **The chaos theme explains why the justice theme is not resolved here.** God's answer to the justice question is not an argument but an exhibit: *the thing you cannot govern is mine, and I have not destroyed it.* A God who bounded chaos without abolishing it is a God whose justice is real and not yet visible — which is precisely the position Job is in and precisely the position the reader is left in. **The monster is the theodicy.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

### Christological Reading

#### Type of Christological connection

**Trajectory, and one direct New Testament use of the passage's own words. Not typology, and not Christophany** — and both exclusions are worth arguing rather than asserting, because this passage attracts both.

*Not typology.* The candidate is Leviathan as a type of Satan, with Christ as the one who hooks him. Running the four tests: **test 1 (theological category) fails at the first step** — the connection would run through a *creature's identity*, and the text's own category for him is "the one made" (41:33), not "the adversary". **Test 2 (NT precedent):** the NT does apply dragon-language to Satan (Rev 12:9; 20:2), but it does so to Genesis 3's serpent and Isaiah's dragon, **not to Job's Leviathan, which no NT text mentions.** **Test 3 (escalation)** cannot even be run, since there is nothing in Job 41 for an antitype to exceed. **Test 4 (authorial pattern):** the passage raises no unresolved expectation about Leviathan; it closes with him crowned. **Score: passes none. This is allegorising and is omitted.** `[I]` *High confidence in the exclusion.*

*Not Christophany.* The speaker is YHWH out of a storm, unseen. 42:5's "my eye sees You" is the nearest thing, and what it means is contested; **it cannot bear a Christophany claim**, and the overview's "keep honest" note on this point is followed here. `[S: overview]`

#### How the passage points to Christ

**1. The one direct use: Job 41:11 → Rom 11:35, and it is load-bearing.** `[T]`

Paul closes the argument of Romans 1–11 with a doxology, and its last citation is God's words from the whirlwind: **"who has given a gift to him that he might be repaid?"** (Rom 11:35 ← Job 41:11 [Heb 41:3], following the Hebrew against the Old Greek — Tool 11). The next line is "for from him and through him and to him are all things" (11:36).

**The theological work this does is precise.** Job 41:11 establishes that **God is in nobody's debt**: nothing has been given to him that obliges a return. Paul uses it to close an argument that has just said God "did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all" (Rom 8:32) and that mercy to Israel and the nations rests on God's freedom, not their claim (Rom 11:6, 32). **So the verse functions in Romans as the ground of grace**: because nobody has a claim, everything received is gift. *Confidence: high* on the citation, *moderate–high* on the function, which is Paul's own context rather than a reconstruction.

And it reframes the passage Christologically without allegory. **The God who says "whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" and "who has ever put me in my debt?" is the God who gave what was his to those who had no claim.** The unindebtedness of Job 41 is not a cold attribute; **in Romans it is the precondition of the cross.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**2. Trajectory: the question 40:8 asks is the question the cross answers.** `[I]`

40:8's charge — will you condemn me in order to be justified? — names a dilemma it does not resolve: God's justice and Job's innocence appear to be in competition. **The New Testament's answer is not that one of them yields but that both are established at once:** God is "just and the justifier" (Rom 3:26); he "condemned sin in the flesh" (Rom 8:3) so that there is "no condemnation" (8:1). **The zero-sum assumption 40:8 exposes is dissolved at the point where God takes the condemnation himself.** *Confidence: moderate–high.* **This is the unit's strongest Christological line and the one a sermon should carry.**

**3. Trajectory: the Creator of these creatures has been named.** The agent through whom all things were made (John 1:3; Col 1:16; Heb 1:2) is the one who made Behemoth and Leviathan, and **he is the one who slept in a boat on a sea that terrified professional fishermen and then told it to be quiet (Mark 4:39)**. The companion dig made this connection at 38:8–11. `[S: prior dig]` **This half strengthens it:** if 41:11's "whatever is under the whole heaven is mine" is true of the Son, then the disciples' "who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" has an answer in Job's own vocabulary. *Confidence: moderate–high.*

**4. Contrast, and it is sharp: Job was offered the robes and declined; Christ had them and laid them down.** `[I]`
40:10 invites Job to clothe himself with הוֹד וְהָדָר — the regalia Ps 104:1 says God wears. Job cannot and does not. **Phil 2:6–8 describes one who was in the form of God, did not grasp at equality, and emptied himself.** **The contrast is exact at the point of the garment**: Job is offered glory he cannot wear; Christ wears glory he willingly sets aside. *Confidence: moderate.* The connection runs through the established category of divine glory rather than through an incidental detail, which is why it is admissible as contrast; it is **not** offered as typology.

#### Trajectory summary

- **What the passage anticipates or longs for.** A justice that does not require God's conviction; a world whose untameable parts are owned; a sight of God that replaces hearsay.
- **What Christ fulfils.** The just-and-justifier; the Maker who commands the sea in person; the glory worn and laid down; and, at 42:5, "we beheld his glory" (John 1:14).
- **What still remains.** **Leviathan is not killed in Job and he is not killed yet.** The leash holds; the binding is future (Rev 20:2, 10); "the sea was no more" is the last word and it is not spoken here. **And the explanation Job never received is one the New Testament does not supply either** — it supplies a person, a verdict and a promise of sight (1 Cor 13:12).

#### Moralism check

- **The "be like X" temptation.** Two forms here. The crude one is "repent like Job" — an exhortation built on a verse whose meaning is contested and whose supplied object ("myself") is the ESV's, not the Hebrew's. The subtler one is "stop demanding justice", which makes the application an instruction to suppress exactly what 42:7 vindicates.
- **The gospel grounding.** The Accuser asked whether anyone fears God חִנָּם, "for nothing" (1:9). `[S: overview]` **41:11 is the divine side of that question**: God is in nobody's debt, so nothing he gives is wages. **The book's whole argument and the gospel's meet at that verse** — a God who owes nothing can only give grace, and a man who is given nothing can only fear God for himself.
- **Christ as hero, not just example.** The hero of this unit is the speaker: he made, fed, crowned, owns and owes nothing. **Job's part is five verses, four of them quotation.** Preach the speaker.

**Confidence:** *high* on the Rom 11:35 citation and on its function in Romans; *moderate–high* on the 40:8 → Rom 3:26 trajectory and on the Creator line; *moderate* on the Phil 2 contrast; **typology and Christophany explicitly excluded, with the tests shown.**

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 42:6 — what does Job actually say?

**This is the book's most contested verse and the unit's reason for being weighted ⭐ HIGH. The crux is set out at length because the project's own plan flagged it for argument here.** `[S: project plan]`

- **Category:** doctrinal, pastoral, and translational at once.
- **The difficulty.** עַל־כֵּן אֶמְאַס וְנִחַמְתִּי עַל־עָפָר וָאֵפֶר. **The first verb has no object. The second verb's sense is disputed. The preposition could be local or relational. BHS records no variant.** `[T]` Four words carry the book's conclusion.
- **Common mishandlings.** Preaching the ESV's "I despise myself" as though the Hebrew said it; treating the verse as unambiguous self-abasement and then having to explain 42:7; or the opposite error, dissolving the verse into mere "comfort" so that nothing is conceded at all.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** It is the **apodosis of 42:5**: "I had heard of You… **but now my eye sees You; therefore**…". So whatever it means, it is the consequence of **seeing**, not of being argued down. `[T]` And it is the last thing Job says, immediately before the verdict that he spoke rightly.

**What an honest exegesis surfaces — four verified data.**

1. **אֶמְאַס is absolute, and that is normal Joban usage.** מָאַס is used without an object at **7:16** (מָאַסְתִּי, in a context of weariness with life) and **36:5** (וְלֹא יִמְאָס, of God). So the missing object at 42:6 **does not require supplying one.** `[T]` *High.* The ESV supplies "myself"; the NASB95 renders "I retract" and supplies nothing. **Both are interpretations; only one admits it.**
2. **וְנִחַמְתִּי + עַל is a known construction with a known sense.** The exact form stands in **four verses: Jer 18:8; 18:10; 26:3; Job 42:6**, and the three Jeremiah verses all continue עַל־הָרָעָה and mean "**I will relent concerning** the disaster". With a human subject the same construction means "**be comforted concerning**" — 2 Sam 13:39 (נִחַם עַל־אַמְנוֹן, David consoled concerning his dead son) and Ezek 32:31 (Pharaoh comforted concerning his multitude). `[T]` *High.* **Across the Hebrew Bible, נחם + עַל + object does not mean "repent in a place."**
3. **The book's own ash-heap idiom is not עַל.** 2:8 has Job sitting בְּתוֹךְ הָאֵפֶר, "**among** the ashes". `[T]` So where the book wants to locate him in ash, it uses a different preposition.
4. **The Old Greek did not read it locally.** ἥγημαι δὲ ἐγὼ ἐμαυτὸν γῆν καὶ σποδόν — "I count myself earth and ashes" — construing the phrase as a **predicate**, exactly as **Gen 18:27** does (וְאָנֹכִי עָפָר וָאֵפֶר, "and I am dust and ashes"), the only other verse in the canon with the unprefixed phrase. `[T]` *High.*

**The main interpretive options, with what each requires.**

| Reading | Rendering | What it requires | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Locative** | "I retract / despise myself, and repent **in** dust and ashes" | עַל = "upon, in", of place; a supplied object for מאס (ESV) or an absolute "retract" (NASB95) | The majority reading and the one in both English versions. **It requires the one sense of נחם + עַל that is otherwise unattested.** |
| **(b) Relational — "concerning"** | "Therefore I recant, and **am comforted concerning** dust and ashes" | עַל = "concerning"; נחם niphal in its normal construction; מאס absolute as at 7:16 | **Favoured by the syntax.** Gives a Job who is consoled about his mortality, and connects directly to 42:11's comfort five verses later and to the friends' failed comfort (2:11; 16:2). |
| **(c) Predicate — the Greek's reading** | "Therefore I recant, and repent: **I am** dust and ashes" | עַל construed loosely, or the phrase read as a predicate complement | The Old Greek's reading, and it makes the **Gen 18:27** link exact — Abraham's self-description while pressing God about justice. |
| **(d) "Relent concerning"** | "Therefore I recant, and **relent concerning** dust and ashes" | The Jeremiah sense with a human subject | Grammatically the closest to the four-verse distribution of the form, but awkward in sense. |

**Where the textual evidence points, and the honest statement of confidence.** **The syntax favours (b) or (c) over (a).** The construction נחם + עַל is well attested and does not mean what (a) needs it to mean; the book's own ash-idiom is בְּתוֹךְ; the oldest version read it as (c); and (b) makes the best sense of נחם's distribution within Job, where the verb is otherwise always about comfort between persons — including five verses later.

**But this reading is held at *moderate*, not higher, for three reasons stated plainly.** (i) **עַל can be locative**, and "repent in sackcloth and ashes" is an idiom elsewhere in the canon even if not with this verb. (ii) **The history of interpretation runs strongly the other way** and has not been checked for this run — Delitzsch, Lange, Andersen and Konkel–Longman are all in the library and none has been consulted on the preposition. `[S: methods note]` (iii) The project plan records that **"נחם עַל is still to argue in the 42:6 dig"** `[S: project plan]`, which is what this section does — but arguing a construction is not the same as establishing a consensus, and a reading that overturns both English versions on the book's climactic verse needs the commentators heard before it goes near a pulpit.

- **Suggested handling for the preacher.** **Do not resolve it from the pulpit, and do not let the ESV resolve it silently either.** The form of words that is honest and preachable: *the Hebrew says "therefore I recant, and I am comforted" — or "and I repent" — "concerning dust and ashes." Your Bible says "I despise myself"; the "myself" is the translators'. What is certain is the word before it: Job says this because he has **seen**.* Then preach 42:5 as the hinge and let 42:6's reticence stand — **particularly because the next verse says he spoke what was right.**

#### 41:34 [Heb 41:26] — "king over all the sons of pride" read as a description of Satan

- **Category:** doctrinal, and the most common misreading of the passage.
- **The difficulty.** The verse crowns a monster at the end of a divine speech, and the canon elsewhere calls Satan a dragon. A congregation primed by Revelation will hear "Satan" whether or not the preacher says it.
- **Common mishandlings.** Preaching Leviathan as Satan, and the chapter as a lesson in spiritual warfare — which inverts its comfort into a threat.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** The verse is the **climax of an exhibit** in an argument about who can govern (40:8–14). Its function is to close the investiture: *the post is filled, and not by you.*
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** Three things. **(i)** The text classifies him: הֶעָשׂוּ, "the one made" (41:33), and Behemoth likewise "which I made" (40:15). `[T]` **(ii)** **שָׂטָן occurs 14× in 11 verses of Job and never after 2:7** — the book's own Adversary has been absent for thirty-nine chapters, and no NT text connects him to Job's Leviathan. `[T]` **(iii)** Ps 104:26, the only canonical verse pairing Leviathan with "play", makes him God's recreation. **So the identification is a canonical overlay, not an exegetical result.** *Confidence: high* that the passage does not present Leviathan as the Adversary; *moderate* on how much the later dragon-tradition may legitimately be heard behind him, since Isa 27:1 does eventually promise his punishment.
- **Suggested handling.** Name the misreading and correct it from 41:33. The pastoral stake is large: **Leviathan as devil makes the chapter a warning; Leviathan as creature makes it the comfort the sufferer needs.**

#### 40:8 — "will you condemn Me that you may be justified?" used to silence complaint

- **Category:** pastoral, with a doctrinal edge.
- **The difficulty.** It is the harshest thing God says in the book, and it is said to a man who has lost ten children and is sitting in ash.
- **Common mishandlings.** Generalising it into "do not question God", which makes it contradict 42:7; or softening it until it says nothing.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** It is the **charge that opens a case**, followed immediately by an offer (40:10–14). It is not a verdict, and the narrator supplies none.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The לְמַעַן is doing the work: the fault named is **not** complaint, doubt or accusation but the *purpose* clause — condemning God **in order to** be justified oneself. `[T]` *High.* **The text identifies a motive, not a genre of speech.** And 42:7 settles what is not being condemned: Job's speech, as speech, was right.
- **Suggested handling.** Make the distinction explicit and early, because the congregation will hear a prohibition on lament otherwise. *God does not rebuke Job for speaking. He names what Job was trying to achieve by it.*

#### 40:10–14 — the investiture read as a straightforward challenge to strength

- **Category:** interpretive, and it flattens the passage.
- **The difficulty.** Read as "you're not strong enough", 40:10–14 is a truism. Read as a coronation offer in the Psalter's own vocabulary (Headline 1), it is an argument.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** הוֹד + הָדָר + לבשׁ is exclusive to Ps 104:1 and this verse; the duties in 40:11–13 are royal duties; and 40:14's apodosis makes the whole a conditional — **govern, and I concede your case.** `[T]`
- **Suggested handling.** Preach it as the offer it is. It is the one place in the book where Job's position is taken completely seriously and followed to its conclusion.

#### Pastorally sensitive, to think about before the pulpit

- **Anyone facing something untameable** — a diagnosis, an addiction in the family, a decline. **This chapter is for them**, and preaching Leviathan as a demon takes it away from them.
- **Anyone who has been told their complaint is faithless.** 40:8 will land as confirmation unless the לְמַעַן is explained.
- **Anyone carrying self-contempt.** "I despise myself" at 42:6 in the pulpit Bible will be heard as the text's endorsement of it. **Say what the Hebrew has.**
- **The recently bereaved.** The second speech contains none of the first's tenderness, and a sermon that supplies warmth the text withholds will not help; the honest route is 41:11 forward into Rom 8:32.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: Leviathan as Satan

- **What it looks like:** "Here is your enemy — the dragon, king over all the sons of pride. And you cannot defeat him; only Christ can." Often with Rev 12 on the screen.
- **Why it's wrong:** The text classifies him twice as something God **made** (40:15; 41:33 [Heb 41:25]). **שָׂטָן occurs fourteen times in eleven verses of Job and never after 2:7** — the book's own Adversary has been off the page for thirty-nine chapters. No New Testament passage connects Satan to Job's Leviathan. And **Ps 104:26 — the only verse in the canon pairing Leviathan with "play" — makes him God's recreation**, which is the verse 41:5's question depends on.
- **The corrective:** Quote 41:33. Preach him as exhibit B in an argument about government, and let the comfort land: *the thing you cannot handle is handled.* If the later dragon-tradition is to be mentioned, mention it as the canon's **later** word (Isa 27:1; Rev 20:2) and say so.

### Pitfall: "I despise myself" preached as the text

- **What it looks like:** A sermon whose application is self-contempt, built on 42:6 in the pulpit Bible, usually with 42:5 as the lead-in and "see yourself as you really are" as the turn.
- **Why it's wrong:** **אֶמְאַס has no object in the Hebrew**, and the book uses this verb absolutely at 7:16 and 36:5. "Myself" is the ESV's supply; the NASB95 does not supply it. And **וְנִחַמְתִּי עַל** is, everywhere else it occurs, "relent / be comforted **concerning**" — not "repent **in**". **Then 42:7 says Job spoke what was right.** A sermon that ends in self-loathing has to get past that verse, and it cannot.
- **The corrective:** Tell the congregation what the Hebrew has and what their Bible has supplied — briefly, once, without making a performance of it. Preach **42:5** as the hinge (hearing replaced by sight) and let 42:6 be as reticent as it is. The honest sentence: *whatever Job concedes here, he concedes it because he has seen, and God's next word is that he spoke rightly.*

### Pitfall: Using 40:8 to forbid lament

- **What it looks like:** "Will you condemn God to justify yourself? That is what your complaining amounts to." Delivered to a congregation that contains people in real trouble.
- **Why it's wrong:** The force of the verse is in **לְמַעַן** — God names a *purpose*, not a genre of speech. The fault is condemning God **in order to** be justified, which is a specific trade and not the same as lament, protest or a demand for a hearing. **God grants the hearing** (38:1), and at 42:7 vindicates Job's speech against the friends' orthodoxy. The companion dig established that lament addressed *to* God is what the book defends. `[S: prior dig]`
- **The corrective:** Make the distinction in one sentence before you exposit the verse. Then the verse can do its real work, which is diagnostic and uncomfortable in a way that generalised rebuke never is.

### Pitfall: Treating the investiture as a strength contest

- **What it looks like:** "Have you an arm like God? Can you thunder? No — so be humble." Forty minutes on divine omnipotence.
- **Why it's wrong:** 40:10's vocabulary is **quotation**: הוֹד + הָדָר + לבשׁ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Ps 104:1 and here. The duties of 40:11–13 are **royal duties**. 40:14 supplies an **apodosis**. **This is a job offer with a conditional attached, not a flex.** Reduced to a strength contest it becomes a truism nobody disputes, and the sermon loses the one place where the book takes Job's position completely seriously.
- **The corrective:** Preach it as the offer. *Put on the robes; govern the proud; and I will concede that you can save yourself.* Then let the two animals be the job description. The congregation will feel the force of it, because most of them have at some point believed they would run the world better.

### Pitfall: The nature-documentary sermon, second edition

- **What it looks like:** As with the first speech — wonder at the hippo and the crocodile, with the lawsuit stripped out. Easier to fall into here, because the Leviathan passage is magnificent on its own.
- **Why it's wrong:** The animals arrive **after** a charge (40:8) and an offer (40:10–14), and they are the offer's content. Behemoth carries Wisdom's title (40:19 / Prov 8:22); Leviathan carries the crown Job was invited to claim (41:34). **Every image is load-bearing in an argument about government.**
- **The corrective:** Set up 40:8–14 first and spend real time on it. Then each panel lands as a reply. The structural sentence to preach from: *God offers Job the throne, and then shows him who already sits on it.*

### Pitfall: Making 42:6 the resolution and stopping there

- **What it looks like:** The sermon ends at 42:6 with Job broken and restored-in-spirit, and 42:7–17 becomes an appendix.
- **Why it's wrong:** The narrator evaluates nothing between 2:10 and 42:7 (Tool 6), and **the evaluation that comes is about the friends and vindicates Job's speech.** The restoration comes after the verdict and "when he prayed for his friends" (42:10). Ending at 42:6 makes the book's conclusion a psychological one when the book's own conclusion is a judicial one.
- **The corrective:** If Sermon 8 ends at 42:6, say explicitly that the verdict is still to come and point forward. If the unit is preached as 38:1–42:6, the same applies with more force, because the congregation will otherwise hear the whole divine answer as a dressing-down that worked.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties
