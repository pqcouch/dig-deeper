A note before the text

The epilogue’s verse-numbering is the one place in the last three chapters where the three traditions agree: Hebrew, Swete and the English all number 42:7–17 identically, and all three give chapter 42 seventeen verses. After two chapters in which English 41:1 stood at Hebrew 40:25 and Swete numbered the whole Leviathan panel differently again, that is worth saying plainly, because it is the reason this report needs no bracketed Hebrew references.

What the traditions do not agree on is where the book stops. The Hebrew ends at 42:17 with five words. Swete ends at 42:17 and then adds five further sections — a resurrection notice and a genealogy — which the Hebrew does not have. Those are treated under Textual Variants and under Original Audience Reception, as reception rather than text.

Three ketiv forms stand in these eleven verses — 42:10, 42:11, 42:16 — which is 27 % of the unit’s verses against a canonical average of 4.7 %. Two of the three are inert orthography; one (42:10) is the restoration formula itself. All three are reported under Textual Variants.

Headline Findings

1. Job predicted this scene in chapter 13, in its own idiom — and the “partiality” he condemned is what God ends by showing him. [T] for the distributions, [I] for the design. Confidence: high for the words, moderate–high for the design.

At 13:8–10 Job turned on his friends: הֲפָנָיו תִּשָּׂאוּן — “will you show partiality for Him?” — and then, “הוֹכֵחַ יוֹכִיחַ אֶתְכֶם, He will surely rebuke you, אִם־בַּסֵּתֶר פָּנִים תִּשָּׂאוּן, if in secret you show partiality.” Two claims: you are flattering God at my expense, and He will rebuke you for it.

Both come true in 42:7–9, and the vocabulary is Job’s own:

  • נָשָׂא + פָּנִים (“lift the face”, the idiom of partiality) stands in 9 verses of Job: 11:15, 13:8, 13:10, 22:8, 22:26, 32:21, 34:19, 42:8, 42:9. Every speaker uses it. Elihu twice denies doing it (32:21; 34:19); Eliphaz calls a great man נְשׂוּא פָנִים (22:8); Job accuses the friends of it (13:8, 13:10). The last two occurrences are God’s: “I will lift his face” (42:8) and “the LORD lifted up the face of Job” (42:9).
  • יָכַח (“rebuke, arbitrate”) stands in 15 verses of Job, 17 occurrences: 5:17, 6:25, 6:26, 9:33, 13:3, 13:10, 13:15, 15:3, 16:21, 19:5, 22:4, 23:7, 32:12, 33:19, 40:2. God is the subject at 5:17 (object: the man), 22:4 (object: Job) and 13:10 (object: the friends); at 40:2 the relation reverses. So 13:10 is the only verse where God reproves the friends — a class narrow enough to have exactly one member. And the verb does not occur anywhere in 42:7–17: its last appearance in the book is 40:2. What 42:7 says is חָרָה אַפִּי בְךָ. The epilogue fulfils the prediction thematically, not lexically.

v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling B3: this Headline is reframed and downgraded. The count above was wrong in the first version of this report (twelve verses, because a search was truncated), and the design claim built on it does not survive a chance baseline: at three of four rarity ceilings chapter 13 shares no rare lexical material at all with the epilogue, ranking 20th–38th of 41 Job chapters, and the epilogue’s real lexical partner is the prologue, by roughly a factor of two. The partiality idiom also shifts sense — culpable flattery at 13:8–10, favourable acceptance at 42:8–9 — and what God does for Job at 42:8–9 is, in the same words, what 34:19 says God never does for princes. Retained as a thematic correspondence: Job predicted God would rebuke the three, and God does. The נָשָׂא + פָּנִים distribution (nine verses, the last two God’s) stands as verified. The lexical design does not. Rating: moderate as a thematic correspondence; the lexical design discarded.

So the epilogue is not a loose happy ending bolted to a poem. It is the discharge of a prediction made twenty-nine chapters earlier, in the predicting verse’s own two verbs. And there is a sting: the thing God does is exactly the thing Job called a fault. Job said “you lift a face”; God lifts Job’s. Partiality is wrong in a witness and is precisely what grace looks like in a judge.

2. The verb the whole wager was fought over is the verb of the book’s last act of God. [T]. Confidence: high.

בָּרַךְ (“bless”) stands in exactly 8 verses of Job: 1:5, 1:10, 1:11, 1:21, 2:5, 2:9, 31:20, 42:12. Sort them and the book’s shape appears:

Verse Subject Sense
1:5 Job’s sons (feared) the euphemism — “perhaps my sons have blessed God in their hearts”
1:10 God “You have blessed the work of his hands” — the Accuser’s charge
1:11 Job (predicted) the euphemism — “he will bless You to Your face”
1:21 Job “blessed be the name of the LORD” — the real thing, from the ash-heap
2:5 Job (predicted) the euphemism, repeated
2:9 Job’s wife the euphemism — “bless God and die”
31:20 the poor “his loins blessed me”
42:12 the LORD blessing, plainly, with God as subject

Four of the eight are the scribal euphemism in which “bless” means curse. 42:12 is the only verse in the book where God is the subject of בָּרַךְ. The Accuser staked everything on Job blessing God to His face in the bad sense (1:11; 2:5); the book closes וַיהוָה בֵּרַךְ — the LORD blessed — with the parties reversed and the word restored to its own meaning. The one who was supposed to curse is the one who is blessed, by the one he was supposed to curse.

3. Bildad’s promise comes true in Bildad’s own words, one chapter after God says Bildad spoke wrongly. (v1.1 — claim audit 6, ruling B4: the word-pair is confirmed and was already in the book overview’s Echo Table at high, so this is a confirmation of the overview, not a fresh finding; the דּוֹר element is a word in common rather than a contact — 8:8 means ancestors and 42:16 descendants — and the “answered twice” design is discarded, since chapter 8’s rank among epilogue partners swings from 3rd to 15th on the rarity ceiling alone and one of its shared rare words is the gentilic “Shuhite”. The רֵאשִׁית / אַחֲרִית pair stands, and is strong.) [T] for the pair, [I] for the irony. Confidence: high for the distribution, moderate–high for the design.

אַחֲרִית (“latter end”) stands in exactly 2 verses of Job — 8:7 and 42:12. רֵאשִׁית (“beginning”) stands in exactly 3 — 8:7, 40:19, 42:12. A co-occurrence search returns exactly two verses in the book, 8:7 and 42:12, and six in the whole Hebrew Bible (Num 24:20; Deut 11:12; Isa 46:10; Job 8:7; Job 42:12; Eccl 7:8).

Bildad, 8:7: וְאַחֲרִיתְךָ יִשְׂגֶּה מְאֹד — “though your beginning was small, your latter end will greatly increase.” The narrator, 42:12: בֵּרַךְ אֶת־אַחֲרִית אִיּוֹב מֵרֵאשִׁתוֹ — “the LORD blessed Job’s latter end more than his beginning.”

Bildad has the sentence right and the man wrong. He said it as a conditional — if you seek God, if you are pure — and God says five verses before the fulfilment that Bildad has not spoken rightly. The outcome Bildad predicted arrives on terms that destroy his reason for predicting it.

And chapter 8 is answered twice, not once: דּוֹר (“generation”) stands in exactly 2 verses of Job — 8:8 and 42:16. Bildad said “ask the former generation”; Job ends by seeing four generations of his own. The appeal to ancestral tradition is answered by a man who becomes the tradition.

4. The Hebrew Bible’s formula for the end of the exile is used of a single human being exactly once, and this is the verse.

v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling B6: Confirmed with nuance — the only measured claim in this batch to survive largely intact. An auditor classified all 26 contexts independently from the Hebrew and the “only individual” step holds: twenty-five have a nation, people, land, city or lineage as subject. The two borderline cases both fall collective — Ezek 16:53 adds “and her daughters” twice and its first object is a third feminine plural, and Lam 2:14 addresses a city whose prophets and iniquity are named in the same verse. Three nuances to carry. שְׁבוּת never occurs without שׁוּב, so the 26-verse figure is simply the noun’s frequency and not a filtered result. Job 42:10 is a ketiv — BHS reads Qere שְׁבוּת, Ketiv שְׁבִית — so the claim is true of the qere, and nine of the 26 carry an unpointed form. And Swete has no restoration idiom here at all: ηὔξησεν τὸν Ἰώβ, “the Lord increased Job” — the finding is about the Hebrew idiom alone. [T] for the distribution, [I] for the force. Confidence: high for the count, moderate–high for the reading.

שׁוּב + שְׁבוּת (“restore the fortunes”) co-occur in 26 verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deut 30:3; Jer 29:14; 30:3; 30:18; 31:23; 32:44; 33:7; 33:11; 33:26; 48:47; 49:6; 49:39; Ezek 16:53; 29:14; 39:25; Hos 6:11; Joel 4:1; Amos 9:14; Zeph 2:7; 3:20; Ps 14:7; 53:7; 85:2; 126:4; Job 42:10; Lam 2:14.

Read the other twenty-five and every one of them has a collective subject — Israel, Judah, Jacob, Jerusalem, Moab, Ammon, Elam, Egypt, Sodom and Samaria, “my people”. Job 42:10 is the only verse in which the formula is applied to one named person. Alongside it, מִשְׁנֶה (“double”) is a hapax in Job — the book uses the word once, here, for what God adds.

The book has already placed Job outside Israel (Uz; the Temanite, the Shuhite, the Naamathite; no covenant, no law, no land). At the end it describes his restoration in the one idiom the prophets reserve for the nation’s homecoming. Whatever the book thinks it is doing with this man, it does not think he is a private case.

5. The narrator supplies at the end the agent he withheld at the beginning — and his construction bears on the crux five verses above it.

v1.1 — claim audit 6 (16 Nov 2026), ruling A11: the second half is reframed with the companion dig’s Headline 5. The first half is untouched and confirmed: נָחַם + רָעָה in exactly two verses of Job, with the agent withheld at 2:11 (a participle) and named at 42:11 (a finite verb, in narration). The second half changes. The ordered phrase עַל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר does stand in exactly four verses, every one governed by נָחַם — the cleanest datum in the audit. But the companion dig’s gloss is withdrawn. נָחַם followed by עַל occurs in 39 verses of the Hebrew Bible and the object of עַל is always a decreed action or outcome, or a person mourned — never a substance or a place. So “relent concerning dust and ashes” has no clean parallel either, and what the construction supports is the self-designation reading: Job is consoled concerning himself, who is dust and ashes — as Gen 18:27, Job 30:19 and Swete at both verses take the phrase. And first-person נִחַמְתִּי means “I repented” at Jer 31:19, so the verb’s sense alone settles nothing. 42:11 remains the nearest internal parallel; what it supports is narrower than claimed.

v1.2 — Logos round, 16 Nov 2026 [revised: logos-research]. The Logos round upgraded the companion dig’s reading to moderate–high. Boda independently makes the object-type argument — נחם + עַל “emphasize actions, persons, and emotional states, not substances” — and Pohl adds that the Masoretic accentuation marks עַל as the object for both verbs. But Muenchow notes the Old Greek may render a different Hebrew Vorlage, so the appeal to Swete weakens. 42:11’s evidential value is unchanged: it remains the book’s own nearest instance of the construction. [T]. Confidence: high for the data; and this materially strengthens the companion dig’s Headline 5.

נָחַם (“comfort”) + רָעָה (“evil”) co-occur in exactly 2 verses of Job — 2:11 and 42:11 — the two visits that frame the poem.

  • 2:11 — the friends come וַיָּנֻדוּ … לְנַחֲמוֹ, to console and comfort him, עַל כָּל־הָרָעָה הַזֹּאת הַבָּאָה עָלָיו — “concerning all this evil that had come upon him.” A participle. No agent.
  • 42:11 — the family come, וַיָּנֻדוּ … וַיְנַחֲמוּ אֹתוֹ, עַל כָּל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר־הֵבִיא יְהוָה עָלָיו — “concerning all the evil that the LORD brought upon him.” A finite verb. The agent named.

Forty chapters of argument about who did this, and the narrator answers it in a subordinate clause in the second-to-last paragraph — not in God’s mouth, where it could be argued with, but in his own, where it cannot.

Then the construction itself. The ordered phrase עַל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר (“concerning the evil that…”) stands in exactly 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible — Exod 32:14; Jer 18:8; Ezek 14:22; Jonah 3:10 — and in every one of the four it is governed by נָחַם. Job 42:11 is a fifth instance of the same construction with כָּל intervening (עַל כָּל־הָרָעָה, which stands in 2 verses: Deut 31:18 and Job 42:11).

So נָחַם + עַל + הָרָעָה is a fixed idiom, attested five times, and it always means “relent / be consoled concerning” — never “repent in a place.” The companion dig argued exactly that about 42:6 from four independent data: the objectless אֶמְאַס (7:16; 36:5), the four-verse distribution of וְנִחַמְתִּי, the book’s ash-idiom בְּתוֹךְ at 2:8, and Swete’s predicate reading. The epilogue now supplies a fifth and nearer datum: the book’s own only other נָחַם + עַל, five verses later, means “console concerning”. [S: prior dig] for the claim, [T] for this addition.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level [S: overview]. The overview’s purpose statement: the book exists to dismantle the retribution calculus as a diagnostic instrument while leaving God’s justice intact, and to vindicate lament addressed to God over orthodoxy spoken about God against a sufferer.

Passage level [T] / [I]. The epilogue does four things, in order, and the order is the argument: it delivers a verdict on the speaking (42:7–8), it makes the condemned men dependent on the man they condemned (42:8–9), it turns Job’s fortunes at the moment he prays for them (42:10), and it restores him in patriarchal and national-restoration idiom (42:11–17). Confidence: high.

What it conspicuously does not do is explain. Job is never told the prologue. The reader knows about the Accuser, the wager and the word חִנָּם (“without cause”, 1:9; 2:3); Job dies without them. The book gives the reader the heavenly scene and withholds it from the sufferer, which is the structural form of its warning: you are not in a position to tell another person what their suffering is for. [I] Confidence: high.

A purpose note on the shape. Nothing in 42:7–17 is addressed to Job. God speaks to Eliphaz; the narrator reports. After 42:6 Job never speaks again — the last words in his mouth are his retraction, and eleven verses of narration follow. For a book that is 95 % speech, ending in silent narration is a structural statement. [T] for the distribution of speech, [I] for the significance.

2. Context

Immediately before: Job’s reply from the whirlwind (42:1–6), the crux. Immediately after: nothing — the book ends.

Positional Necessity Check (Phase 0.6). Two required questions.

(a) What has the preceding movement predicted, required or set up that this passage provides? Four debts, all paid here:

  1. The friends’ status is unresolved. They stopped speaking at chapter 25 and were never answered. Elihu was never answered either. The dialogue has a live juridical question — who spoke rightly? — that no speech in 3–41 settles, because God’s speeches address Job alone and never mention the three. 42:7 settles it in one sentence. [T]
  2. Job’s prediction at 13:10 is unfulfilled. See Headline 1. [T]
  3. The Accuser’s thesis is untested in the one way that counts. The wager was whether Job serves God for nothing (1:9). Job has served God through total loss. But the thesis can only be disproved by restoration after the fact — if God never restores, the Accuser can say the fear was unrewarded but cannot be shown wrong; if God restores before Job’s integrity holds, the Accuser is right. So the restoration has to come last, and it has to come after 42:6. This is the strongest answer to “why does the epilogue exist?” [I] Confidence: moderate–high.
  4. The prologue’s sacrifice is unmatched. Job’s priestly act at 1:5 has no counterpart. 42:8 supplies it. [T] — see Tool 10.

(b) Why here and not elsewhere? Because the verdict on the friends must follow the speeches that make Job’s speech assessable, and the restoration must follow the verdict, and the prayer must come between them. Move any of the three and the book argues something else. Put the restoration before the verdict and the friends are vindicated; put the verdict without the prayer and the friends are destroyed (“I would have destroyed you”, Swete 42:8); put the prayer before the verdict and it is a strategy rather than obedience. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

The divine name. יְהוָה stands in 23 verses of Job: twelve in the prologue (1:6–2:7), one in the whole dialogue (12:9, inside the four-word phrase shared with Isa 41:20), five in the speech-frames (38:1; 40:1, 3, 6; 42:1), and five in the epilogue — 42:7, 9, 10, 11, 12, eight occurrences in all. The dialogue speaks of אֵל, אֱלוֹהַ and שַׁדַּי; the frame speaks of יְהוָה. The epilogue restores the prologue’s name and uses no other: אֱלֹהִים, שַׁדַּי and אֵל are absent from 42:7–17 entirely. [T] Confidence: high.

And the last five verses name no god at all. 42:13–17 — children, names, beauty, inheritance, years, death — contains no divine name and no divine act. The book that began in the heavenly council ends with a man, his daughters and a number of years. [T]

3. Structure

Four movements (above), with a clean chiastic pressure around the hinge:

A   42:7–9   God speaks; the friends act; God lifts Job's face
      B   42:10   THE TURN: "when he prayed for his friend"
A'  42:11–12  The family come; God blesses
        C   42:13–17  Coda: no divine name, no speech

The Masoretic paragraphing agrees with the movements and is worth reporting carefully. The WLC marks a setumah (ס) after 42:11 and after 42:15, and the book’s final petuchah-class marker after 42:17. So the received paragraphing cuts 42:7–11 / 42:12–15 / 42:16–17 — grouping the blessing with the children rather than with the visit. That is a different division from the one the vocabulary suggests, and it is reception, not composition: the markers are scribal, they are [unchecked — apparatus spread], and they are reported here because a reader using a Hebrew Bible will see them. [T] as the WLC prints it. Confidence: high that the markers are there; the division they imply is not authorially binding.

A structural observation with teeth. 42:7–10 is four verses and contains every theologically load-bearing act in the unit: the verdict, the sacrifice, the intercession, the acceptance, the turn. 42:11–17 is seven verses and contains no act of God but one (42:12a). Three-fifths of the epilogue is a list. Preaching the epilogue as “and then Job got everything back” inverts the proportions: the Hebrew spends its weight on the speaking and the praying, and its inventory on the livestock. [T] / [I]

4. Linking Words

Sparse and load-bearing.

  • וְעַתָּה (“now therefore”, 42:8) — the verdict’s hinge into command. Not a new topic: the imperative is the consequence of 42:7. [T]
  • כִּי … כִּי (42:8) — two causal clauses bracketing the command, the second repeating the first verbatim: because you have not spoken rightly … because you have not spoken rightly. See Tool 10.
  • כִּי אִם (42:8) — the construction that carries the whole theology of the verse, and it is hard. Literally “for if … His face I will lift”; functionally either a strong affirmation (“for I will surely accept him”) or a conditional on which the friends’ escape hangs. Both NASB95 and ESV take it as affirmation. See Difficult Verses. [T] for the construction, interpretation moderate.
  • בְּהִתְפַּלְלוֹ (42:10) — an infinitive construct with בְּ, which is temporal or circumstantial, not causal: “when / as he prayed”, not “because he prayed”. The syntax will not carry a transactional reading, which is exactly what the pulpit most often puts on it. [T] Confidence: high. See Pitfall 2.
  • אַחֲרֵי־זֹאת (42:16) — “after this”, the only temporal marker in the coda, and it marks off Job’s remaining 140 years from the whole narrated action. [T]

5. Parallels

Prose, so parallelism is not the governing form; but two verses are shaped.

42:7 and 42:8 end with the identical clause, and the repetition is the structural device of the movement (Tool 10). 42:17 is a two-word summary with a tight pair: זָקֵן (“old”) / וּשְׂבַע יָמִים (“and full of days”). The same pair ends Isaac’s life at Gen 35:29 and David’s at 1 Chr 23:1 — see Tool 11.

6. Narrator’s Comment

This is the narrator’s unit, and it carries the book’s most consequential narratorial statement. [T] Confidence: high.

The narrator of Job intervenes rarely and devastatingly: 1:22 and 2:10 (“in all this Job did not sin with his lips”), and here. The epilogue’s comments:

  1. “the LORD lifted up the face of Job” (42:9) — the narrator ratifies the acceptance, rather than leaving it as God’s promise.
  2. “all the evil that the LORD brought upon him” (42:11) — Headline 5. This is the book’s answer to its own causation question, and it is placed in narration, where no character can dispute it. The Accuser acted, but under permission (1:12; 2:6), and the narrator’s final attribution names the LORD.
  3. “and the LORD blessed Job’s latter end” (42:12) — the narrator, not a character, makes the retribution-shaped claim; which is why it cannot be read as the friends being right. The same voice that says the LORD brought the evil says the LORD gave the blessing.

Note what the narrator does not say. He does not say Job was wrong. He does not say the friends repented. He does not say Job was told anything. And he never mentions the Accuser again — see Tool 10.

(b) נְכוֹנָה — the word the verdict turns on. [T] Confidence: high for the distribution.

A niphal participle of כּוּן, whose lemma stands in 15 verses of Job. But the written form נְכוֹנָה appears, on a plain ordered search of the whole Hebrew Bible, in 4 verses: 1 Kgs 2:46; Ps 5:10 [Eng 5:9]; Job 42:7; Job 42:8. (A skeletal pass adds Gen 37:21, Ezek 16:7 and Prov 19:29, which are three different words sharing the consonantal skeleton — a homograph trap, recorded in the Declaration.)

Of the four, 1 Kgs 2:46 means “established” (“the kingdom was established in Solomon’s hand”). Only Ps 5:10 and Job 42:7–8 use it absolutely, as the content of speech. And Ps 5:10 is about a mouth that has none: אֵין בְּפִיהוּ נְכוֹנָה, “there is nothing reliable in his mouth”, of the deceitful and bloodthirsty.

So God’s charge against the friends is laid in the one word the Psalter uses for a mouth with nothing trustworthy in it. The lexical observation is solid; the allusion claim is one word, so it stays low. Confidence: high for the distribution, low–moderate as an allusion. Queued.

(c) נַחֲלָה — the word that changes hands. [T] / [I]. Confidence: high for the distribution, moderate–high for the reading.

נַחֲלָה (“inheritance, portion”) stands in exactly 4 verses of Job: 20:29, 27:13, 31:2, 42:15. The first three are a single idea:

  • 20:29 (Zophar) — “this is the portion of a wicked man from God”
  • 27:13 (Job, quoting Zophar) — “this is the portion of a wicked man with God”
  • 31:2 (Job) — “what is the portion from the Almighty above?”

So in Job’s first three uses, נַחֲלָה is what God doles out to the wicked — a dreadful word, the vocabulary of the retribution scheme at its harshest. Its fourth and last use is the inheritance Job hands to his three daughters. The word is taken out of the hands of the men arguing about what God allots and put into the hands of a father dividing property among his girls. A deliberate retirement of a loaded word.

(d) חָרָה + אַף — where the anger goes. [T]. Confidence: high.

The pair stands in exactly 5 verses of Job: 19:11, 32:2, 32:3, 32:5, 42:7. Three are Elihu’s anger (32:2, 3, 5 — the word that introduces him). The remaining two are the only places the book speaks of God’s anger with this idiom:

  • 19:11 — Job: “He has kindled His anger against me” (וַיַּחַר עָלַי אַפּוֹ)
  • 42:7 — God: “My anger is kindled against you” (חָרָה אַפִּי בְךָ), to Eliphaz

Job’s one claim about the divine anger is that it is aimed at him. God’s one statement about it aims it at the friends. The book’s final word on God’s wrath is a redirection. Preachers who use the storm speeches to crush the questioner have the target wrong, and the vocabulary says so.

(e) עֶבֶד — the title, and when it is given. [T]. Confidence: high. עֶבֶד stands in 10 verses of Job, 12 occurrences: 1:8; 2:3; 3:19; 4:18; 7:2; 19:16; 31:13; 40:28 [Eng 41:4]; 42:7; 42:8. Of these, five occurrences in four verses are God calling Job “My servant” — 1:8; 2:3; 42:7; 42:8 (×3). Two in the prologue, three in a single verse of the epilogue. Nobody in the dialogue ever calls Job God’s servant, and Job never claims it. The title is used only by God, and only outside the poem.

And the placement: the only other עֶבֶד in either divine speech is 41:4 [Heb 40:28], “will you take him as a servant forever?” — of Leviathan. The question “will you make a servant of the untameable?” is followed, four verses later, by God naming His own servant three times in one sentence. [T] for the distribution — the juxtaposition was first noted in the companion dig and is confirmed here. [S: prior dig] for the observation, [T] for the data.

So the English plural is a versional reading against the Masoretic singular, and the apparatus names it as a proposal. Nothing doctrinal hangs on it, but it is a clean example of the versions quietly shaping an English text, and the singular is marginally the harder and better-attested Hebrew.

(d) 42:8 — “according to your folly”. The Hebrew is לְבִלְתִּי עֲשׂוֹת עִמָּכֶם נְבָלָה, literally “so as not to do folly with you” — with no possessive. Both English versions supply one (“according to your folly” / “with you according to your folly”), making the folly theirs. The Hebrew could as easily mean God refraining from doing something outrageous to them. Swete resolves it differently again: “for if not for his sake, I would have destroyed you”. 9. Tone and Feel

Four registers in eleven verses, and the shift between the first and the last is the point. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

42:7–8 is forensic and hot. “My anger is kindled against you” opens; the charge is stated, the remedy commanded, and the charge restated verbatim as the sentence closes. There is no greeting, no preamble and no name for the offence beyond the speaking. God addresses Eliphaz in the second person singular and the other two obliquely (“your two friends”), which is how a court addresses a principal. [T]

42:9–10 is terse to the point of bluntness. Four clauses: they went, they did, the LORD lifted Job’s face, the LORD turned his fortunes. No dialogue, no reaction, no repentance recorded. The three men who spoke 450 verses say nothing at all.

42:11 is warm, and it is the only warmth in the book. People eat bread in Job’s house. They shake their heads with him. They give him presents — a piece of money and a gold ring each. Nobody argues. After 39 chapters in which human speech is the instrument of injury, the restoration of Job’s social world is described without a single word being spoken in it.

42:13–17 is a register the book has not used: inventory and genealogy. Numbers, three names, a note about beauty, a legal provision, a span of years, a death. The Hebrew of 42:17 is five words. The book does not end on a crescendo; it ends the way Genesis ends a patriarch.

One absence belongs in the tone section. There is no scene of Job and God. Job saw Him (42:5) and is not shown seeing Him again; he is not told he was right; he receives no explanation and no apology. The warmth of 42:11 is horizontal. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

10. Repetition

The densest tool in the unit.

(a) The verdict is stated twice in identical words. [T] Confidence: high. The clause כִּי לֹא דִבַּרְתֶּם אֵלַי נְכוֹנָה כְּעַבְדִּי אִיּוֹב closes both 42:7 and 42:8. An ordered phrase search for לֹא דִבַּרְתֶּם אֵלַי נְכוֹנָה returns Job 42:7 and Job 42:8, and nothing else in the Hebrew Bible. Fourteen words of God’s speech in this unit are a single sentence said twice. In a book where the friends spoke for twenty-three chapters, God’s assessment of them is one sentence, and He repeats it rather than extending it.

(b) “My servant Job” three times in one verse. [T] 42:8 has עַבְדִּי אִיּוֹב, וְאִיּוֹב עַבְדִּי, כְּעַבְדִּי אִיּוֹב — with the elements reversed in the middle instance. Three namings in twenty-six words, immediately after the single naming in 42:7. See Tool 7(e).

(c) The burnt offering frames the book. [T] Confidence: high. עוֹלָה stands in exactly 2 verses of Job — 1:5 and 42:8.

  • 1:5 — Job rose early and offered burnt offerings for his children, “for Job said, perhaps my sons have sinned and blessed God in their hearts.”
  • 42:8 — the friends offer a burnt offering for themselves, and Job prays for them, because they certainly have sinned, and with their mouths.

The man who sacrificed against his children’s possible silent sin ends by interceding for his friends’ actual spoken sin. And the sacrifice at the end is not his own: they bring it, he prays. The priestly role he exercised privately in verse 5 of the book is exercised publicly in its last movement, and the people who need it are the ones who came to correct him.   • 7:13 — Job hopes his bed will comfort him

(f) What is never repeated: the Accuser. [T] Confidence: high. שָׂטָן stands in 14 occurrences across 11 verses of Job, every one of them in 1:6–2:7. The narrator who names the LORD as the agent of the evil (42:11) had the option of naming the Accuser and does not take it. The book introduces a figure to make its question possible and then drops him, and the epilogue is where the dropping becomes conspicuous, because this is the one place a tidy ending could have been supplied.

Six elements in the same order: נחם + עַל + (כָּל־)הָרָעָה + אֲשֶׁר + the hiphil of בּוֹא + עַל + the sufferer. A co-occurrence of the three lemmas נָחַם, רָעָה and בּוֹא returns exactly 4 verses in the whole Hebrew Bible — Ezek 14:22; Job 2:11; Job 42:11; Lam 1:21 — and of those, only Ezek 14:22 and Job 42:11 carry the full construction with אֲשֶׁר + a finite hiphil.
  • Ezek 14:23 — וִידַעְתֶּם כִּי לֹא חִנָּם עָשִׂיתִי, “you will know that not without cause have I done all that I have done in her.” חִנָּם is the Accuser’s word (1:9) and God’s admission (2:3); it stands in exactly four verses of Job (1:9; 2:3; 9:17; 22:6 — overview confirmed) and in Ezekiel at 6:10 and 14:23. Job was afflicted חִנָּם on God’s own word; Jerusalem, says Ezekiel, was not.

So Ezek 14 names Job, reproduces the construction of Job 42:11, and uses the word that defines Job’s case to deny it of Jerusalem — within ten verses. Direction of dependence: Ezekiel names the man, so Ezekiel knows the figure, which makes Ezekiel the likelier borrower here; but a shared idiom is not a quotation, and חִנָּם on its own is a common word carrying little weight. Rated moderate–high as a cluster; the חִנָּם element alone is low. [S: audit 5] settles the related wording question — see Corrections, where audit 5’s “only these two” is corrected.

(ii) The patriarchal death-formula. [T] Confidence: high — and it corrects the overview.

The overview’s row reads: 42:17 “old and full of days”, Isaac’s formula exactly (Gen 35:29), with Gen 25:8 also cited, rated high. That is right about Isaac and incomplete. The ordered phrase זָקֵן וּשְׂבַע יָמִים stands, on plain and skeletal passes alike, in exactly 3 verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 35:29 (Isaac), Job 42:17, and 1 Chr 23:1 (David). Abraham’s Gen 25:8 has זָקֵן וְשָׂבֵעַ — the same formula without יָמִים.

So Job dies in the words used of exactly two other people in Scripture: the second patriarch and the king. For a book that has gone to lengths to put its hero outside Israel, the death-notice is a deliberate naturalisation. The overview’s row should add 1 Chr 23:1. See Corrections for the method point, which matters: the lemma co-occurrence for this phrase misses 1 Chr 23:1 altogether.

(iii) קְשִׂיטָה — the patriarchal coin. [T] Confidence: high; overview confirmed. The word stands in exactly 3 verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 33:19 (Jacob buys the plot at Shechem), Josh 24:32 (Joseph is buried in it), Job 42:11. An archaic unit of weight, used in the Hebrew Bible only of a patriarchal land-purchase, its sequel, and Job’s gifts. Swete does not recognise it: it reads ἀμνάδα μίαν, “one ewe lamb”. The money in Job’s last scene is money nobody in Israel’s monarchy or exile would have handled. Together with (ii), the epilogue’s two rarest nouns are both patriarchal-era.

The נֶזֶם (“ring”) is a different matter: 14 verses canon-wide, including Gen 24:47 and Gen 35:4 (patriarchal) but also Exod 32:2–3 (the golden calf) and Judg 8:24–26 (Gideon’s ephod), where gold rings become an idol. That resonance is almost certainly accidental — the word is common and the contexts unrelated — and is recorded here only so that nobody builds on it. Confidence: low; not a finding.

(iv) שׁוּב + שְׁבוּת — the exile formula. Headline 4. [T] for the 26-verse distribution; [I] for the force. The overview rates this “high (verbal); function [I]” and the function can now be stated more sharply: not merely that Job’s restoration borrows exile vocabulary, but that this is the formula’s only application in the Hebrew Bible to one named individual.

Move 3 (OT→OT) and the triad. No formula citation occurs in this unit — Job never says “as it is written,” and the epilogue quotes nothing. The OT Citation Triad is therefore N/A in its strict form, and (i)–(iv) are handled as allusions with Move 1 (source context) and, where the material allows, Move 2 (the book’s own usage). Reason for N/A stated as the method requires.

The New Testament. Jas 5:10–11 is the only New Testament text that refers to the epilogue, and it makes a distinction the English proverb destroys. [T] from SBLGNT. Confidence: high.

Jas 5:10 — ὑπόδειγμα λάβετε … τῆς μακροθυμίας τοὺς προφήτας — “take as an example … the patience of the prophets” Jas 5:11 — τὴν ὑπομονὴν Ἰὼβ ἠκούσατε, καὶ τὸ τέλος κυρίου εἴδετε — “you have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the outcome of the Lord”

In consecutive verses James assigns μακροθυμία (“patience, long-suffering”) to the prophets and ὑπομονή (“endurance, holding up under”) to Job. The distinction is deliberate and it is the opposite of the proverb: “the patience of Job” attributes to Job the very word James withheld from him and gave to somebody else. ὑπομονή is what a man does under a weight — which is what chapters 3–31 are. This is the hardest available warrant for Pitfall 1, and it is a single verse a congregation can check.

And τὸ τέλος κυρίου — “the Lord’s outcome”, not “Job’s end” — is the Greek counterpart of אַחֲרִית at 42:12. The overview rates this pairing [I]; it stands, and Headline 3 strengthens it, since אַחֲרִית is one of only two in the book.

12. Genre

Prose narrative closing a poetic book — and the generic shift is itself a finding. [T] / [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

The book runs: prose frame (1–2) → poetry (3:1–42:6) → prose frame (42:7–17). The epilogue belongs with the prologue, and the vocabulary proves it rather than merely matching it: עוֹלָה, שָׂטָן’s absence, יְהוָה exclusively, “My servant Job”, the livestock inventory, the seven-and-three. Tool 7’s table is genre evidence — the epilogue introduces the words a narrative needs (pray, double, folly, beautiful, ring, money) and none of the words a lament needs.

Within prose narrative, 42:13–17 shifts again, into the patriarchal death-notice: age at death, generations seen, the formula “old and full of days”, and in 42:14 a naming scene. Genesis ends its patriarchs this way. The genre of the book’s last five verses is Genesis. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

One genre caution. The epilogue is not a wisdom conclusion. It states no maxim, draws no moral and offers no “therefore”. Ecclesiastes ends with a verdict (12:13–14); Proverbs ends with a poem of praise; Job ends with a death-notice. Any sermon that supplies the “therefore” the text withholds is supplying a genre the book declined. [I] Confidence: moderate.

13. Copycat

Partial N/A, and the reason is the point. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

The tool asks what in the passage is for imitation. In the epilogue almost nothing is, and the one thing that is, is the thing nobody preaches.

  • The restoration is not imitable — it is reported, not commanded, and no condition is attached to it that a hearer could meet. Reading it as a template (“endure and God will double it”) is Pitfall 2.
  • The friends’ sacrifice is not imitable — it is a specific command to three named men, in a sacrificial economy the hearer does not inhabit.
  • The daughters’ inheritance is not a precedent — Job is not under the law, the text draws no conclusion from it, and no later text appeals to it. See Pitfall 6.
  • Job’s intercession for the men who wronged him is imitable, is commanded nowhere in the passage, and is the one thing in the unit the New Testament asks of its readers directly (Matt 5:44; Luke 6:28). The epilogue’s imitable act is the one it does not tell anyone to imitate.

14. Bible Timeline

Where this sits. [S: overview] for the book-level placement, [T] for the data in the text. The epilogue supplies the book’s only chronological data: 140 further years, four generations seen, death “old and full of days”.

The 140 is a doubling of the conventional span — “the days of our years are seventy” (Ps 90:10) — which puts Job’s years in the same arithmetic as his livestock. [I] Confidence: moderate (the text does not do the sum, and a long patriarchal life needs no psalm to explain it).

Swete does the sum differently and openly. Its 42:16 reads: “Job lived after the plague 170 years, and all the years he lived were 240.” The Greek supplies a pre-affliction age of seventy that the Hebrew never gives — and then has Job live, in total, less than Abraham (175) and more than Joseph (110). An ancient reader filling a silence; see Textual Variants.

Redemptive-historical placement [S: overview]. Nothing in the epilogue dates the book. The patriarchal furniture — the קְשִׂיטָה, the death-formula, the household sacrifice with no priest and no tabernacle, the wealth reckoned in livestock — places the story in the patriarchal era, as the overview says, and says nothing about when the book was composed. The distinction is the whole of Tool 14’s discipline here: the epilogue’s setting is Genesis; its date of writing is unknown, and this report makes no claim about it.

15. Who Am I in This Story?

The honest answer is the friends, and the text makes it hard to be anyone else. [I] Confidence: moderate–high.

The reader of a sermon on Job identifies with Job — the sufferer, the vindicated, the one whose protest God approves. But the epilogue gives the reader almost nothing to do as Job. Job says nothing, does one thing (prays), and receives. The people with agency in 42:7–9 are the three men who were wrong, and what they do is take the sacrifice, go to the man they have been lecturing, and stand there while he prays for them.

Three other positions, with the text’s own warrant:

  • The family of 42:11 — who come late, bring food and a small gift, shake their heads, and explain nothing. The only successful comforters in the book. [T]
  • The daughters — named, beautiful, and given an inheritance; the only characters in the book whose names we are told and whose words we never hear. [T]
  • The reader who knows the prologue — the position the book constructs and the one Pitfall 3 is about. Only the reader knows about the Accuser; Job never does. The knowledge is given precisely so that it will not be used on anyone else. [I] Confidence: high.

16. So What?

Raw material for /point-purpose, not a Teaching Point. Five lines the exegesis will support, each with its warrant:

  1. God’s verdict falls on the speaking. Twice, verbatim, and on nothing else: not on the friends’ motives, their travel, their silence, or their theology in the abstract — on what they said to Him about a suffering man. [T]
  2. The man who was crushed is made the means of his accusers’ rescue, and he prays before anything is given back. The turn is at the prayer (42:10), the syntax is circumstantial and not causal, and the prayer is for the people who hurt him. [T] for the data, [I] for the shape.
  3. The narrator says the LORD brought it, and says it in the same breath as the comfort. No dualism, no “the devil did it”, no explanation. [T]
  4. The restoration is grace after vindication, not payment for endurance — and the arithmetic proves it, because the animals double and the children do not. [T]
  5. Nobody gets an explanation, and the book is content. Job dies not knowing what the reader knows, and the last five verses name no god at all. [T] / [I]

Extensions

Original Language Observations

Beyond Tool 7, four grammatical points bear on preaching.

(a) בְּהִתְפַּלְלוֹ (42:10) will not carry a transaction. [T] Confidence: high. An infinitive construct of פָּלַל (hithpael) with the preposition בְּ and a pronominal suffix. בְּ + infinitive construct is temporal or circumstantial — “when / as / while he prayed.” Hebrew has causal constructions available (יַעַן, כִּי, עֵקֶב, בַּעֲבוּר) and the narrator used none of them. The text puts the restoration and the prayer in the same moment; it does not make the one the price of the other. The English “when he prayed” is right; the sermon’s “because he prayed” is a step the grammar does not take. The most frequently over-read syntax in the unit.

(b) The hithpael of פָּלַל is the only prayer-verb in the book, and its two occurrences are both intercession for others. [T] Confidence: high. 42:8 (יִתְפַּלֵּל עֲלֵיכֶם, “he will pray for you”) and 42:10 (בְּעַד רֵעֵהוּ, “on behalf of his friend”). Job addresses God constantly in 3–31 — the book is full of second-person speech to God — but the lexeme for prayer never appears until Job prays for someone else. The distribution is a fact; the inference that the book is defining prayer by it is mine. Confidence: high for the distribution, moderate for the inference.

(c) כִּי אִם־פָּנָיו אֶשָּׂא (42:8) is genuinely ambiguous, and the versions split on it. [T] The particle chain כִּי אִם can introduce (i) a strong affirmation after an implied oath — “for I will surely accept him” — or (ii) a condition — “for if I accept him…”. NASB95 and ESV both take (i). Swete takes neither and rewrites: “for if not for his sake, I would have destroyed you.” Nothing in the morphology settles it. See Difficult Verses. Confidence: moderate for (i); the ambiguity is high-confidence.

(d) שִׁבְעָנָה (42:13) is a written form found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. [T] Confidence: high for the uniqueness. An ordered phrase search on the plain pass returns Job 42:13 and nothing else (the skeletal pass returns Gen 24:37, Ps 90:14, Ps 123:3 and Job 9:18, all different words with the same consonantal skeleton). The expected form is שִׁבְעָה. The anomalous -ānā ending is what has allowed some ancient and modern readers to take it as a dual, “twice seven” — fourteen sons. Swete reads plain ἑπτά. The overview flags the question and its judgement stands: the WLC’s text as it stands gives the same number as 1:2, and Headline-grade weight must not be put on an ambiguous morpheme. See Textual Variants.

Textual Variants

Five apparatus findings, all with the witness named; one of them is the most consequential variant in the book for preaching. Every item is [unchecked — apparatus spread] and capped at moderate: the corpus holds one Hebrew manuscript tradition and can verify wording but can never report how widely a reading is attested. “mlt Mss” is BHS’s own claim about spread, not something this run has tested. None of these carries a Headline.

(a) 42:7 and 42:8 — כְּעַבְדִּי or בְּעַבְדִּי? The variant that decides whether the book contradicts itself. [T] as BHS prints it. Confidence: MT reading preferred at moderate; the exegetical stake is high.

BHS records at 42:7ᵃ: mlt Mss בְּע׳ cf 8ᵇ, and at 42:8ᵇ: mlt Mss בְּע׳ cf 7ᵃ — that is, many manuscripts read the preposition בְּ where the printed text has כְּ.

  • MT (כְּ): “you have not spoken of Me what is right as My servant Job has.” → God endorses Job’s speech.
  • Variant (בְּ): “you have not spoken of Me what is right concerning My servant Job.” → God condemns the friends’ speech about Job, and says nothing about the rightness of Job’s own.

And the Old Greek agrees with the variant at 42:8: οὐ γὰρ ἐλαλήσατε κατὰ τοῦ θεράποντός μου Ἰὼβ ἀληθές — “you have not spoken what is true against my servant Job.”

The stake is the central difficulty of the whole book (see Difficult Verses): on the MT, God commends as “right” the speech He has just rebuked as “without knowledge” (38:2; 42:3). On the variant, the contradiction evaporates. That is precisely why the MT should be preferred — the harder reading, and the one the ancient translator had reason to smooth — but a preacher should know that the tidy solution has manuscript support and the Greek behind it, and should not present the MT reading as unanimous. Preferred at moderate, disclosed at the Difficult Verse, barred from the overview until the spread is checked in a critical edition.

(b) 42:10 — the ketiv/qere in the restoration formula. [T] as BHS prints Leningrad. BHS 42:10ᵃ: Qere שְׁבוּת, Ketiv שְׁבִית. Both are forms of the same noun and both yield “restore the fortunes”; the variation is dialectal or orthographic and nothing turns on it. Headline 4’s count was run on the lemma and survives either way. Reception, not composition.

(c) 42:10 — the versions read “friends”, the MT reads “friend”. [T] BHS 42:10ᵇ: Vrs pl, prp רֵעָיו. Reported under Tool 8(c). The English plural follows the versions; the singular is the Masoretic text.

(d) 42:11 and 42:16 — two further ketivs, and one versional plus. [T] BHS 42:16ᵃ: Qere וַיִּרְאֶה, Ketiv וַיַּרְא — both “and he saw”, inert. The WLC also carries an unpointed אחיתיו at 42:11 (“his sisters”), orthographic. And BHS 42:11ᵃ records one manuscript and the Greek adding וְיִּשְׁתּוּ, “and they drank” — which is exactly what Swete has (φαγόντες δὲ καὶ πιόντες). A harmonising plus; the MT’s “they ate bread with him” is the shorter reading.

(e) BHS records no variant at 42:13. Worth stating because the שִׁבְעָנָה question is often assumed to be textual. It is not: the consonants are undisputed and the question is morphological. The same was true of 42:6 in the companion dig. Two cruxes in one chapter, neither of them a textual problem.

Hebrew/Greek divergence — the triage. Every divergence is assigned a category.

Category 1 — translation loss (reported under Tools 7–10, not as splits): the נְכוֹנָה / ἀληθές shift from “what is right” to “what is true”; the loss of the חָרָה אַף idiom; the loss of the נָשָׂא פָּנִים idiom at 42:9 (Swete: “he released their sin because of Job”); קְשִׂיטָה rendered “a ewe lamb”; the loss of the אַחֲרִית / רֵאשִׁית pair’s link to 8:7.

Category 2 — substantive divergence (each with evidence, a stated position, and a flag; none resolved by a blanket preference for either text):

  1. 42:7 — Swete adds a verdict the Hebrew does not have: Ἥμαρτες σὺ καὶ οἱ φίλοι σου, “you have sinned, you and your friends.” The Hebrew says only that God’s anger is kindled and that they spoke wrongly. Position: the Greek is interpreting, and interpreting in the direction the Hebrew’s reticence invites. [T] moderate–high.
  2. 42:8 — Swete’s “against my servant Job” — see (a). Position: a real variant with manuscript support in Hebrew too; MT preferred at moderate.
  3. 42:8 — Swete’s “I would have destroyed you” (ἀπώλεσα ἂν ὑμᾶς) for the Hebrew’s “so as not to do folly with you”. Position: the Greek resolves the Hebrew’s obscure נְבָלָה clause by substituting an explicit threat. [T] moderate–high.
  4. 42:9–10 — Swete twice adds forgiveness language absent from the Hebrew: ἔλυσεν τὴν ἁμαρτίαν αὐτοῖς διὰ Ἰώβ, “he released their sin because of Job” (42:9), and ἀφῆκεν αὐτοῖς τὴν ἁμαρτίαν, “he forgave them the sin” (42:10). The Hebrew says God lifted Job’s face and restored Job’s fortunes; it never says the friends were forgiven. Position: substantive. The Greek supplies the theological outcome the Hebrew leaves as an inference — and, notably, supplies a doctrine of mediated forgiveness. [T] high that the divergence is there; moderate on its origin. This is the single most theologically loaded plus in the epilogue and it matters for the Christological reading below, because an interpreter reaching for “Job the mediator” is reaching for the Greek’s sentence, not the Hebrew’s.
  5. 42:10 — Swete has ηὔξησεν τὸν Ἰώβ, “the Lord increased Job”, for שָׁב אֶת־שְׁבִית, “restored the fortunes”. Position: the Greek does not render the exile formula at all. Headline 4 is a feature of the Hebrew, and a Greek reader could not see it. [T] high.
  6. 42:11 — Swete removes רָעָה: “they marvelled (ἐθαύμασαν) at all that the Lord brought upon him.” Position: substantive, and in the direction of piety. The Greek will not say the Lord brought evil, and will not say they comforted him about it — it has them amazed instead. [T] high. Headline 5’s narratorial statement exists only in the Hebrew.
  7. 42:16 — Swete’s 170 years after the affliction and 240 in total, against the Hebrew’s 140 after. Position: the Greek supplies a figure the Hebrew withholds. [T] high.
  8. 42:17 — the five-part addition. Treated below and under Original Audience Reception.

No **Translation-tradition split:** is declared, because no category-2 item here divides the ancient and the modern traditions on a rendering the moderns agree about; the divergences are between the Hebrew and the Old Greek, with NASB95 and ESV both following the Hebrew.

Category 3 — the New Testament arguing from the Greek: no instance in this unit. Jas 5:11 refers to the epilogue’s shape rather than its wording, and names Job rather than quoting him. The companion dig recorded the reverse case at Rom 11:35 / Job 41:11, where the New Testament follows the Hebrew against the Old Greek.

The addition at 42:17 — what it is, and what it is not. [T] for its content as Swete prints Codex Vaticanus; [I] for its significance. Confidence: high that it is there; high that it is reception.

Swete’s 42:17 continues past the Hebrew’s five words with five further sections:

  1. “And it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up.” γέγραπται δὲ αὐτὸν πάλιν ἀναστήσεσθαι μεθ᾽ ὧν ὁ κύριος ἀνίστησιν. An explicit resurrection notice, appended to the book, and introduced with “it is written” — a citation formula for a text we cannot identify.
  2. A note that the material is “translated from the Syriac book”, placing Job in Ausitis on the borders of Idumea and Arabia, and giving his former name as Jobab.
  3. A genealogy: an Arabian wife, a son Ennon, a father Zare “of the sons of Esau”, “so that he was fifth from Abraham.”
  4. The kings of Edom, reproducing Gen 36:31–35 — Balak son of Beor, then “Jobab who is called Job”, then Hasom of the Temanite country, then Hadad son of Barad.
  5. The three friends made kings: Eliphaz “of the sons of Esau, king of the Temanites”, Baldad “tyrant of the Sauchaeans”, Sophar “king of the Minaeans.”

Checked against the Hebrew of Gen 36: the identification rests on Gen 36:33, “Jobab son of Zerah of Bozrah”, and Gen 36:4, 10–11, where Eliphaz is Esau’s son and Teman is Eliphaz’s son. The addition has read Bozrah, a place, as the mother’s name (μητρὸς δὲ Βοσόρρας) — which shows it working from the Greek of Gen 36 rather than from a source with independent information. [T] Confidence: high.

What it is: the earliest surviving reading of the book, and it answers two questions the Hebrew refuses — where does Job belong? and is death the end for him? — by supplying a genealogy and a resurrection. What it is not: evidence about the Hebrew book. It stands outside the Hebrew entirely, it is self-described as an addition from another source, and it contains a demonstrable misreading of its own source text. It is reported here because an interpreter who finds Job’s placelessness unbearable is in very old company — and because the Hebrew’s refusal is thereby shown to be a feature, not a gap.

Historical and Cultural Background

Four items, kept to what a point actually hangs on.

(a) Seven bulls and seven rams (42:8). [T] for the parallels, [I] for the significance. The only sacrifice-quantity in the book. The same pair and number stands at Num 23:1, 29 (Balaam’s sevenfold altars before a word from God) and at 1 Chr 15:26 (seven bulls and seven rams when the ark is moved). The overview records the Balaam link at moderate and it stands: another non-Israelite seer, another sevenfold offering, another divine word. Confidence: moderate. What the background supplies is simply that this is a large, royal-scale offering, not a household one — three men bringing fourteen animals.

(b) A sacrifice with no priest, no altar named, and no sanctuary. [T] / [I] Confidence: moderate–high. The friends offer for themselves (בַּעַדְכֶם), and the mediating figure is Job, who prays. There is no priesthood in the book — as at 1:5, where Job sacrifices for his children. The arrangement is pre-Sinai household religion, and it is one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the patriarchal setting. It also means the epilogue cannot be read through the Levitical system; the man who stands between God and the guilty here is a layman and a Gentile.

(c) The קְשִׂיטָה and the נֶזֶם. [T] A weight of uncoined metal and a gold ring: wealth before currency. Coinage reaches the region in the Persian period; the epilogue’s gifts are reckoned the way Gen 33:19 reckons them. Confidence: high for the practice, and see Tool 11(iii) for the distribution.

(d) Daughters inheriting. [T] for the data, [I] for the force. Confidence: moderate. In the law, daughters inherit when there is no son (Num 27:1–11, Zelophehad’s daughters; the restriction at Num 36; the case worked out at Josh 17:3–6). Job has seven sons and gives his daughters an inheritance anyway, “among their brothers” (בְּתוֹךְ אֲחֵיהֶם). Whether that is extraordinary in its own cultural setting cannot be settled from this text — the book never says Job is under any law, and the narrator draws no conclusion — but against the law Israel did have, it is a provision the law did not require. What can be said: it is unusual enough that the narrator mentions it, and he mentions it next to the daughters’ names and their beauty, not next to the sons. See Pitfall 6 for what must not be built on it.

Original Audience Reception

What a first hearer would have noticed, and where that cuts against modern instincts. [I] throughout unless marked.

  1. The verdict first, and the money last. A modern reader races to 42:10 and the doubling. An ancient hearer of a lawsuit-shaped narrative would have been waiting for the verdict since chapter 3, and 42:7 delivers it in one sentence. The inventory is the consequence, and it is reported in the flat tone of a property record. Confidence: moderate–high.
  2. Three men standing in silence while the man they lectured prays for them. The social force of 42:8–9 is hard to overstate and the text spends no words on it: no apology is recorded, no reply, no reconciliation scene. They go, they do it, and the narrative moves on. Confidence: moderate–high.
  3. The family’s late arrival would have registered as a reproach. They come after the restoration (42:11, following 42:10), and “all who had known him before” come with them. The friends came in the ashes and made it worse; the relatives come when it is safe and get it right. The text does not comment. Confidence: moderate.
  4. The names would have been heard as what they are. קְצִיעָה is “cassia”, a spice (cf. the plural at Ps 45:9); פּוּךְ is eye-paint — the noun stands in 4 verses of the Hebrew Bible: 2 Kgs 9:30 (Jezebel painting her eyes), Isa 54:11, Jer 4:30 (Jerusalem painting hers), 1 Chr 29:2 — so “Horn of Eye-Paint” is a cosmetic jar. [T] for the distribution. The daughters are named for perfume and cosmetics, in the one verse of the book that uses a word for beauty. Two readings are available and the text does not choose: a father’s delight, or a wry note about where the restored household’s attention now goes. Confidence: high for the words, low–moderate for either reading. Note the method point: Job 42:14 does not appear in the lemma list for פּוּךְ, because the name is indexed as a proper noun.
  5. “Old and full of days” would have been heard as a patriarch’s epitaph — see Tool 11(ii). Confidence: high.
  6. The Old Greek shows a real ancient audience’s reception, and what it wanted was a genealogy and a resurrection. See the addition above. [T] Confidence: high.

Biblical-Theological Themes

Four, each held to what this unit contributes.

(a) Intercession. [T] / [I] Confidence: moderate–high. The epilogue is the Old Testament’s clearest narrative of an innocent sufferer interceding for the guilty and the guilty being spared on his account. The elements are all textual: God’s anger (42:7), a sacrifice the guilty must bring (42:8), a mediator who is not a priest, and God’s acceptance of the mediator’s person (42:8–9). The line runs to Abraham at Sodom (Gen 18:22–33), Moses after the calf (Exod 32:11–14, whose verse 14 is one of the four עַל־הָרָעָה אֲשֶׁר verses), Samuel (1 Sam 12:23), and the servant who “interceded for the transgressors” (Isa 53:12).

(b) Blessing, and who gives it. [T] Confidence: high. Headline 2. The theme the book opens by putting in question — does God bless because He is bought? — closes with God blessing after the price has been shown not to exist.

(c) Restoration and return. [T] / [I] Confidence: moderate–high. Headline 4. The epilogue describes one man’s restoration in the vocabulary of Israel’s return from exile, and it does so for a man outside the covenant. If the formula means anything here, it means that what God does for his people has a shape, and that shape is recognisable when He does it for anyone. Stated as an inference; the text does not comment.

(d) Comfort. [T] Confidence: high for the chain. Tool 10(e). The book’s comfort-word succeeds once, in its last occurrence, performed by people who explain nothing. The theme runs on to Isa 40:1 and 51:19 (where נוד + נחם meet again — one of only five verses in the Hebrew Bible where that pair stands, with Nah 3:7, Ps 69:21, Job 2:11 and Job 42:11) and to 2 Cor 1:3–4.

Christological Reading

Trajectory, a type reached through an established category, and a contrast. No typology is asserted that the text will not bear, and no Christophany. [S: overview] for the three-part frame, [T] / [I] for what this unit adds.

1. The type: the servant who is accepted, and on whose account others are spared. Confidence: moderate–high — the strongest single Christological datum in the book, and it is in this unit.

The category is the servant, established in Isaiah, and the overview rates the type moderate. This unit supplies its load-bearing elements, all textual:

  • God names Job “My servant” five times in two verses (42:7, 8 ×3, with 1:8 and 2:3 behind them) — the only mouth in the book that uses the title. [T]
  • Job was ruined חִנָּם, “without cause”, on God’s own admission (2:3). [T]
  • The guilty are spared on the servant’s account. God’s reason for not dealing with the three “according to folly” is פָּנָיו אֶשָּׂא, “I will lift his face” — the acceptance of a person, not a procedure. [T]
  • The servant prays for the men who wronged him, and that is the moment of his own restoration. [T]
  • The pattern of Isa 53:12, וְלַפֹּשְׁעִים יַפְגִּיעַ, “and he interceded for the transgressors”, is close. But the verbs differ — Isaiah’s is פָּגַע, Job’s is פָּלַל — so this is a pattern parallel, not a lexical link, and Isaiah is not quoted. The overview rates this [I] and that rating is correct; this dig confirms it rather than upgrading it.

The escalation test passes, and it should be stated as the limit rather than the triumph. Job’s prayer spares three named men from one specific consequence; Christ’s intercession saves a people from judgement. Job needs a sacrifice brought by the guilty themselves (42:8) and is not himself the offering. The epilogue has a mediator who prays and a sacrifice that is not him; the New Testament has a mediator who is the sacrifice. The type is real, the gap is wide, and the gap is the thing to preach.

A caution that the versions force. It is the Old Greek, not the Hebrew, that says the friends were forgiven and that it happened διὰ Ἰώβ, “because of Job” (Swete 42:9–10). The Hebrew says God lifted Job’s face and restored Job’s fortunes, and leaves the friends’ standing unstated. An interpreter who preaches “Job the mediator of forgiveness” is preaching Swete’s sentence. The Hebrew supports the shape of mediation and is reticent about its result — and that reticence is worth keeping, because it is what stops the type from doing Christ’s work. [T] Confidence: high that the divergence is there.

2. The trajectory: the one who was rebuked is the one who is vindicated. Confidence: moderate. The book’s longing is for a מוֹכִיחַ, an umpire who could lay a hand on both parties (9:33) — the word that recurs at 32:12 and 40:2 and whose verb stands at 13:10, where Job predicts God will rebuke the friends. In the epilogue there is still no umpire. God simply pronounces, and the pronouncement goes Job’s way. What the epilogue shows is not the mediator arriving but the verdict arriving without one — which leaves the longing of 9:33 unanswered at the end of the book. [I] That unanswered longing, not a fulfilment, is the honest trajectory. 1 Tim 2:5 names the figure; Swete’s μεσίτης at 9:33 is the lexical bridge and is the Greek’s word, not the Hebrew’s [S: overview].

3. The contrast: Job is restored; he is not raised. Confidence: high. Job dies (42:17), and the Hebrew book ends there. Everything he receives is in this life, and the last five verses name no god. The Old Greek could not leave it there and appended a resurrection; the Hebrew leaves it exactly there. The contrast is therefore not merely that Christ is sinless where Job is not — it is that the vindication Job receives is a longer life and more livestock, and the book is honest that this is what it is. The ending that satisfies is not in Job; the addition at 42:17a is the first recorded attempt to put it there.

4. One further line, lower confidence. [I] Confidence: low–moderate. The epilogue’s imitable act — praying for those who wronged you — is commanded by Jesus in almost those terms (Matt 5:44; Luke 6:28). Offered as the New Testament’s own use of the pattern, not as typology. See Tool 13.

Moralism check. The epilogue is the single likeliest passage in Job to be preached as a reward for virtue, and the text’s own three defences against that are: the restoration follows the verdict, not the endurance; the syntax of 42:10 is circumstantial, not causal; and the children are not doubled. A Christological reading that makes Job’s prayer the efficient cause of his blessing has reinstated the Accuser’s premise — that there is a price at which godliness is bought — on the last page of the book that refutes it.

Difficult and Contested Verses

(a) 42:7 — “you have not spoken of Me what is right, as My servant Job has.” How can this stand beside 38:2 and 42:3? The central difficulty of the book, and it falls in this unit. Confidence: the difficulty is real; my resolution is moderate.

The problem stated plainly: at 38:2 God says Job “darkens counsel by words without knowledge”, and at 42:3 Job agrees. Five verses later God says Job spoke נְכוֹנָה and the friends did not. Either the book contradicts itself or the two verdicts are about different things.

Four candidate resolutions, with what each costs:

  1. Different objects. God faults Job’s understanding (38:2 — he spoke beyond his knowledge) and commends Job’s direction (42:7 — he spoke אֵלַי, “to Me”). The friends spoke about God to a sufferer; Job spoke to God about his suffering. The preposition אֵלַי is in the text and is doing work: God’s commendation is of speech addressed to Him. This is the overview’s resolution and the one this dig finds best supported. [T] for the preposition; [I] for the resolution. Confidence: moderate–high.
  2. Different standards. נְכוֹנָה is “right, reliable” rather than “accurate” — cf. Ps 5:10, the only other verse where the form means right speech, and there it describes a mouth with nothing trustworthy in it (Tool 7b). On this reading the friends’ sentences were true and their speech was not trustworthy. Confidence: moderate.
  3. The variant. On the בְּעַבְדִּי reading (Textual Variants (a)), God says nothing at all about Job’s speech being right and the contradiction vanishes. This is the cheapest solution and it is why it should be suspected: it is also the reading an ancient translator would produce if the difficulty troubled him, and the Greek has it. MT preferred at moderate.
  4. Sequence. Job’s last word before the verdict was a retraction (42:6). God commends a man who has just stopped talking. Confidence: moderate, and compatible with (1).

What the preacher must not do is resolve it by making Job faultless, or by making God’s rebuke in 38–41 a mere clearing of the throat. Both verdicts are God’s and both stand.

(b) 42:8 — “so as not to do with you folly.” [T] Confidence: the ambiguity is high-confidence; the reading is moderate. נְבָלָה is a hapax in Job and the clause has no possessive: לְבִלְתִּי עֲשׂוֹת עִמָּכֶם נְבָלָה. Readings: (i) your folly — God will not treat you as your folly deserves (both English versions, by supplying the pronoun); (ii) God will not do something outrageous to you — i.e. will not deal with you in a way that would itself be shocking; (iii) Swete’s rewrite, “I would have destroyed you.” (i) is the natural sense in context and is probably right; the pronoun is nonetheless supplied, and a congregation should not be told the Hebrew says “your”.

And a related note, from the Corrections list: the overview’s Echo Table links the wife’s “you speak as one of the foolish women” (2:10) to this verse. The connection is by root, not by lemma — 2:10 uses נָבָל (2 verses in Job: 2:10, 30:8) and 42:8 uses נְבָלָה (1 verse), and the corpus files them as separate entries. The echo is real and should be stated as cognate.

(c) 42:13 — seven sons, or fourteen? See Original Language Observations (d) and Textual Variants (e). The form is unique in the Hebrew Bible; the consonants are undisputed; Swete reads seven. The honest position: the text as it stands gives the same number as 1:2, the doubling therefore stops at the children, and a sermon that needs fourteen sons is building on a morpheme. Confidence: moderate–high for “seven”.

(d) 42:11 — “the evil that the LORD brought upon him.” [T] Confidence: high for the text; the theology is the difficulty. The difficulty is not textual but doctrinal, and it is sharpened by the fact that the Old Greek removes the word (Textual Variants, category 2.6). The narrator attributes the calamity to the LORD, having shown the reader that the Accuser performed it under permission (1:12; 2:6). The book holds both and resolves neither into the other. What a preacher may say: the Bible does not let us explain Job’s suffering by handing it to the devil, and does not let us make God its author in the sense of its approving it. What a preacher may not say: that one of these two statements is the real one.

(e) 42:15 — the daughters’ inheritance. Handled under Historical Background (d) and Pitfall 6. The difficulty is the use made of it, not the verse.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: “The patience of Job”

How it sounds. “We all know about the patience of Job” — followed by 1:21 and 2:10 and nothing else.

Why it is wrong. Three independent reasons, and the third is new to this dig. (i) Chapters 3–31 are the book’s bulk and they are not patient. (ii) God says it was Job who spoke rightly (42:7), after those chapters. (iii) James, whom the proverb comes from, does not say Job was patient. In Jas 5:10 he assigns μακροθυμία, “patience”, to the prophets; in 5:11, the next verse, he assigns ὑπομονή, “endurance”, to Job. The proverb takes the word James gave to somebody else and pins it on the one man he withheld it from. [T] from SBLGNT.

Corrective. Preach ὑπομονή — holding up under a weight, which is what the lament chapters are. The epilogue vindicates a man who complained to God for twenty-nine chapters.

Pitfall 2: The epilogue as payment

How it sounds. “Hold on, and God will give it back double.” Or, worse: “Pray for the people who hurt you, and watch God restore you.”

Why it is wrong. The text blocks it three ways, and two are grammatical. (i) בְּהִתְפַּלְלוֹ at 42:10 is בְּ + infinitive construct: temporal or circumstantial, not causal. The narrator had causal constructions available and used none. (ii) The restoration follows the verdict (42:7–9), so it rests on God’s assessment of Job’s speech, not on Job’s endurance. (iii) The arithmetic refuses it: every animal is doubled and no child is. Ten children are born; ten are not returned.

Corrective. The turn and the prayer are in the same moment, and the moment is God’s. Preach 42:10 as grace that arrives while Job is busy with someone else’s problem — and say out loud that the restoration is not a reversal of the bereavement.

Pitfall 3: Supplying the explanation

How it sounds. At a bedside: “God is testing you, like Job.” “Satan has asked for you.”

Why it is wrong. [S: overview], confirmed. Job is never told the prologue, and the epilogue does not tell him either. He dies knowing less than the reader. The heavenly scene is given to the reader so that the reader will not claim to know which scene lies behind another person’s suffering — and the epilogue proves the point by closing without an explanation. And the narrator’s own final attribution is to the LORD (42:11), not to the Accuser, so a dualist version of the comfort is unavailable too.

Corrective. The thing the friends did wrong is the thing their defenders do: explain. The family at 42:11 brings bread and a small gift and explains nothing, and the text lets that stand.

Pitfall 4: Preaching the friends’ restoration as cheap, or as owed

How it sounds. “And then everyone was friends again.” Or the opposite: the three dismissed as villains who get what they deserve.

Why it is wrong. The text is harder than either. God’s anger is kindled (42:7); the charge is repeated verbatim rather than softened (42:8); fourteen animals are required; and the escape runs through the man they wronged. Swete, reading the Hebrew’s reticence, makes it explicit: “you have sinned” and “I would have destroyed you.” Yet nothing in the Hebrew says they were forgiven — that sentence is the Greek’s (42:9–10), and the Hebrew leaves their standing unstated. No apology is recorded and no reconciliation scene is given.

Corrective. Say what the text says: they were wrong, they were commanded, they obeyed, and they were spared on Job’s account. Resist both the sentimental ending and the vindictive one.

Pitfall 5: Making the daughters a decoration

How it sounds. The names read out as charming local colour, then straight on to the 140 years.

Why it is wrong. [T] / [I] 42:14–15 is the only place in the book where anyone is named besides Job, his friends and Elihu — and the three named people are his daughters, while the seven sons are not named. The book’s only word for beauty is used of them (יָפֶה, 42:15, one verse in Job). The narrator reports that they were given נַחֲלָה — the word used three times earlier in the book for what God allots the wicked. Four distinct lexical facts converge on five lines of text, which is not decoration.

Corrective. Preach the names. A man who cursed the day of his birth (3:1–10) ends by naming three daughters after spices and giving them a share of the land. That is what recovery looks like in this book, and it is reported without comment.

Pitfall 6: Using 42:15 as a proof-text

How it sounds. “Even in the patriarchal era Job gave his daughters equal inheritance — so Scripture teaches…” — in either direction.

Why it is wrong. [T] / [I] The narrator draws no conclusion, no later text appeals to it, and the book never places Job under any law. It is a narrative detail about one Gentile household outside Israel, reported in half a verse. Under the law Israel did have, daughters inherited when there was no son (Num 27:1–11; Num 36; Josh 17:3–6), and Job had seven sons. So the verse is genuinely unusual — and a text’s being unusual is not the same as its being a norm.

Corrective. Let it do what it does: show a restored father being generous beyond requirement. Note it, do not legislate from it, and do not deny that it is striking.
