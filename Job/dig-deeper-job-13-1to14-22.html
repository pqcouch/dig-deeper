Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools:

  1. Job stops speaking about God to the friends and starts speaking to God — and charges the friends with speaking for him. [T]
    • The prepositions. “But I would speak to (אֶל,”to“) the Almighty, and I desire to argue with (אֶל) God” (13:3). “Will you speak what is unjust for God (הַלְאֵל,”for God“), and speak what is deceitful for Him (וְלוֹ,”for him“)? … Will you contend for God (לָאֵל)?” (13:7–8). Job speaks to God; the friends speak for him. אֱלִל (“worthless”, 13:4), elsewhere the Bible’s word for idols, sits between the two (Tool 7).
    • The address. The speech turns at 13:20. From 13:2 to 13:17 there are 23 second-person plural forms, addressed to the friends; 13:18–19 have none; from 13:20 to 14:20 there are 41 second-person singular forms, addressed to God, and no plural at all (WLC morphology). The friends are never addressed again in this speech.
    • The court. יכח (“argue, reprove”) at 13:3, 10 (×2), 15; רִיב (“contention”, “contend”) at 13:6, 8, 19; מִשְׁפָּט (“case, judgement”) at 13:18; 14:3; צדק (“be in the right”) at 13:18 (lemma). “I have prepared my case; I know that I will be vindicated” (13:18) reverses 9:2, “In truth I know that this is so; but how can a man be in the right before God?” ידע (“know”) with צדק in one verse occurs only at Gen 38:26; Job 9:2; 13:18 (lemma).
    • The book’s verdict takes up both halves. Job warns that God will “surely reprove” those who “show partiality” (נשׂא פָּנִים, literally “lift the face”, 13:8, 10) for him. At the end God’s wrath is kindled against the friends, and he says of Job, “I will accept him (פָּנָיו אֶשָּׂא, literally”I will lift his face“)” (42:8), “because you have not spoken of Me (אֵלַי, literally”to me“) what is right, as My servant Job has” (42:7–8). The preposition is the one of 13:3; the English versions render it “of”. Job’s charge against the friends at 13:7, עַוְלָה (“what is unjust”) with רְמִיָּה (“deceit”), becomes his own oath at 27:4 — the only other verse with the pair (lemma).
    • Surfaced by: Original Languages (the address test), Structure, Vocabulary, Linking Words, Move 4. High. The introductory-sermon backbone rests its second movement on 13:3, 7–8; this confirms it with the morphology.
  2. Job pleads his case in the words of Isaiah’s Servant (Isa 50:6–9) — but he has no helper, and it is he who wears out like a moth-eaten garment. [T] for the words; [I] for the inversion
    • The courtroom question. “Who will contend (יָרִיב,”contend“) with me?” (13:19) // “Who will contend (יָרִיב) with Me?” (Isa 50:8). מִי (“who?”) with רִיב in one verse occurs only at these two verses in the Hebrew Bible (lemma). Isa 50:8 also holds צדק (“vindicate”) and מִשְׁפָּט (“judgment”: “Who has a case against Me?”); Job 13:18–19 holds the same four words across two verses. And Job’s מִי־הוּא (“who is he who …?”) with a first-person object is the form of Isa 50:9, “Who is he who condemns Me?” (מִי־הוּא יַרְשִׁיעֵנִי).
    • The confidence. “I know (יָדַעְתִּי) that I will be vindicated (אֶצְדָּק)” (13:18) // “I know (וָאֵדַע) that I will not be ashamed. He who vindicates (מַצְדִּיקִי) Me is near” (Isa 50:7–8).
    • The moth. “Like a garment (כְּבֶגֶד) that is moth-eaten (אֲכָלוֹ עָשׁ)” — “he wastes away” (יִבְלֶה) (13:28) // “they will all wear out (יִבְלוּ) like a garment (כַּבֶּגֶד); the moth (עָשׁ) will eat them” (Isa 50:9). בלה (“wear out”) with בֶּגֶד (“garment”) and עָשׁ (“moth”) in one verse occurs only at these two verses (lemma).
    • The face. The Servant “did not cover (לֹא הִסְתַּרְתִּי) My face” (Isa 50:6); Job asks why God “hides (תַסְתִּיר) [His] face” (13:24) and promises “I will not hide (אֶסָּתֵר) from Your face” (13:20).
    • The inversion. The Servant has a vindicator “near” and says twice, “the Lord GOD helps (יַעֲזָר) Me” (Isa 50:7, 9); his accusers wear out. Job has a case and no helper, and it is Job who wears out like the garment. The Greek keeps the link: Swete’s Job 13:19 τίς … ὁ κριθησόμενός μοι against Isa 50:8 τίς ὁ κρινόμενός μοι; Job 13:28 ἱμάτιον σητόβρωτον (“a moth-eaten garment”) against Isa 50:9 ὡς ἱμάτιον … σής.
    • Psalm 44 sits beside it. Job 13:24, “Why do You hide Your face?”, is word for word Ps 44:25 [Eng 44:24], the lament of the faithful who have “not forgotten” God (Ps 44:18 [Eng 44:17]). Paul’s climax in Rom 8:33–36 uses both texts — Isa 50:8–9 (“God is the one who justifies; who is the one who condemns?”) [S] and Ps 44:23 [Eng 44:22] by quotation (SBLGNT).
    • Surfaced by: Tool 11 (triad), Vocabulary, Translations (the Greek), Christological Reading. High on the data; moderate–high for a deliberate link; the direction of dependence is open (Tool 11). Load-bearing and unaudited — see the claim-audit note at the end.
  3. “If a man dies, will he live again?” (14:14). Job gives the tree a hope he denies to man, and asks the question in the words the canon later uses to answer it. [T] for the words; trajectory [I]
    • The tree. “There is hope (תִּקְוָה,”hope“) for a tree, when it is cut down, that it will sprout again (יַחֲלִיף,”sprout again“)” (14:7). Its root (שֹׁרֶשׁ) grows old and its stump (גִּזְעוֹ, “its stump”) dies in the dust (14:8). גֶּזַע (“stump”) with שֹׁרֶשׁ (“root”) in one verse occurs only at Job 14:8 and Isa 11:1, “a shoot will spring from the stem (מִגֵּזַע) of Jesse, and a branch from his roots (מִשָּׁרָשָׁיו)” (lemma).
    • The man. “So man lies down and does not rise (לֹא־יָקוּם). Until the heavens are no longer, he will not awake (לֹא יָקִיצוּ)” (14:12). The phrase לֹא יָקִיצוּ (“they will not awake”) occurs elsewhere only at Jer 51:39, 57, “sleep a perpetual sleep and not wake up” (phrase search, confirmed by lemma).
    • The answer in his own words. קוּם (“rise”) with קיץ (“awake”) meets in one verse only at Hab 2:7, Isa 26:19 and Job 14:12; קיץ with עָפָר (“dust”) only at Isa 26:19 and Dan 12:2 (lemma). Isa 26:19: “Your dead (מֵתֶיךָ) will live (יִחְיוּ); their corpses will rise (יְקוּמוּן). You who lie in the dust (עָפָר), awake (הָקִיצוּ) and shout for joy”. Dan 12:2: “Many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake (יָקִיצוּ)” — the very form Job negates. Job’s question has מות (“die”) and חיה (“live”) in one verse, the only such verse in Job (14:14); Isa 26:19 answers with both.
    • Renewal. The tree “will sprout again” (חלף, 14:7); Job will wait “until my change (חֲלִיפָתִי,”my change, renewal“) comes” (14:14) — one root. The tree’s word becomes the man’s hope.
    • The Greek answers the question. Swete’s 14:14 reads “If a man dies, he will live (ζήσεται) … I will wait until I come to be again (ἕως πάλιν γένωμαι)” — and this line is Old Greek, not asterisked in the Rahlfs export. The asterisked line at 14:12 has οὐκ ἐξυπνισθήσονται ἐξ ὕπνου (“they will not be awakened from sleep”); ἐξυπνίζω occurs in the New Testament only at John 11:11, of Lazarus.
    • Surfaced by: Parallels, Vocabulary, Repetition, Tool 11, Translations, Christological Reading. High on the words; moderate on whether Job is reaching beyond the grave — his own answer in 14:18–22 is no.
  4. The face hidden, the man hidden: Job’s one sustained hope is that God would hide him, remember him, and cover his sin (14:13–17). [T] for the words; the grammar [I]
    • The verb turns three times. סתר (“hide”): “I will not hide (אֶסָּתֵר) from Your face” (13:20); “Why do You hide (תַסְתִּיר) Your face?” (13:24); “Oh that You would … conceal me (תַּסְתִּירֵנִי) until Your wrath returns” (14:13). Job will not hide from God; God hides from Job; Job asks God to hide him.
    • The limit turns. חֹק (“limit”): the bound God sets “so that he cannot pass” (14:5) becomes “set a limit (חֹק) for me and remember me” (14:13). The barrier becomes an appointment.
    • The sin is named in the Exodus words. “How many are my iniquities (עֲוֹנוֹת) and sins (חַטָּאוֹת)? Make known to me my rebellion (פִּשְׁעִי) and my sin (חַטָּאתִי)” (13:23). The three words of Exod 34:7 — “who forgives iniquity, transgression and sin” — stand together in one verse at Exod 34:7; Lev 16:21; Ps 32:5; Isa 59:12; Ezek 21:29; Dan 9:24; Job 13:23 (lemma).
    • The hope is forgiveness. “For then (כִּי־עַתָּה) You would number my steps; You would not keep watch over my sin; my transgression would be sealed up in a bag, and You would cover over my iniquity” (14:16–17, ESV). In Job, כִּי־עַתָּה after a wish or a condition introduces the unreal consequence (6:3; 8:6; 13:19; and 3:13 after the “why”s), and 14:13 opens with the wish formula מִי יִתֵּן (“Oh that”). The NASB95 instead reads present surveillance (“For now You number my steps … You wrap up my iniquity”), as does Swete. Moderate–high for the ESV’s reading. 7:21 had asked, “Why then do You not pardon my transgression (פִשְׁעִי) and take away my iniquity (עֲוֹנִי)?”; 14:17 has the same two nouns with the same suffix, and imagines the answer.
    • The friends’ plaster and God’s. טפל (“smear, plaster”) occurs only at 13:4 (“you smear with lies”), 14:17 (“and You plaster over my iniquity”, literally) and Ps 119:69 (lemma). The friends plaster lies over the truth; Job hopes God will plaster over his sin.
    • Surfaced by: Linking Words, Vocabulary, Translations, Repetition, Tool 11, Move 4. High on the words.
  5. “You destroy man’s hope” (14:19). Job takes the friends’ sentence on the wicked and makes God its subject and mankind its object — and the whole speech closes in flesh and soul. [T] for the words; [I] for the effect
    • Hope perishing. תִּקְוָה (“hope”) with אבד (“perish”) meets in Job only at 8:13 (Bildad: “the hope of the godless will perish”), 11:20 (Zophar, the last line of the speech Job is answering: “their hope is to breathe their last”), and 14:19, “So You destroy (הֶאֱבַדְתָּ) man’s hope” (lemma). Bildad and Zophar said the wicked lose hope. Job says God destroys the hope of אֱנוֹשׁ (“man, mortal”) — anyone.
    • The tree replanted. תִּקְוָה with עֵץ (“tree”) occurs only at 14:7 and 19:10, “He has uprooted my hope like a tree” (lemma). What Job grants the tree here he will say God has torn up in him.
    • The inclusio. נֶפֶשׁ (“life, soul”) with בָּשָׂר (“flesh”) occurs in Job only at 12:10, 13:14 and 14:22 (lemma) — the opening, middle and close of Job’s reply. God holds “the life of every living thing” in his hand (12:10); Job takes “my flesh in my teeth and puts my life in my hand” (13:14); the speech ends, “his flesh (בְּשָׂרוֹ) pains him, and his soul (וְנַפְשׁוֹ) mourns” (14:22). These are the two things the Accuser asked to touch: “his bone and his flesh (בְּשָׂרוֹ)” (2:5) and “his life (נַפְשׁוֹ)” (2:6).
    • Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Move 4, Tone. High on the data; moderate on reading 14:22 against 2:5–6.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level (overview, confirmed). The book shows a righteous man who will neither curse God nor buy relief by confessing sins he did not commit, exposes the friends’ tidy theology as speaking wrongly of God (42:7), and brings the sufferer to a fear of God that wants God himself. Chapters 13–14 supply the clearest single text in the dialogue for the overview’s reading of 42:7: right speech is speech to God, not speech for him (Headline 1). [I]

Unit level. 13:1–14:22 does four things. [T] / [I] - It dismisses the friends as God’s advocates. They speak “for God” (13:7–8), smear lies (13:4), and are “worthless physicians” (13:4). Job warns them that the God they defend will examine them (13:9–11). [T] - It makes the lawsuit real. In 9–10 Job imagined a trial and found it impossible. Here he announces that he has “prepared my case” (13:18), asks for two conditions (13:20–21), offers the procedure — “call, and I will answer; or let me speak, then reply to me” (13:22) — and asks for the charge sheet (13:23). [T] - It sets man’s brevity against God’s scrutiny. 14:1–6: a man born of woman, short-lived, a flower, a shadow — and God opens his eyes on such a one and brings him to judgement (14:3). [T] - It asks the question of life after death, and lets it go. The tree’s hope (14:7–9), man’s sleep (14:10–12), the wish to be hidden in Sheol and remembered (14:13–17), and then the erosion of all hope (14:18–22). [T] for the movement; [I] that the wish is the speech’s high point.

Selection. Job says nothing about: - his children (except “his sons”, 14:21, in the general third person); - the specific sins the friends have hinted at (8:4; 11:6, 14); - any sin he could name — he asks God to name it (13:23).

He speaks to the friends about how they speak, and then to God about the trial, man’s frailty and death. [T] for the absences; [I] for the effect.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. [T] - Zophar (11) has said that Job’s talk silences men (יַחֲרִישׁוּ, 11:3), that God exacts less than Job’s guilt deserves (11:6), that God would speak against Job if he would only open his lips (11:5, the wish formula מִי־יִתֵּן), and that if Job puts iniquity away “you would trust, because there is hope (תִּקְוָה)” (11:18). His last line is the wicked man’s fate: “their hope (וְתִקְוָתָם) is to breathe their last” (11:20). - Job (12) has opened his reply by mocking their wisdom (“Truly then you are the people, and with you wisdom will die!”, 12:2), said “I am not inferior to you” (12:3), and given a catalogue of God’s overturning power (12:13–25), with “In whose hand is the life of every living thing” (12:10). - Round one as a whole has given each friend his turn (4–5; 8; 11). Job’s speech in 9–10 found no court and no umpire (9:33) and ended asking to be left alone (10:20).

Necessity answer. 13–14 must come here, at the end of round one, and must be Job’s. [I] - Zophar’s silencing needs its answer. He said Job’s talk silences men (11:3); Job tells the friends to be silent (13:5, 13). He wished God would speak (11:5); Job wishes the friends would be silent (13:5) and asks God to call (13:22), with the same wish formula. - Zophar’s hope needs its answer. He promised hope to the repentant and its loss to the wicked (11:18, 20). Job grants hope to a tree (14:7) and says God destroys the hope of man (14:19). - 9:2’s question needs its sequel. “How can a man be in the right before God?” (9:2) becomes “I know that I will be vindicated” (13:18). - 9:34 needs to be prayed. “Let not dread of Him terrify me” (9:34) becomes “let not the dread of You terrify me” (13:21) — the same nouns and verb, now addressed to God (9–10 report, Headline 5). - The round needs a close. Each round of the dialogue ends with Job. This speech ends round one with death, as chapter 3 began the dialogue with death (Tool 3).

Implication. 13–14 is where Job abandons the friends as a court and turns to God as both judge and only hope. A sermon on these chapters must hold the two halves together: the defiance of 13:13–19 and the plea of 14:13–17 are addressed to the same God. [I]

Surrounding context

  • Before: chapter 12, the first third of this reply. Its “I am not inferior to you” (12:3) is repeated at 13:2 (the phrase לֹא־נֹפֵל אָנֹכִי מִכֶּם occurs only at these two verses — phrase search; BHS cross-refers them). Its catalogue of God’s power (12:13–25) is what Job’s eye has “seen” (13:1).
  • After: Eliphaz opens round two (15). He picks up “born of woman” (15:14; Headline 3, Tool 11 Move 4), charges, “you do away with reverence” (15:4), and says “your own mouth condemns you” (15:6).
  • Section: the end of round one of the dialogue (4–14). This is Job’s third reply.
  • Masoretic paragraphing: one paragraph, 12:1–14:22, with a petuḥah after 11:20 and after 14:22 (Leningrad, as BHS prints it). The chapter divisions at 13:1 and 14:1 are not Masoretic breaks. [T]; [unchecked — apparatus spread].

Stanza lengths. 12, 7, 9, 6, 11, 5 verses. The ESV paragraphs fall at 13:1, 13:13, 14:1, 14:7 and 14:18; it does not break at 13:20, where the address changes. [T]

Device: two speeches in one, hinged on silence. [T] for the markers; [I] for the design. - The first half (I–II) is to the friends and ends with “For then I would be silent (אַחֲרִישׁ) and die” (13:19). - The second half (III–VI) is to God and ends with death described in the third person (14:20–22). - חרשׁ (“be silent”) marks the joints: “O that you would be completely silent (הַחֲרֵשׁ תַּחֲרִישׁוּן)” (13:5); “Be silent (הַחֲרִישׁוּ) before me so that I may speak” (13:13); “For then I would be silent (אַחֲרִישׁ) and die” (13:19). The friends must be silent so that Job can speak; Job will be silent only if someone can contend with him.

Two matching appeals. “Then call, and I will answer (וּקְרָא וְאָנֹכִי אֶעֱנֶה)” (13:22) and “You will call, and I will answer You (תִּקְרָא וְאָנֹכִי אֶעֱנֶךָּ)” (14:15) stand at the head of the second half and at its high point. The first is a demand for a hearing; the second is a longing to be called back. [T] for the words; [I] for the shift. High.

The arc of hope. The second half runs: God’s face hidden (13:24) → man’s brevity (14:1–6) → the tree’s hope (14:7–9) → man’s sleep (14:10–12) → the wish (14:13–17) → hope destroyed (14:18–22). The two תִּקְוָה (“hope”) verses, 14:7 and 14:19, bracket the stanzas on the tree and man. [T] for the words; [I] for the bracket.

Bookend with chapter 3, and the end of a round. The dialogue began with Job wishing he had “lain down (שָׁכַבְתִּי) … slept (יָשַׁנְתִּי)” in death (3:13). Round one ends with “man lies down (שָׁכַב) and does not rise … nor be aroused out of his sleep (מִשְּׁנָתָם)” (14:12). Both verses join שׁכב (“lie down”) to a word for sleep, and both are Job’s. In chapter 3 the sleep of death was rest; here it is the sleep from which no one wakes — and Job wishes for a waking. [T] for the words (lemma: שׁכב with יָשֵׁן in Job only at 3:13, with שֵׁנָה only at 14:12); [I] for the bookend. Moderate–high.

4. Linking Words

  • הֵן (“behold”, 13:1, 15) and הִנֵּה (“behold”, 13:18). Three exclamations mark the speech’s claims: what Job has seen (13:1), what God may do to him (13:15), and what Job has done (13:18). [T]
  • אוּלָם (“but, however”, 13:3, 4; 14:18). The strong adversative. “But I would speak to the Almighty” (13:3) sets Job against the friends’ knowledge; “But you smear with lies” (13:4) sets the friends against Job’s intent; “But the falling mountain crumbles away” (14:18) sets erosion against the hope just voiced. The third אוּלָם undoes the wish of 14:13–17. [T] for the word; [I] for the effect.
  • The rhetorical questions of 13:7–11. Introduced by הַ (“will you?”, 13:7, 8, 9, 11) and paired with אִם (“or”, 13:8, 9). Each asks the friends whether advocacy for God will survive God’s own examination. [T]
  • אִם (“if”, 13:10; 14:5, 7, 8, 14). “He will surely reprove you if (אִם) you secretly show partiality” (13:10). In chapter 14 the conditionals carry the tree and the man: “if it is cut down” (14:7), “though its roots grow old” (14:8), “If a man dies” (14:14). [T]
  • The questions with מָה (“what?”, 13:13, 14, 23, 24). “Let come on me what (מָה) may” (13:13); “Why (עַל־מָה) should I take my flesh in my teeth?” (13:14); “How many (כַּמָּה) are my iniquities?” (13:23); “Why (לָמָּה) do You hide Your face?” (13:24). The question of 13:24 is the one “why” addressed to God in the speech. [T]
  • הֵן יִקְטְלֵנִי (“Though He slay me”, 13:15). הֵן here is concessive (“even if, though”) or presentative (“behold, He will slay me”). The decision belongs with the ketiv/qere at the same verse (Textual Variants; Difficult Verses). [T] for the word; the force is contested.
  • גַּם־הוּא (“This also”, 13:16). “This also will be my salvation”: the הוּא (“this, it”) points back to the act of arguing before God’s face (13:15), or forward to the reason “for a godless man may not come before His presence”. The ESV reads it as forward: “This will be my salvation, that the godless shall not come before him.” [T] for the words; the reference [I].
  • כִּי־עַתָּה (“for then / for now”, 13:19; 14:16). In Job the phrase introduces the consequence of a wish or condition at 6:3, 8:6 and 13:19 (and at 3:13 after the “why”s); at 4:5, 6:21 and 7:21 it means “for now, as things are” (phrase search). At 13:19 the sense is unreal: “For then I would be silent and die”. At 14:16 it follows the wish of 14:13 (מִי יִתֵּן, “Oh that”), so the unreal sense is the more natural (Headline 4). [T] for the data; [I] for 14:16. Moderate–high.
  • אָז (“then”, 13:20). “Only two things do not do to me, then (אָז) I will not hide from Your face.” The condition of Job’s appearing in court. [T]
  • כִּי (“for”, 14:7). “For there is hope for a tree” — a new start, contrasting the tree with the man of 14:1–6. [T]
  • וְ (“but”, 14:10, 12). “But man (וְגֶבֶר) dies” (14:10); “So man (וְאִישׁ) lies down” (14:12). The conjunction sets man against the tree. [T]
  • אַךְ (“only, surely”, 13:15, 20; 14:22). “Nevertheless (אַךְ) I will argue my ways before Him” (13:15); “Only (אַךְ) two things do not do to me” (13:20); “But (אַךְ) his body pains him” (14:22). The restrictive particle marks the turns. [T]

5. Parallels

  • 13:1 — synonymous. “My eye has seen all this, my ear has heard and understood it.” עַיִן (“eye”), אֹזֶן (“ear”), ראה (“see”) and שׁמע (“hear”) meet in one verse in Job only at 13:1, 29:11 and 42:5 (lemma; Move 4). [T]
  • 13:4 — synonymous, with the metaphor of the physician. “You smear with lies (טֹפְלֵי־שָׁקֶר); you are all worthless physicians (רֹפְאֵי אֱלִל).” The physicians plaster rather than heal. [T]
  • 13:7–8 — four synonymous questions in two verses. Speak unjustly for God // speak deceit for him; show partiality for him // contend for God. [T]
  • 13:12 — synonymous, sarcastic. “Your memorable sayings are proverbs of ashes (אֵפֶר); your defences are defences of clay (חֹמֶר).” אֵפֶר with חֹמֶר meets only here and at 30:19, where Job says God “has cast me into the mire (לַחֹמֶר), and I have become like dust and ashes (וָאֵפֶר)” (lemma). The material he mocks in their arguments becomes what he says he is. [T] for the words; [I] for the irony.
  • 13:14 — synonymous. “Take my flesh in my teeth // put my life in my hands.” Both idioms mean risking one’s life. נֶפֶשׁ (“life”) with כַּף (“palm”) and שׂים (“put”) occurs at Judg 12:3; 1 Sam 19:5; 28:21 and here (lemma), always of someone who risked death. [T]
  • 13:25 — synonymous. “Will You cause a driven leaf (עָלֶה נִדָּף) to tremble? Or will You pursue (תִּרְדֹּף) the dry chaff?” עָלֶה נִדָּף occurs only here and at Lev 26:36, which also has רדף (“pursue”) (Tool 11). [T]
  • 13:27 — synthetic, three lines. Stocks, watched paths, marked soles. 33:11 quotes the first two lines (Move 4). [T]
  • 14:1–2 — the brevity of man in four images. Born of woman; short of days, full of turmoil; a flower that withers; a shadow that flees. [T]
  • 14:7–9 against 14:10–12 — an extended antithetical comparison. The tree has hope; it sprouts again; its shoots do not fail; at the scent of water it flourishes. “But man dies … expires, and where is he?” (14:10). The two halves are linked by death: the tree’s stump “dies” (יָמוּת, 14:8); man “dies” (יָמוּת, 14:10). [T]
  • 14:11–12 — a simile across two verses. Water vanishing from the sea and a river drying up // a man who lies down and does not rise. The phrase וְנָהָר יֶחֱרַב וְיָבֵשׁ (“and a river becomes parched and dried up”) occurs elsewhere only at Isa 19:5 (Tool 11). [T]
  • 14:18–19 — four images of erosion, then the point. Mountain, rock, stones, soil — “So You destroy man’s hope.” [T]
  • 14:21 — antithetical. “His sons achieve honour, but he does not know it; or they become insignificant, but he does not perceive it.” [T]
  • 14:22 — synonymous. “His flesh pains him // his soul mourns for him.” [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A for the speech itself — 13:1–14:22 is character speech with no narrative intrusion. The narrative formula stands at 12:1 (“Then Job responded”). [T]

What the narrator has already said stands over the speech. The narrator has told the reader that the Accuser asked to touch Job’s “bone and his flesh” (2:5) and was forbidden his “life” (2:6). When Job takes “my flesh in my teeth and puts my life in my hands” (13:14) and ends “his flesh pains him, and his soul mourns” (14:22), the reader knows who has had his flesh and who has guarded his life (Headline 5). When Job warns the friends that God will “reprove” those who show partiality for him (13:10), the reader will see it happen: “My wrath is kindled against you … because you have not spoken of Me what is right” (42:7). [T] for the texts; [I] for their bearing.

9. Tone and Feel

The register is defiance turning to longing: a man who will risk death to be heard, and then asks to be hidden in death until God remembers him. [T] for the devices; [I] for the register. - 13:1–12 — scorn. Short, sarcastic lines. The friends are plasterers of lies and quacks (13:4); their silence would be their wisdom (13:5); their maxims are ash and their defences clay (13:12). Then the warning turns grave: will they deceive God as one deceives a man (13:9)? Will not his majesty terrify them (13:11)? - 13:13–19 — risk. “Let come on me what may” (13:13). “Why should I take my flesh in my teeth?” (13:14). “Though He slay me” (13:15). The courage of a man who expects to die and will speak anyway. Then confidence, abruptly: “I know that I will be vindicated” (13:18). - 13:20–28 — the plea and the charge. The voice drops to direct address — “Only two things do not do to me” (13:20) — and then rises into complaint: the hidden face, the enemy, the driven leaf, the stocks. It ends in decay, moth and rot (13:28). - 14:1–12 — elegy. The tone becomes general and quiet. Man, flower, shadow, hired man (14:1–6). Then a moment of brightness — the tree that sprouts at the scent of water (14:7–9) — quenched by “But man dies” (14:10). - 14:13–17 — longing. Three wishes in one breath: hide me, conceal me, remember me (14:13). The question (14:14). Then the tenderest lines in Job’s speeches so far: “You will call, and I will answer You; You will long for the work of Your hands” (14:15). - 14:18–22 — erosion. Mountain, rock, stones, soil, hope. The last verses are in the third person, as though Job has already gone: a man who does not know whether his sons are honoured, alone with his flesh and his soul.

The soundtrack. A courtroom speech that becomes a funeral lament with one verse of hope in the middle. For the preacher: - Do not lift 13:15 out as serene trust; it is spoken by a man who expects God to kill him and will argue anyway. - Let 14:13–17 be heard as the high point. It is the first time in the book that Job imagines God wanting him. - Do not end at 14:15. The speech ends at 14:22, in the dark, and round two begins there.

Move 2 — Book usage. Move 3 — OT-to-OT. The felled tree with a living stump returns in Isa 6:13 (“the holy seed is its stump”, a different word), Isa 53:2 (“He grew up before Him like a tender shoot (כַּיּוֹנֵק), and like a root (וְכַשֹּׁרֶשׁ) out of parched ground”) and Jer 23:5 (“a righteous Branch”). What it adds. Job imagines what the canon promises of David’s line: a felled tree with hope, a dead stump that sprouts “at the scent of water”. He grants it to the tree and denies it to man (14:10). The canonical reader knows a Man of whom it is said. [I] Moderate. See Christological Reading.

12. Genre

Hebrew poetry: a disputation speech that becomes a lawsuit plea, then an elegy on mortality with a prayer at its heart. [T] - Embedded forms. - Disputation with the friends (13:1–12). Rhetorical questions, sarcasm, proverbs turned (13:5, 12). - A plaintiff’s declaration (13:13–19). “I have prepared my case” (13:18) — the language of a party about to plead. - A complaint to God (13:20–28). Conditions, a request to be told the charge, a “why”, images of persecution. - An elegy on man (14:1–12, 18–22). Wisdom’s language of brevity: flower, shadow, water, stone. - A wish-prayer (14:13–17). Three wishes introduced by מִי יִתֵּן (“Oh that”). - Reading rules. - It is character speech; God will rebuke Job’s words “without knowledge” (38:2) and say he spoke what is right (42:7). Report it as what Job says. - It is poetry: God does not literally put feet in stocks (13:27) or write bitter things (13:26). - The wish of 14:13–17 is a wish. It shows what Job hopes for, not what he believes will happen. - What the genre does not license. - It does not teach that there is no resurrection (14:12); it reports a man’s lament, which the canon answers. - It does not teach that God is Job’s enemy (13:24); it reports how it feels to Job. 16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. To recognise false comfort that defends God at the expense of truth, and to fear it. To see a righteous man risk everything to bring his case to God himself. To feel the full weight of human mortality, and to long with Job for a God who would hide, remember, call and cover. [T] / [I]

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.

Domain Application
Worldview God does not need advocates who lie for him (13:7–8). Human life is brief and under God’s eye (14:1–6). Death, seen from below, has no tree’s hope (14:7–12) — but God is the one who could call the dead (14:15), and the canon says he will (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2)

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. The story’s world is patriarchal and outside Israel (overview). The composition date is [S] and not needed here.

What the original audience knew. 1. The physician. Healers in Israel and its neighbours combined remedies with ritual and explanation [S]. Job’s “worthless physicians” (13:4) have a diagnosis — sin — and the wrong one. Moderate. 2. Partiality in court. “Lifting the face” of a party was the classic corruption of a judge (Lev 19:15; Deut 1:17; 10:17; 16:19) [T]. Job accuses the friends of acting as corrupt judges in God’s favour. [T] for the texts; [I] for the application. 3. The plaintiff’s procedure. Preparing a case (ערך מִשְׁפָּט), calling and answering, and asking the opponent to state the charge belong to the language of litigation at the gate (cf. Isa 41:1, 21; 43:9, 26) [T] / [I]. 4. The stocks. A wooden frame for the feet of prisoners (Jer 20:2–3; 29:26, a different word) [T]. The “marks on the soles” (13:27) may be brands or cords that let a prisoner’s tracks be followed [S]. Uncertain. 5. The tree that sprouts from the stump. Olive, sycamore and terebinth regenerate from the stump when cut [S] — the sight behind Isa 6:13 and 11:1. Moderate. 6. Sheol. The realm of the dead, below, from which no one returns (7:9; 10:21) [T]. Job’s wish is to be hidden there temporarily — a thought with no Old Testament precedent in these terms. [I] 7. The bag and the seal. Valuables and documents were tied in a pouch and sealed (Gen 42:35; 1 Sam 25:29; Jer 32:10–14) [T]. A sealed bag can be kept as evidence or put away for good — the ambiguity of 14:17. [I]

What this changes. Job speaks the language of the courtroom to friends who have played corrupt judges, and the language of a prisoner to a God he thinks is his jailer. Then he reaches for an image he knows from the orchard — the cut tree that grows again — and for a Sheol that might be a hiding-place rather than a prison. [I]

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Readers of the Writings with the Torah and the Prophets behind them, who have just read the Psalter, and who know the exile’s laments (Ps 44; Lam 3) and the Servant’s courtroom (Isa 50). [I]

What they would have heard. - A friend’s rebuke that fits their own comforters. Those who had heard “your suffering proves your sin” would hear 13:4–12 as their own reply. [I] - Psalm 44 in a single man’s mouth. The communal lament of the faithful-but-defeated, “Why do You hide Your face?”, spoken by one righteous man. [I] - The Servant without his help. “Who will contend with me? … I know that I will be vindicated” — the Servant’s confidence — followed by the moth eating the speaker rather than his enemies (13:28). They would feel the gap. [I] - A question about the dead in the vocabulary of Isaiah and Daniel. “Rise”, “awake”, “live”, “dust”. A reader who knew Isa 26:19 would hear Job ask the question the prophet had answered. [I]

Expectations created. - That God will answer the summons (13:22). He does (38:1). - That the friends will be judged for their partiality (13:10). They are (42:7–9). - That the tree’s hope (14:7) will return. It does, torn up (19:10). - That the question of 14:14 will be pressed. It is (19:25–27).

What would have surprised them. That a man who calls God his enemy (13:24) also imagines God longing for him (14:15) — the only verse in the Hebrew Bible with God as the subject of כסף (“long for”). [T] for the data; [I] for the surprise.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. Speaking rightly of God — to him, not for him. [T] / [I] - Job 13:3, 7–8 → 42:7–8 (“you have not spoken of Me what is right”). - Advocates for God who say what is untrue are judged (13:9–11; 42:7). The prophets condemn the false “Peace, peace” (Jer 6:14; 8:11) and those who whitewash (טוּחַ, “plaster”) a flimsy wall (Ezek 13:10–15) — a different verb, the same image as טפל (13:4). [T] for the texts. - For the preacher: the warning of 13:7–11 is addressed to the professional speakers for God.

2. The righteous sufferer’s lawsuit. [T] - Job 13:18–19 → Isa 50:7–9 → Rom 8:33–34. - The Servant’s courtroom is won before it opens: “He who vindicates Me is near”. Job’s is not yet. Paul gives the Servant’s verdict to all “in Christ Jesus”.

3. The hidden face. [T] - Deut 31:17–18; 32:20 (God hides his face from the faithless) → Ps 13:2 [Eng 13:1]; 44:25; 88:15 (the faithful ask why) → Job 13:24 → Isa 50:6 (the Servant does not hide his face from shame) → Isa 53:3 (“like one from whom men hide their face”) → Num 6:25–26 (the blessing: the LORD make his face shine, lift up his face). - The irony of 13:24 and 42:8–9: Job complains that God hides his face; at the end God “lifts Job’s face”. [T] for the idiom; [I] for the bracket.

4. Covering sin. [T] - Exod 34:7 → Ps 32:1, 5 → Job 7:21; 13:23; 14:16–17 → Mic 7:18–19 → Rom 4:7–8. - Job imagines God sealing transgression and plastering over iniquity. The canon’s word for the act is “cover” and “bear” (כסה, נשׂא). The atonement is the means Job does not name.

5. Death as sleep and the hope of waking. Christological Reading

(For the OT passage: the canonical trajectory, offered as raw material for /point-purpose.)

1. The Servant’s courtroom, won (Isa 50:6–9 → Rom 8:33–34). [T] for the words; [I] for the trajectory - Job pleads “I know that I will be vindicated … Who will contend with me?” in the Servant’s words, without the Servant’s help (Headline 2). - The Servant who did not hide his face (Isa 50:6) is the Christ of the passion: struck, spat on (Mark 14:65; 15:19), silent before his accusers, vindicated in resurrection. - Paul builds his courtroom from the Servant’s: “Who will bring a charge against God’s elect? God is the one who justifies; who is the one who condemns? Christ Jesus is He who died, yes, rather who was raised” (Rom 8:33–34) [S] for the Isaiah source. - Category: trajectory — Job’s courtroom, in the Servant’s words, finds its verdict in the Servant’s work. Moderate–high.

2. “For Your sake we are killed all day long” (Ps 44 → Rom 8:36). [T] - Job’s “Why do You hide Your face?” (13:24) is Psalm 44’s. Paul quotes Ps 44:23 of the church in Christ, and answers it: “in all these things we overwhelmingly conquer through Him who loved us” (Rom 8:36–37). - Category: trajectory — the innocent sufferer’s lament is taken up by those united to the innocent Sufferer. Moderate–high.

3. “If a man dies, will he live again?” (14:14 → John 11). [T] for the Greek; [I] for the trajectory - Job: man “will not be awakened from his sleep” (14:12, Swete, asterisked: οὐκ ἐξυπνισθήσονται). Jesus: “Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep; but I go, so that I may awaken him (ἐξυπνίσω)” (John 11:11) — the only ἐξυπνίζω in the New Testament. Then: “I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in Me will live even if he dies” (John 11:25). - The Old Greek of 14:14 already answers “he will live” (ζήσεται); John 11:25 has the same verb, ζήσεται. - Category: trajectory, through the Greek. Moderate — the lexical link runs through a Hexaplaric line; the thematic link stands without it.

4. The stump of Jesse (Isa 11:1 → 14:7–9). [I] - Job’s tree, “cut down”, with a dead stump, sprouting “at the scent of water”, is the image Isaiah uses for the Messiah from David’s felled line. Job grants the tree what he denies to man; in the Messiah the two meet — a Man who is the shoot from the stump, raised from death. - Category: contrast and trajectory. Moderate.

5. “You will call, and I will answer You; You will long for the work of Your hands” (14:13–17). [I] - Job’s one sustained hope: to be hidden through wrath, remembered at a set time, called back by a God who longs for his creature, his sin sealed and covered. - The gospel gives the shape of it: hidden “with Christ in God” (Col 3:3); a set time for the dead to hear his voice (“an hour is coming, in which all who are in the tombs will hear His voice, and will come forth”, John 5:28–29); sin covered (Rom 4:7) and its record “canceled … nailed to the cross” (Col 2:14). - Category: trajectory. Moderate. Do not make Job a believer in these things; let his wish be the shape the gospel fills.

6. “Born of woman” (14:1 → Gal 4:4). [I] - Job’s phrase for frail mankind (Swete γεννητὸς γυναικός, the phrase of Matt 11:11). “God sent forth His Son, born of a woman (γενόμενον ἐκ γυναικός)” (Gal 4:4) — a different Greek verb, the same human condition. The Son took the “few days and full of trouble” of 14:1. Uncertain–moderate as a link; moderate–high as theology.

The moralism check. The trap is to preach Job as a model of “faith that holds on” from 13:15 alone. Job holds on by arguing, and his hope (14:13–17) is for something God must do. The sermon should point to what God has done in Christ, not to Job’s tenacity as our resource. [I]

Difficult / Contested Verses

13:15 — “Though He slay me”. - Category: textual and doctrinal (a weaponised verse of piety). - Honest exegesis: the qere gives hope; the ketiv gives its absence; both keep the second half, “I will argue my ways to his face”. The verse is not resignation. Even on the qere, the hope is the hope of a man who will contend. - Handling: read the whole verse; say that the Hebrew can be read two ways; do not build the sermon on “trust” alone.

13:24 — “consider me Your enemy”. - Category: doctrinal and pastoral. - Honest exegesis: the language of lament (Ps 44; 88; Lam 2:4–5, “The Lord has become like an enemy”). Elihu will quote it as a charge (33:10). God does not call Job his enemy; he calls him “My servant” (42:7–8). - Handling: let it stand as how it feels; set beside it what God says.

14:4 — “Who can make the clean out of the unclean? No one!” - Category: doctrinal (a proof-text for original sin). - Honest exegesis: in context it is a plea, not a doctrine: man is frail and impure by nature, so why scrutinise him (14:3) — “turn Your gaze from him” (14:6). The formula may even be a wish (“Oh that a clean thing could come from the unclean!”). Eliphaz and Bildad make it a doctrine against Job (15:14–16; 25:4–6). The Greek (“who will be clean from filth? not one, even if his life be one day”) fed the later tradition. - Handling: the truth of human impurity is biblical (Ps 51:5); this verse uses it to ask for mercy. Preach it as Job uses it, and note that the friends’ use of it is rebuked (42:7).

14:12, 14 — resurrection. - Category: apologetic. - Honest exegesis: 14:12 denies that the dead wake “until the heavens are no longer”; 14:14 asks whether a man will live again and, in the context of 14:13–17, wishes it. Job does not affirm resurrection here; he reaches for it. - Handling: honest lament, answered by the canon (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2; John 11). Don’t make the verse a doctrine, and don’t smooth it away (sweep).

14:16–17 — surveillance or forgiveness? - Category: translational, with pastoral weight. - Honest exegesis: the כִּי־עַתָּה pattern and the wish of 14:13 favour the ESV/NIV84 (future, hope of forgiveness). The Greek and NASB95 read present surveillance. The sealed bag is ambiguous (Hos 13:12, sin stored; Dan 9:24, sin ended). - Handling: with the ESV in the pew, preach it as Job’s hope; mention the alternative only if the NASB95 is being used. Moderate–high.

14:19, 22 — no hope. - Category: pastoral. - Honest exegesis: the end of the speech is dark. It is the end of round one, not of the book. - Handling: don’t end a sermon at 14:15 as though the speech did. Name the dark, and point forward (19:25; 42:5, 17) and beyond (John 11).

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: “Though He slay me” as serene trust

  • What it looks like: 13:15a quoted on its own, as the motto of quiet faith in suffering.
  • Why it’s wrong: it rests on a qere (the ketiv reads “I have no hope”), and the second half — “I will argue my ways to his face” — is defiance. The verse is spoken by a man who expects to die for speaking.
  • The corrective: read the whole verse, and 13:13–14 before it. The faith here is the faith that will not stop speaking to God, even when God seems to be the killer. Say that the Hebrew can be read two ways.

Pitfall: Preaching against the friends without seeing ourselves

  • What it looks like: “Job’s friends were bad comforters; don’t be like them” — and the congregation agrees comfortably.
  • Why it’s wrong: 13:7–11 is aimed at people who speak for God: preachers, elders, Christians with an answer. The friends’ fault is not unkindness but untruth in God’s defence. That is a preacher’s sin.
  • The corrective: let the warning land on the pulpit first. “Will you speak what is unjust for God?” is a question the preacher must answer before the congregation does.

Pitfall: Making 14:14 Job’s creed

  • What it looks like: “Job believed in the resurrection: ‘If a man dies, shall he live again? … till my renewal should come.’”
  • Why it’s wrong: 14:12 denies it, 14:13–17 wishes for something like it, and 14:18–22 lets the hope erode. Job asks; he does not affirm. (The Old Greek turns the question into an affirmation — an early reading, not the Hebrew.)
  • The corrective: preach the question as Job’s, and the answer as the canon’s (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2) and Christ’s (John 11:25). The question is more powerful honestly asked.

Pitfall: Using 14:4 as a proof-text detached from its plea

  • What it looks like: “Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? Not one” — cited for original sin, and the sermon moves on to doctrine.
  • Why it’s wrong: Job uses human impurity to plead for mercy (“look away from him”, 14:6). The friends turn it into an accusation (15:14; 25:4), and they are rebuked (42:7).
  • The corrective: if the doctrine is drawn, draw it as Job does: our frailty is a reason to ask God for mercy, not a stick to beat the suffering.

Pitfall: Moralising the tree

  • What it looks like: “Like the tree, you can bounce back — just find the scent of water.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the tree is the contrast: “But man dies” (14:10). The point is that man, unlike the tree, has no natural renewal.
  • The corrective: let the tree show what we lack, and then the Shoot from the stump (Isa 11:1) who gives it.

Pitfall: Stopping at 14:15

  • What it looks like: a sermon on “You would long for the work of your hands” that ends on the high note.
  • Why it’s wrong: the speech ends at 14:22, in erosion and mourning, and the round ends there. Ending early teaches a congregation that lament always resolves in the same breath.
  • The corrective: read through 14:22. Name the dark. Then point beyond Job’s speech to the book’s end and to Christ.
