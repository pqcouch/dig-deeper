## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools:

1. **Job curses his day, not God, and he does it by turning the first words of creation backwards.** `[T]`
   - The narrator's verb is קלל ("curse", 3:1). Its object is יוֹמוֹ ("his day"), not God. The Accuser's prediction (1:11; 2:5) fails at the very moment Job breaks his silence.
   - The first thing the poem asks is יְהִי חֹשֶׁךְ ("let there be darkness", 3:4). The only other time the Hebrew Bible says "let there be darkness" in this jussive form is the ninth plague on Egypt (Exod 10:21; phrase search). Its mirror, יְהִי אוֹר ("let there be light"), occurs only at Gen 1:3.
   - Every creation-word in 3:3–9 is one of Genesis 1's words: אוֹר ("light"), חֹשֶׁךְ ("darkness"), יוֹם ("day"), לַיְלָה ("night"), שָׁנָה ("year") (lemma; Gen 1:2–18).
   - *Surfaced by:* Tool 11, Vocabulary, Repetition, Narrator's Comment, Move 4. *High on the words; high on the Genesis design; moderate on the Exodus design.*

2. **The lament speaks to no one — yet it names God twice, and when Job asks its central question again, he asks it of God.** `[T]`
   - There is no second-person form anywhere in 3:2–26 (WLC morphology, every verb, pronoun and suffix). Positive control: chapter 10 has 41.
   - God is named twice, in the third person: אֱלוֹהַּ ("God") is asked not to seek the day (3:4) and is the one who "has hedged in" the man (3:23). These are the first two occurrences of אֱלוֹהַּ in the book (lemma).
   - At 10:18 Job repeats 3:11 — לָמָּה ("why"), מֵרֶחֶם ("from the womb"), יצא ("come out"), גוע ("expire") — but now as "Why then did **You** bring me out of the womb?". לָמָּה + רֶחֶם + יצא in one verse occurs only at Jer 20:18, Job 3:11 and Job 10:18 (lemma co-occurrence).
   - The lament addressed to no one becomes a lament addressed to God.
   - *Surfaced by:* Structure, Linking Words, Original Languages, Move 4; claim-audit finding 5 `[S: audit]`, now mechanically confirmed. *High.*

3. **The poem moves from curse to "why?", and ends by showing that the dead have what Job lacks.** `[T]`
   - **Three movements.** A curse on the day and the night (3:3–10); "Why did I not die at birth?" (3:11–19); "Why is light given to him who suffers?" (3:20–26). The questions are marked by לָמָּה ("why", 3:11, 20) and מַדּוּעַ ("why", 3:12).
   - **What the dead have.** The rest-words run through the second movement and all return, negated, in the last verse (lemma):
     - נוח ("rest"): 3:13, 17, 26;
     - שׁקט ("be quiet"): 3:13, 26;
     - רֹגֶז ("turmoil"): 3:17, 26.
     - 3:26 reads: "I am not at ease, nor quiet, nor at rest — and turmoil comes."
   - **What comes.** בוא ("come") turns over, five times (lemma). Twice it is wished away: "let it not come", "let no joyful shout come" (3:6, 7). Three times it simply happens: sighing comes, what I dread comes, turmoil comes (3:24, 25, 26).
   - *Surfaced by:* Structure, Repetition, Tone, Parallels. *High.*

4. **God's first speech answers this chapter in its own words.** `[T]` (synthetic)
   - **38:8** gathers four of chapter 3's words into one verse: סכך ("hedge, shut in", 3:23), דֶּלֶת ("door", 3:10), רֶחֶם ("womb", 3:11) and יצא ("come out", 3:11). It is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where "door" and "womb" meet.
   - **38:12** asks whether Job has "commanded the morning since your days began" (ESV) and "caused the dawn (שַׁחַר) to know its place". שַׁחַר occurs in Job only at 3:9, 38:12 and 41:10 [Eng 41:18]; Swete marks the same three verses with one word, ἑωσφόρος ("morning star").
   - **38:17** has "the gates of deep darkness (צַלְמָוֶת)" (3:5).
   - **38:19** puts "the way (דֶּרֶךְ)" where "light (אוֹר) dwells (שׁכן)" beside "darkness (חֹשֶׁךְ)"; all four words are in chapter 3 (3:4, 5, 9, 23).
   - **38:7** has the morning stars (כּוֹכָב) singing (רנן), against 3:7, 9: "let no joyful shout (רְנָנָה) enter it … let its stars (כּוֹכְבֵי) be darkened".
   - **39:5, 7** have the free wild donkey and the unheard driver (3:18–19).
   - **41:10 [Eng 41:18]** has the eyelids of the dawn, and **41:2 [Eng 41:10]** rousing Leviathan (3:8, 9).
   - All by lemma in the WLC.
   - *Surfaced by:* Move 4, Repetition, Vocabulary, Tool 11. *High on the words; moderate–high on the design.* The sweep's first headline, extended here.

5. **Job curses his birth in the words of a faithful prophet, and roars in the words of the forsaken psalmist.** `[T]`
   - **Jeremiah 20:14–18.** The lemma overlap is dense:
     - ארר ("curse"), Jer 20:14–15 / Job 3:8;
     - "the day … I was born in it", יוֹם + ילד + בּוֹ, Jer 20:14 / Job 3:3;
     - מות ("die") + מֵרֶחֶם ("from the womb"), only Num 12:12, Jer 20:17 and Job 3:11;
     - לָמָּה + רֶחֶם + יצא, only Jer 20:18 and Job 3:11 (and 10:18);
     - עָמָל ("trouble"), Jer 20:18 / Job 3:10.
   - **Psalm 22.** Job's שַׁאֲגֹתָי ("my cries", literally "roarings", 3:24) is the noun of Ps 22:2 [Eng 22:1], "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me? Far from my deliverance are the words of my groaning (שַׁאֲגָתִי, literally 'roaring')". The noun occurs in only seven verses of the Hebrew Bible (lemma).
   - **Psalm 22, again.** The same psalm pairs מֵרֶחֶם with מִבֶּטֶן ("from the womb … from my mother's womb You have been my God", Ps 22:11 [Eng 22:10]) — one of only five verses where the two nouns meet, with Job 3:11. The Job 2 dig found its potsherd at 2:8 (Ps 22:16 [Eng 22:15]). Three touchpoints in two chapters.
   - The curse is not the cry of the wicked. It is the speech of the suffering righteous.
   - *Surfaced by:* Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Vocabulary, Original Audience, Christological Reading. *High for Jeremiah (direction open); moderate–high for Psalm 22 (synthetic).*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level (overview, confirmed).** The book shows a righteous man who, stripped of everything, will neither curse God nor buy relief by confessing sins he did not commit. It exposes the friends' tidy theology as speaking wrongly of God (42:7), and it brings sufferer and reader to a fear of God that wants God himself, reached through God's self-disclosure rather than an explanation. The introductory sermon's reading of 42:7 — right speech is speech *to* God — is confirmed below (Tool 3; Headline 2). `[I]`

**Unit level.** Chapter 3 does four things. `[T]` / `[I]`
- **It breaks the silence of 2:13, and it is Job who breaks it.** The friends came "to show him sympathy and comfort him" (2:11) and said nothing for seven days. The first word of the debate is a curse and a cry, not an argument. `[T]`
- **It tests the Accuser's prediction and refutes it.** Job curses, but not God: the object of קלל is his day (3:1). `[T]`
- **It gives the debate its subject.** Every friend's opening will answer it:
  - Eliphaz picks up "comes" (4:5), "roaring" (4:10) and "dread" (4:14);
  - the later speeches reply to Job's further speeches, but chapter 3 is the one Eliphaz answers first.

  The subject is not "why do the righteous suffer?" in the abstract. It is a man who wishes he had never been born, and who asks why light is given to the bitter of soul. `[T]` for the pickups; `[I]` for the characterisation.
- **It plants the questions God will answer from the storm** (Headline 4). The chapter is the book's second great planting ground after the prologue. `[I]`

**Selection.** Job says nothing about:
- his children or his possessions;
- the boils (after 2:7–8);
- his wife or his friends;
- sin, his own or anyone's;
- the Accuser.

He does not ask "why me?". He asks why anyone is born to suffer (3:20) and why light is given to "a man" (3:23). He moves from his own day to the general human case, and back to himself only in 3:24–26. `[T]` for the absences; `[I]` for the effect.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** The prologue has done three things.
- It shows Job worshipping (1:20–21) and receiving good and evil from God (2:10).
- It narrows the verdict to "did not sin with his lips" (2:10).
- It ends with three friends silent for seven days, "for they saw that his pain was very great" (2:13).

Three things are left open at its end `[T]`:
- the Accuser's prediction that Job will "curse You to Your face" (2:5);
- the silence;
- the question the prologue gave the reader but never Job: does he fear God "for nothing"?

**Necessity answer.** Chapter 3 must come here, and must be Job's. `[I]`
- **The silence needs a first word.** The friends will not speak first (4:2). The book will not let the prologue's worship stand as Job's whole response.
- **"Did not sin with his lips" (2:10) needs a test.** The next thing the narrator says is that Job "opened his mouth" (3:1).
- **The Accuser's prediction needs its answer.** It comes in the verb: קלל for the first time in the book, the plain word, not the euphemism. It falls on a day, not on God.
- **The debate needs its subject.** Without chapter 3 the friends would have nothing to answer, and God nothing to reply to in 38–41.

**Implication.** Chapter 3 is not a lapse from 1:21. It is its companion. The man who blessed the name of the LORD now curses the day of his birth, and he does both without cursing God. A sermon must hold 1:21 and 3:3 together: both are what faith sounds like in the dark. God's verdict (42:7) is given on a man who spoke both. `[I]`

#### Surrounding context

- **Before:** 2:11–13, the friends' coming and silence. The Leningrad petuḥah after 3:1 closes the prologue in the Hebrew. In the Greek, 3:1 flows into the poem ("cursed his day, saying", Swete) (Unit 1 report).
- **After:** 4:1–5:27, Eliphaz's first speech. Its opening words, "If one ventures a word with you, will you become impatient?" (4:2), answer the silence Job broke.
- **Section:** chapter 3 stands outside the three rounds (4–27). With 29–31 it brackets the debate: Job's opening soliloquy and his closing speech (overview arc map; `[S: audit]`).
- **Book:** the first poetry. The poetic accentuation runs from here to 42:6 (WLC as transcribed; `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`).

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Mode | Key markers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3:1–2 | Narrative frame | prose | "opened his mouth … cursed his day" (3:1); petuḥah; "Job answered and said" (3:2) |
| 3:3–10 | **I. The curse: let the day and the night be undone** | wishes | sixteen third-person verbs of wishing in 3:3–9 — eight parsed jussive in the WLC morphology, eight imperfects with jussive force — from יֹאבַד ("let it perish", 3:3) to יִרְאֶה ("let it not see", 3:9); then כִּי ("because", 3:10) |
| 3:11–19 | **II. Why did I not die at birth? — the rest of the dead** | questions, then description | לָמָּה (3:11), מַדּוּעַ (3:12); כִּי־עַתָּה ("for now", 3:13); שָׁם ("there", 3:17 ×2, 19) |
| 3:20–26 | **III. Why is light given to the sufferer? — and to me** | question, then first person | לָמָּה (3:20); the general "him who suffers … a man" (3:20–23); then כִּי ("for", 3:24, 25) and "I am not at ease" (3:26) |

`[T]` for every marker (lemma).

**Device: descent from wish to question to present.** `[T]` for the grammar; `[I]` for the effect.
- **Movement I** wishes the past undone: jussives.
- **Movement II** imagines the past otherwise: "I would have lain down … I would have slept" (3:13), perfects of the unreal.
- **Movement III** describes the present: "comes … comes … comes" (3:24–26).

The poem begins with a man trying to cancel a day and ends with a man who cannot stop the days coming.

**Bookends.**
- **גֶּבֶר ("man").** At 3:3 the night announces, "A man (גֶּבֶר) is conceived". At 3:23 light is given "to a man (לְגֶבֶר) whose way is hidden". The word occurs in chapter 3 only at these two verses (lemma): the man announced at conception is the man hedged in. `[T]` *Moderate–high* as an inclusio.
- **Rest and turmoil.** 3:13, 17 against 3:26 (Headline 3).

**Stanza lengths.** 8, 9 and 7 verses (3:3–10, 11–19, 20–26). No refrain; the questions mark the turns. `[T]`

**Where God stands in the structure.** He is named at the end of the first stanza's opening line (3:4) and near the end of the last (3:23). The first names him as one who should not "seek" the day; the last as the one who has "hedged in" the man. God frames the poem without being addressed in it. `[T]` for the positions; `[I]` for the frame.

### 4. Linking Words

- **כִּי ("because", 3:10).** It closes the curse with its reason: "because it did not shut the doors of my womb". The curse on the day is a grievance against the day's failure to prevent his life. `[T]`
- **לָמָּה (3:11, 20) and מַדּוּעַ (3:12) — "why".** The poem's hinges. There are four questions in all (3:11, 12a, 12b, 20), with 3:23 elliptical — it borrows the verb of 3:20, "Why is light given?". The three "why"s are never answered in the chapter, or by the friends. God will answer with questions of his own (38:2–3). `[T]`
- **כִּי־עַתָּה … אָז ("for now … then", 3:13).** The unreal perfects: what would have been. `[T]`
- **אוֹ ("or", 3:15, 16).** The alternatives Job could have joined in death: kings and counsellors, princes with gold — or the miscarried child. The dead range from the greatest to those who never lived. `[T]`
- **שָׁם ("there", 3:17 ×2, 19).** Sheol described by its adverb, never by its name. `[T]`
- **כִּי ("for", 3:24, 25).** Job's own case gives the reason for his general question. `[T]`
- **No connective introduces 3:26.** It is three flat negations and a finite verb. `[T]`

### 5. Parallels

- **3:3 — day and night personified, synonymous.** "Let the day perish on which I was to be born / and the night which said, 'A man is conceived'." The night speaks; birth and conception, day and night, are the two ends of one beginning. `[T]`
- **3:4–5 and 3:6–9 — two panels.** The first is on the day: darkness, God not seeking it, no light. The second is on the night: gloom, no place in the calendar, barrenness, no joyful shout, no dawn. Each panel moves from darkness to the absence of light. `[T]`
- **3:11 — synonymous.** "Why did I not die at birth [literally, from the womb], come forth from the womb and expire?" מֵרֶחֶם ("from the womb") and מִבֶּטֶן ("from the belly") are paired. The two nouns stand together in one verse only at Jer 1:5, Job 3:11, 31:15, Ps 22:11 [Eng 22:10] and Ps 58:4 (lemma). At Jer 1:5 God knew and consecrated Jeremiah "before you came forth from the womb". At Ps 22:11 the forsaken psalmist says, "from my mother's womb You have been my God". Job wishes he had died from it. `[T]` for the data; see Tool 11.
- **3:17 — antithetical in content, synonymous in form.** "There the wicked cease from raging, and there the weary are at rest." The oppressor and the oppressed arrive at the same rest. `[T]`
- **3:19 — merism.** "The small and the great are there": death levels the whole social scale. `[T]`
- **3:25 — synonymous, with a cognate accusative.** פַחַד פָּחַדְתִּי ("a dread I dreaded") // "what I feared". `[T]`
- **3:26 — the break.** The poem's last verse is not a parallel pair. It is three negated verbs and a fourth that is not negated: לֹא שָׁלַוְתִּי וְלֹא שָׁקַטְתִּי וְלֹא־נָחְתִּי וַיָּבֹא רֹגֶז ("I am not at ease, and I am not quiet, and I am not at rest — and turmoil comes"). The pattern breaks where the man breaks. `[T]` for the form; `[I]` for the effect.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**3:1 — the narrator's last word before the poetry, and his interpretation of what follows.** `[T]`
- **אַחֲרֵי־כֵן ("afterward").** The seven days are over.
- **פָּתַח אִיּוֹב אֶת־פִּיהוּ ("Job opened his mouth").** After 2:10's "with his lips", the narrator marks the mouth. פתח with פֶּה occurs in 25 verses of the Hebrew Bible (lemma co-occurrence), so the idiom is not rare. What is marked is its position.
- **וַיְקַלֵּל אֶת־יוֹמוֹ ("and cursed his day").** The narrator names the object before the poem begins, so that the reader hears the whole poem as a curse on the day. After 1:22 and 2:10, the narrator gives no verdict here. He neither says "Job sinned" nor "Job did not sin". The next verdict is God's, and it is "right" (42:7). `[T]` for the absence.

Everything from 3:2 to 42:6 is character speech. The narrator's next comment is 32:1–5. `[T]`

### 7. Vocabulary

**קלל ("to curse; to be light, slight", 3:1).**
- The plain word for "curse", after the book's four euphemisms with ברך ("bless", 1:5, 11; 2:5, 9). Its basic sense is "be light", so in the piel "treat as light, curse" (lemma 7043).
- In Job: 3:1 (curse); 7:6 and 9:25 ("swift"); 24:18 (cursed); 40:4 — Job's first reply to God, הֵן קַלֹּתִי ("Behold, I am insignificant").
- **The man who treated his day as light finally counts himself light before God.** `[T]` for the lexeme; the echo is *moderate* (the lexeme is wide; the sweep rated it moderate).

**יוֹם ("day", 3:1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8) and לַיְלָה ("night", 3:3, 6, 7).**
- Six verses have יוֹם, three have לַיְלָה (lemma; 3:6 has בִּימֵי, "among the days").
- **The "day" thread** runs from the prologue (Unit 1 report: יוֹם ×8 in 1:1–3:1) into the poem. The form יוֹמוֹ ("his day") joins 1:4 to 3:1. `[T]`
- **Day and night together** are the pair God made and named (Gen 1:5) and owns: "Yours is the day, Yours also is the night" (Ps 74:16). `[T]`

**The darkness vocabulary.**
- חֹשֶׁךְ ("darkness", 3:4, 5), with the verb חשך ("be dark") at 3:9;
- צַלְמָוֶת ("black gloom, deep darkness", 3:5);
- עֲנָנָה ("cloud-mass", 3:5 — its only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible, lemma);
- כִּמְרִירֵי יוֹם ("blackness of the day", 3:5; a crux, see Textual Variants);
- אֹפֶל ("darkness", 3:6).

Six words for dark in four verses. Against them stand אוֹר ("light", 3:9, 16, 20; elliptical at 23) and נְהָרָה ("light", 3:4; its only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible, lemma). `[T]`

**צַלְמָוֶת ("deep darkness", 3:5).**
- **The count.** 18 occurrences in 17 verses of the Hebrew Bible, 10 of them in 9 verses of Job (lemma; 24:17 has it twice). The sweep's "10 of 18" is confirmed.
- **In Job it recurs at:**
  - 10:21–22, Job's "land of darkness and deep darkness";
  - 12:22, where God "brings deep darkness (צַלְמָוֶת) into light";
  - 38:17, "the gates of deep darkness".
- **Elsewhere.** Amos 5:8 praises the LORD "who changes deep darkness (צַלְמָוֶת) into morning". Ps 23:4 has "the valley of the shadow of death".
- **What it adds.** Job asks that deep darkness claim his day. God, by Job's own later confession (12:22), is the one who brings deep darkness into the light. `[T]` *High* on the data.

**גאל ("to redeem, reclaim", 3:5).**
- יִגְאָלֻהוּ חֹשֶׁךְ וְצַלְמָוֶת ("let darkness and black gloom claim it"). The WLC lemma is 1350 a, the redeemer's verb — not 1350 b, "defile". Some read it as "let darkness defile (stain) it" `[S]`.
- In Job the lemma 1350 a occurs only here and at 19:25: גֹּאֲלִי חָי ("my Redeemer lives").
- **Job asks darkness to be his day's kinsman-redeemer — the one who claims it back. Later he knows that his Redeemer lives.** `[T]` for the lemma and the two verses; *moderate* on any intended echo, since the sense at 3:5 is disputed.

**לִוְיָתָן ("Leviathan", 3:8).**
- Five verses in the Hebrew Bible: Isa 27:1; Job 3:8; 40:25 [Eng 41:1]; Ps 74:14; 104:26 (lemma).
- **In Job:** here, invoked by those "prepared to rouse (עֹרֵר)" it; and in God's speech, where "no one is so fierce that he dares to rouse him (יְעוּרֶנּוּ)" (41:2 [Eng 41:10]). `[T]`

**שַׁחַר ("dawn", 3:9).** בְּעַפְעַפֵּי־שָׁחַר ("the eyelids of the dawn"). In Job only 3:9, 38:12 and 41:10 [Eng 41:18] (lemma); "the eyelids of the dawn" as a phrase only at 3:9 and 41:10 (sweep, verified). `[T]`

**The womb and its doors.**
- דַּלְתֵי בִטְנִי ("the doors of my womb", 3:10); מֵרֶחֶם ("from the womb"), מִבֶּטֶן ("from the belly", 3:11).
- 38:8 is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where דֶּלֶת ("door") and רֶחֶם ("womb") meet (sweep, verified). `[T]`

**עָמָל / עָמֵל ("trouble / one who toils in trouble", 3:10, 20).**
- One root, two lemmas (5999, 6001): the day did not "hide trouble (עָמָל) from my eyes" (3:10); light is given "to him who suffers (לְעָמֵל)" (3:20).
- Jer 20:18 has the noun: "to look on trouble (עָמָל) and sorrow". `[T]`

**סתר / טמן ("hide").**
- **סתר:** the day did not "hide (וַיַּסְתֵּר) trouble" (3:10); the man's "way is hidden (נִסְתָּרָה)" (3:23).
- **טמן:** "a hidden (טָמוּן) miscarriage" (3:16); the dead are sought "more than hidden treasures (מַטְמוֹנִים)" (3:21).

What should have been hidden, trouble, was not; what should be plain, his way, is hidden. `[T]` for the data; `[I]` for the reversal.

**The rest vocabulary.**
- נוח ("rest", 3:13, 17, 26); שׁקט ("be quiet", 3:13, 26); שׁלה ("be at ease", 3:26); רֹגֶז ("turmoil", 3:17, 26).
- Job has 5 of רֹגֶז's 7 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible (3:17, 26; 14:1; 37:2; 39:24, lemma).
- At 14:1 Job generalises his own case: "Man, who is born (יְלוּד) of woman, is short-lived [literally, few of days, יָמִים] and full of turmoil (רֹגֶז)". ילד + יוֹם + רֹגֶז in one verse occurs only there (lemma co-occurrence); the three words are chapter 3's (3:3, 26). `[T]`

**חָפְשִׁי ("free", 3:19) and נֹגֵשׂ ("taskmaster", 3:18).**
- **חָפְשִׁי** is the word of slave-release in the law (Exod 21:2, 5, 26–27; Deut 15:12–18; Jer 34:9–16) and of Ps 88:6 [Eng 88:5], "free (חָפְשִׁי) among the dead". In Job only 3:19 and 39:5 (lemma).
- **נֹגֵשׂ** is Egypt's taskmaster (Exod 3:7; 5:6–14). Its verb is used of the servant at Isa 53:7: "He was oppressed (נִגַּשׂ) … yet He did not open His mouth". In Job only 3:18 and 39:7 (lemma).
- `[T]` *High* on the data.

**שַׁאֲגָה ("roaring", 3:24).**
- NASB95 "my cries", ESV "my groanings". The noun is a lion's roar.
- It occurs in seven verses: Isa 5:29; Ezek 19:7; Zech 11:3; Ps 22:2 [Eng 22:1]; 32:3; Job 3:24; 4:10 (lemma).
- Eliphaz's first speech uses it of lions: "the roaring (שַׁאֲגַת) of the lion" silenced (4:10). God's voice "roars" (the verb שׁאג) in Elihu's storm (37:4). `[T]` See Tool 11 and Move 4.

**פחד and יגר ("dread", 3:25).**
- פַחַד פָּחַדְתִּי ("what I fear") — noun and verb together, and in Job again only at 4:14, where Eliphaz's night-vision begins פַּחַד קְרָאַנִי … הִפְחִיד ("Dread came upon me … made my bones shake") (lemma: the verb occurs in Job only at 3:25; 4:14; 23:15).
- יָגֹרְתִּי ("I dread") is a rare verb: Deut 9:19; 28:60; Job 3:25; 9:28; Ps 119:39 (lemma).
  - At Deut 28:60 it is the covenant curse: "all the diseases of Egypt, of which you were afraid (יָגֹרְתָּ)".
  - At Job 9:28 Job says it again: "I am afraid (יָגֹרְתִּי) of all my pains".
- `[T]` for the data; see Tool 11.

**אֱלוֹהַּ ("God", 3:4, 23).** Its first two occurrences in the book (lemma; the name then occurs 41 times in Job, 60 in the Hebrew Bible, overview). `[T]`

**Proper-noun inventory (Question 0).** Job; God (אֱלוֹהַּ); Leviathan. No places or peoples. Numbers: none. Leviathan receives the full check (Tool 11).

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The register is lament at its rawest: a curse, then questions, then collapse.** `[T]` for the devices; `[I]` for the register.
- **Movement I (3:3–10) is incantation.** Sixteen wishes in seven verses, most of them short, most of them "let … not". The images pile up: darkness, gloom, cloud, blackness, thick darkness, barrenness, no song, no stars, no dawn. The sound is heavy with negatives (אַל, "let not", six times in 3:4–9).
- **Movement II (3:11–19) is almost tender.** The questions of a child who wishes he had never been fed (3:12). Then a still picture of the dead: kings among their ruins, princes with their gold, the miscarried child, the prisoner who no longer hears the taskmaster, the slave free at last. The voice slows; the lines lengthen. Death is imagined as sleep and rest.
- **Movement III (3:20–26) closes in.** The question widens to all sufferers (3:20–22), who dig for death like treasure and rejoice at a grave. Then it narrows to "a man" (3:23), and then to "me" (3:24–26): sighing instead of bread, roaring poured out like water, the dread that came. The last line has three flat negatives and a verb.

**The soundtrack.** A single voice after seven days of silence, unaccompanied. First a low chant, then a lullaby for the dead, then breaking. **For the preacher:**
- Do not preach it cheerfully.
- Do not hurry to chapter 38; the book makes the reader wait thirty-five chapters.
- Do not tidy the ending. Chapter 3 ends on רֹגֶז ("turmoil") and nothing follows but Eliphaz.

**Near-repetition worth noting.**

**Paronomasia check (words repeated three or more times).** יוֹם, לַיְלָה, אוֹר, נוח and בוא were checked. One sound-play emerges: יוֹם ("day", 3:8) against the conjecture יָם ("sea") in BHS. **Genesis 1:2–18 → 3:3–9** *(high)* `[T]` / `[I]` (synthetic)

*Move 1 — Source context.*
- Gen 1:2–5 opens with darkness (חֹשֶׁךְ) over the deep. God's first word is יְהִי אוֹר ("Let there be light", 1:3). He separates light from darkness and names them יוֹם ("day") and לַיְלָה ("night") (1:4–5).
- On the fourth day the lights are set "to separate the day from the night … for seasons and for days (יָמִים) and years (שָׁנִים)" (1:14).
- The register is ordered, untroubled command: the first act of creation brings light into darkness and gives time its shape.

*The verbal data.*
- Job 3:4 יְהִי חֹשֶׁךְ ("let there be darkness") mirrors Gen 1:3 יְהִי אוֹר. The jussive יְהִי with אוֹר occurs only at Gen 1:3 (phrase search).
- Every creation-noun in Job 3:3–9 is a noun of Gen 1:2–18: אוֹר, חֹשֶׁךְ, יוֹם, לַיְלָה, שָׁנָה (lemma).
- Job 3:6 "let it not come into the days of the year (בִּימֵי שָׁנָה) … the number of the months" takes up Gen 1:14's "days and years", to cut the day out of the calendar the lights were made to keep.

*Move 2 — Book usage.*
- This is the first use of Genesis 1 in Job. Genesis 2–3 is already live (1:21 עָרֹם, "naked", with Gen 2:25; the dust of Gen 3:19 at 1:21), in the Job 1 dig.
- **Genesis 1 returns in God's reply** (38:4–38): the foundations, the sea, the morning, the dwelling of light and darkness (38:19), "Have you commanded the morning since your days began?" (38:12). The book uses Genesis 1 twice: once in Job's mouth, to undo it, and once in God's, to show it. `[T]`

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.*
- Genesis 1 had already been reversed in the canon before Job, in a lament over judgement: "I looked on the earth, and behold, it was formless and void (תֹהוּ וָבֹהוּ); and to the heavens, and they had no light (אוֹרָם)" (Jer 4:23; lemma).
- Jeremiah's un-creation is national, a vision of God's judgement. Job's is personal, and it is Job who speaks it.
- The same Jeremiah curses his birthday (next entry). `[T]` for the links.

*What it adds.* Job does not only wish he had died. He tries to speak the first day of creation backwards over his own first day. He asks that "God above" not seek that day (3:4) — as God once looked on the light and saw that it was good. God's answer does not argue with the curse. It takes Job back to creation and shows him who holds the day and the night (38:12–15, 19–20). `[I]`

**Exodus 10:21–23 → 3:4, 6** *(high on the phrase; moderate on the design)* `[T]` / `[I]` (synthetic)

*Move 1 — Source context.*
- The ninth plague. "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Stretch out your hand toward the sky, that there may be darkness (וִיהִי חֹשֶׁךְ) over the land of Egypt, even a darkness which may be felt'" (Exod 10:21).
- "There was thick darkness (חֹשֶׁךְ־אֲפֵלָה) in all the land of Egypt for three days. They did not see one another … but all the sons of Israel had light (אוֹר) in their dwellings" (10:22–23).
- It is the plague before the death of the firstborn: judgement on Egypt's god-king, with light kept for God's people.

*The verbal data.*
- The jussive "let there be darkness" (יהי חשך) occurs only at Exod 10:21 and Job 3:4. Exod 10:22 has the narrative "and there was darkness" (phrase search; positive control Job 3:4 returned).
- Exod 10:22's אֲפֵלָה ("thick darkness") shares its root with Job 3:6's אֹפֶל ("darkness") — different lemmas, one root.

*Move 2 — Book usage.* Exodus has not been in the overview. The Job 2 dig found the only other זרק + הַשָּׁמַיְמָה ("throw toward heaven") at Exod 9:8, 10 — the sixth plague — behind the friends' gesture at 2:12.
- **Two plague-texts now stand in consecutive chapters of Job:** the sixth plague (boils; dust thrown toward heaven) behind chapter 2, and the ninth (darkness) behind chapter 3. `[T]` for each link.
- *Synthetic, and capped at moderate:* the pairing is a pattern, not a proof. It joins the prologue's cross-reference patterns listed for audit in the plan.

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.* Genesis 1 and Exodus 10 were already in conversation before Job: the plague of darkness undoes the first day over Egypt, and keeps the light for Israel (10:23). The canonical reader hears Gen 1:3 behind Exod 10:21. Job 3:4 stands on that junction. `[I]`

*What it adds.* The words Job uses to curse his day are the words God used to curse Egypt. Job calls down on his own birth the darkness that fell on Pharaoh's land. The man God called "my servant" (1:8; 2:3) speaks of himself as though he were Egypt. The friends will treat him as though he were (4:7–9). The reader knows from 2:3 that he is not. `[I]` *Moderate.*

**Jeremiah 20:14–18 → 3:1–11, 20** *(high for the link; direction open)* `[T]`

*Move 1 — Source context.*
- Jeremiah has been beaten and put in the stocks by Pashhur the priest (Jer 20:1–2). He complains that God has "deceived" him, and that the word of the LORD has become "a reproach and derision all day long" (20:7–8).
- He resolves not to speak, but the word is "like a burning fire shut up in my bones" (20:9).
- He turns to confidence ("the LORD is with me like a dread champion", 20:11) and to praise ("Sing to the LORD!", 20:13).
- Then, without transition, comes the curse: "Cursed be the day when I was born (אָרוּר הַיּוֹם אֲשֶׁר יֻלַּדְתִּי בּוֹ) … Cursed be the man who brought the news to my father, saying, 'A baby boy has been born to you!' … because he did not kill me before birth (לֹא־מוֹתְתַנִי מֵרָחֶם) … Why did I ever come forth from the womb (לָמָּה זֶּה מֵרֶחֶם יָצָאתִי) to look on trouble (עָמָל) and sorrow?" (20:14–18).
- The register is praise collapsing into the deepest lament in the prophets. It is the last of Jeremiah's "confessions".

*The verbal data* (lemma, WLC). The overview listed four; this run adds two.

| Shared element | Jeremiah 20 | Job 3 | Only? |
|---|---|---|---|
| ארר ("curse") | 20:14, 15 | 3:8 | the only ארר in Job |
| יוֹם + ילד + בּוֹ ("the day … I was born in it") | 20:14 | 3:3 | יוֹם + ילד + ארר in one verse: Jer 20:14 only |
| מות ("die") + מֵרֶחֶם ("from the womb") | 20:17 | 3:11 | only Num 12:12; Jer 20:17; Job 3:11 — **new** |
| הרה ("conceive / pregnant") | 20:17 (הֲרַת) | 3:3 (הֹרָה) | one root, two lemmas — **new** |
| לָמָּה + רֶחֶם + יצא | 20:18 | 3:11 | only Jer 20:18; Job 3:11; 10:18 |
| עָמָל ("trouble") | 20:18 | 3:10 (and עָמֵל 3:20) | — |
| קֶבֶר ("grave") | 20:17 | 3:22 | — |

*Move 2 — Book usage.* This is the first use of Jeremiah in Job. The overview maps Lamentations 3 as live (16:10, 12; 19:8). Job returns to this complaint at 10:18, where it becomes address to God. `[T]`

*Move 3 — OT-to-OT.*
- Jeremiah's curse undoes his own call. "Before I formed you in the womb (בַבֶּטֶן) I knew you, and before you came forth from the womb (מֵרֶחֶם) I consecrated you" (Jer 1:5). רֶחֶם and בֶּטֶן meet in one verse only at Jer 1:5, Job 3:11, 31:15, Ps 22:11 and Ps 58:4 (lemma).
- So Job 3:11 stands beside two texts that speak of the womb as the place of God's claim:
  - Jer 1:5, of the prophet's consecration;
  - Ps 22:11 [Eng 22:10], of the sufferer's trust: "from my mother's womb You have been my God".
- Job wishes he had died there. `[T]` for the data; `[I]` for the pattern.

*What it adds.*
- **The curse on one's birthday is the speech of a faithful prophet in the depths.** It sits beside his praise (Jer 20:13) and does not cancel his calling. Job's curse puts him in the company of the suffering righteous, not the wicked.
- **Direction of dependence is open.** The Nevi'im precede the Ketuvim, so the canonical reader meets Jeremiah first and hears Job as the prophet's echo. But the texts do not settle which is prior. `[I]`

**The sea-monster texts — Ps 74:13–17; Isa 27:1 → 3:8** *(moderate, Move 1)* `[T]`

- **Ps 74:13–17.** A lament over the ruined sanctuary turns to God's ancient kingship: "You divided the sea (יָם) … You broke the heads of the sea monsters in the waters. You crushed the heads of Leviathan … Yours is the day, Yours also is the night (לְךָ יוֹם אַף־לְךָ לָיְלָה); You have prepared the light and the sun" (74:13–16). In three verses the psalm puts together Leviathan crushed and day and night owned by God — two of Job 3's three subjects (3:3–8). `[T]` (lemma: לִוְיָתָן 74:14; יוֹם + לַיְלָה 74:16).
- **Isa 27:1.** "In that day the LORD will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent … and He will kill the dragon who lives in the sea". This is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible where יָם ("sea") and לִוְיָתָן meet (lemma co-occurrence) — relevant to the BHS conjecture at 3:8 (Textual Variants).
- *What it adds.* In the Psalter, Job's canonical neighbour, the God who crushes Leviathan is the God to whom day and night belong. Job's curse summons both: let the day be dark, let those who rouse Leviathan curse it. God's speeches answer in the psalm's terms. He owns the dawn (38:12), and Leviathan is his creature, whom no one dares to rouse (41:2 [Eng 41:10]). `[I]` *Moderate.* The magical background of "those prepared to rouse Leviathan" (day-cursers, sorcerers) is `[S]`.

**Psalm 22:2, 11 [Eng 22:1, 10] → 3:11, 24** *(moderate–high, synthetic; Move 1 and Move 2)* `[T]`

- *Move 1 — Source context.*
  - "My God, my God, why (לָמָה) have You forsaken me? Far from my deliverance are the words of my groaning (שַׁאֲגָתִי, literally 'roaring')" (22:2).
  - The psalmist then remembers: "You are He who brought me forth from the womb (מִבָּטֶן) … Upon You I was cast from birth (מֵרָחֶם); You have been my God from my mother's womb (מִבֶּטֶן אִמִּי)" (22:10–11).
  - It is the lament of the righteous sufferer, and it ends in praise (22:23–32).
- *The verbal data.*
  - שַׁאֲגָה ("roaring") occurs in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isa 5:29; Ezek 19:7; Zech 11:3; Ps 22:2; 32:3; Job 3:24; 4:10.
  - רֶחֶם + בֶּטֶן meet in one verse only at five places, Ps 22:11 and Job 3:11 among them.
  - All by lemma.
- *Move 2 — Book usage.* The Job 2 dig found Ps 22:16 [Eng 22:15]'s potsherd at 2:8. With 3:11 and 3:24, Psalm 22 touches Job three times in two chapters. **The Psalter's neighbour is live.** It is not yet in the overview's map.
- *What it adds.* Job roars as the forsaken psalmist roared. But the psalmist's womb is the place where God was already "my God"; Job's is the place he wishes he had died. The same words carry opposite postures, and the book does not rebuke Job for his (42:7). `[I]`
- *Ps 32:3* — "When I kept silent about my sin, my body wasted away through my groaning (בְּשַׁאֲגָתִי) all day long". The psalmist's roaring came from unconfessed sin; Job's has no such cause (2:3). *Contrast; moderate.*

**Deuteronomy 28:60 → 3:25** *(moderate, Move 1)* `[T]`

- "He will bring back on you all the diseases of Egypt of which you were afraid (אֲשֶׁר יָגֹרְתָּ), and they will cling to you" (Deut 28:60), in the covenant curses.
- יגר ("dread") occurs in five verses only: Deut 9:19; 28:60; Job 3:25; 9:28; Ps 119:39 (lemma).

- *What it adds.* Job speaks of his calamity in the covenant-curse's vocabulary of dread, as he wears its boils. The friends will draw Deuteronomy's conclusion. The reader, who has heard "without cause" (2:3), may not. `[I]` *Moderate* (one rare verb; the pattern is synthetic).

**Isaiah 40:27–28 → 3:23** *(moderate, Move 1)* `[T]`

- "Why (לָמָּה) do you say, O Jacob … 'My way is hidden (נִסְתְּרָה דַרְכִּי) from the LORD, and the justice due me escapes the notice of my God'? Do you not know? … The Everlasting God, the LORD, the Creator of the ends of the earth, does not become weary or tired. His understanding is inscrutable (אֵין חֵקֶר)" (Isa 40:27–28).

- *What it adds.* The complaint of a hidden way is answered in Isaiah by the Creator, as Job's will be answered by the Creator from the storm. "Inscrutable" (אֵין חֵקֶר) is Eliphaz's word (5:9) and Job's (9:10). *Moderate.*

**Amos 5:8 → 3:5–9** *(moderate, Move 1)* — "He who made the Pleiades and Orion, and changes deep darkness (צַלְמָוֶת) into morning (בֹּקֶר), who also darkens (הֶחְשִׁיךְ) day (יוֹם) into night (לַיְלָה) … the LORD is His name". A doxology of the Creator who does to day and night, at will, what Job wished on his: darkens day, and turns צַלְמָוֶת into morning. `[T]` (lemma). *Moderate.*

**Psalm 88:6 [Eng 88:5] → 3:19** *(moderate, Move 1)* — "Forsaken among the dead (בַּמֵּתִים חָפְשִׁי, literally 'free among the dead'), like the slain who lie in the grave (קֶבֶר)". The darkest psalm, already live in Job (overview: 19:13–14), gives Sheol's "freedom" as abandonment. Job gives it as release (3:19). חָפְשִׁי is otherwise the law's word for a slave's release (Exod 21; Deut 15; Jer 34), with 1 Sam 17:25 and Isa 58:6; only Ps 88:6 and Job 3:19 use it of the dead, and Job 39:5 of the wild donkey (lemma). `[T]` *Moderate.*

**Numbers 12:12 → 3:11, 16** *(uncertain)* — "Do not let her be like one dead, whose flesh is half eaten away when he comes from his mother's womb (מֵרֶחֶם אִמּוֹ)", Aaron's plea for Miriam. It is one of the three verses with מות + מֵרֶחֶם. A stillborn image, as at Job 3:16. Shared idiom; no design claimed.

**Handoff — Ecclesiastes 6:3–5; 7:1; 4:2–3** *(moderate–high on the words; direction open)* `[T]`

- Eccl 6:3–5: "I say, 'Better the miscarriage (הַנֵּפֶל) than he, for it comes in futility and goes into obscurity (בַחֹשֶׁךְ) … It never sees the sun (שֶׁמֶשׁ לֹא רָאָה) … it is better off (literally, has more rest, נַחַת) than he.'"

- *What it adds.* In the BHS order, Ecclesiastes comes after Job, Proverbs, Ruth and the Song. Its reader meets Job's miscarriage, darkness, unseen light and rest again, turned from anguish into sober reflection. With Eccl 5:14 ← Job 1:21 (Job 1 dig), this is the **second verified handoff from Job to Ecclesiastes**. `[T]` for the words; direction open.

*Addressee-differentiation.*
- **Parties from the prologue.** The Accuser (never named in chapter 3, or again); the wife (absent); the friends (present and silent; not addressed); God (named twice, not addressed).
- **Of the three parties who could have been addressed — the friends, God and the reader — Job addresses none.** The friends answer anyway (4:1); God answers in chapter 38.
- **From chapter 6 Job begins to address the friends, and from 7:7 God.** Chapter 3 is the only speech in the book with no second person (claim audit `[S: audit]`; mechanically confirmed here). `[T]`

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew poetry: a lament in the form of a curse and a series of questions, set inside a prose frame.** `[T]`

- **Embedded forms.**
  - *A birthday curse (3:3–10).* Jer 20:14–18 is the only other in the canon.
  - *A "why" lament (3:11–26).* Compare Ps 22:2; Ps 88; Lam 3.
  - *A description of Sheol as rest (3:13–19).* Compare Ps 88:6; Eccl 4:2–3; 6:3–5.
- **Reading rules.**
  - It is poetry, so the images are not metaphysics. Job does not teach that Sheol is a place of blessed rest; he says that death would be better than this.
  - It is character speech, not the narrator's verdict.
  - It is lament, and lament is not resolved within the poem. The reader must wait.
  - It is addressed to no one. It is not yet prayer, and not yet argument; it is the raw material of both.
- **What the genre does not license.**
  - It does not make magic real (3:8).
  - It does not teach that the dead are "free" (3:19).
  - It does not approve the death-wish (3:20–22).

  It reports them as the speech of a man in anguish, and the book later judges that man to have spoken rightly *to* God (42:7). `[I]`

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross. Three things have happened between Job 3 and us. `[I]`
- **The Son has spoken Ps 22:2 from the cross:** "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Mark 15:34; SBLGNT). The "why" of the forsaken righteous has been spoken by the Righteous One, and answered by resurrection.
- **The Son has said, "My soul is deeply grieved, to the point of death"** (Matt 26:38, περίλυπος … ἕως θανάτου). The longing of the righteous in anguish is not foreign to him.
- **The Son has offered the rest Job sought in death:** "Come to Me, all who are weary (οἱ κοπιῶντες) and heavy-laden, and I will give you rest (ἀναπαύσω)" (Matt 11:28). Swete's Job 3:17 has "there the weary (κατάκοποι) rested (ἀνεπαύσαντο)". Shared vocabulary, no citation; *moderate*, conceptual.

**How it reframes the reading.** The Christian may not preach Job 3 as though the "why" had no answer in history. But the Christian must not hurry to the answer either. Job waited thirty-five chapters, and the church still prays Ps 22 and Ps 88 in the dark. `[I]`

### 15. Who Am I?

| Speaker / figure | Function |
|---|---|
| **Job** | The **righteous sufferer** speaking honestly in the dark. **Representative** of every believer who has wished not to exist. An **example** — of the fact that such speech need not be blasphemy, and that it can be turned into prayer (10:18) — not of cursing one's day as a technique. His pattern runs to Christ, who spoke the forsaken psalmist's "why" (Christological Reading) |
| **The reader** | Still in heaven's gallery. We know "without cause" (2:3); Job does not. We are asked to hear chapter 3 without judging it as the friends will |
| **The friends** | Silent listeners here — and a warning. What they do next with this speech (4:1–5:27) is the book's first lesson in how not to answer a lament |
| **God** | Named and unaddressed; the one who "hedged in" (3:23). Not yet speaking — and when he does, answering this chapter point by point |
| **The dead** (3:14–19) | Not examples; figures in Job's imagination of rest |

**The "Job-is-me" trap here is the opposite of the usual one.** Congregations are more likely to distance themselves from Job 3 ("I would never speak like that") than to identify with it. The text invites identification for the suffering, and patience from everyone else. `[I]`

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** To hear that a righteous man may say this — curse his day, ask why he was born, long for death — without cursing God, and without being condemned by God (42:7). And to learn to listen to such speech without answering it too quickly. `[T]` / `[I]`

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | Lament is part of faith's language, not its failure. God is big enough to be spoken of in anguish, and later spoken to. The darkness Job calls down is not beyond God; he brings deep darkness into light (12:22) and owns the day and the night (Ps 74:16) |
| **Stop** | Treating lament as unbelief. Silencing the suffering with explanations (the friends' error, 4:1ff.). Preaching Job 3 as a warning. Hearing "what I feared has come upon me" as a law of spiritual cause and effect |
| **Start** | Letting the suffering speak without interruption. Turning our own darkest speech toward God, as Job does at 10:18. Using the Psalter's laments (22; 88) as the church's permission and vocabulary |
| **Motivation** | Not "be as honest as Job" as a technique. God is the one who answers lament, as he answered Job (38:1), and he has answered it finally in the Son, who spoke the lament of the forsaken and rose again |

**Four audiences.**
- **For me:** Have I ever let myself say what Job says here to God? Or do I keep my darkness from him, and so from prayer?
- **For a Christian friend:** When someone near me says "I wish I had never been born", my first calling is to stay and listen, as the friends did for seven days — and then not to do what they did next. And to make sure they are safe, and help them find help.
- **For the church:** Make room in our gatherings for lament. Pray Ps 88 aloud as well as Ps 23.
- **For an unbeliever:** The Bible does not hide from despair. It gives it a voice, and then it gives the answer God spoke from the storm, and finally from the cross.

**Prayer.**
- Thanks that God has given words for the darkness.
- Confession of the times we have hurried others past their lament, or hidden our own from God.
- A request for the courage to bring our "why" to God.
- Mercy for all who wish they had never been born.

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**
- **Sixteen wishes, and no one addressed (3:3–9).** Eight verbs are parsed jussive in the WLC morphology and eight are imperfects with jussive force. All sixteen are third person: Job speaks about the day, the night, the darkness and God, and to none of them. There is no second person anywhere in the chapter (WLC morphology). `[T]`
- **The unreal perfects (3:13).** כִּי־עַתָּה שָׁכַבְתִּי וְאֶשְׁקוֹט יָשַׁנְתִּי אָז יָנוּחַ לִי ("For now I would have lain down and been quiet; I would have slept then, I would have been at rest"). Perfects of what would have been, if he had died. `[T]`
- **The elided question (3:23).** לְגֶבֶר אֲשֶׁר־דַּרְכּוֹ נִסְתָּרָה ("to a man whose way is hidden") has no verb. It borrows "Why is light given" from 3:20. The English versions supply "light" (NASB95, ESV) or "life" (NIV84). `[T]`
- **The cognate accusative (3:25).** פַחַד פָּחַדְתִּי ("a dread I dreaded"): emphasis. `[T]`
- **3:26 — the last line.** Three perfects negated, then a wayyiqtol unnegated: לֹא שָׁלַוְתִּי וְלֹא שָׁקַטְתִּי וְלֹא־נָחְתִּי וַיָּבֹא רֹגֶז. The sequence of three denials and one arrival carries the ending. `[T]`

- **יוֹם / יָם ("day / sea", 3:8).** Only if the BHS conjecture is accepted (see Textual Variants). The Hebrew as it stands has "day".
- **קלל ("be light", 3:1) and קַלֹּתִי ("I am slight", 40:4).** One lexeme; the echo is semantic, not a pun.
- **The night "said" (אָמַר, 3:3).** Personification rather than sound.

### Textual Variants

**Variation inside the Hebrew** (BHS apparatus for chapter 3, *as BHS prints it*):
- **3:5 כִּמְרִירֵי יוֹם ("the blackness of the day").**
  - *The issue:* the form is unique (lemma 3650, only here). BHS proposes reading the כ as the preposition "like", with a noun כמריר, "blackness". It cross-refers a Qumran reading with ב.
  - *The options:* "the blackness of the day" (NASB95, ESV); or, with a different root (מרר, "bitter"), "the bitternesses of the day" `[S]`.
  - *Preaching implication:* none. Every option is a darkening of the day.
  - *Confidence:* moderate; *uncertain* on the exact sense.
- **3:6 אַל־יִחַדְּ ("let it not rejoice").**
  - *The issue:* the Masoretic vocalisation takes the verb from חדה ("rejoice"). The Greek has εἴη ("may it be") and the Vulgate *computetur* ("let it be counted"). BHS proposes יֵחַד ("let it not be joined [to the days of the year]", from יחד).
  - *Most likely original:* the consonants are secure; the question is the vowels. "Be joined" fits the calendar image of 3:6b. "Rejoice" fits 3:7 ("let no joyful shout enter it"). NASB95 and ESV follow the Masoretic vowels; NIV84 the proposal.
  - *Preaching implication:* small. The ESV's "rejoice" is the Masoretic reading.
  - *Confidence:* moderate.
- **3:8 אֹרְרֵי־יוֹם ("those who curse the day").**
  - *The issue:* BHS: *orig frt* יָם ("originally perhaps 'sea'"). This would make "those who curse the Sea", a pairing with Leviathan (Isa 27:1 is the only verse where יָם and לִוְיָתָן meet; Job 7:12 pairs "sea" with "sea monster").
  - *Evidence:* no manuscript reads יָם. It is an editor's conjecture. Swete reads "day" (τὴν ἡμέραν). NIV84 puts "the sea" in its margin.
  - *Most likely original:* יוֹם ("day"), as transmitted and as the Greek read it. It also fits the chapter, in which the day is cursed six times.
  - *Preaching implication:* preach "day". Mention the monster-pairing only as colour.
  - *Confidence:* moderate–high for the Masoretic text.
- **3:22 אֱלֵי־גִיל ("to exultation").**
  - *The issue:* BHS proposes גַל ("heap", i.e. the grave-mound): "who rejoice at the heap".
  - *Evidence:* no manuscript support noted. All the English versions translate the Masoretic text.
  - *Confidence:* moderate for the Masoretic text; the proposal makes a neat parallel with "the grave" (3:22b).
- **Paragraphing.** A petuḥah after 3:1 and after 3:26, and none within 3:2–26, in Leningrad as BHS prints it. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`. Only the petuḥah after 3:1 carries any weight in this report (Unit 1 report, Open Question 1).
- **Ketiv.** None in chapter 3. This was checked mechanically: no unpointed word in the chapter.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** The story's world is patriarchal and outside Israel (overview). The composition date is `[S]` and not needed here.

**What the original audience knew.**
1. **Birthday curses.** The canonical reader knew Jeremiah's (Jer 20:14–18). Cursing the day of one's birth was the most extreme form of lament available. `[T]` for Jeremiah; the wider ancient Near Eastern practice is `[S]`.
2. **Day-cursers and Leviathan.** "Those who curse the day, who are prepared to rouse Leviathan" (3:8) assumes a world in which people were believed to be able to blight days by incantation and to stir the chaos-monster. Job borrows the image without endorsing it. `[S]` for the magical practice; the monster is `[T]` in the canon (Ps 74:14; 104:26; Isa 27:1).
3. **Sheol.** The dead in the Hebrew Bible go to a place of silence and shadow (Ps 88:6, 11–13 [Eng 88:5, 10–12]), where kings and slaves alike lie. Job's picture of it as "rest" (3:13, 17) is the wish of the weary. Elsewhere the same place is abandonment (Ps 88). `[T]` for the texts.
4. **Birth.** "The knees" that received the newborn (3:12) are the knees of the father or the midwife acknowledging the child (compare Gen 30:3; 50:23) `[S]`, *moderate*. The breasts are the mother's.
5. **Kings and their ruins (3:14).** "Who rebuilt ruins for themselves" — or "who built tombs (חֳרָבוֹת)" — the great of the earth with their monuments `[S]`. The point is that the greatest men lie in the same rest as the stillborn (3:16).

**What this changes.** Job's curse is not a spontaneous obscenity. It is a known form — the prophet's form — taken to its limit, with the monster-lore of his world as imagery. The canonical reader who knew Jeremiah would hear a righteous man in the depths, not a blasphemer. `[I]`

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Readers of the Writings who have the Torah and the Prophets behind them, including Jeremiah's confessions and the exile, and who have just read the Psalter's laments. `[I]`

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- **Shocking:** the man who said "Blessed be the name of the LORD" (1:21) now says "Let the day perish". The man God called "my servant" speaks the plague on Egypt over his own birth. `[T]`
- **Surprising:** he still does not curse God. The Accuser loses the wager in the very chapter that sounds most like its fulfilment. `[T]`
- **Comforting:** the words of a prophet (Jer 20) and of the Psalter (Ps 22; 88) are available to the sufferer. Speaking them is not the end of faith. `[I]`
- **Disturbing:** the chapter ends without answer, and the friends' answer (4:1ff.) will be worse than silence. `[I]`

**What we bring that they didn't.**
- A therapeutic instinct that treats the speech as a symptom to be managed.
- A pietistic instinct that treats it as sin to be rebuked.
- A modern cultural silence about death, which makes the longing of 3:20–22 unspeakable in church.
- Knowledge of suicide as a public-health subject (see Difficult Verses).

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**(1) Creation and un-creation.**
- **Before:** Gen 1:2–5, 14; the plague of darkness (Exod 10:21–23); Jeremiah's vision of the earth "formless and void" (Jer 4:23).
- **After:** God's speeches, which take Job back to creation (38–41); "the darkness is passing away and the true light is already shining" (1 John 2:8); "there will no longer be any night" (Rev 22:5).
- **What it contributes:** Job's curse is the most personal un-creation in the canon. God's answer re-creates Job's world by showing him its Maker.
- **Confidence:** high.

**(2) The righteous sufferer's lament.**
- **Before:** Jer 20:14–18; Ps 22; Ps 88.
- **After:** Christ in Gethsemane (Matt 26:38) and on the cross (Mark 15:34, citing Ps 22:2); the martyrs' "How long?" (Rev 6:10).
- **What it contributes:** Lament addressed to no one (Job 3) becomes lament addressed to God (Job 10). The Psalter already does this, and the Son does it perfectly.
- **Confidence:** high.

**(3) Rest.**
- **Before:** the Sabbath rest of creation (Gen 2:2–3, a different verb, שׁבת); the rest in the land.
- **After:** "Come to Me … I will give you rest" (Matt 11:28); the Sabbath rest that remains (Heb 4:9–11).
- **What it contributes:** Job looks for rest in death (3:13, 17). The canon moves it to God, and finally to Christ.
- **Confidence:** moderate (the verbal links are via the Greek and conceptual).

**Relation.** *Climactic convergence.* The one who undoes the darkness (1) is the one who spoke the lament of the forsaken (2), and gives the weary the rest Job sought in the grave (3). The three meet in Christ. `[I]`

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection:** trajectory (the righteous sufferer's lament), contrast, and a pastoral line through the Greek.

**How the passage points to Christ.**
- **The lament of the righteous, spoken by the Righteous One.**
  - Job 3:24's "roaring" is the noun of Ps 22:2, "My God, my God, why …?". Christ prays that psalm from the cross (Mark 15:34, SBLGNT: Ἐλωῒ ἐλωῒ λεμὰ σαβαχθάνι). Job's "why" (3:11, 20) belongs to the lament tradition Christ enters.
  - *Tests:* category passes (the righteous sufferer); NT precedent is strong for the category (the passion psalms) and absent for Job 3 specifically; escalation passes (Christ's forsakenness is the real one); authorial pattern is partial (Job 3 shares Ps 22's vocabulary).
  - *Moderate.*
- **Contrast — the mouth that opened, and the mouth that did not.**
  - "Job opened (פָּתַח) his mouth (פִּיהוּ)" (3:1). The servant "was oppressed (נִגַּשׂ) … yet He did not open (לֹא יִפְתַּח) His mouth" (Isa 53:7). נגשׂ is the taskmaster of 3:18.
  - The idiom is common (25 verses), so the link is conceptual. Job opened his mouth to curse his day. The Servant, under the taskmaster's hand, kept silent and bore it.
  - *Contrast; moderate* (1 Pet 2:23 draws the same contrast of Christ with the reviled).
- **Rest.** The rest Job sought in death (Swete 3:17: "there the weary rested") is offered by Christ to the weary in life (Matt 11:28). *Moderate*, conceptual, via the Greek.
- **The Redeemer.** Job asks darkness to "claim" (גאל) his day (3:5). Later he confesses "my Redeemer (גֹּאֲלִי) lives" (19:25). The New Testament names the Redeemer (Gal 3:13; Titus 2:14). *Moderate* (the sense at 3:5 is disputed).

**Trajectory.**
- **What the passage longs for:** rest; release from the taskmaster; an end to the turmoil; darkness claiming the day; an answer to "why".
- **What Christ fulfils:** he enters the lament (Ps 22 on the cross); he gives rest (Matt 11:28); he is the Redeemer (19:25); he is the light the darkness did not overcome (John 1:5).
- **What still remains:** the end of night (Rev 22:5), and the church's patience in lament meanwhile.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Be honest like Job" as a technique of emotional health. Or the reverse: "Don't be like Job in chapter 3".
- **The gospel grounding:** God receives the lament of the righteous because he has himself, in the Son, borne the forsakenness the lament names, and answered it.
- **Christ as hero:** Christ is not a better lamenter to imitate. He is the one whose forsakenness makes our lament safe to speak, and whose resurrection answers it.

**Confidence.** *Moderate* throughout. No direct prophecy; the trajectory runs through the righteous-sufferer category and Ps 22.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**3:20–22 — the longing for death.**
- **Category:** pastoral — a real landmine, especially at the bedside and in the care home.
- **The difficulty:** Job speaks of "those who long for death, but there is none, and dig for it more than for hidden treasures, who rejoice greatly when they find the grave".
- **Rhetorical function in context:** a question addressed to no one — "Why is light given …?" — not a plan. Job longs for death; he does not seek to take his own life. He speaks the longing aloud, and it becomes prayer (10:18).
- **Common mishandlings:**
  - skipping the verses;
  - rebuking the longing as sin;
  - romanticising death as rest.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text reports the longing without approving it. God's verdict (42:7) is on a man who spoke it. The book's answer is not death but God himself (42:5).
- **Handling:**
  - Name the difference between longing for death and acting on it, gently.
  - Commend speaking the darkness to God and to trusted others.
  - Point to help — pastoral, medical, a helpline — without promising what that help will involve.
  - Be aware of those in the room.

**3:1–10 — is cursing one's day sin?**
- **Category:** doctrinal / pastoral.
- **Options:**
  - (a) sin, later repented of (42:6);
  - (b) lament, not sin, since the object is the day and God does not rebuke it as such (42:7).
- **Honest exegesis:** the narrator gives no verdict at 3:1 (contrast 1:22; 2:10). God's rebuke (38:2) is of words "without knowledge", not of lament. 42:6's "I retract" has no object in the Hebrew (overview, trap 6).
- **Handling:** do not pronounce chapter 3 sinful. Do not pronounce it exemplary either. Let 42:7 stand.

**3:8 — Leviathan and "those who curse the day".**
- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** Does Job believe in magic and sea-monsters?
- **Honest exegesis:** it is poetic imagery drawn from the beliefs of his world, used to intensify the curse. The canon uses Leviathan as a figure of chaos subject to God (Ps 74:14; 104:26).
- **Handling:** a sentence at most. Do not identify Leviathan with Satan here — the text does not.

**3:25 — "What I fear comes upon me".**
- **Category:** weaponised.
- **The difficulty:** the verse is used in "positive confession" teaching — fear attracts calamity; speak faith, not fear.
- **Honest exegesis:** 2:3 says the calamity came "without cause" and by permission in heaven, not because of Job's fear. The verse reports Job's experience: the thing he dreaded has happened. The cognate construction intensifies the dread; it does not describe a mechanism.
- **Handling:** name and refute the misuse if the congregation is likely to have met it.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: Preaching chapter 3 as Job's fall from faith

- **What it looks like:** "In chapter 1 Job trusted; in chapter 3 he cracked. Don't let suffering make you bitter."
- **Why it's wrong:**
  - The object of the curse is the day, not God (3:1). The Accuser's wager is lost here, not won.
  - The narrator gives no verdict.
  - God's final verdict is that Job, not the friends, spoke what is right (42:7) — the man who spoke chapter 3.
  - Jeremiah spoke the same curse and remained God's prophet.
- **The corrective:** preach chapter 3 as faith's darkest speech, not faith's end. Hold 1:21 and 3:3 together.

### Pitfall: "What you fear will come upon you" (3:25)

- **What it looks like:** Job's fear opened the door to calamity; speak faith, not fear.
- **Why it's wrong:** 2:3 says the calamity came "without cause". The verse describes what Job dreaded and what happened; it states no law of cause and effect. Linking it to 1:5 is an inference at best.
- **The corrective:** if the congregation has met the teaching, name it and refute it from 2:3.

### Pitfall: Hurrying to the answer, or preaching the lament cheerfully

- **What it looks like:** ten minutes on chapter 3, then "But God answered from the whirlwind…"; or a bright application ("Job teaches us to be honest with our feelings!").
- **Why it's wrong:** the book makes the reader wait thirty-five chapters. Chapter 3 ends on "turmoil", and what follows is Eliphaz, not God.
- **The corrective:** let the sermon stay in the dark long enough to be believed. Name the answer (38:8; 12:22; Christ) without rushing to it, and end with the permission to lament.

### Pitfall: Ignoring or mishandling the death-wish (3:20–22)

- **What it looks like:** skipping the verses; or treating them as a sin to repent of; or speaking of death as rest in a way that sounds like comfort to the suicidal.
- **Why it's wrong:** some who hear the sermon will know this longing. The text neither approves the wish nor silences it.
- **The corrective:** name the difference between longing and acting, gently. Commend speaking the darkness to God (as Job does at 10:18) and to others. Point to help, and know where it is before the service.

### Pitfall: Answering the lament as the friends did

- **What it looks like:** the sermon moves at once to what Job should have said, or to reasons for his suffering.
- **Why it's wrong:** that is Eliphaz's move (4:1ff.). He hears Job's roar as a lion's and says it will be silenced (4:10). God condemns the friends (42:7).
- **The corrective:** preach the silence of 2:13 as the friends' best moment, and chapter 3 as the speech they should have heard. Apply it to how the church listens.
