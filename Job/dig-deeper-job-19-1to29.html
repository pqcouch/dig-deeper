Dig Deeper: Job 19:1–29

Primary texts: - Hebrew: BHS (Logos export, _texts/logos-exports/01-Old-Testament/03-Ketuvim/02-Job-BHS.txt), read first, with the BHS apparatus for chapter 19 (02-Job-BHS-App.txt). The WLC lemma and morphology index (_texts/hebrew-wlc/) is used for every count and chain. - Greek: Swete (_texts/greek-lxx-swete/) for the wording, and the Rahlfs export (02-Job-LXX.txt) for the Hexaplaric signs. The two agree in verse numbering for this chapter. - New Testament: SBLGNT for every Greek check.

The text crux. The series plan asked for the BHS apparatus and Rahlfs at 19:25–27 to be in hand before the run. Both were already in the _texts exports and were read first. BHS prints no note at 19:25 or 19:26, and at 19:27 records only that one manuscript lacks two words. The Greek reads a different text (see Textual Variants). The crux is one of grammar and meaning, not of the Hebrew wording.

Study text: NASB95 (Logos export). Pulpit text: ESV, declared for the series on 28 September 2026 and confirmed for this run. Every ESV quotation below was checked against the Logos export; NIV84 renderings come from its export. Date: 5 October 2026

Book-overview context: Job/book-overview-job.md (v0.1.1), front-loaded at Phase 0.5. Neighbouring reports consulted at the checking stage: - the sweep’s Unit 9, 18:1–19:29 (Job/dig-deeper-job-sweep.md); - the Unit 1 report on 1:1–3:1, the opening unit and so a permanent neighbour; - the Job 3 report (3:5 גאל); - the consolidation report on 4:1–14:22, with its solo digs on 4–5, 9–10 and 13–14; - the 16:1–17:16 report (v. 5 October, with the Logos rounds 4–5 corrections); - claim audit 2; - the Logos assessments of rounds 2–5 (Andersen, Lange with Delitzsch and Dillmann, Barnes and Laurin on 19:25–26 in round 2).

Series context: the second of the two HIGH solo digs inside Sermon 4 (15:1–21:34). The unit report for Sermon 4 will be a consolidation dig on 15:1–21:34.

Extent. - The speech. Job’s reply to Bildad’s second speech (18:1–21) runs from 19:1 to 19:29. BHS prints no paragraph marker inside it. - Chapter 18. This dig works 19:1–29, the unit the series plan names. It reads chapter 18 as the speech Job is answering, because Job answers its first and last lines (Headline 5). - Chapters 16–17. It reads Job’s previous speech as the case he is continuing: the two speeches share three words found nowhere else in Job (Headline 5).

Warrant tags: - [T] derivable from the text itself; - [I] a reasonable inference from the text; - [S] supplied by a secondary source and held provisionally; - [S: audit] a verdict of this project’s claim audits, accepted.

Counts and chains. - Method. Every count is a count in the Hebrew, made against the WLC lemma index (by lemma, never by surface form) unless it says “phrase search”. Phrase searches normalise maqqef, paseq and sof pasuq, strip the pointing, and run a second skeletal pass. - Ketiv. Chapter 19 carries one ketiv, 19:29 שדין (qere שַׁדּוּן, “that there is judgement”), found mechanically. No chain depends on it. - Positive controls. Every “only” was run against a positive control: the matcher’s control is חֶסֶד (“kindness”) in Job, which returns 6:14, 10:12 and 37:13. - Lemma splits. Four homograph splits inside the chapter were met and used, because each is a sound-play: - זוּר I (“be a stranger”, 19:13, 15, 27) and זוּר II (“be loathsome”, 19:17); - חנן I (“be gracious, implore”, 19:16, 21) and חנן II (“be loathsome”, 19:17); - נקף II (“go round, surround”, 19:6) and נקף I (“strike off”, 19:26); - גוּר I (“sojourn”, 19:15) and גוּר III (“be afraid”, 19:29). - Baselines. Three “is this source distinctive?” questions were tested by ranking every chapter of the Hebrew Bible (887, Job excluded) by the rare lemmas (in 150 verses or fewer) that its best window shares with a span of Job 19. A fourth test ranked all 120 pairs of speeches in Job 4–27 by shared rare vocabulary. The results are reported where they bear, including the ones that came back negative. - The log is ~/j19/chk_helpers.py, base.py and the runs recorded with them.

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims, each surfaced by several tools:

  1. “I know” is the chapter’s leading word. Bildad closed by consigning Job to “him who does not know God”; Job answers with five uses of ידע (“know”), from the acquaintances who no longer know him to “I know that my Redeemer lives”. [T] for the words; [I] for the design
    • ידע (“know”) occurs five times in nineteen verses (WLC, lemma 3045):
      • “Know then (דְּעוּ־אֵפוֹ) that God has wronged me” (19:6);
      • “my acquaintances (וְיֹדְעַי, ‘those who know me’) are completely estranged from me” (19:13);
      • “my intimate friends (וּמְיֻדָּעַי, ‘those known to me’) have forgotten me” (19:14);
      • “As for me, I know (וַאֲנִי יָדַעְתִּי) that my Redeemer lives” (19:25);
      • “so that you may know (תֵּדְעוּן) there is judgment” (19:29).
    • It answers Bildad’s last line. “Surely such are the dwellings of the wicked, and this is the place of him who does not know God (לֹא־יָדַע־אֵל)” (18:21). The chapter’s first ידע is the reply: “Know then that God (אֱלוֹהַּ) has wronged me” (19:6). Bildad’s verb and Bildad’s noun, turned. [T] for the words; [I] for the answer. High.
    • The stranger turns. זוּר I (“be a stranger”) runs through the list of the estranged: “completely estranged (זָרוּ)” (19:13); “consider me a stranger (לְזָר)” (19:15). Its third and last use is in the confession: “whom my eyes will see and not another (וְלֹא־זָר, ‘and not a stranger’)” (19:27). In Job the lemma occurs otherwise only at 15:19 (lemma 2114 a). 19:17’s זָרָה (“my breath is offensive”) is a homograph, זוּר II, so the count is three — the sweep’s correction stands. High.
    • Job’s other “I know” sayings. יָדַעְתִּי (“I know”) with an object clause occurs in his speeches at 9:2, 9:28, 10:13, 13:18, 19:25 and 30:23 (and in the qere of 42:2). Every one except 19:25 and 30:23 introduces its clause with כִּי (“that”). The two without it are a pair, and both end on חַי (“living”):
      • “I know: my Redeemer lives (גֹּאֲלִי חָי)” (19:25);
      • “I know: You will bring me to death and to the house of meeting for all living (לְכָל־חָי)” (30:23).
      • Lange’s observation that no כִּי follows יָדַעְתִּי at 19:25 (Logos round 2) is confirmed, and the corpus gives it a partner. [T]
    • Those who knew him return. “Brothers” (אָח) with ידע (“know”) occurs in Job only at 19:13 (“He has removed my brothers far from me, and my acquaintances …”) and 42:11 (“all his brothers and all his sisters and all who had known him (יֹדְעָיו) before came to him”) (lemma). The estrangement of 19:13 is reversed, in its own words, in the epilogue. [T] High. New to the Echo Table.
    • Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Move 4, Original Languages, Structure. High on the words; moderate–high on the design.
  2. The Redeemer “lives” and “will rise on the dust”. Each word reaches back into Job and out into the canon: גאל to 3:5, “the last” to Isa 44:6, “rise” to the witness of 16:8 and the sleeper of 14:12, and “live … rise … dust” to Isa 26:19. [T] for each verbal link; [I] for the composite
    • גאל (“redeem”) occurs in Job only at 3:5 (“let darkness … claim it”, יִגְאָלֻהוּ) and 19:25 (גֹּאֲלִי, “my Redeemer”) (lemma 1350 a). Logos round 2 found the sense “reclaim, redeem” at 3:5 (Gesenius; TLOT) and a history of interpretation that cross-refers the two (Lange; Barnes). Queue #8’s trigger is satisfied for this dig. [T] for the data; [S] for the lexicons. Moderate–high on the sense; moderate on design.
    • “Redeemer” with “last” (גֹּאֵל + אַחֲרוֹן) meet in one verse only at Isa 44:6 and Job 19:25 (lemma): “Thus says the LORD, the King of Israel and his Redeemer (וְגֹאֲלוֹ) … ‘I am the first and I am the last (אַחֲרוֹן)’”. The one other text that pairs the words names the Redeemer as the LORD (sweep; confirmed). High on the words; moderate on what it implies.
    • “Rise” (קום). The same form יָקוּם (“he will rise”) stood at 14:12: “So man lies down and does not rise (וְלֹא־יָקוּם)”. At 19:25 the Redeemer “at the last … will rise (יָקוּם) upon the dust”. In Job’s speeches קום also has a court sense: at 16:8 his leanness “rises up (וַיָּקָם) against me” as a witness, the verb of the witness law (“A single witness shall not rise up (יָקוּם) against a man”, Deut 19:15; 19:16). So the Redeemer stands up where the sleeper could not rise, and where the hostile witness rose. [T] for the forms; [I] for the reading. Moderate–high.
    • “Live … rise … dust.” The three together — life (חיה / חַי), rising (קום) and dust (עָפָר) — occur in one verse only at Isa 26:19 and Job 19:25 (lemma; verb and adjective counted together): “Your dead will live (יִחְיוּ); their corpses will rise (יְקוּמוּן). You who lie in the dust (עָפָר), awake and shout for joy”. Isa 26:19 also has “awake” (קיץ), the verb Job denied at 14:12 (“he will not awake”). קיץ with קום occurs only at Hab 2:7, Isa 26:19 and Job 14:12 (lemma). High on the words; the direction and the design are open. This extends queue #26 (Isa 26:19–21 at 14:12–14 and 16:18): Isaiah’s three consecutive verses now meet Job at 14:12–13, 16:18 and 19:25. [I] Moderate (synthetic).
    • “Upon the dust.” עַל־עָפָר in Job is the place where the dead lie: “it lies down with him in the dust (עַל־עָפָר)” (20:11); “together they lie down in the dust” (21:26); “shall we together go down into the dust (עַל־עָפָר)?” (17:16) (phrase search). The Redeemer stands over the grave. The ESV and NASB95 “on the earth” lose this; the NIV84 margin “upon my grave” keeps it. [T] High.
    • The kinsman’s two duties. The גֹּאֵל buys back and avenges blood. Deut 19 holds both of the institutions Job has invoked: the avenger of blood (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם, 19:6, 12) and the malicious witness who “rises up” (19:15–19), which the 16–17 dig found behind 16:8 (queue #60). Job asked the earth not to cover his blood (16:18); now he names the one whose office it is to answer for blood. Ps 72:14 is the only verse in which גאל, חָמָס (“violence”) and דָּם (“blood”) meet: the king “will rescue (יִגְאַל) their life from oppression and violence (מֵחָמָס), and their blood (דָּמָם) will be precious in his sight”. In Job חָמָס occurs only at 16:17 and 19:7. [T] for the data; [I] for the connection. Moderate.
    • Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Move 4, Tool 11, Historical Background, Christological Reading. High on the words; moderate on the composite.
  3. Job wants his words written for “the last” — and the two texts that share his vocabulary of writing are Isa 30:8, a written witness “for the time to come”, and Jer 17:1, sin engraved “with an iron stylus”. [T] for the words; [I] for the reading
    • Isa 30:8. “Now go, write it (כָתְבָהּ) on a tablet before them and inscribe it (חֻקָּהּ) on a scroll (סֵפֶר), that it may serve in the time to come (לְיוֹם אַחֲרוֹן, ‘for the last day’) as a witness forever (לָעַד).”
      • כתב (“write”) + סֵפֶר (“book, scroll”) + חקק (“inscribe”) occur together only at Isa 30:8 and Job 19:23 (lemma).
      • Isa 30:8 also has אַחֲרוֹן (“last”), which Job uses two verses later (19:25), and לָעַד, which Job uses in 19:24. The WLC index files Isaiah’s לָעַד as עֵד (“witness”), the reading the NASB95 follows (“as a witness”), and Job’s as עַד (“forever”).
      • In Isaiah the writing is a witness against a people who will not listen (30:9). Job’s writing would be a witness for him.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

  • The purpose the book states. The book states its purpose only through the frame: the question “Does Job fear God for nothing?” (1:9) and the verdict “you have not spoken of Me what is right as My servant Job has” (42:7). [T]
  • This speech’s place in that purpose. Chapter 19 is where Job, stripped of every human relation, confesses what he knows of God. It is the clearest answer in the dialogue to the Accuser’s question. Job holds to God with nothing left — not his honour, his household, his wife, his friends, or his skin — and expects to see him. [T] for the losses; [I] for the answer.
  • For the reader who knows the prologue.
    • The reader knows whose hand “touched” Job (1:11; 2:5), and Job names it: “the hand of God has struck (נָגְעָה) me” (19:21). He is not wrong: God said, “you incited Me against him” (2:3) (overview Echo Table).
    • The reader knows that the friends came “to sympathize with him and comfort him” (2:11). Job now begs them, “Pity me, pity me” (19:21), and warns them of the sword (19:29).
    • The reader also knows how the book ends. The wish of 19:23 (“Oh that my words were written!”) is granted by the book in the reader’s hands. [I]

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

  • What the preceding movement requires.
    • Job’s previous speech placed a witness in heaven (16:19) and asked God to stand surety with God (17:3), then ended with his hope going down to Sheol: “Will it go down to the bars of Sheol? Shall we descend together into the dust?” (17:16).
    • Bildad then gave the second cycle’s harshest portrait of the wicked man (18:5–21): his light put out, his feet in six kinds of trap, his skin devoured, his roots dried up, his memory perished, “no offspring or posterity” (18:19), and the last line, “this is the place of him who does not know God” (18:21).
    • The book now needs Job’s answer to a speech aimed at a bereaved father with diseased skin. [T] for the content; [I] for the necessity.
  • Why this speech is here.
    • It answers Bildad’s opening and close (Headline 5) and turns his last verb, “know”, into its own leading word (Headline 1).
    • It takes Job’s hope one step beyond 16:19: from a witness in heaven to a Redeemer who “lives” and “will rise upon the dust”. And it is spoken from the bottom of the list of losses (19:13–20), which 17:16 had reached.
    • After it, Zophar answers (20) and Job turns to the prosperity of the wicked (21). The mediator line does not return in Job’s mouth; it is next heard from Elihu (33:23). [T] for the sequence; [I] for the function.

Surrounding context

  • Bildad, 18:1–21. Rebuke (18:2–4), then the wicked man’s end (18:5–21).
  • Zophar, 20:1–29. The short triumph of the wicked. Zophar opens with Job’s own word: “I listened to the reproof which insults me (כְּלִמָּתִי)” (20:3) — the כלם of 19:3 (“you have insulted me”). [T] for the lemma (כלם, 3637 / כְּלִמָּה, 3639 are cognate); [I] for the reply. Moderate.
  • Job, 21:1–34. “Listen carefully to my speech, and let this be your way of consolation” (21:2).

3. Structure

The address test. Every second-person form in the chapter is plural, addressed to the friends: fifteen verbs, one pronoun and one suffix (19:2, 3, 5, 6, 21, 22, 28, 29). There is no second-person singular (WLC morphology). In each of Job’s speeches from 6–7 to 16–17 the second-person singular turns to God (6–7: 20 forms; 9–10: 44; 12–14: 47; 16–17: 11). Chapter 19 speaks of God only in the third person. Job does not address God again in his own voice until 30:20–23. [T] High.

4. Linking Words

  • אֵפוֹ (“then, now”) — 19:6 and 19:23. “Know then (דְּעוּ־אֵפוֹ) that God has wronged me” (19:6); “Oh that then (מִי־יִתֵּן אֵפוֹ) my words were written” (19:23). The particle marks the two moves from complaint to demand: first to the friends, then to the future. In Job it occurs at 9:24; 17:15; 19:6, 23; 24:25 (lemma). [T]
  • וַאֲנִי (“as for me”, 19:25). The emphatic pronoun with waw sets Job’s knowledge against the friends’ “you” before it (19:21–22) and after it (19:28–29), and against “those who know me” who have gone (19:13–14). The pronoun returns at 19:27: “whom I myself (אֲנִי) shall behold”. [T]
  • כִּי … (“for”, 19:21; 19:29). “Pity me … for (כִּי) the hand of God has struck me” (19:21): the reason the friends should pity is the very thing they take as proof of guilt. “Be afraid of the sword … for (כִּי) wrath brings the punishment of the sword” (19:29). [T]
  • וְאַחֲרוֹן … וְאַחַר (“and at the last … and after”, 19:25–26). The root אחר twice in adjacent lines: the Redeemer stands “at the last”; Job sees God “after” his skin is struck off. [T] High on the words.
  • The conditional frame of 19:4–5 and 19:28. “Even if (וְאַף) I have truly erred” (19:4); “If indeed (אִם־אָמְנָם) you vaunt yourselves” (19:5); “If (כִּי) you say, ‘How shall we persecute him?’” (19:28). Job argues from the friends’ premises to God’s judgment. [T]

5. Parallels

  • The siege, in matched lines. “He has walled up my way so that I cannot pass // And He has put darkness on my paths” (19:8); “He has stripped my honor from me // And removed the crown from my head” (19:9). Way // paths; honour // crown. The second line intensifies the first. [T]
  • The estranged, in pairs. Brothers // acquaintances (19:13); relatives // intimate friends (19:14); guests // maids (19:15); wife // brothers (19:17); “all my associates” // “those I love” (19:19). Twelve groups in seven verses, five of them in parallel pairs, moving from the household’s edge to its centre — the wife (19:17) — and out again to the children and the intimates. [T]
  • The confession, in a chain. “I know that my Redeemer lives // and at the last He will take His stand on the earth. // Even after my skin is destroyed, // yet from my flesh I shall see God; // whom I myself shall behold, // and whom my eyes will see and not another” (19:25–27). Three verbs of seeing (חזה twice, ראה once) and three first-person markers in 19:27 (אֲנִי, the verb, לִּי; Andersen, round 2, confirmed). [T]
  • The doubled plea. “Pity me, pity me (חָנֻּנִי חָנֻּנִי)” (19:21), the only doubled plea in the book (sweep). [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A in the body of the speech: the only narrative line is the formula “Then Job responded” (19:1). The narrator’s evaluation of all the speeches is reserved for the epilogue (42:7–9). [T]

9. Tone and Feel

  • The arc of feeling.
    • Exasperation at the friends (19:2–6): “How long …?”; “These ten times …”; “You are not ashamed”.
    • Lament under siege (19:7–12): no answer, no justice; a wall, darkness, a stripped crown, an uprooted tree, an army camped round a tent.
    • Desolation (19:13–20): a catalogue in the flat voice of loss, ending in the grim joke of the “skin of my teeth”.
    • Pleading (19:21–22): the only doubled plea in the book.
    • Longing for permanence (19:23–24): iron, lead, rock.
    • Certainty (19:25–27): “As for me, I know”.
    • Warning (19:28–29): the sword.
    • [T] for the markers; [I] for the labels.
  • The confession is not calm. It ends in longing, not repose: “my kidneys are consumed within me” (19:27, literally). כלה is the verb of the soul that “longs, yea, faints” for God (Ps 84:3 [Eng 84:2]) and of eyes that “fail” while waiting (Ps 69:4 [Eng 69:3]). The ESV’s “My heart faints within me!” keeps the exclamation. [T] for the verb; [I] for the tone. High.
  • The voice turns away from God. Chapter 19 is the first speech since chapter 3 in which Job never addresses God in the second person (Structure, the address test). He speaks of God’s hand, God’s troops, God’s net, and finally of the Redeemer and of seeing God — to the friends and to the future. The certainty of 19:25 is spoken, not prayed. [T] for the data; [I] for the tone. High on the data.
  • The humour of extremity. “I have escaped only by the skin of my teeth” (19:20) — whatever the idiom means exactly (see Difficult Verses), it is the voice of the last survivor, the messengers’ “I alone have escaped” (1:15–19), turned on himself. [I]

12. Genre

  • The form. A disputation speech in poetry that combines known forms:
    • a rebuttal of the opponents (19:2–6; 19:28–29);
    • an individual lament, with the enemy-siege motif (19:7–12) and the complaint of abandonment by kin and friends (19:13–20), both at home in the Psalter and Lamentations;
    • a plea for pity (19:21–22);
    • a wish (מִי־יִתֵּן, “Oh that …”, 19:23–24), the formula Job uses throughout the book (6:8; 11:5 [Zophar]; 13:5; 14:4, 13; 19:23; 23:3; 29:2; 31:31, 35);
    • a confession of confidence (19:25–27), in the form “I know that …”, which the Psalter also uses (“Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed”, Ps 20:7 [Eng 20:6]; “This I know, that God is for me”, Ps 56:10 [Eng 56:9]);
    • a warning with a purpose clause (19:28–29).
    • [T] for the elements; [I] for the classification.
  • What the genre forbids. Lament is not a doctrinal statement about God. “God has wronged me” (19:6) is Job’s complaint, which the book neither endorses (38:2; Elihu at 34:12) nor condemns as speech addressed to the right court (42:7). Equally, the confession of 19:25–27 is not a creed with every term defined: its referent, its time and the state in which Job sees God are what the poem leaves open (Difficult Verses). [I]
  • What the legal form adds. The chapter is framed by the lawsuit’s words: “prove” (יכח, 19:5), “wronged” (19:6), “no justice” (מִשְׁפָּט, 19:7), “judgment” (19:29), and the written record (19:23–24; cf. 31:35). Job’s גֹּאֵל is a legal figure, the kinsman who takes up a case (Prov 23:11: “their Redeemer is strong; He will plead their case (יָרִיב אֶת־רִיבָם) against you”), not a mystical intermediary. [T] for the vocabulary; [I] for the frame.

13. Copycat

  • The cautions.
    • Job’s accusation (19:6) and his description of God’s siege (19:7–12) are not offered as a pattern of how to speak of God. The book corrects their content (38:2).
    • Job’s threat to his friends (19:28–29) is not offered as a pattern for answering critics.
    • Job’s confession is not a technique for producing certainty: it is spoken out of the knowledge of who God is (Isa 44:6), not out of a resolve to feel sure.
  • What can be copied.
    • The sufferer abandoned by every human relation may still say “I know” about God.
    • The friend may learn what the friends would not: “Pity me” (19:21).
    • [I]

14. Bible Timeline

  • Setting. The story is set in a patriarchal world (overview). The book’s composition and date are not stated [S].
  • Canonical position. Ketuvim, after the Psalter (BHS order). The reader of the Ketuvim comes to Job 19 having read:
    • the Torah’s גֹּאֵל laws (Lev 25; Num 35; Deut 19);
    • the Prophets’ Redeemer (Isa 41–63), Isaiah’s writing “for the time to come” (Isa 30:8), Jeremiah’s iron stylus (Jer 17:1) and Habakkuk’s complaint (Hab 1–2);
    • the Psalter’s laments (Ps 69; 88; 102).
    • In the canonical Ketuvim, Lamentations comes after Job, so its contacts are read forward from Job.
    • [T] for the order; direction of dependence open.
  • The New Testament end of the line. The reader who knows the New Testament hears 19:25–27 alongside “I am the first and the last, and the living One; and I was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore” (Rev 1:17–18); Martha’s “I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day” (John 11:24, where the Greek of Job 19:25–26 also has “I know” and “raise up”); and “we know that when He appears, we will be like Him, because we will see Him just as He is” (1 John 3:2). 15. Who Am I?

  • Like Job, the reader who has lost every relation — whose family keeps away, whose friends have become accusers, who is a stranger in his own house. “My relatives have failed, and my intimate friends have forgotten me” (19:14).
  • Like the friends, the reader who explains another’s suffering. “The root of the matter is found in him” (19:28, ESV) is the voice of every comforter who locates the cause of suffering in the sufferer.
  • Like Job’s household, the reader who keeps away from the long-term sick: the guests, the maids and the servant (19:15–16) are not accused of doctrine, only of absence.
  • Unlike Job, the reader who knows the prologue — and so knows that the hand that touched Job (19:21) was permitted, and that the God Job calls his enemy (19:11) calls Job “My servant” (1:8; 2:3). [I]

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the speech seeks. [I] - From the friends: to stop pursuing Job “as God does” (19:22) and to pity him (19:21) — and to fear the judgment their own theology describes (19:29). - From the future reader (19:23–24): to read Job’s words as a witness, kept “forever”, and to hear his confession that his Redeemer lives and will stand at the last. - The book grants both: the words are written, and the friends do meet God’s anger (42:7).

Original Language Observations

  • 19:25 וַאֲנִי יָדַעְתִּי גֹּאֲלִי חָי (“As for me, I know: my Redeemer lives”). The object clause stands without כִּי (“that”), as only at 30:23 among Job’s “I know” sayings (Headline 1). גֹּאֲלִי חָי is a nominal clause with the adjective as predicate: “my Redeemer is living”. [T]
  • 19:25 וְאַחֲרוֹן (“and at the last” / “and as the Last”). אַחֲרוֹן is an adjective used adverbially (“finally, at the last”) or as a substantive (“the last one”). With no article, either is possible; Isa 44:6 and 48:12 use the substantive of God (“I am the last”). [T] for the form; [I] for either reading.
  • 19:25 עַל־עָפָר יָקוּם (“upon the dust he will rise / stand”). קום with עַל can mean “stand up over” (in court, “stand up against”, Deut 19:15–16; Ps 27:12) or simply “arise”. The imperfect is future in context. In Job the phrase עַל־עָפָר names the place of the dead (Headline 2). [T]
  • 19:26 וְאַחַר עוֹרִי נִקְּפוּ־זֹאת. Literally “and after my skin they have struck off this”. The verb is a third-person plural piel (or pual vocalised as piel) of נקף I, “strike off, cut down”, used impersonally (“after my skin has been struck off”). זֹאת (“this”) is feminine and has no clear antecedent: “this [body]”, or adverbial “thus” (ESV “thus destroyed”). The syntax is compressed; the sense “after my skin is destroyed” is common to all the versions consulted except the Greek. [T] for the forms; [I] for the construal.
  • 19:26 וּמִבְּשָׂרִי אֶחֱזֶה אֱלוֹהַּ (“and from my flesh I shall see God”). The preposition מִן admits two readings, both attested:
    • privative, “apart from / without my flesh” — as in “you could lift up your face without blemish (מִמּוּם)” (11:15) and “their houses are safe from fear (מִפָּחַד)” (21:9). Lange, Delitzsch and Dillmann argued this case (Logos round 2), with the observation that “from behind” would need מִבַּעַד. [S: Lange]; the parallels [T].
    • standpoint, “from (within) my flesh” — the use of מִן with verbs of seeing for the place from which one looks: “The LORD looks from heaven (מִשָּׁמַיִם הִבִּיט)” (Ps 33:13); “from His dwelling place (מִמְּכוֹן־שִׁבְתּוֹ) He looks out” (Ps 33:14); “looking through the windows (מִן־הַחַלֹּנוֹת)” (Song 2:9); and in Job, “man beholds from afar (מֵרָחוֹק)” (36:25) and the friends who “lifted up their eyes at a distance (מֵרָחוֹק)” (2:12). [T]
    • The verb tips the balance a little. With אֶחֱזֶה (“I shall see”) following, the standpoint reading is the more natural Hebrew; the privative reading needs the sense “without my flesh I shall see” to override it. Andersen reads it so (as a man), Barnes and Lange the other way (Logos round 2). [I] The grammar leans to standpoint; it does not decide.
    • Either way, Job sees God after his skin is struck off, and sees him himself: “whom I myself shall behold … my eyes, and not another” (19:27).
  • 19:27 אֲשֶׁר אֲנִי אֶחֱזֶה־לִּי (“whom I myself shall see for myself”). לִּי can be the dative of advantage (“on my side, for me”), as in “God is for me (לִי)” (Ps 56:10 [Eng 56:9]). The three first-person markers in the line (Andersen) put the emphasis on personal sight. [T] for the forms; [I] for the nuance.
  • 19:27 וְעֵינַי רָאוּ (“and my eyes will have seen”). A perfect in a future context — the prophetic or certain perfect, as if already done. [T] for the form; [I] for the force.
  • 19:27 וְלֹא־זָר (“and not a stranger”). See Vocabulary. Grammatically, זָר may be the subject (“my eyes, and not a stranger[’s], will see”) or a predicate of God (“[whom I shall see] and [he will] not [be] a stranger”). [T] for the ambiguity.
  • 19:23–24 the passives. וְיִכָּתְבוּן (“that they were written”), וְיֻחָקוּ (“inscribed”), יֵחָצְבוּן (“engraved”) — two of the three have the archaic final nun (paragogic -ûn). Job does not ask anyone in particular to write; he wishes the words written. [T]
  • 19:3 תַּהְכְּרוּ (hapax). BHS: two manuscripts read תחברו; “probably read with a few manuscripts” תחכ׳. Meaning uncertain (“wrong”, “deal harshly”). [T] for the apparatus.
  • 19:29 שַׁדּוּן / שדין. The qere שַׁדּוּן = שֶׁ (“that”) + דִּין (“judgement”) with an unusual vowel — “that there is judgement” (BHS: ut sciatis esse iudicium). The ketiv שדין has been read as the same with a different vowel, or as a play on שַׁדַּי (“the Almighty”; NIV84 margin). [T] for the forms.

Textual Variants

Variation inside the Hebrew (BHS apparatus for chapter 19, as BHS prints it):

19:25–27 — the crux

  • No note at 19:25 or 19:26. BHS records no variant in the Hebrew of either verse. Laurin’s claim that “the Hebrew text has been very badly preserved … all agree … it has to be changed” (Logos round 2) is contradicted by the apparatus. [T]
  • 19:27 ᵃ–ᵃ. One manuscript lacks אֲשֶׁר אֲנִי (“whom I”). Minor.
  • The Greek reads a different text (Swete; Rahlfs agrees in substance):
    • 19:25: οἶδα γὰρ ὅτι ἀέναός ἐστιν ὁ ἐκλύειν με μέλλων — “for I know that he is everlasting who is about to deliver me”. חַי (“lives”) becomes “everlasting”; גֹּאֲלִי becomes “the one who will release me” (ἐκλύειν, only here in Swete).
    • 19:25b–26a: ἐπὶ γῆς ἀναστήσαι τὸ δέρμα μου τὸ ἀνατλῶν ταῦτα — “on earth may he raise up my skin that endures these things”. The Hebrew “he will rise upon the dust” is read with “my skin” as its object: the Greek makes it the raising of Job’s body. Both editions accent ἀναστήσαι (aorist optative, “may he raise”); the overview prints ἀναστῆσαι (infinitive), a minor point. Swete prints ἀναντλοῦν, Rahlfs ἀνατλῶν (forms of “endure”).
    • 19:26b–27: παρὰ γὰρ κυρίου ταῦτά μοι συνετελέσθη, ἃ ἐγὼ ἐμαυτῷ συνεπίσταμαι, ἃ ὁ ὀφθαλμός μου ἑόρακεν καὶ οὐκ ἄλλος — “for from the Lord these things were accomplished for me, which I know in myself, which my eye has seen and not another”. The Greek has no “I shall see God”. What Job’s eyes have seen are his sufferings.
    • 19:27c: πάντα δέ μοι συντετέλεσται ἐν κόλπῳ — “all things have been accomplished for me in [my] bosom”: כָּלוּ (“are consumed”) read as “are completed”.
    • Category 2 (a different reading of the consonants) and Category 1 (interpretation). The Greek adds resurrection (with 42:17a, “it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up”) and removes the vision of God.
  • Most likely sense of the Hebrew: the Masoretic text stands, unemended, as Andersen, Lange (Delitzsch, Dillmann) and Barnes all expound it (round 2).
  • Preaching implication: real. The famous verses are textually secure in Hebrew; their difficulty is grammatical (19:26). The Greek’s resurrection reading is the earliest interpretation, not the text.
  • Confidence: high on the absence of Hebrew variants (as BHS prints it); the Greek readings [T].

Historical and Cultural Background

  • The גֹּאֵל. In Israel’s law the nearest kinsman had two duties:
    • to buy back (Lev 25:25–34) — land sold under hardship, and kin sold into debt-slavery (Lev 25:47–55), as Boaz redeems Elimelech’s land and marries Ruth (Ruth 4:1–10);
    • to avenge blood, as “avenger of blood” (גֹּאֵל הַדָּם, Num 35:19–27; Deut 19:6, 12; Josh 20:3–9), within the limits the cities of refuge set.
    • [T] for the texts.
    • Deut 19 sets the avenger beside the witness law. “The avenger of blood might pursue (יִרְדֹּף) the manslayer … though he was not deserving of death” (19:6); “A single witness shall not rise up (יָקוּם) against a man” (19:15); “If a malicious witness rises up …” (19:16). Job’s two speeches in the second cycle draw on both: the hostile witness that “rises up” (16:8), the blood that cries (16:18), and now the גֹּאֵל who “lives” and “will rise” (19:25). The friends, meanwhile, “pursue” him (19:22, 28) like an avenger after an innocent man. [T] for the words; [I] for the combination. Moderate.
  • Writing that lasts. An iron stylus (עֵט בַּרְזֶל, 19:24; Jer 17:1) cut letters in stone; lead (עֹפֶרֶת) may have been run into the cut letters, or used as a writing surface. [S] Monumental inscriptions in rock are well attested in the ancient Near East (the Behistun inscription is the best-known example). [S] The point of 19:24 is permanence: “forever” (לָעַד).
  • The kidneys. In Hebrew idiom the kidneys (כְּלָיוֹת) are the seat of the inner self, tested by God (Jer 11:20; 17:10; Ps 26:2) (lemma). “My kidneys are consumed within me” is longing, not physical decay. [T] for the usage.
  • “By the skin of my teeth.” The idiom is unexplained: the gums, the enamel, or simply “with nothing”. [S] NIV84’s margin “only my gums” is one guess. The sense is clear from context: Job has survived with nothing at all.
  • The household. “Those who live in my house” (גָּרֵי בֵיתִי) are resident aliens or dependants under Job’s protection (cf. 31:32, the stranger who lodged with him). Maids (אֲמָהוֹת) and a servant (עֶבֶד) complete a patriarchal household. A master who must “implore” his servant (19:16) has lost all standing. [T] for the terms; [I] for the social sense.
  • The disease and the breath. “My breath is offensive to my wife” (19:17) belongs with the descriptions of Job’s skin (2:7–8; 7:5; 30:30) and his wasting (16:8; 19:20). No diagnosis is given in the text. [T]

Original Audience Reception

  • What a reader of the Torah and Prophets would hear in גֹּאֵל. A kinsman with a legal duty, and — after Isaiah — the LORD himself as Israel’s Redeemer from exile (Isa 41–63). Job is not an Israelite (1:1), but the book is written in Israel’s Hebrew for Israel’s readers, who would hear both. [I]
  • What they would hear in “upon the dust”. The place of the dead, where Job had said he would lie (7:21; 17:16), and where the wicked lie (20:11; 21:26). A Redeemer who stands there stands over the grave. [I]
  • What they would expect of such a speech. In the Psalter, a lament that turns to “I know” ends in praise (Ps 20:7; 56:10; 140:13). Job’s turn does not end in praise; it ends in longing (19:27) and a warning (19:29). The book has not yet given the answer. [I]
  • What the Greek-speaking reader heard. A confession of bodily resurrection (“may he raise up my skin”) and, at the end of the book, “it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up” (42:17a). The earliest readers of Job in Greek read 19:25–26 as resurrection hope. [T] for the Greek.

Biblical-Theological Themes

  • The Redeemer. From the kinsman of Lev 25 and Ruth, through Isaiah’s “Redeemer, the LORD of hosts … the first and … the last” (Isa 44:6), to the one who “partook of the same” flesh and blood “that through death He might render powerless him who had the power of death” (Heb 2:14) and who, as “the living One”, “was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore” (Rev 1:18). [T] for the texts; [I] for the line.
  • Seeing God. From the elders on Sinai who “saw God, and … ate and drank” (Exod 24:11), through Job’s “I shall see God” (19:26) and “now my eye sees You” (42:5), to “we will see Him just as He is” (1 John 3:2). [T] for the texts.
  • The dust and the rising. “You are dust, and to dust you shall return” (Gen 3:19) → “man lies down and does not rise” (14:12) → “He will rise upon the dust” (19:25) → “Your dead will live … you who lie in the dust, awake” (Isa 26:19) → “many of those who sleep in the dust of the ground will awake” (Dan 12:2) → “I know that he will rise again … on the last day” (John 11:24). [T] for the texts; [I] for the trajectory.
  • The abandoned righteous. The sufferer estranged from his brothers (Ps 69:9; Job 19:13–17) and his friends (Ps 88:19; Job 19:14, 19) is a type the Psalter develops and the Gospels apply to Jesus: “zeal for Your house will consume me” (Ps 69:10 [Eng 69:9], quoted at John 2:17); “all the disciples left Him and fled” (Matt 26:56). [I]
  • The written witness. Isaiah’s scroll “for the time to come” (Isa 30:8), Habakkuk’s vision “for the appointed time” (Hab 2:2–3), and Job’s words “written … forever” (19:23–24): the word committed to writing because its vindication lies ahead. [I]

Christological Reading

The trajectory — from the umpire to the Redeemer. High on the trajectory; low on any claim that Job foresaw Christ. - The sequence in Job (overview, confirmed): - 9:33 — “There is no umpire between us”; - 16:19–21 — “my witness is in heaven … that He may argue for a man with God”; - 17:3 — “Lay down, now, a pledge for me with Yourself” (16–17 dig); - 19:25–27 — “I know that my Redeemer lives”; - 33:23–24 — Elihu’s “angel as mediator … I have found a ransom”. - The movement is from denial, to hope, to petition, to knowledge. [T] for the wording; [I] for the sequence. - The canonical answer. The kinsman-Redeemer who must share our flesh and blood (Heb 2:14–17), who “always lives to make intercession” (Heb 7:25), who is “the first and the last, and the living One” (Rev 1:17–18), and whom we shall see “just as He is” (1 John 3:2). The redemption Job hoped for “from my flesh” Paul calls “the redemption of our body” (Rom 8:23). [T] for the texts. - Martha and the Greek of Job. “I know (οἶδα) that he will rise again (ἀναστήσεται) in the resurrection on the last day” (John 11:24). The Greek of Job 19:25–26 has οἶδα (“I know”) and ἀναστήσαι (“may he raise up”), and the Hebrew has אַחֲרוֹן (“at the last”). Jesus’ answer, “I am the resurrection and the life” (John 11:25), names the Redeemer Job confessed. [T] for the Greek; [I] for the connection — colour, not a quotation. - Typology — the abandoned righteous sufferer. Job estranged from brothers, friends, servants and wife, struck by the hand of God and pursued by those who should pity him, is a type of the Christ whom “all the disciples left … and fled” (Matt 26:56) and who cried “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (Matt 27:46, from Ps 22:2, a psalm already live in Job, queue #7). Moderate. [I] - The contrast. Job confesses a Redeemer; Christ is the Redeemer. Job wishes his innocence engraved; Christ’s innocence is vindicated by resurrection (Rom 1:4). Job’s “God has wronged me” (19:6) is not Christ’s word: Christ “kept entrusting Himself to Him who judges righteously” (1 Pet 2:23). [I] - Keep honest. - The referent of גֹּאֵל in Job’s mouth is contested: God himself (Andersen; Lange with Delitzsch and Dillmann; Laurin — round 2, strong [S]); a heavenly third party; or Job’s vindication personified. The Isa 44:6 contact favours God himself; the 16:19–21 and 17:3 context favours God appealed to against God. - 19:26 does not, by itself, prove bodily resurrection; the Hebrew allows “apart from my flesh”. The Greek reads resurrection; the canon (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2; John 11; 1 Cor 15) supplies it. - Handel’s “I know that my Redeemer liveth” (with the KJV’s supplied “worms” and “body”) is a legitimate Christian appropriation; it is an appropriation.

The moralism check. The trap is “have faith like Job”. Job’s confession is not a model of positive thinking under trial; it is a statement about God — that he lives and will stand at the last. The sermon’s subject is the Redeemer, not Job’s grip. [I]

Difficult / Contested Verses

19:6 — “God has wronged me”

  • The difficulty: doctrinal. Job says God has perverted his case (עִוְּתָנִי), using the verb Bildad thought unthinkable (8:3).
  • Honest exegesis: Job is not lying about what he experiences: the reader knows the hand was God’s (2:3). But the book does not adopt his conclusion: God will say Job spoke “words without knowledge” (38:2), and Elihu answers with the same verb, “the Almighty will not pervert justice” (34:12). The verdict of 42:7 honours what Job said to God, not this charge about him (see the book backbone on 42:7, revised in Logos round 3).
  • Preaching: let the charge stand as lament; do not endorse it and do not rush to correct it.
  • Confidence: high on the text; the theology is the book’s own tension.

19:13 — “He has removed my brothers far from me”

  • The difficulty: pastoral and textual. Is the estrangement God’s doing (MT “He”) or the brothers’ (Greek, Syriac, one manuscript, “they”)?
  • Honest exegesis: the Masoretic “He” continues the run of God’s acts from 19:6. Either way the brothers are gone, and either way they return at 42:11.
  • Preaching: do not use 19:13–19 to blame the families of the long-term sick; the text lays the estrangement at God’s door before it lays it at theirs.

19:17 — “the sons of my womb”

  • The difficulty: contradiction? Job’s children are dead (1:18–19). Who are “the sons of my womb (בְּנֵי בִטְנִי)”?
  • Honest exegesis: “my womb” is most naturally his mother’s womb, as at 3:10 (“the doors of my womb”, בִטְנִי, his mother’s), so these are his brothers — ESV “the children of my own mother”. Other suggestions: grandchildren, the sons of concubines (the Greek), or a stock phrase. [T] for 3:10; [I] for the inference.
  • Confidence: moderate–high for “brothers”.

19:25–27 — Whom does Job see, when, and in what state?

  • The positions:
    • (a) God himself, after death, without the body — Lange with Delitzsch and Dillmann; Barnes, who says bodily resurrection “cannot be proved” from the verse (round 2).
    • (b) God himself, in the body — at a future vindication, or a resurrection — Andersen (round 2); the ESV’s “in my flesh”; the Greek’s “may he raise up my skin”.
    • (c) A heavenly third party (the witness of 16:19 as distinct from God) who will vindicate Job before God.
  • What the text says [T]:
    • the Redeemer “lives” and “will rise upon the dust” (the grave);
    • it is “after my skin is struck off” — after death or at its door;
    • Job will “see God” and see him “myself … my eyes, and not a stranger”;
    • the Isa 44:6 pairing names the Redeemer as the LORD.
  • What it leaves open [I]: whether the seeing is in the body (standpoint מִן, which the verb favours) or apart from it (privative מִן, which 11:15 and 21:9 allow), and whether the time is before or after death.
  • Assessment: the text is clearest on the person and the seeing, least clear on the state. (a) and (b) agree that Job will see God himself after his skin is destroyed; they differ on the body. (c) is weakest, since 19:26 names the one Job sees as אֱלוֹהַּ. Moderate–high for “God himself”; open on the body.
  • The book’s own resolution: “now my eye sees You” (42:5) — in this life. The seeing Job longed for is granted before his death; what lies beyond is left to the canon. [T] for 42:5; [I] for the reading.

19:28–29 — Job threatens his friends

  • The difficulty: ethical. Is Job’s warning of the sword vindictive?
  • Honest exegesis: the warning applies the friends’ own doctrine to them: if suffering proves guilt, those who pursue an innocent man should fear judgement. The book carries it out, but mercifully: God’s anger is kindled against the friends (42:7), and Job prays for them (42:8–10). [T] for 42:7–10; [I] for the irony.
  • Preaching: the warning is fulfilled by grace, through Job’s intercession.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Lifting 19:25–27 off 19:13–22

  • What it looks like: the confession preached alone, at a funeral or at Easter, as if it were a hymn of serene assurance.
  • Why it’s wrong: the speech reaches its confession only after the list of everyone who has left him (19:13–20), the failed plea to his friends (19:21–22) and the wish to be written (19:23–24). The confession ends in longing (“my kidneys are consumed within me”), not repose.
  • Corrective: restore 19:13–22. Preach the confession as what is known at the bottom, not at the top.

Pitfall: Building the resurrection of the body on מִבְּשָׂרִי alone — or denying any hope beyond death

  • What it looks like: (a) “Job here teaches bodily resurrection — ‘in my flesh’”; or (b) “Job means only that he will be vindicated in this life”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the Hebrew of 19:26 allows “from (within) my flesh” and “apart from my flesh” (Original Language Observations). Against (b): the time is “after my skin is struck off”, and the Redeemer stands “upon the dust”, the place of the dead. Against (a): the grammar leans to the embodied reading but does not decide it.
  • Corrective: preach what is clear — the living Redeemer, after Job’s death, and Job seeing God himself. Let the canon supply the body (Isa 26:19; Dan 12:2; John 11:24–25; Rom 8:23; 1 Cor 15).

Pitfall: “Job foresaw Christ” — or “Christ has nothing to do with it”

  • What it looks like: (a) “Job knew that Jesus his Redeemer would come”; or (b) a reading that stops at Job’s own horizon.
  • Why it’s wrong: (a) claims more than the text: Job’s Redeemer is God, appealed to against God, named in the language Isaiah uses of the LORD. (b) ignores the canon that names the Redeemer who shared our flesh (Heb 2:14) and lives (Rev 1:18).
  • Corrective: a trajectory, not a prediction (overview: high on the trajectory, low on foresight).

Pitfall: A sermon about bad relatives

  • What it looks like: 19:13–19 used to rebuke families and churches that neglect the sick.
  • Why it’s wrong: the text lays the estrangement first at God’s door (“He has removed my brothers”, 19:13). The wife and the brothers are described, not accused. A congregation will contain both the abandoned and those who stayed away, and the second group may have reasons the sermon does not know.
  • Corrective: let 19:13–19 make the hearers feel Job’s isolation; let 42:11 show what return looks like.

Pitfall: “Have faith like Job”

  • What it looks like: “Even at his lowest, Job said ‘I know’ — so must you.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it makes the confession a technique. Job’s “I know” rests on who the Redeemer is (“lives”, “the last”, “God”), not on Job’s resolve. And Job does not address God in this speech at all; this is not a model prayer.
  • Corrective: preach the Redeemer as the subject.
