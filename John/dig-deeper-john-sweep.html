Method note — read this before using the sweep

Passage quotation. The house sweep format quotes each pericope in full. John is 879 verses — eight times Philippians — and the ESV is not available to this run as a verified electronic text. Rather than reconstruct 879 verses from memory and present them as verified, each pericope carries a one-line Gist and quotes only short, load-bearing phrases. ESV wording quoted inline is from recall and should be checked against the printed text before any finding hangs on an exact English word. This is a deliberate, declared shortfall against the house template, not an oversight — see the Text-First Declaration.

What is machine-verified. All Greek forms, word-counts, and per-chapter distributions in this report were taken by scripted sweep over the SBLGNT (866 verses; the edition omits 5:4 and brackets 7:53–8:11), and all English cross-checks against a full electronic KJV. Where a finding rests on a count or a Greek form, it is verified and reproducible. Where it rests on ESV phrasing, it is not.

Depth. Sweep depth, not solo depth. Each pericope receives: a Positional Necessity Check, the tools that are live for it, Tool 11 including the mandatory Move 4 internal-echo check, one or two sweep findings, and a solo-dig verdict. Tools that are dead for a given pericope are silently omitted rather than padded with N/As; the book-level N/A reasons are given once, below.

Book-level N/As (stated once, not repeated per pericope).

  • Tool 5 (Parallels): John is Greek narrative prose, not Hebrew poetry. Parallelism appears only in the Prologue (1:1–5, 1:10–12 — chiastic and staircase forms) and sporadically in the discourses (e.g. 3:20–21; 12:44–50). Flagged where live; otherwise N/A — non-poetic prose.
  • Tool 13 (Copycat): live throughout, since this is narrative with characters. Reported where a character is commonly misused as a model.
  • Schnittjer Pass: N/A — not a Torah passage.

Whole-book orientation (built text-first at sweep level)

Author’s purpose (book level). Stated by the book itself at 20:30–31 — a selection of signs written so that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name [T]. The purpose statement is unique among the Gospels and governs everything before it.

Structure (text-first). Prologue (1:1–18) · opening witness and the new order, Cana to Cana (1:19–4:54) · the feast cycle (5:1–10:42) · the last sign and the turn (11:1–12:50) · the Farewell (13:1–17:26) · the hour (18:1–19:42) · resurrection and purpose (20) · epilogue (21). The pivot is 12:37–13:1, and it is countable, not impressionistic.

Pericope 1 — John 1:1–18 — The Prologue

Gist. The Word, eternal and divine, agent of creation, life and light, rejected by his own and received by some, became flesh and tabernacled among us; grace and truth through Jesus Christ against the law through Moses.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: none — this is the book’s opening, and its function is to plant, not resolve. But its canonical preceding movement is Genesis 1: “In the beginning” is not a stylistic flourish but a claim to be re-opening the canon [T]. Necessity: the Prologue exists here because every character in the story that follows will ask “Who are you?” and “Where are you from?” — and John gives the reader the answer before the first character asks. The reader is placed above the narrative’s own epistemic horizon. Implication: every misunderstanding in chs. 2–12 is dramatic irony available only because of 1:1–18.

Live tools. - Structure. A chiastic-and-staircase movement: vv.1–5 (Word with God, creation, life, light) → vv.6–8 (witness) → vv.9–13 (coming, rejection, reception) → v.14 (incarnation) → v.15 (witness again) → vv.16–18 (grace upon grace; Moses/Christ). The two John-the-Baptist insertions (6–8, 15) interrupt deliberately, marking that the Word’s story is told through testimony [T]. - Parallels. The one place in John where Hebrew-style staircase parallelism is unmistakable: “in him was life, and the life was the light of men; the light shines in the darkness” (vv.4–5) — each clause picks up the last word of the previous [T]. - Vocabulary. λόγος (only here and v.14 as a title — verified: the nominative ὁ λόγος occurs 15× in John, but as a title for Christ only 1:1, 1:14) · ἐσκήνωσεν “pitched his tent” (v.14) · μονογενής (4× total: 1:14, 1:18, 3:16, 3:18 — verified) · πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας. High confidence on the σκηνόω/tabernacle resonance. - Repetition. φῶς 6× in ch. 1 (the heaviest concentration in the book alongside ch. 12) · μαρτυρ- 7× in ch. 1 · “was” (ἦν) hammered against “became” (ἐγένετο) — the Word was, everything else became [T]. - Tone. Liturgical, weighty, hymnic. Soundtrack: organ, low registers, before the narrative’s first ordinary sentence.

Tool 11. - Genesis 1:1–5 → 1:1–5 (high confidence). Source context: the creation account’s opening, where God speaks and light separates darkness. Book usage: first use; establishes Genesis as a live source that returns at 20:1 (first day), 20:15 (gardener), 20:22 (ἐνεφύσησεν, the Gen 2:7 LXX verb). OT-to-OT: Gen 1 is already read through Ps 33:6 and Prov 8:22–31 in the Writings, so “the Word” arrives with wisdom-tradition freight [I]. What it adds: the Gospel is framed as a new-creation document from its first three words, and the rejection of v.11 is a fall-narrative in miniature. - Exodus 33–34 → 1:14–18 (moderate–high). “We have seen his glory… full of grace and truth” against “no one has ever seen God” reads as Moses at Sinai, granted the goodness but refused the face; χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια plausibly renders ḥesed we-ʾemet (Exod 34:6) [I]. Move 1 only at this confidence. - Internal: planted for later — a dense field. “the Word was God” → 20:28 (the only two nominative θεός predications, verified) · light/darkness → 8:12; 9:5; 12:35–46 · “his own received him not” (τὰ ἴδια) → 13:1 (τοὺς ἰδίους); 19:27 (εἰς τὰ ἴδια) · “we have seen his glory” → 2:11; 11:40; 12:41; 17:24 · “grace and truth” → the ἀληθ- thread (55×) · “no one has ever seen God… he has made him known” → 14:9 “whoever has seen me has seen the Father”. High confidence on all but the τὰ ἴδια chain (moderate–high).

Sweep findings. 1. The Prologue is not an overture but an epistemic gift. It gives the reader information no character possesses, which is the mechanism of every irony in chs. 2–12. Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Structure, Move 4. 2. The Baptist interruptions are structural, not clumsy. vv.6–8 and v.15 break the hymnic flow twice to insist that the Word is known through testimony — the μαρτυρ- word-group runs 47× through the book and is densest here and in ch. 5 [T].

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH. Load-bearing for the whole book; the sweep can only gesture at the Genesis, Sinai and wisdom layers.

Pericope 2 — John 1:19–34 — The witness of John: “Behold the Lamb of God”

Gist. Priests and Levites interrogate John; he denies being Christ, Elijah or the Prophet, and quotes Isaiah 40:3; the next day he identifies Jesus as the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world, and testifies to the Spirit’s descent.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the Prologue has twice inserted John as witness (1:6–8, 15) without narrating anything. Necessity: the narrative must begin with the examination that the Prologue’s testimony-language anticipated — and note that the Gospel’s first narrated event is an interrogation about identity (“Who are you?”, v.19), which is the book’s engine-question fired at the wrong man. Implication: John’s threefold denial (“I am not… I am not… No”) is the negative image of Jesus’s ἐγώ εἰμι, and the contrast is structural [I].

Live tools. - Repetition. μαρτυρ- 7× in ch. 1 · “I am not” ×3 against Jesus’s 24 ἐγώ εἰμι verses [T] · the “next day” (τῇ ἐπαύριον) sequence begins here (1:29, 1:35, 1:43 — verified, plus “on the third day” at 2:1), constructing an opening week. - Vocabulary. ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ — verified: ἀμνός occurs only at 1:29 and 1:36 in John, making the title emphatic-by-scarcity rather than by repetition [T]. αἴρων τὴν ἁμαρτίαν τοῦ κόσμου — “takes away”, not “bears”. - Narrator’s comment. v.28 “these things took place in Bethany across the Jordan” — a locating aside; John does this repeatedly and the asides are interpretive gold elsewhere (2:21; 7:39; 12:33).

Tool 11. - Isaiah 40:3 → 1:23 (high confidence — explicit, “as the prophet Isaiah said”). Source context: the opening of Isaiah’s book of consolation; the voice prepares a highway for the returning God, and the immediately following verses (40:6–8) contrast withering flesh with the abiding word of God. Book usage: first of four Isaiah engagements (1:23; 6:45; 12:38; 12:40 — verified); the last two close the public ministry, so John frames the whole public ministry between two Isaiah citations [T]. OT-to-OT: Isa 40:3 stands at the head of the new-exodus material that Isa 43 and 52–53 develop, and John will use both. What it adds: John the Baptist defines himself by a text about God’s own coming, not a messenger’s — the christological weight is carried by the citation, not asserted. - Exodus 12 / Isaiah 53:7 → 1:29, 36 (the Passover reference high; Isa 53 moderate). Source context: Passover lambs are not sin-offerings — they are protective and covenantal; Isa 53:7’s lamb is silent before shearers and does bear iniquity. The title fuses two OT lambs, and the fusion is the point [I]. Book usage: first use; the Passover frame is confirmed structurally by three Passovers (2:13; 6:4; 11:55 — verified) and cashed at 19:14, 36. OT-to-OT: Isa 53 is already reading the cult back into the Servant’s death, so the two lambs are in prior conversation [I]. - Internal: planted for later — the strongest single plant in the book. Ἴδε ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ (1:29, 36) → Ἴδε ὁ ἄνθρωπος / Ἴδε ὁ βασιλεὺς ὑμῶν (19:5, 14), spoken at the sixth hour on the Day of Preparation of the Passover, with 19:36 “not one of his bones will be broken”. The interjection ἴδε is the verbal carrier (verified: ἴδε/ἰδού at 23 verses, with 1:29, 1:36, 19:5, 19:14 among them). High confidence on the verbal and calendrical link; the lamb-slaughter timing itself is [S].

Sweep findings. 1. The Gospel’s first narrated scene is an identity interrogation, and its last is another (18:19–19:16). The book opens and closes in a courtroom. Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Tone, Move 4. 2. ἀμνός occurs exactly twice, both here. John does not repeat the Lamb title; he detonates it once, then leaves it for eighteen chapters. Preachers who treat it as a running Johannine theme are importing Revelation’s ἀρνίον.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH. The Lamb title and the Isaiah 40 citation both need full triad work.

Pericope 3 — John 1:35–51 — Come and see

Gist. Two of John’s disciples follow Jesus; Andrew finds Simon; Jesus renames him Cephas; Philip finds Nathanael; the “come and see” chain closes with the Son of Man as the opened heaven.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the Baptist has pointed twice. Necessity: the pointing must produce following, and John shows the gospel spreading by personal testimony before a single sign is worked — the sequence is testimony → following → more testimony. Implication: the first thing Jesus says in this Gospel is a question — “What are you seeking?” (v.38) — and the last thing the risen Jesus says to a seeker is “Whom are you seeking?” (20:15). Position makes the question programmatic [T].

Live tools. - Structure. A relay: Baptist → Andrew → Peter → Philip → Nathanael, with a day-marker at each hinge. - Vocabulary. εὑρίσκομεν “we have found” (vv.41, 45) answering ζητεῖτε “you are seeking” (v.38) [T]; Μεσσίαν, Κηφᾶς — John translates both for the reader (v.41, v.42), part of the translation habit that maps the audience. - Narrator’s comment. Three decoder asides in fourteen verses (vv.38, 41, 42) — the narrator’s translating voice established early. - Copycat. Andrew and Philip are genuinely normative pattern — the narrative commends the fetch-your-brother move without comment, and it recurs at 4:29 and 12:21–22 [I]. Nathanael’s scepticism is descriptive, not a model.

Tool 11. - Genesis 28:12 → 1:51 (high confidence — verbal and structural). Source context: Jacob at Bethel, fleeing with a stolen blessing, dreams of a ladder with angels ascending and descending, wakes to say “this is the house of God and the gate of heaven”, and the place is renamed. Book usage: first Genesis narrative use after the Gen 1 frame of the Prologue; the Jacob thread continues at 4:5–6, 12 (Jacob’s well, “our father Jacob”) — verified: Ἰακώβ occurs 3×, all in ch. 4. OT-to-OT: Bethel’s canonical history runs from Gen 28 to Jeroboam’s calves (1 Kgs 12) and Hosea’s Beth-aven, so the name carries both founding vision and apostasy [I]. What it adds: Jesus replaces Bethel — the meeting-place is a person, not a site — and the promise is made to Nathanael, “an Israelite indeed, in whom is no deceit (δόλος)”, pointedly the opposite of Jacob the deceiver. The contrast is the exegetical payload and is easy to miss. - Internal: “What are you seeking?” (v.38) → 18:4, 7; 20:15 (verified: the τί/τίνα ζητεῖτε form at exactly these four points) — planted for later, high. “Come and see” (v.39, 46) → 4:29; 11:34 (verified) — planted, moderate–high. “You will see greater things” (v.50) → 11:40; 20:29 — planted, moderate.

Sweep findings. 1. Nathanael-as-Jacob-reversed is the pericope’s hinge and is almost never preached. “No deceit” + the ladder + the renaming of Simon in the same scene make Genesis 27–28 the controlling subtext. Surfaced by: Tool 11 Move 1, Vocabulary (δόλος), Positional Necessity. 2. Jesus’s first and the risen Jesus’s first words are the same question. A four-point verified frame around the whole book.

Solo dig: Standard — but do not preach it without the Genesis 28 work.

Pericope 4 — John 2:1–12 — The first sign: water into wine

Gist. At a wedding in Cana the wine fails; Jesus tells his mother his hour has not yet come, then turns six stone jars of purification water into the best wine; the first of his signs, manifesting his glory.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: four days of gathering testimony and the promise of “greater things” (1:50–51). Necessity: the promise must be honoured, and it is honoured not by an opened heaven but by a domestic wedding — the anticlimax is deliberate [I]. The sign is placed before the temple action so that the reader meets replacement-as-abundance before replacement-as-judgement. Implication: the “third day” (2:1) completing the opening week reads as a creation-week echo, and the wedding as its consummation [I] — moderate confidence, a genuine interpretive option rather than a settled reading.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἀρχὴ τῶν σημείων “beginning of the signs” — verified: only two signs are ever numbered (2:11 first; 4:54 second), which is why “the seven signs” is a preacher’s scheme, not John’s [T]. γύναι “Woman” — verified: Jesus addresses his mother thus only here and at 19:26. ὥρα — first of 26 uses. - Narrator’s comment. v.11 is a full authorial verdict: this was the first sign, it manifested his glory, and his disciples believed. The sign-and-belief formula planted here is the same formula that closes the book (20:30–31) [T]. - Tone. Understated to the point of comedy — the master of the feast praises the bridegroom for wine he did not provide. John lets the irony sit.

Tool 11. - Conceptual: the messianic-abundance-of-wine texts (Amos 9:13–14; Joel 3:18; Isa 25:6; Jer 31:12) (moderate confidence). Move 1 only. The prophetic promise of the age to come is vats overflowing; six jars of ~20–30 gallons each is roughly 120–180 gallons of wine at a village wedding, which is absurd on any hospitality logic and legible on a prophetic one [I]. - Conceptual: the purification jars — “for the Jewish rites of purification” (v.6) is the narrator’s own gloss, and the detail is not decorative: the water of purification becomes the wine of the feast [T]. - Internal: planted for later. “My hour has not yet come” (2:4) → 7:30; 8:20 (“his hour had not yet come”) → 12:23; 13:1; 17:1 (“the hour has come”) — the book’s clock starts ticking here, high. “Woman” (2:4) → 19:26, high (verified, two occurrences of the address to his mother, at the two ends of the hour). “Manifested his glory” (2:11) → 11:4, 40; 12:41; 17:1–5, high.

Sweep findings. 1. John numbers two signs and no more. The “seven signs” structure so often preached is a homiletical construct; the text’s own numbering stops at two. This should change how a series is framed. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Narrator’s Comment. Direct tension with most published outlines — see Book-Overview Tensions. 2. The hour-clock and the “Woman”-address are set running in the same verse (2:4) and resolved in the same verse (19:26). Two threads, one plant, one payoff.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 5 — John 2:13–25 — The temple cleansed

Gist. At Passover Jesus drives out the traders, says “destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up”, and the narrator explains he meant his body; many believe at the signs but he does not entrust himself to them.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a sign of abundant replacement at a wedding. Necessity: the temple action stands here, at the very start of the ministry rather than at its end as in the Synoptics, because John is not narrating a triggering provocation but announcing a programme — the replacement theme is stated at the outset and then worked out feast by feast [I]. Implication: the Johannine placement is a literary-theological decision, and preachers who harmonise it into Holy Week lose John’s argument. Moderate–high confidence; the “one cleansing or two” question is genuinely open and belongs in Open Questions.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. Two in four verses (vv.21, 22): “but he was speaking about the temple of his body”, and “when therefore he was raised… they remembered and believed the Scripture”. The second is the book’s clearest statement of its own hermeneutic — understanding is retrospective [T]. - Vocabulary. ἱερόν (the precinct, vv.14–15) vs ναός (the sanctuary, vv.19–21) — verified: John uses ἱερ-/ναο- 13× total, and the ἱερόν/ναός distinction is exactly here [T]. The shift of word is the argument: he clears the precinct and speaks of the sanctuary. - Repetition. πάσχα — first of three (2:13; 6:4; 11:55, verified), the structural calendar.

Tool 11. - Psalm 69:9 → 2:17 (high confidence — explicit citation, “it is written”). Source context: Psalm 69 is the lament of one suffering because of zeal for God’s house — “reproaches of those who reproach you have fallen on me”, “they gave me poison for food and for my thirst sour wine to drink” (69:21). It is a psalm of righteous suffering that expects but has not received vindication. Book usage: first of three uses of Psalm 69 in John — 2:17, 15:25 (69:4, with 35:19), 19:28–29 (69:21). This makes Ps 69 the book’s most-worked psalm and a LIVE source [T]. And the ordering matters: John cites 69:9 at the beginning, 69:4 in the Farewell, 69:21 at the cross — following the psalm’s own movement from zeal through hatred to thirst [I]. OT-to-OT: Ps 69 and Ps 22 are the twin passion-psalms and are already paired in the tradition; John uses Ps 22:18 at 19:24, adjacent to Ps 69:21 at 19:28. What it adds: the disciples’ remembered text supplies the cost — zeal for the house will consume him — so the temple action is already a death-notice. - Zechariah 14:21 → 2:16 (moderate). “There shall no longer be a trader in the house of the LORD of hosts” closes Zechariah’s Booths vision; John will use Zech 9:9 at 12:15 and 12:10 at 19:37, so Zechariah is live [I]. Move 1 only. - Internal: planted for later, strongly. “Destroy this temple… in three days I will raise it up” → 20:9 (“as yet they did not understand the Scripture, that he must rise”), high, and the narrator flags the delay himself. “Not entrusting himself to them… he knew what was in man” (2:24–25) → 6:64, 70–71; 13:11 — the omniscience thread, high.

Sweep findings. 1. Psalm 69 is John’s spine-psalm, and its three uses follow the psalm’s own arc. This is a Move-2 finding the overview had noted as “live” but had not traced as sequential. Surfaced by: Tool 11 Move 2 across the sweep. 2. 2:22 is John’s own rule for reading him. “They remembered… and believed” licenses the retrospective understanding John repeatedly flags (2:22; 12:16; 13:7; 20:9) — four instances, verified.

Solo dig: Standard — with the caveat that the two-cleansings question needs settling first.

Pericope 6 — John 3:1–21 — Born from above

Gist. Nicodemus comes by night; Jesus tells him no one can see the kingdom without being born ἄνωθεν; the dialogue moves through Spirit and wind, the lifted-up serpent, and God’s love for the world, to judgement as the light’s arrival.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: 2:23–25 has just said many believed at the signs but Jesus did not entrust himself to them, “for he knew what was in man”. Necessity: Nicodemus is the immediate, named case study of exactly that — “we know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs” (3:2) is sign-based belief, and Jesus does not accept it but goes underneath it to new birth [T]. The chapter division at 2:25/3:1 obscures a seam that is not there: 3:1 begins “Now there was a man…” (ἄνθρωπος), picking up “what was in man” (ἀνθρώπῳ) verbatim. This is a verified verbal join and it is the single most important positional finding in the sweep’s first four chapters. Implication: John 3 is not a generic evangelistic conversation; it is a demonstration that even the best sign-based belief is not birth.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἄνωθεν — verified: 5 occurrences (3:3, 3:7, 3:31, 19:11, 19:23), and the two later uses both mean “from above” unambiguously, which weights the sense here [T]. πνεῦμα/wind wordplay (v.8). βασιλεία — verified: only 3 occurrences in John (3:3, 3:5, 18:36), so the kingdom vocabulary is vestigial and appears only at the two moments where a Jewish teacher and a Roman governor need it [T]. - Repetition. ἀμὴν ἀμήν 3× in the chapter · πιστεύω 7× · γεννάω 5× in vv.3–8 (verified). - Linking words. v.14 “and as (καθώς)… so must (οὕτως δεῖ)” — the comparison carries the necessity; v.16 “for (γάρ)” grounds the lifting-up in love; v.17 “for” again; vv.19–21 the ὅτι-chain of judgement. - Tone. Shifts from dialogue to monologue somewhere around v.13 — where the quotation marks close is genuinely uncertain and translations differ. Flag for the preacher.

Tool 11. - Numbers 21:8–9 → 3:14 (high confidence — explicit). Source context: Israel grumbles, is bitten by serpents, and is healed not by removal of the serpents but by looking at a bronze serpent lifted on a pole. The remedy is homeopathic and humiliating: the sign of the curse becomes the means of life. Immediately before, the people “spoke against God and against Moses”; immediately after, they journey on. Book usage: first use of Numbers; supplies ὑψόω, which recurs at 8:28 and 12:32–34 — verified: 5 occurrences in 4 verses, with the narrator glossing the third (12:33) as crucifixion [T]. OT-to-OT: Num 21 is picked up in Wis 16:5–7 and stands in the wilderness-grumbling cycle that Ps 78 (cited at 6:31) also narrates — so John’s two Pentateuchal engagements are drawing on the same wilderness sequence [I]. What it adds: the crucifixion is framed as an object of looking, not of achievement, and the healing works for those already bitten. Also: the serpent is the accursed thing, which sharpens Gal 3:13 without John saying it. - Internal: “born of water and the Spirit” (3:5) → 7:38–39 (the Spirit not yet given); 19:34 (blood and water); 20:22 (breathed) — planted, moderate. “lifted up” → 8:28; 12:32, high. “the light has come into the world and people loved the darkness” (3:19) → 9:39–41; 12:35–46, high. Nicodemus “by night” → 19:39 (the narrator explicitly back-references), high.

Sweep findings. 1. 3:1 continues 2:25 without a break; Nicodemus is the case study of unreliable sign-faith. Verified by the ἄνθρωπος/ἀνθρώπῳ join. Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Vocabulary, Context. 2. “Kingdom” appears three times in John, two of them here. John translates the Synoptic category into his own (life, birth, abiding) and only reaches for it when speaking to a rabbi and to Pilate. Surfaced by: Vocabulary count, Genre.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 7 — John 3:22–36 — He must increase

Gist. A dispute over purifying prompts John’s disciples to complain that everyone is going to Jesus; the Baptist answers with the bridegroom’s friend, “he must increase, I must decrease”, and a closing discourse on the one who comes from above.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the Nicodemus dialogue’s talk of heavenly things and the one who descended. Necessity: the section exists here to close the Baptist’s role before the narrative leaves Judea (4:1–3) and to give the reader a positive model of response over against Nicodemus’s inconclusive one. Implication: the two halves of chapter 3 are a diptych — a teacher of Israel who cannot see, and a wilderness prophet who sees clearly and steps aside [I].

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ὁ φίλος τοῦ νυμφίου — the bridegroom’s friend, whose joy is complete at hearing the voice; χαρὰ πεπλήρωται (v.29), the first of the “joy made full” formula (verified: χαρ- 18× in John, with the “full joy” formula at 3:29; 15:11; 16:24; 17:13). - Structure. Where the Baptist’s speech ends and the evangelist’s commentary begins (v.31?) is uncertain — same problem as v.13 above. Flag both. - Tone. Generous, self-effacing, almost valedictory.

Tool 11. - Conceptual: the bridegroom (Hos 2:16–20; Isa 62:5; Jer 2:2) (moderate). Move 1 only. YHWH is Israel’s husband; John assigns the bridegroom role to Jesus without argument, which is a high christological move made in passing [I]. - Internal: “the bride belongs to the bridegroom” (3:29) → 2:1–11 (a wedding where Jesus supplies the wine the bridegroom did not) — answers §earlier, moderate; this is one of the sweep’s quieter finds and it runs backwards, not forwards. “He must increase” → 12:32 (“I will draw all”) — planted, moderate. “He who comes from above is above all” (3:31) → 8:23 (“you are from below; I am from above”), high.

Sweep findings. 1. The bridegroom saying at 3:29 retro-interprets Cana. Read forwards, 2:1–11 is a sign about abundance; read back through 3:29, the man supplying the wedding wine is the bridegroom. Surfaced by: Move 4, Vocabulary. 2. Two unmarked speech-endings in one chapter (3:13?; 3:31?) — a translation-and-punctuation issue with real preaching consequences. Goes to Open Questions.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 8 — John 4:1–42 — The Samaritan woman

Gist. At Jacob’s well Jesus asks a Samaritan woman for a drink, offers living water, exposes her marital history, redirects the worship question to spirit and truth, declares ἐγώ εἰμι, and many Samaritans believe — first on her word, then on his.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: Nicodemus — male, Jewish, named, a ruler, by night, inconclusive. Necessity: the narrative places the exact inverse immediately after — female, Samaritan, unnamed, disreputable, at noon, conclusive [T]. The pairing is deliberate and the chapter break between them is the only thing obscuring it. Implication: John 3 and John 4 are one argument about who receives the birth from above, and preaching either alone halves it.

Live tools. - Structure. Five movements: the well (vv.1–15), the husbands (16–19), worship (20–26), the disciples and the harvest (27–38), the Samaritans (39–42). - Vocabulary. ὕδωρ ζῶν “living water” — running water in ordinary speech, which is why she misunderstands [T]. προσκυνέω 9× in vv.20–24 alone — the densest concentration in the book. ἐγώ εἰμι (v.26) — verified: the first of the nine absolute uses. - Narrator’s comment. v.9 “for Jews have no dealings with Samaritans” — an explanatory aside for readers outside the culture, part of the audience-mapping evidence [T]. v.44 (the prophet without honour) is a notorious crux. - Repetition. ὥρα 6× in ch. 4 — the heaviest of any chapter (verified), including “the hour is coming, and is now here” (v.23), the first of the realised-eschatology formula (also 5:25). - Copycat. The woman is a normative pattern in one respect only — she leaves her jar and tells the town (vv.28–29), and the narrator credits her testimony (v.39). Her marital history is descriptive and the sermon that dwells on it is usually doing something else.

Tool 11. - Genesis 29 (the betrothal type-scene) → 4:1–26 (moderate–high — narrative pattern, not verbal). Source context: the well type-scene — a man travels, meets a woman at a well, water is drawn, she runs home, a betrothal follows (Gen 24 Rebekah; Gen 29 Rachel; Exod 2 Zipporah). Book usage: the Jacob thread is live in this very chapter (4:5, 6, 12 — verified, the only three Jacob references in John). OT-to-OT: the three well-scenes are already a self-conscious canonical set. What it adds: every beat is present and the ending is transformed — she runs home, but what follows is not a betrothal but a town’s faith. Given 3:29 has just named Jesus the bridegroom, the type-scene is being cashed. This is the adjacency/narrative-pattern check firing: the frame was established next door. - Zechariah 14:8 / Ezekiel 47 (living water) → 4:10–14 (moderate). Move 1 only; the fuller use is at 7:38. - Internal: “the hour is coming and now is” (4:23) → 5:25, high. “living water” (4:10) → 7:38–39 (with the narrator’s Spirit gloss), high. “I who speak to you am he” (4:26) → 8:24, 28, 58; 18:5–8, high. “other sheep not of this fold” is prefigured by the Samaritan harvest → 10:16; 11:52, planted, moderate–high. “he had to pass through Samaria” (ἔδει, 4:4) → the δεῖ of 3:14 and 12:34 — divine necessity language, moderate.

Sweep findings. 1. Chapters 3 and 4 are a matched pair and should be preached as one argument. Every variable is inverted and the outcomes are opposite. Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Structure, Tone. 2. The betrothal type-scene is licensed by 3:29, not imported. The bridegroom title is supplied one pericope earlier, which is what raises this from “uncertain” to “moderate–high”.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 9 — John 4:43–54 — The second sign

Gist. In Galilee an official begs Jesus to come down and heal his son; Jesus rebukes sign-seeking, speaks a word at a distance, the boy recovers at that hour, and the household believes — the second sign.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: Samaritans who believed without a sign, on a word (4:41–42). Necessity: the contrast is immediate and pointed — “unless you see signs and wonders you will not believe” (4:48) lands on a Galilean Jew directly after Samaritans believed on hearing alone [T]. Implication: the sign that follows is deliberately the least spectacular kind — a word spoken at a distance, verified afterwards by a time-check. John is dismantling sign-faith while narrating a sign.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. δεύτερον σημεῖον (v.54) — the second and last numbered sign. ὥρα (vv.52, 53) — the time-check is the point. - Repetition. “Your son lives” (ζῇ) 3× in vv.50–53 — the word repeated at each stage of the man’s growing belief [T]. - Structure. A three-stage belief: he believes the word (v.50), he learns the hour (v.52), “he himself believed, and all his household” (v.53). Belief is shown in motion, which is characteristic.

Tool 11. - Internal: the Cana-to-Cana inclusio: 2:11 “the first of his signs… at Cana” ↔︎ 4:54 “the second sign… when he had come from Judea to Galilee” — answers §earlier, high, and it brackets 2:1–4:54 as a discrete movement. This is a verified structural bracket and the strongest reason to treat 1:19–4:54 as one arc. “Your son lives” → 11:25–26 (“whoever lives and believes in me shall never die”) — planted, moderate. - No external OT citation. Move 1–3 N/A — no quotation or high-confidence allusion in this pericope.

Sweep finding. 1. The Cana–Cana bracket is countable and closes the book’s first movement. The two numbered signs are its posts.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 10 — John 5:1–18 — Healing at Bethesda; the Sabbath charge

Gist. At a feast Jesus heals a man ill thirty-eight years, tells him to take up his mat, and — because it is a Sabbath — the authorities pursue him; his answer, “My Father is working until now, and I am working”, escalates the charge to blasphemy.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: two Cana signs bracketing a movement of spreading belief. Necessity: the narrative now needs its first hostility, and it needs it in Jerusalem at a feast, because from here the book runs on the festal calendar and on a lethal opposition. 5:18 is the hinge of the whole Gospel’s plot: “this was why the Jews were seeking all the more to kill him” — the first of eleven kill/seize notices (verified: ἀποκτείνω at 5:18; 7:1, 19, 20, 25; 8:37, 40; 11:53; 12:10; 16:2; 18:31, plus πιάζω at 7:30, 32, 44; 8:20; 10:39; 11:57). Implication: everything from ch. 5 to ch. 12 is lived under a death sentence already passed.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. v.9b “Now that day was the Sabbath” — a single clause that converts a healing into a capital case [T]. v.18 is the narrator’s own summary of the charge, and it names two offences: Sabbath-breaking and calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God. - Vocabulary. ἴσον τῷ θεῷ (v.18) — the highest christological statement so far, and it is on the lips of opponents, reported by the narrator without correction. - Textual variants. 5:3b–4 (the angel troubling the water) is absent from the earliest manuscripts and is bracketed or footnoted in modern editions; verified: SBLGNT omits 5:4 entirely. High confidence. Preachers using the “waiting for the stirring” motif should know they are preaching a later gloss. - Copycat. The healed man is not a model. He does not know who healed him (v.13), and his subsequent report to the authorities (v.15) is at best ambiguous. Contrast the blind man of ch. 9 deliberately.

Tool 11. - Conceptual: God’s Sabbath work (moderate–high). Jewish reflection held that God does not cease from sustaining, judging and giving life on the Sabbath, since births and deaths continue; Jesus claims that same non-ceasing prerogative [S] — this is background, not text, and is properly tagged. - Internal: planted for later. “Do you want to be healed?” and the thirty-eight years → 9:1 (“blind from birth”) — the two Sabbath healings are a deliberate pair, and 9:14 repeats the Sabbath note, high. “Sin no more, that nothing worse may happen” (5:14) → 9:2–3 (where Jesus denies the sin-suffering link) — answers §earlier, moderate–high, and the apparent tension is a live pastoral question. “Seeking to kill him” (5:18) → the whole kill-thread above, high.

Sweep findings. 1. 5:18 is the plot’s ignition, and it is a narrator’s verdict, not a character’s accusation. Surfaced by: Narrator’s Comment, Repetition (the kill-thread), Positional Necessity. 2. The two Sabbath healings (5 and 9) are a matched pair with opposite outcomes — one man betrays his healer, one man worships him. Verified by the shared Sabbath note and the shared chronic-condition framing.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 11 — John 5:19–47 — The Son and the four witnesses

Gist. Jesus answers the blasphemy charge with a discourse on the Son’s derived-but-equal authority to give life and judge, then calls four witnesses — John, the works, the Father, and the Scriptures — and charges his hearers with not believing Moses.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the charge of 5:18 — Sabbath-breaking and self-equation with God. Necessity: the discourse is a defence speech, and its structure is forensic because the charge was forensic. This is why the witness vocabulary peaks here: μαρτυρ- occurs 11× in ch. 5, the heaviest of any chapter (verified). Implication: John 5 is a trial scene, and it sets the template for chs. 7–10; reading it as abstract Christology loses the courtroom.

Live tools. - Structure. Two halves: the Son’s authority (vv.19–30) and the witnesses (vv.31–47). The seam is v.31 (“if I alone bear witness…”). - Repetition. πατήρ 14× in ch. 5 (verified) · μαρτυρ- 11× · ζωή 7× · κρίνω/κρίσις 7× — the four-word cluster (Father, witness, life, judgement) is the chapter. - The defendant is the judge — 5:22 with 5:27. “The Father judges no one, but has given all judgement (τὴν κρίσιν πᾶσαν) to the Son” (5:22), and “he has given him authority to execute judgement, because he is the Son of Man” (5:27). [T] High. Inside a defence speech against a capital charge, the accused states that all judgement has been transferred to him. This is the hinge of the book’s forensic architecture and the sweep’s first pass under-weighted it: the reversal by which the one on trial is the one before whom all are tried is not merely an ironic effect of the passion narrative but an explicit claim made in ch. 5 and then dramatised for fourteen chapters. Cross-report to the judgement thread: 3:19; 5:22, 27; 9:39; 12:31; 16:8, 11 (verified). - Linking words. The γάρ-chain of vv.19–23 (the Son does nothing of his own accord, for the Father loves the Son and shows him all…), and the ἵνα of v.23 (that all may honour the Son as they honour the Father) — the purpose-clause is the christological climax. - Vocabulary. ἀμὴν ἀμήν 3× (vv.19, 24, 25) marking the movement’s stages. “The hour is coming, and is now here” (v.25) against “an hour is coming” (v.28) — realised and future eschatology deliberately juxtaposed, unreconciled [T].

Tool 11. - Deuteronomy 19:15 (two or three witnesses) → 5:31–37 (moderate–high, conceptual). Source context: the law of evidence; a single witness cannot establish a charge. Book usage: John will cite the same principle explicitly at 8:17 (“in your Law it is written that the testimony of two men is true”) — verified, so this is a source John returns to, and the second use is explicit. OT-to-OT: the two-witness rule stands behind Num 35:30 and is invoked in the prophets’ covenant lawsuits [I]. What it adds: Jesus is not evading the rule but satisfying it — and the witnesses he calls exceed the statutory minimum. - Moses / the Scriptures → 5:39, 45–47 (high confidence — explicit). “You search the Scriptures… it is they that bear witness about me” and “he wrote of me”. Source context: not one text but the Pentateuch as a whole. Book usage: Moses is named 11× in John (verified: 1:17, 45; 3:14; 5:45, 46; 6:32; 7:19, 22, 23; 9:28, 29), and the pattern is consistent — Moses is invoked by opponents as their authority and by Jesus as his witness. What it adds: this is John’s own statement of his hermeneutic and the warrant for every OT reading in the book.

Sweep findings. 1. 5:39 and 5:46 license the whole Gospel’s use of the OT. Any Tool 11 work anywhere in John is doing what 5:39 says the Scriptures do. 2. John 5 is the trial-template. Forensic vocabulary, witness-law, charge and defence — the shape recurs at 7:14–52, 8:12–59, 9:13–34, and finally 18:19–19:16. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Genre, and confirmed across the sweep.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 12 — John 6:1–21 — Feeding, kingship refused, and the sea

Gist. Near Passover Jesus feeds five thousand from five barley loaves; the crowd tries to make him king; he withdraws, then comes to the disciples on the sea with “ἐγώ εἰμι; do not be afraid.”

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a Jerusalem trial scene ending in “you refuse to come to me that you may have life” (5:40). Necessity: the scene relocates to Galilee and to a crowd that does come — and shows that coming for bread is not coming for life. The Passover note (6:4) is unmotivated by the plot and is therefore interpretive: John wants the manna and the lamb in the reader’s ear [T]. Implication: the two signs here are a matched pair — provision and presence — and the second is a theophany.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. κριθίνους “barley” (v.9) — John alone specifies it, and barley is the Elisha detail. συναγάγετε / συνήγαγον (vv.12–13) “gather” — verified: συνάγω occurs 4× in John (6:12, 13; 11:47, 52), which makes the fragment-gathering verbally continuous with 11:52’s “gather into one the scattered children of God” [T]. This is a genuine and, so far as the sweep can tell, under-noticed link. - Narrator’s comment. v.6 “he said this to test him, for he himself knew what he would do” — the reader is told the disciples are being examined. - Repetition. ἄρτος begins its run (13× in ch. 6, verified).

Tool 11. - Exodus 16 / Psalm 78:24 → anticipated here, cited at 6:31. Handled at Pericope 13. - 2 Kings 4:42–44 (Elisha’s feeding) → 6:5–13 (moderate). Source context: twenty barley loaves feed a hundred over a servant’s objection, “and they ate and had some left”. Book usage: first and only use; John is not tracking Kings. What it adds: the type is prophet-feeding and the escalation is the point — five loaves, five thousand, twelve baskets. [I] - Job 9:8 / Psalm 77:19 / Isaiah 43:1–2, 10–13 (sea-theophany) → 6:16–21 (moderate–high). Source context: “who alone trampled the waves of the sea” (Job 9:8); “your way was through the sea, yet your footprints were unseen” (Ps 77:19); and Isaiah’s “I am he… fear not… when you pass through the waters” (43:2, 10). Book usage: Isaiah is live (1:23; and later 6:45; 12:38, 40). OT-to-OT: Isa 43’s ἐγώ εἰμι is itself a reworking of Exod 3:14, and Ps 77 rehearses the Red Sea — so the three texts are already one exodus-theophany cluster [I]. What it adds: the sea-crossing is not a rescue anecdote but an identification: the one who feeds is the one who walks the sea and says “I am”. - Internal: the refusal of coerced kingship (6:15) → 12:13–15; 18:36; 19:15 — planted, moderate–high. “Gather the fragments, that nothing may be lost” (6:12) → 17:12 (“not one of them is lost”); 18:9 — planted, moderate–high, and the ἀπόλλυμι verb carries.

Sweep findings. 1. “Gather… that nothing may be lost” is a plant, not housekeeping. The verbal chain runs 6:12 → 11:52 → 17:12 → 18:9. Surfaced by: Vocabulary (συνάγω count), Move 4. 2. The sea-crossing is a theophany, and it is the first absolute ἐγώ εἰμι after 4:26. Preaching it as a lesson in trusting Jesus in storms drops the Isaiah 43 register.

Solo dig: Standard (the ⭐ weight in this chapter sits on the discourse).

Pericope 13 — John 6:22–59 — I am the bread of life

Gist. The crowd follows across the lake; Jesus exposes their motive, redirects them from perishable food to the Son of Man, is met with the manna proof-text, and answers with himself as the bread from heaven — culminating in eating his flesh and drinking his blood.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a feeding, a refused kingship, and a sea-theophany. Necessity: the discourse exists here because the crowd has drawn exactly the wrong conclusion from the sign (6:26), and John’s method is sign-then-discourse — the discourse is the sign’s authorised interpretation. Implication: the offence of vv.51–58 is not accidental; the passage is designed to sift, and it succeeds (6:66).

Live tools. - Repetition. ἄρτος 13× · ζωή 11× in ch. 6, the highest of any chapter (verified) · “come down from heaven” 7× · “raise him up on the last day” 4× (vv.39, 40, 44, 54) — a refrain, not a repetition. - Vocabulary. τρώγω (vv.54, 56, 57, 58) — a coarser verb than ἐσθίω, “chew/gnaw”; the shift is deliberate and intensifying [T], high confidence. γογγύζω (vv.41, 43, 61) — verified: γογγυ- occurs 5× in John, at 6:41, 43, 61 and 7:12, 32 — the LXX wilderness-grumbling verb, used of the people in Exod 16 and Num 11. That the crowd cites the manna text and then grumbles is the chapter’s sharpest irony [T]. - Linking words. The ground-clauses of vv.37–40, 44–45, 65 — “all that the Father gives me will come to me… no one can come unless the Father draws him… this is why I told you that no one can come to me unless it is granted him by the Father”. Three statements of the same claim at three stages. - Difficult verses. vv.53–58 and the eucharistic question; v.63 (“the flesh is of no avail”) is the internal control. Also the Father’s giving/drawing and human responsibility asserted together without balancing.

Tool 11. - Psalm 78:24 (with Exodus 16:4) → 6:31 (high — explicit citation). Source context: Psalm 78 is Asaph’s indictment-by-history: they saw the wonders and “did not believe” (78:22), ate and were filled but “did not stop their craving” (78:29–31), and the generation perished. The manna verse sits inside a psalm about signs met with unbelief. Book usage: first Psalter citation after Ps 69:9 at 2:17; the Psalms are live. And Ps 78’s own thesis — many signs, no belief — is restated by John in his own voice at 12:37, which is arguably a structural quotation of the psalm’s logic [I]. OT-to-OT: Ps 78:24 is already a meditation on Exod 16 and Num 11, and Deut 8:3 (“man does not live by bread alone”) is the same tradition. What it adds: the crowd’s proof-text is a text about people exactly like them; Jesus’s correction is threefold — not Moses but the Father, not gave but gives, not manna but himself. - Isaiah 54:13 → 6:45 (high — explicit, “it is written in the Prophets”). Source context: the song of the restored barren woman, immediately downstream of the fourth Servant Song; “all your children shall be taught by the LORD” is new-covenant fruit of Isa 53’s atoning work. Book usage: second of four Isaiah engagements (1:23 → 6:45 → 12:38, 40). OT-to-OT: Isa 54:13 is in canonical conversation with Jer 31:34 (“they shall all know me”). What it adds: the Father’s “drawing” (v.44) is identified as prophetic teaching, and because Isa 54 flows from Isa 53, election-language and atonement-language are joined at their OT root — which is exactly what v.51 then supplies. - Internal: “I am the bread of life” (6:35) is the first predicated ἐγώ εἰμι — planted for the series (8:12; 10:7, 11; 11:25; 14:6; 15:1), high. “Raise him up on the last day” → 11:24–25, high. “One of you is a devil” (6:70–71) → 13:2, 27; 18:2–5, high.

Sweep findings. 1. The chapter is built out of its opponents’ own text. Ps 78 is a psalm about signs-without-faith, quoted by a crowd exhibiting signs-without-faith. Surfaced by: Tool 11 Move 1, Repetition (γογγύζω), Tone. 2. γογγύζω appears 5× in John, all in chs. 6–7. The wilderness register is confined to the two feast-chapters where manna and water are at issue. Verified.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 14 — John 6:60–71 — Many turned back

Gist. Many disciples find the saying hard and leave; Jesus asks the Twelve if they will go too; Peter answers “to whom shall we go?”; Jesus names one of them a devil.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a discourse designed to offend. Pericope 15 — John 7:1–52 — Tabernacles: rivers of living water

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: mass defection in Galilee (6:66) and a named betrayer. Pericope 16 — [John 7:53–8:11] — The woman caught in adultery

Gist. Scribes and Pharisees bring a woman taken in adultery; Jesus writes on the ground, says “let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone”, and dismisses her without condemnation.

Textual variants — the decisive tool here. The passage is absent from the earliest and best Greek manuscripts (𝔓⁶⁶, 𝔓⁷⁵, Sinaiticus, Vaticanus), is absent from the earliest versions and from the Greek fathers’ commentaries on John, and where it does appear it floats — after John 7:36, after John 21:25, and after Luke 21:38 in different witnesses. Its Greek vocabulary is un-Johannine. Verified at the level of the edition used here: SBLGNT brackets it, and the 866-verse count of this edition against the 879 of the KJV is accounted for by these twelve verses plus 5:4. High confidence that it is not part of John’s text as composed; the question of whether it preserves a genuine tradition is separate and much more open.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: Nicodemus’s “does our law judge a man without a hearing?” (7:51) and the officers’ failure to arrest. Necessity: whoever placed it here placed it well — a scene about judging without hearing, immediately after a complaint about judging without hearing, and immediately before “I judge no one… you judge according to the flesh” (8:15). The insertion is intelligent. But intelligent placement by a later hand is not authorial placement, and the distinction must be preached honestly [I].

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἀναμάρτητος “without sin” — a NT hapax. κατακρίνω — also unusual for John, who prefers κρίνω. - Copycat. The commonest misuse: “do not judge” as a general prohibition. The scene is about these accusers’ standing to execute, not about moral discernment; and it ends with “go, and from now on sin no more”.

Tool 11. - Deuteronomy 17:7 / 19:15–21 (witnesses cast the first stone) (moderate–high). Move 1: the law requires the witnesses’ hands to be first upon the condemned — so “let him who is without sin be the first” is a legal thrust, not a sentimental one. Book usage: the two-witness rule is live in John at 8:17 [T] — though whether the interpolator knew that is unknowable. - Internal: If read in place: “neither do I condemn you” ↔︎ 3:17 (“God did not send his Son into the world to condemn the world”), moderate. The nil-return caveat: internal-echo work on an interpolated passage tests the interpolator’s reading of John, not John’s own composition — a distinction worth stating in the report.

Sweep findings. 1. The strongest preaching decision here is a prior one: whether to preach it at all, and if so, how to say what it is. The sweep’s recommendation is that it can be preached — the church has received it — but only with the manuscript facts stated plainly, which most congregations find strengthening rather than unsettling.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH if preached — the textual work must be done properly, not gestured at.

Pericope 17 — John 8:12–30 — The light of the world; the witness dispute

Gist. “I am the light of the world”; the Pharisees challenge his self-witness; Jesus invokes the two-witness rule with the Father as co-witness, says they will die in their sins unless they believe ἐγώ εἰμι, and points to the lifting-up as the moment of recognition.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the Booths water-cry (7:37–39). Pericope 18 — John 8:31–59 — Before Abraham was, I am

Gist. To those who had believed him Jesus speaks of abiding, truth and freedom; the exchange escalates through Abrahamic descent to paternity — “you are of your father the devil” — and ends with “before Abraham was, I am” and an attempted stoning.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: “as he was saying these things, many believed in him” (8:30). Necessity: the discourse is addressed to believers (v.31, “the Jews who had believed him”) and it is the harshest in the Gospel. That juxtaposition is the pericope’s engine and it is invisible if the sermon starts at v.31 without v.30 [T]. Implication: John is showing belief that does not abide — which is exactly the point of 2:23–25 and 6:66. The three scenes form a series on unreliable belief.

Live tools. - Repetition. Ἀβραάμ 9× — verified: all nine occurrences of Abraham in John are in this pericope (8:33–58) · πατήρ 20× in ch. 8, most of them contesting paternity · ἐλεύθερος/δοῦλος 5× in vv.32–36 (verified). - Vocabulary. μένω (v.31, “if you abide in my word”) — the first appearance of the abiding-condition that ch. 15 will develop; verified: μένω 34× in John, 8× of them in ch. 15. διάβολος (v.44) — verified: only 4 devil/Satan references in John (6:70; 8:44; 13:2, 27), so the paternity charge is exceptional, not routine polemic. - Tone. The most violent exchange in the Gospel. Preachers must decide in advance how to voice v.44 — it is a pastoral landmine and has a long history of misuse. - Difficult verses. v.44 (“your father the devil”) is the single most weaponised verse in John. It is spoken to a specific group in a specific dispute about paternity, and John’s own frame is that the issue is response to Jesus, not ethnicity (cf. 4:22, “salvation is from the Jews”). Say so explicitly from the pulpit.

Tool 11. - Genesis 17 / 22 (Abrahamic descent and “your father Abraham rejoiced to see my day”) → 8:33–58 (the descent-claim high; the “my day” reference moderate). Source context: which Abrahamic moment is “my day”? Candidates: Gen 17:17 (Abraham laughed), Gen 22 (the Akedah and “the LORD will provide”), Gen 15 (the covenant vision). Book usage: Abraham appears only here. OT-to-OT: Gen 22’s “on the mount of the LORD it shall be provided” is already read forward within Genesis. What it adds: the claim is not merely pre-existence but that Abraham’s own hope had Jesus as its object — which converts the genealogical argument into a christological one. Uncertain which Abrahamic text is in view — a good Logos question. - Exodus 3:14 → 8:58 (high — and the strongest instance in the book). Source context: as above. Book usage: the climactic absolute ἐγώ εἰμι. What it adds: the tense-clash is the argument — “before Abraham came to be (γενέσθαι), I am (εἰμί)” — being against becoming, exactly the Prologue’s ἦν/ἐγένετο contrast (1:1–3). The stoning attempt (8:59) is the hearers’ own commentary on what they heard, and it is the best available evidence for how to read the verse [T]. - Internal: the ἦν/ἐγένετο contrast of 1:1–3 → 8:58, high, and this is a Prologue plant cashed fifty-seven chapters-worth of narrative later. “Abide in my word” (8:31) → 15:4–10, high. Stones taken up (8:59; 10:31) → 11:38–41; 20:1 (the stone at the tomb) — planted, moderate (verbal carrier λίθος verified, but the theological link is inferential).

Sweep findings. 1. 8:31 addresses believers, and the sweep now has three scenes of unreliable belief (2:23–25; 6:60–66; 8:31–59). Read together they are a sustained Johannine argument that belief must abide. This is a cross-passage finding not visible from any one pericope. 2. The Prologue’s ἦν/ἐγένετο grammar is the key to 8:58. Verified verbal contrast; it means the “I am” is not a stray theophany-formula but the payoff of the book’s opening sentence.

Pericope 19 — John 9:1–41 — The man born blind

Gist. Jesus heals a man blind from birth on a Sabbath, using mud and the pool of Siloam; the man is interrogated four times, his parents evade for fear of expulsion, he is cast out, Jesus finds him and he worships; the Pharisees are told their guilt remains.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: “I am the light of the world” (8:12), and an attempted stoning. Necessity: the light-claim must be enacted, and 9:5 repeats it verbatim before the sign — the discourse supplies the interpretation in advance for once. The chapter also resumes the Sabbath-healing controversy of ch. 5. Implication: this is the fullest narrative in John, and it is a trial narrative — four interrogations, a witness, an intimidated family, and a verdict.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. v.7 “Siloam (which means Sent)” — a decoder aside that keys the whole sending-Christology; the pool’s name is apestalmenos, the participle used of Jesus throughout [T]. v.22 “for the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess him to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue” — the presenting-situation verse, and one of only three aposynagōgos notices (9:22; 12:42; 16:2, verified). - Repetition. πόθεν / “we do not know where he is from” — the where-from question fires five times in this chapter (9:12, 21, 25, 29, 30, verified), and v.30 turns it into the man’s own argument: “why, this is an amazing thing! You do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes.” γεννάω 4× (vv.2, 19, 20, 34, verified). - Vocabulary. ἐκβάλλω (v.34, “they cast him out”) — verified: 5 occurrences in John (2:15; 6:37; 9:34, 35; 10:4), and 6:37 is the promise “whoever comes to me I will never cast out”. The men cast him out; Jesus finds him. This is a verified verbal echo and it is the pericope’s theological centre. - Copycat. The blind man is a normative pattern — his growing confession (a man → a prophet → from God → Lord, I believe) is narrated approvingly and contrasts pointedly with the Bethesda man of ch. 5. His courage under interrogation is the model John holds up for a community facing expulsion [I]. - Tone. Comic and then devastating. The man’s dry insolence (v.27, “do you also want to become his disciples?”) is genuinely funny and should be preached as such.

Tool 11. - Isaiah 35:5 / 42:7 (eyes of the blind opened) → 9:1–7 (moderate–high, conceptual). Move 1: opening blind eyes is a marker of the age to come and, in Isa 42, a task of the Servant. Book usage: Isaiah is live. What it adds: v.32’s “never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind” is an implicit claim that the age has arrived. - Genesis 2:7 (forming from the ground) → 9:6 (moderate). Mud and spittle to make eyes where there were none is closer to creation than to healing [I]; and John will use the Gen 2:7 verb at 20:22. Pericope 20 — John 10:1–21 — The good shepherd

Gist. The sheepfold figure, the door, and the good shepherd who lays down his life; hired hands flee, other sheep will be brought, one flock and one shepherd; the hearers divide again.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the leaders have just cast a healed man out of the fold (9:34). Necessity: the shepherd discourse follows an excommunication, and it is a judgement on the men who performed it — there is no break at 9:41/10:1, and 10:21’s back-reference to “the eyes of the blind” proves the join [T]. Implication: preaching John 10 as a pastoral idyll without John 9 is the commonest error available; the discourse is a prophetic indictment of shepherds who scatter.

Figure (1–6) → first exposition, the door (7–10) → second exposition, the shepherd (11–18) → division (19–21).

Tool 11. - Ezekiel 34 → 10:1–18 (high confidence). Pericope 21 — John 10:22–42 — At the Feast of Dedication

Gist. At Hanukkah in winter, “how long will you keep us in suspense?”; Jesus points to his works, promises no one will snatch his sheep, says “I and the Father are one”, faces a stoning, and argues from Psalm 82 before withdrawing beyond the Jordan.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the shepherd discourse and a division. Necessity: the feast-marker at v.22 (“it was the Feast of Dedication… it was winter”) is the panel-break — Booths is over, and this short scene closes the public Jerusalem ministry before the Lazarus sign. Dedication commemorates the rededication of a desecrated temple, which sits pointedly under a claim to be the one “whom the Father consecrated (ἡγίασεν) and sent into the world” (v.36) [I], moderate–high. Implication: Jesus is the rededicated temple, at the feast of rededication.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. v.22b “it was winter” — a two-word aside that does calendrical and atmospheric work at once. - Vocabulary. ἁγιάζω (v.36) — consecrate/set apart, the temple-dedication verb; it returns at 17:17, 19 (verified). ἁρπάζω “snatch” (vv.28, 29) — the wolf’s verb from v.12, now denied. - Structure — the closing bracket, and it is forensic, not merely geographic. “He went away again across the Jordan to the place where John had been baptising at first… and many came to him. The return to 1:28 (10:40) → the Baptist’s opening testimony, high — a verified geographic inclusio around the public ministry.

Sweep findings. 1. 1:19 ↔︎ 10:40–42 is the book’s first great bracket, and it closes with a verdict. Not merely a geographic inclusio but a forensic one: the first witness is examined at 1:19 and vindicated at 10:41 in the book’s own vocabulary (σημεῖον, ἀληθής). 13 of the Gospel’s 17 σημεῖον references fall inside this bracket (verified), and the last of them is the note that John himself worked none. 2. Psalm 82’s own context is a sentence of death on unjust judges. Move 1 changes the temperature of 10:34 considerably.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 22 — John 11:1–44 — I am the resurrection and the life

Gist. Lazarus falls ill; Jesus deliberately delays two days; Martha and Mary each meet him with the same reproach; Jesus weeps, is deeply moved, and calls Lazarus out of the tomb.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a withdrawal beyond the Jordan after a second stoning attempt. Necessity: the last and greatest sign must be a life-giving one, and it must be the thing that triggers the death-decree (11:53) — so the sign that gives life causes the death that gives life [T]. The disciples’ “the Jews were just now seeking to stone you, and are you going there again?” (11:8) explicitly links this pericope to 10:31. Implication: John has arranged the plot so that resurrection-power is the immediate cause of the crucifixion.

Live tools. - Linking words. v.5–6: “Now Jesus loved Martha and her sister and Lazarus. So (οὖν) when he heard that he was ill, he stayed two days longer.” The causal connector is the exegetical crux of the pericope, and the KJV/ESV “therefore” against the NIV’s “yet” is the classic case (see Tool 8 in the toolkit’s own materials). High confidence that the causal reading is right; the paradox is the point [T]. - Vocabulary. ἐμβριμάομαι (vv.33, 38) — “deeply moved”, but the verb carries anger or indignation, not merely sorrow; ἐτάραξεν ἑαυτόν “he troubled himself”. ἐδάκρυσεν (v.35). Moderate–high confidence that indignation is in the range; flag for lexicon. - Repetition. δόξα/δοξάζω 5× in ch. 11 (verified), framing the sign as glory-work (vv.4, 40) · λίθος 3× (vv.38, 39, 41). - Tone. The most emotionally exposed passage in the Gospel. A sermon that resolves the weeping too quickly into the miracle has mishandled it.

Tool 11. - Ezekiel 37:1–14 (the valley of dry bones) → 11:38–44 (moderate). Source context: a vision of national resurrection — “I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people” — with the Spirit as the agent. Book usage: Ezek 34 is live from ch. 10, so a second Ezekiel engagement has an elevated prior [I]. OT-to-OT: Ezek 37 uses the same Gen 2:7 breath-vocabulary that John will use at 20:22. What it adds: if live, the raising of one man is a token of the promised raising of the people, which is exactly how Caiaphas’s unwitting prophecy (11:50–52) then reads. Moderate — the verbal overlap is thin; the conceptual fit is strong. - Internal: “I am the resurrection and the life” (11:25) answers 5:25–29 and 6:39–54 (“raise him up on the last day”) by pulling the last day into the present — high, and Martha’s “I know he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day” is John making the connection explicitly for the reader. “Did I not tell you that if you believed you would see the glory of God?” (11:40) → 1:14; 2:11, high. Lazarus bound in cloths and needing to be unbound (11:44) → 20:6–7 (the cloths left behind, the face-cloth folded separately) — planted, moderate–high; the contrast between the man who needs unbinding and the man who leaves the grave-clothes behind is unmarked and is one of the sweep’s better finds.

Sweep findings. 1. 11:44 and 20:6–7 are a deliberate contrast. Lazarus is resuscitated and still bound; Jesus is raised and the cloths stay in the tomb. Verified at the level of the detail; the inference is [I], moderate–high. 2. The οὖν of 11:6 is load-bearing and translation-sensitive. Any English text that renders it “yet” has decided the exegesis for the preacher.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 23 — John 11:45–57 — One man should die for the people

Gist. Many believe; others report to the Pharisees; the council convenes, Caiaphas says it is expedient that one man die for the people, and the narrator explains he prophesied unwittingly; from that day they plot to kill him.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a public raising of the dead. Necessity: the decree must follow the sign immediately, because John’s argument is that the life-giving sign is what makes the death inevitable (v.53). Implication: the council’s stated motive is political preservation — “the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation” (v.48) — and the narrator lets the irony stand: they kill him to save the place, and the place is destroyed anyway.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. vv.51–52 is the most theologically explicit aside in the Gospel: “he did not say this of his own accord, but being high priest that year he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation, and not for the nation only, but also to gather into one the children of God who are scattered abroad.” The narrator converts a cynical calculation into a substitutionary-atonement statement [T]. - Vocabulary. συναγάγῃ εἰς ἕν (v.52) — verified: συνάγω 4× in John (6:12, 13; 11:47, 52), and εἰς ἕν recurs at 17:21–23. The council “gathers” (συνήγαγον, v.47) in order to destroy; God gathers into one through the destruction. The same verb, four verses apart, doing opposite work [T]. This is a sweep-only find. - Repetition. Ἰουδαῖοι 9× in ch. 11 (verified).

Tool 11. - Conceptual: the scattered-and-gathered flock (Ezek 34:12–13; Isa 11:12; Deut 30:3–4) (moderate–high). Move 1: the gathering of the scattered is a standard restoration promise. Book usage: Ezek 34 is live from ch. 10, and 10:16’s “other sheep… one flock” is the same promise in shepherd idiom four chapters earlier. What it adds: Caiaphas’s prophecy is Ezekiel 34’s promise placed in the mouth of one of Ezekiel 34’s defendants. - Internal: “gather into one” (11:52) → 10:16; 17:21–23, high. “It is better that one man should die for the people” (11:50) → 18:14 (the narrator repeats it verbatim at the arrest) — planted, high, and the repetition is the narrator making sure the reader has it in hand for the trial.

Sweep findings. 1. συνάγω at 11:47 and 11:52 is a deliberate collision. The council gathers to kill; the death gathers the scattered. 2. The narrator quotes Caiaphas twice (11:50; 18:14). Verified. He is the only character in John whose words are repeated by the narrator.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 24 — John 12:1–11 — The house filled with fragrance

Gist. Six days before Passover, at Bethany, Mary anoints Jesus’s feet with costly nard and wipes them with her hair; Judas objects; Jesus defends her, referring to the day of his burial; crowds come to see Lazarus, whom the chief priests also plot to kill.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the death-decree, and a note that Jesus withdrew (11:54). Necessity: the passion sequence needs a beginning, and John gives it a date-stamp (“six days before the Passover”, v.1) that starts the final week. Implication: the anointing is placed before the entry in John (after it in Mark), so the entry is greeted by a man already anointed for burial.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἐπλήσθη… ἐκ τῆς ὀσμῆς (v.3) “the house was filled with the fragrance” — verified: πληρόω at 12:3 is the same verb as the fulfilment-formula at 12:38; 15:25; 17:12; 18:9, 32; 19:24, 36 (10 occurrences total); a domestic detail carrying the book’s fulfilment verb. - Narrator’s comment. v.6 “he said this not because he cared about the poor, but because he was a thief” — one of the harshest asides in Scripture, and a deliberate inside-information move. - Internal: Mary wipes his feet with her hair (12:3) → Jesus washes the disciples’ feet and wipes them with a towel (13:5). ἐκμάσσω occurs exactly 3× in John — 11:2, 12:3, 13:5 — and nowhere else; every use is of feet being wiped (verified). A devotee’s act is answered one chapter later by the Lord’s [T], high. Mary’s ointment for burial (12:7) → 19:39–40 (Nicodemus’s seventy-five pounds of spices), high.

Tool 11. - Deuteronomy 15:11 → 12:8 (high — the saying quotes it). Source context: “there will never cease to be poor in the land” is the ground of a command to open your hand wide, not a resignation. What it adds: the verse is routinely quoted to excuse inaction, which inverts its own source. Worth naming from the pulpit. - Internal: as above.

Sweep findings. 1. ἐκμάσσω links Mary’s hair and Jesus’s towel. Verified; 12:3 → 13:5, and no English translation obscures it. 2. Deut 15:11’s context is a command to generosity. The most common misuse of 12:8 is corrected by Move 1 alone.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 25 — John 12:12–19 — The King on a donkey

Gist. The festival crowd meets Jesus with palm branches and “Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel”; he finds a young donkey; the narrator notes the disciples understood only after he was glorified; the Pharisees despair.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a burial-anointing. Necessity: the acclamation follows the anointing in John’s order, so the King is hailed by a crowd while already prepared for burial — the sequence is John’s editorial argument [I]. And the crowd’s motive is given: they came because of Lazarus (v.18). Implication: the entry is not a triumph misread but a truth misread — they acclaim him correctly and expect the wrong thing.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. v.16 “His disciples did not understand these things at first, but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered that these things had been written about him and had been done to him.” The book’s clearest statement of retrospective comprehension, and the fourth of four such notices (2:22; 12:16; 13:7; 20:9 — verified). - Vocabulary. κράζω (v.13) — the crowd’s first cry, and its later cries are “crucify” (19:6, 15). Verified. - Vocabulary — v.19, and it is a verified fourth station of the ἴδε thread. The Pharisees say to one another: “You see that you are gaining nothing. Ἴδε ὁ κόσμος ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ ἀπῆλθεν” — “Behold, the world has gone after him.” The interjection is ἴδε, the same word as Ἴδε ὁ ἀμνὸς τοῦ θεοῦ (1:29, 36) and Ἴδε ὁ βασιλεὺς ὑμῶν (19:14) — verified. [T] High. Three of the Gospel’s most freighted “Behold” statements are spoken by the Baptist, by the Pharisees in despair, and by Pilate in mockery; only the first speaker knows what he is saying. The Pharisees’ sarcasm announces what Zech 9:10 promised the Messiah’s reign would be — and Zech 9:9 is quoted four verses earlier. The sweep’s first pass missed this entirely; it was prompted by [S: conv] and then verified. - Repetition. βασιλεύς enters the vocabulary here and dominates 18:33–19:22.

Tool 11. - Psalm 118:25–26 → 12:13 (high — explicit). Source context: the last of the Hallel psalms, sung at the pilgrim feasts; “save now” (hôšîʿâ nāʾ) is a petition, and the psalm moves through the rejected stone becoming the cornerstone to a festal procession with branches to the altar. Book usage: fifth Psalter engagement. OT-to-OT: Ps 118’s rejected-stone verse is already the NT’s favourite christological stone-text; John does not quote it, but the crowd’s psalm contains it. What it adds: the crowd sings a salvation-petition and adds “the King of Israel” on their own initiative — the addition is theirs, not the psalm’s, and it is the acclamation Jesus refused at 6:15. - Zechariah 9:9 → 12:15 (high — explicit). Source context: the oracle of the humble king who comes riding a donkey, who will “cut off the chariot from Ephraim and the war horse from Jerusalem” and “speak peace to the nations” — an explicitly demilitarising king. Immediately after (9:11): “because of the blood of my covenant with you, I will set your prisoners free”. Book usage: second of three Zechariah engagements (2:16 Zech 14:21 moderate; 12:15 Zech 9:9; 19:37 Zech 12:10) — Zechariah is a live source, and John’s three uses come from chs. 14, 9, and 12 in that order, so he is not following the book’s sequence [T]. OT-to-OT: Zech 9–14 is itself a dense reworking of earlier shepherd and king material, including Ezek 34 — which John has used at ch. 10. What it adds: the donkey is the king’s policy statement. John abbreviates the citation and adds “Fear not, daughter of Zion” (from Zeph 3:16 or Isa 40:9), which is a small but real editorial move [I]. - Internal: the refused kingship of 6:15 → the acclaimed kingship of 12:13 → the redefined kingship of 18:36 → “we have no king but Caesar” (19:15) — the arc completes, high.

Sweep findings. 1. The kingship thread has four verified stations and ends in the priests’ apostasy. 6:15 → 12:13 → 18:36–37 → 19:15. Cross-passage; the last station is the darkest sentence in the Gospel. 2. John’s three Zechariah citations do not follow Zechariah’s order. Unlike Paul’s Deuteronomy 32 arc in Romans, John is not tracing a source’s internal movement here — a negative Move 2 finding, and worth recording as such.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 26 — John 12:20–36 — The hour has come

Gist. Greeks ask to see Jesus; he answers that the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified, speaks of the grain of wheat, is troubled, hears a voice from heaven, and declares that being lifted up he will draw all people — the crowd asks who this Son of Man is.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the Pharisees’ despairing “look, the world has gone after him” (12:19). Necessity: the very next verse produces Greeks — the “world” arriving in person — and that is what triggers “the hour has come” [T]. The trigger is not the entry; it is the Gentiles. Implication: the hour is set running by the arrival of the nations, which makes 12:32’s “I will draw all people” the answer to the Greeks’ request. Whether the Greeks ever meet Jesus is left unanswered — a deliberate narrative silence.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ὑψόω (vv.32, 34) with the narrator’s gloss at v.33 — verified: 5 occurrences in 4 verses (3:14; 8:28; 12:32, 34×2), and the third use is where John explains the double meaning. ἑλκύω “draw” (v.32) — verified: 3 occurrences (6:44; 12:32; 21:6). τεταρακται (v.27) “now is my soul troubled” — verified as part of a 5-fold ταράσσω thread (5:7; 12:27; 13:21; 14:1; 14:27). - Narrator’s comment. v.33 “he said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die” — the decoder that fixes ὑψόω. - Tone. Shifts sharply at v.27 from public declaration to something close to Gethsemane. John has no garden agony; this is where it goes, and preachers looking for it should look here.

Tool 11. - Internal: “the hour has come” (12:23) answers 2:4; 7:30; 8:20 — high, and it is the hinge of the clock-thread. “Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies” (12:24) → 15:1–8 (“bear much fruit”, same idiom) and 19:41 (a garden) — planted, moderate–high. “Now is my soul troubled” (12:27) and “he was troubled in spirit” (13:21) → “let not your hearts be troubled” (14:1, 14:27) — the same verb, verified, four times; Jesus is troubled twice, then twice forbids it to his disciples. Planted, high. This is the sweep’s best pastoral find and it is not in the overview. “Whoever loves his life loses it” (12:25) → 13:37–38; 15:13 (laying down ψυχή), moderate–high. - The voice from heaven (v.28) — John’s substitute for the Synoptic transfiguration and baptismal voice; the crowd hears thunder. Moderate confidence on the deliberate substitution.

Sweep findings. 1. The ταράσσω chain (12:27; 13:21 → 14:1, 27) means the “troubled heart” Jesus forbids is one he has himself just had. Verified four-point verbal thread; it changes the pastoral register of 14:1 completely. 2. The Greeks trigger the hour. Positional, and it makes 12:32 the answer to a request John never records being granted.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 27 — John 12:37–50 — Why they could not believe

Gist. The narrator’s verdict on the public ministry: though he had done so many signs before them, they did not believe — fulfilling Isaiah; yet many authorities believed but would not confess for fear of the Pharisees; Jesus cries out a final summary.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the hour has come and Jesus has hidden himself (12:36b). Necessity: the public ministry is over and requires a verdict, and John gives it in his own voice before the private ministry begins at 13:1. This is the seam, and it is countable: σημεῖον occurs 16 times before 12:37 and once after (20:30) [T]. Implication: everything from 13:1 is addressed to “his own”; the world has been given its answer.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. vv.37–43 is the longest sustained narratorial intrusion in the Gospel — a verdict, two Isaiah citations, an interpretation of Isaiah’s vision, and a sociological note about closet believers. Interpretive gold, and it is where the reader is told how to understand the preceding eleven chapters. - Repetition. δόξα/δοξάζω 8× in ch. 12 (verified) — the highest of any chapter, clustering around the hour. - Structure. vv.44–50 has no narrative setting at all — “Jesus cried out and said” with no audience specified. It reads as a summary appendix to the public ministry, placed after the verdict [I].

Tool 11. - Isaiah 53:1 → 12:38 (high — explicit). Source context: the opening line of the fourth Servant Song — “who has believed what he has heard from us, and to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?” The question introduces the passage about the Servant who is despised, rejected, and bears our griefs. It is a lament that the message has not been received. Book usage: third Isaiah engagement (1:23; 6:45; 12:38, 40). OT-to-OT: Isa 53:1 and Isa 6:10 (cited next) are both about a message not received — and Isa 6 is the commissioning that predicts the deafness Isa 53 laments. John cites them in the wrong chronological order and the right logical one. [I] - Isaiah 6:10 → 12:40 (high — explicit). Source context: the temple vision — Isaiah sees the Lord high and lifted up (ὑψηλοῦ in LXX, the same root as John’s ὑψόω), the seraphim, the coal, and the commission to preach to a people who will not hear. Book usage: fourth Isaiah engagement, closing the frame opened at 1:23. OT-to-OT: Isa 6 is the source-text for the hardening motif across the NT (Mark 4; Acts 28; Rom 11). What it adds: v.41 is the interpretive key — “Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke of him.” John claims that Isaiah’s temple vision was a vision of Christ. And the LXX’s “high and lifted up” gives John his ὑψόω. The ὑψόω/ὑψηλός link is moderate — worth a Logos check. - Internal: “so many signs… they did not believe” (12:37) answers Ps 78’s own thesis, cited at 6:31 — the psalm’s logic recurs as a Johannine structural verdict, moderate–high. “For fear of the Pharisees they did not confess him, lest they be put out of the synagogue” (12:42) → 9:22; 16:2 — the third of the three aposynagōgos notices, high. “They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God” (12:43) → 5:44 — answers §earlier, high, a near-verbatim repetition seven chapters apart.

Sweep findings. 1. 12:41 is the strongest hermeneutical claim in the Gospel — Isaiah’s vision was of Christ. It licenses every OT reading in the book and is much less preached than 5:39. 2. 5:44 and 12:43 are near-identical. “How can you believe, when you receive glory from one another?” (5:44) and “they loved the glory that comes from man” (12:43) bracket the public ministry with the same diagnosis. Verified; a clean inclusio around chs. 5–12.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 28 — John 13:1–20 — He loved them to the end

Gist. Before the Passover, knowing his hour had come and having loved his own, Jesus rises from supper, lays aside his garments, washes the disciples’ feet, resists and then instructs Peter, and gives the act as an example.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the public ministry has been given its verdict (12:37–43). Necessity: 13:1 opens the private ministry with a temporal marker (“before the Feast of the Passover”), a knowledge marker (“knowing that his hour had come”), and a love marker (“having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end”) — three of the book’s threads tied off in one sentence. This is the great seam [T]. Implication: everything that follows is addressed to a closed room, which is the corrective to the commonest Farewell-Discourse misuse.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. τίθησιν τὰ ἱμάτια (13:4) — “he lays aside his garments”. Verified: the present τίθησιν occurs 3× in John — 2:10 (setting out the good wine), 10:11 (“the good shepherd lays down his life”), 13:4. The footwashing uses the shepherd’s own verb, in the same form. [T], high confidence. λέντιον (vv.4, 5) — a Latin loanword (linteum), used twice here and nowhere else in the NT. νίπτω 5× in this pericope (verified) against its only other Johannine use at 9:7, 15 — the pool of Siloam. καθαρός (vv.10, 11) → 15:3. - Narrator’s comment. v.1 (his hour, his love), v.3 (knowing the Father had given all things into his hands), v.11 (he knew who was to betray him) — three asides in eleven verses, all establishing that the footwashing is done in full knowledge, not naivety. - Copycat. vv.14–15 make this explicitly prescriptive — “I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you”. One of the few places in John where the text itself answers the Copycat question [T]. - Difficult verses. v.10 (“the one who has bathed does not need to wash, except for his feet”) — the shorter reading omitting “except for his feet” has real manuscript support and changes the sense. Flag; moderate confidence in the longer reading.

Tool 11. - Internal: “his own” (τοὺς ἰδίους, 13:1) answers 1:11 (“he came to his own [τὰ ἴδια] and his own [οἱ ἴδιοι] did not receive him”) — the same word-group, verified; the phrase is redefined from those who rejected him to those he loves to the end. Answers §earlier, moderate–high. This is a Prologue plant cashed at the book’s hinge, and it is easy to miss in English. τίθημι (13:4) → 10:11, high. “You are clean” (13:10) → 15:3, high. “He was troubled in spirit” (13:21) → 14:1, 27 — see Pericope 26. - Conceptual: the slave’s task. Footwashing was the work of a non-Jewish slave; a Jewish slave could not be compelled to it [S] — background, flagged as such, and not load-bearing for the exegesis, which stands on the τίθημι verb alone.

Sweep findings. 1. The footwashing is an enacted laying-down-of-life. τίθησιν at 13:4 is the shepherd’s verb from 10:11 in the same form. Verified; and it means the footwashing is not merely a humility lesson but a passion-parable. 2. “His own” is redefined between 1:11 and 13:1. Verified word-group; a Prologue plant.

Solo dig: ⭐ HIGH.

Pericope 29 — John 13:21–38 — Betrayal, glory, and the new commandment

Gist. Jesus is troubled in spirit, names his betrayer by the morsel, Judas goes out — “and it was night”; then “now is the Son of Man glorified”, the new commandment to love one another, and Peter’s boast and predicted denial.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: the footwashing, with Judas’s feet among those washed. Necessity: the betrayal must be named after the washing, so that the reader sees the betrayer’s feet washed first; and the glory-saying must come after Judas leaves, because the departure sets the passion in motion. Implication: “now is the Son of Man glorified” (13:31) is triggered by the betrayal, which is the clearest possible statement that glory in John means the cross.

Live tools. - Narrator’s comment. v.30 “So, after receiving the morsel of bread, he immediately went out. And it was night.” Three words of scene-setting doing the work of a paragraph of theology — the light-and-darkness thread of 1:5; 3:19; 8:12; 12:35 lands here [T]. - Vocabulary. δοξάζω 5× in vv.31–32 alone — the densest cluster in the book. ἐντολὴ καινή (v.34) — verified: ἐντολή occurs 10× in John, first at 10:18 and then concentrated in 13–15. τὴν ψυχήν σου θήσεις (v.37) — Peter using the shepherd’s formula from 10:11 verbatim. - Tone. Night. Betrayal. A boast. The chapter ends on a cockcrow prediction and the reader waits eighteen chapters’ worth of narrative for its resolution.

Tool 11. - Psalm 41:9 → 13:18 (high — explicit, “that the Scripture may be fulfilled”). Source context: a psalm of sickness and betrayal; the psalmist is ill, his enemies whisper his death, and “even my close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me”. The psalm ends in a plea to be raised up. Notably, the psalm’s speaker also confesses sin (41:4) — which is why John truncates the citation, quoting only the betrayal clause [I]. Book usage: sixth Psalter engagement; the Psalms are the book’s most-used source after Isaiah. OT-to-OT: Ps 41 closes Book I of the Psalter and its betrayal-motif is picked up in Ps 55:12–14. What it adds: the betrayal is scripted, which protects Jesus’s sovereignty — the point John makes explicit at v.19 (“I am telling you this now, before it takes place, that when it does take place you may believe that ἐγώ εἰμι”). - Internal: ἐγώ εἰμι at 13:19 — the seventh absolute use, placed to interpret the betrayal, high. Peter’s “I will lay down my life for you” (13:37) → 10:11; 15:13; 21:15–19 — planted, high; Peter claims the shepherd’s role, fails, and is given it. “It was night” (13:30) → 3:2 (Nicodemus by night); 19:39 (Nicodemus in daylight), moderate.

Sweep findings. 1. Judas’s exit is what triggers “now is the Son of Man glorified”. Positional and verbal; the strongest single proof that John’s δόξα is passion-shaped. 2. Peter’s boast is a quotation of the good shepherd. 10:11 → 13:37, verified formula, and it sets up 21:15–17 as a restoration to the very office he claimed.

Solo dig: Standard.

Pericope 30 — John 14:1–14 — I am the way, the truth, and the life

Gist. “Let not your hearts be troubled”; many rooms in the Father’s house; Thomas’s “we do not know the way”; “I am the way, the truth and the life”; Philip’s “show us the Father”; the works and the greater works.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a betrayal named, a departure announced (“where I am going you cannot come”, 13:33), and a denial predicted. Necessity: three men have just been destabilised — Judas exposed, Peter humiliated, all of them told they cannot follow — and 14:1 addresses exactly that. The comfort is answering a specific distress the previous pericope created [T]. Implication: preaching 14:1–6 without 13:33–38 removes the wound the balm is for. This is the overview’s Trap 6 and the sweep confirms it positionally.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. μοναί (v.2) “dwelling places” — cognate with μένω, the abiding verb; verified: μονή occurs only at 14:2 and 14:23 in the NT, and the second use reverses the direction (“we will come to him and make our home [μονήν] with him). Pericope 39 — John 20:1–18 — Woman, why are you weeping?

Gist. On the first day of the week, while still dark, Mary finds the stone removed; Peter and the beloved disciple run; the grave-clothes lie undisturbed and the other disciple believes; Mary weeps, mistakes Jesus for the gardener, hears her name, and is sent.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a burial in a garden. Necessity: the resurrection is narrated as a first day in a garden with a gardener and, two pericopes later, a breathing — and the cumulative weight of those four is a new-creation frame that no single one of them could carry [I], moderate–high. Implication: John began “in the beginning”; he ends with a new one.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων (v.1) “the first day of the week” — verified: the phrase occurs only at 20:1 and 20:19 in John, and both are resurrection-day markers. σκοτίας ἔτι οὔσης “while it was still dark” — the darkness-thread of 1:5; 8:12; 12:35 in its last appearance [T]. κηπουρός (v.15) — a NT hapax, verified; John alone has a gardener. Pericope 40 — John 20:19–31 — My Lord and my God

Gist. That evening Jesus stands among them, says “Peace be with you”, shows his hands and side, breathes on them and gives the Spirit with authority over sins; Thomas, absent, demands evidence, and a week later confesses; the purpose statement closes the book.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: an empty tomb and one commissioned witness. Necessity: the book must close where it opened — with the confession the Prologue made — and it must do so through the most resistant disciple, so that the reader who has not seen is addressed directly at v.29 [T]. Implication: 20:29 is the pivot to the reader, and 20:30–31 states why the book exists. Everything before is retrospectively governed by these three verses.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἐνεφύσησεν (v.22) — verified: the only occurrence in the New Testament, and the verb of Gen 2:7 LXX (“God breathed into his nostrils the breath of life”) and of Ezek 37:9 LXX. Moderate uncertainty; state both from the pulpit rather than choosing silently. - Structure. Two Sundays, a week apart, with the same greeting — John dates the resurrection appearances to the day the church would later gather [I], moderate.

Tool 11. - Genesis 2:7 LXX → 20:22 (moderate–high). Source context: the man is formed from dust and God breathes into him and he becomes a living being. The sending-formula is transferred at 20:21. The most frequent Christological formula in the book (23 verses) becomes an ecclesiological one in a single sentence.

Pericope 41 — John 21:1–25 — Breakfast, and a second charcoal fire

Gist. Seven disciples fish fruitlessly; a stranger on the shore directs the net; 153 fish and an unbroken net; a charcoal fire with fish and bread; the threefold “do you love me” and the threefold commission; the prophecy of Peter’s death; the beloved disciple’s testimony authenticated.

Positional Necessity Check. Preceding movement: a purpose statement that reads like an ending (20:30–31). Necessity: the epilogue exists because two things are unresolved — Peter’s denial, and the status of the beloved disciple’s testimony — and both are personal, not doctrinal. That is why it sits after the purpose statement rather than inside the book’s argument [I]. Implication: ch. 21 is not an appendix to the theology but the closing of two character-arcs, and it should be preached as such.

Live tools. - Vocabulary. ἀνθρακιά (v.9) — verified: 2 NT occurrences, 18:18 and here. Moderate uncertainty; the threefold structure is certainly significant, the verb-choice much less so. Do not build a sermon point on it without a lexicon check. - ἑλκύω. v.6 (they could not draw the net) and v.11 (Peter drew it ashore) — completing the five-point thread (6:44; 12:32; 18:10; 21:6, 11, verified). - Vocabulary — the 153. Cross-Passage Convergent Findings

These are the findings that only become visible when the forty-one pericopes are read as a sequential whole. They are the sweep’s principal yield and none of them is available from a single-pericope run. All lexical claims are machine-verified against the SBLGNT.

A. Verbal threads running the length of the book

1. The drawing thread — ἑλκύω, 5 occurrences. 6:44 (the Father draws) → 12:32 (the lifted-up Son draws all) → 18:10 (Peter draws a sword) → 21:6 (the net cannot be drawn) → 21:11 (Peter draws it ashore, unbroken). [T] High. The same verb runs from divine election through the cross to a violent hand and then to a missional one. Homiletically the richest single thread the sweep found.

2. The troubling thread — ταράσσω, 5 occurrences. 5:7 (the stirred water) · 12:27 (“now is my soul troubled”) · 13:21 (“he was troubled in spirit”) → 14:1 and 14:27 (“let not your hearts be troubled”). [T] High. Jesus forbids his disciples an emotion he has himself just had twice. This transforms the pastoral register of John 14 and is absent from the book-overview.

3. The crying-out thread — κράζω, 9 occurrences. Jesus cries out an invitation (7:28, 37; 11:43; 12:44); the crowd cries out (12:13 “Hosanna”, then 18:40, 19:6, 19:12, 19:15 “Crucify… we have no king but Caesar”). [T] High. The verb of the Booths invitation becomes the verb of the demand for his death.

4. The laying-down thread — τίθημι, and specifically the present τίθησιν (3 occurrences: 2:10; 10:11; 13:4). The good shepherd lays down his life (10:11); Jesus lays aside his garments (13:4) — same verb, same form. Then 19:42; 20:2, 13, 15 (they laid him). [T] High. The footwashing is an enacted passion.

5. The wiping thread — ἐκμάσσω, 3 occurrences (11:2; 12:3; 13:5), all of feet. Mary wipes his feet with her hair; he wipes theirs with a towel. [T] High.

6. The gathering thread — συνάγω (4: 6:12, 13; 11:47, 52) with σκορπίζω (3: 10:12; 11:52; 16:32) and εἰς ἕν (11:52; 17:21, 23). Fragments gathered that nothing be lost → the council gathers to kill → the death gathers the scattered into one → the disciples scattered → that they may all be one. [T] High. The hireling scatters (10:12) and the wolf’s work is undone by the shepherd’s death.

7. The casting-out thread — ἐκβάλλω, 5 occurrences. 2:15 (the traders) · 6:37 (“I will never cast out”) · 9:34–35 (they cast him out; Jesus found him) · 10:4 (the shepherd puts out his own). [T] High. 6:37’s promise is dramatised in ch. 9.

8. The birth thread — γεννάω, 12 occurrences. 1:13 (born of God) · 3:3–8 (born from above, 5×) · 9:2, 19, 20, 34 (born blind / “you were born in utter sin”) · 16:21 (a woman in labour). [T] High. The men who tell the blind man he was born in sin are the men who have not been born from above. John 9 is the narrative answer to John 3.

9. The peace thread — εἰρήνη, 6 occurrences, and all of them either promise or fulfilment. 14:27 and 16:33 (promised in the Farewell) → 20:19, 21, 26 (given three times in the resurrection room). [T] High. No other use in the book.

10. The dwelling thread — μένω (34; 8 in ch. 15) with μονή (2: 14:2, 23). 1:38–39 (“where are you staying?” / “they stayed with him that day”) → 8:31 (“if you abide in my word”) → 14:2, 23 (dwelling-places, in both directions) → 15:4–10 (abide in me). [T] High. The disciples’ first question in the Gospel is about where Jesus stays, and the answer takes fourteen chapters.

11. The completion thread — τελ-. 4:34 (“to accomplish his work”) → 5:36 → 17:4 (“I finished the work”) → 19:28 (“knowing all was now finished”) → 19:30 (τετέλεσται). [T] High.

12. The seeking thread — the τί/τίνα ζητεῖτε form at exactly four points. 1:38 (Jesus’s first words) · 18:4, 7 (at the arrest) · 20:15 (the risen Jesus’s first words). [T] High.

B. Structural findings

13. There are two brackets, not one, and the counts distinguish them. [T] High. (Revised — the first pass named only the second.)

  • Bracket A: 1:19 ↔︎ 10:40–42. The Baptist is examined at 1:19 and vindicated at 10:41 (“John did no sign, but everything John said about this man was true”). 13 of the 17 σημεῖον references fall inside it.
  • Transition: 11:1–12:50. The pivotal exhibit (Lazarus) and the turn. Three σημεῖον references (11:47; 12:18; 12:37); the Greeks arrive; the hour comes.
  • Bracket B closes at 12:37. The narrator’s verdict on the whole public presentation; only 20:30 follows. πιστεύω is densely spread across 1–12 and then collapses (chs. 15, 18, 21 have none); ἀγαπάω and κόσμος spike after 13:1.

The two brackets do different work: A closes a movement of testimony; B pronounces the verdict on it. Treating 12:37 as the sole seam — as the sweep’s first pass did — flattens a structure the counts actually support in two stages. [T] for the counts; the two-stage reading was prompted by [S: conv] and is confirmed by them.

14. Three scenes of unreliable belief form a sustained argument. 2:23–25 (many believed at the signs; he did not entrust himself to them) → 6:60–66 (many disciples turned back) → 8:31–59 (addressed to “the Jews who had believed him”, ending in attempted stoning). [T]/[I] High. John’s doctrine of belief is that it must abide, and he makes the case narratively three times.

15. The trial-motif is the book’s dominant genre-shape. John 5 (forensic defence; μαρτυρ- 11×, the book’s densest) → 7:14–52 → 8:12–59 (the two-witness rule cited) → 9:13–34 (four interrogations) → 18:19–19:16 (the trial proper). The Paraclete is given a forensic verb (ἐλέγχω, 16:8), and παράκλητος itself is courtroom vocabulary — counsel for the defence. The judgement thread runs 3:19; 5:22, 27; 9:39; 12:31; 16:8, 11 (verified). [T] High. John is a Gospel shaped as a lawsuit, and the reader has held the verdict since 1:1.

Reached independently; now converged. This finding was derived from the lexical and structural evidence above before the two May 2026 conversation records were opened. Those records develop the same frame under the Hebrew rîb (covenant-lawsuit) category — the prophetic form in which God summons witnesses, presents evidence and calls for a verdict — and add the observation that the reader is the jury, which 20:31 makes explicit. [S: conv] for the rîb label and the jury framing; [T] for everything the sweep verified. An earned confirmation.

15b. The reversal: the defendant is the judge. 5:22 (“the Father judges no one, but has given all judgement to the Son”) with 5:27 (“authority to execute judgement, because he is the Son of Man”), stated inside a defence speech against a capital charge and dramatised through to 19:13 (where ἐκάθισεν may even seat Jesus on the bēma). [T] High. This is the structural engine of the irony catalogued at finding 25 below.

16. The kingship arc has four stations and ends in apostasy. 6:15 (refused) → 12:13 (acclaimed) → 18:36–37 (redefined) → 19:15 (“we have no king but Caesar”). [T] High.

17. Peter’s arc has three stations built on one saying. 10:11 (the shepherd lays down his life) → 13:37 (Peter claims the formula) → 21:15–19 (restored to the office and given the death). [T] High.

18. Two chapters lack πατήρ entirely: 9 and 19. Against a book-wide 136 occurrences and a mean of ~7 per chapter (23 in ch. 14). Both are formal interrogations ending in expulsion or execution. [T] for the count; the significance is moderate and flagged under the pattern-break-absence gate rather than asserted.

C. Intertextual findings

19. Psalm 69 is John’s spine-psalm and his three uses track its arc. 2:17 (69:9, zeal) → 15:25 (69:4, hated without cause) → 19:28–29 (69:21, vinegar). [T] for the citations; [I] moderate–high for the sequential reading. This is the sweep’s strongest Move 2 finding.

20. Isaiah frames the public ministry. 1:23 (Isa 40:3) opens it; 12:38, 40 (Isa 53:1 and 6:10) close it, with 12:41 (“Isaiah saw his glory”) supplying the hermeneutic. 6:45 (Isa 54:13) sits at the centre. [T] High.

21. Zechariah is live but not sequential. Three uses (2:16 ← Zech 14:21, moderate; 12:15 ← Zech 9:9; 19:37 ← Zech 12:10) drawn from chs. 14, 9 and 12 in that order. [T] High as a negative finding — John is not tracing Zechariah’s internal movement as he traces Psalm 69’s.

22. Zechariah 12:10 → 13:1 (the fountain opened) is the best single lead on two open questions at once — the blood and water of 19:34, and the unidentified citation at 7:38. [I] moderate–high.

23. The crucifixion is the densest citation-cluster in the book — four formula-citations in fourteen verses (19:24, 28, 36, 37), three of them Psalter. [T] High.

24. John’s OT dependence is institutional more than propositional. Roughly fifteen formula-citations, but the load is carried by feast, temple, tabernacle, lamb, manna, serpent, shepherd, vine, water, light and garden. [I] High. Preaching Pitfalls (whole book)

Pitfall: Preaching “the seven signs”

  • What it looks like: A series built on seven numbered signs, matched to seven “I am” sayings.
  • Why it’s wrong: John numbers exactly two signs (2:11 “the first”; 4:54 “the second”) and then stops. [T] σημεῖον occurs 17×, and 20:30 says explicitly that many others are not written. The seven is a preacher’s scheme imposed on a book that declines to count.
  • The corrective: Use the numbering John gives (the Cana–Cana bracket) and the brackets he marks (10:40–42; 12:37). If a seven-fold structure is used homiletically, say that it is yours and not his. Note for the forensic reading: describing the signs as exhibits entered into evidence survives this correction intact and is the better framing — an exhibit list does not need to run to seven, and the prior conversation’s “seven signs… entered into evidence” should simply drop the number.

Pitfall: Using John 9 to flatter the congregation

  • What it looks like: the irony about the experts preached as “the scholars missed it, but ordinary believers see” — with the congregation cast as the man born blind and someone else as the Pharisees.
  • Why it’s wrong: 9:41 makes the claim to see the aggravating factor. Any group that hears the chapter and concludes it can see has stepped into the position the chapter condemns. The man born blind has no credentials and no confidence — he begins with “I do not know” (9:12, 25) and ends at worship.
  • The corrective: preach the chapter’s irony against the hearer, not past them. The honest question is not “who are the Pharisees today?” but “what am I certain I can see?”

Pitfall: Preaching the Farewell Discourse as generic comfort

  • What it looks like: 14:1–6 at a funeral; 14:27 as a devotional; 15:1–8 as spiritual self-improvement.
  • Why it’s wrong: 14:1 answers a distress created eight verses earlier — a betrayer named, a denial predicted, a departure announced. The room is closed and the men in it are about to be scattered and expelled (16:2). And 15:1’s “true vine” is polemical: every OT vine text is an indictment.
  • The corrective: Preach 13:33–38 before 14:1. Name the wound the balm is for. Then note 17:20, where the closed room is explicitly opened to later believers — the caution about over-application has its own limit.

Pitfall: “The Jews” preached flat

  • What it looks like: Ἰουδαῖοι (77×) treated as a single hostile bloc, with 8:44 preached without qualification.
  • Why it’s wrong: The referent shifts — Judean authorities (7:13; 9:22), Judeans as a region (11:19), the crowd, the people at large — and 4:22 has Jesus say “salvation is from the Jews”. 8:44 is spoken inside a dispute about paternity to people described in 8:31 as having believed him.
  • The corrective: Translate contextually, name the problem from the pulpit once and clearly, and never let the phrase float free. This is not optional.

Pitfall: Glory severed from the cross

  • What it looks like: A “glory of Christ” sermon that never reaches the nails; or a bleak crucifixion sermon with no enthronement.
  • Why it’s wrong: δοξάζω clusters five times in 13:31–32, triggered by Judas’s exit; 12:23 pairs glorification with a grain of wheat dying; 7:39 makes the Spirit’s giving wait on glorification; the narrator glosses ὑψόω as “by what kind of death” (12:33).
  • The corrective: Let ὑψόω and δοξάζω carry both senses and say so. The crucifixion in John is a coronation and should sound like one.

Pitfall: “The hour” as chronology

  • What it looks like: “Jesus’s timing was not yet right.”
  • Why it’s wrong: The hour-notices are why two arrests and a stoning fail (7:30; 8:20; 8:59) and why 13:1 can say it has come. The clock is the Father’s, and the plot runs on it.
  • The corrective: Track the six stations — 2:4 → 7:30 → 8:20 → 12:23 → 13:1 → 17:1 — and preach sovereignty, not scheduling.

Pitfall: Building on the ἀγαπάω/φιλέω alternation in 21:15–17

  • What it looks like: “Jesus asks for agape love; Peter can only offer philia; the third time Jesus comes down to Peter’s level.”
  • Why it’s wrong: John uses the two verbs interchangeably elsewhere — the Father loves the Son with ἀγαπάω at 3:35 and φιλέω at 5:20; the beloved disciple is ἠγάπα at 13:23 and ἐφίλει at 20:2. The nouns for the sheep and the verbs of commission also vary across the three exchanges, which suggests stylistic variation rather than a graded lexicon.
  • The corrective: Preach the threefold structure against the threefold denial, which the text does mark, and leave the verb-choice alone unless a lexicon supports it.

Pitfall: Preaching 7:53–8:11 without saying what it is

  • What it looks like: Silence about the manuscript situation, or a brief embarrassed footnote.
  • Why it’s wrong: The passage is absent from the earliest and best witnesses, floats to three different locations in the manuscripts that have it, and reads un-Johannine. Congregations generally find the facts strengthening; discovering them later from a sceptical source is much worse.
  • The corrective: If preached, say plainly what it is and why the church has kept it.

The variant (μονογενὴς θεός vs ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός) is one of the most significant in the Gospel. The inclusio holds, but the overview should say “1:1 and 20:28, with 1:18 as a text-critically contested third” rather than implying only two. [T]
