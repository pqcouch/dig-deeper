The Passage

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

  1. The Prologue’s whole argument is carried by two verbs, and the distribution is countable. [T] ἦν occurs 11 times and is reserved for the Word; ἐγένετο occurs 6 times (plus γέγονεν twice) and is used of everything else — all things (v3), a man named John (v6), the world (v10) — until verse 14, where the one who only ever “was” ἐγένετο σάρξ. Surfaced by: Original Languages, Repetition, Structure, Linking Words. The incarnation is not asserted in v14; it is detonated, because the reader has been trained for thirteen verses to expect ἦν of this subject and gets ἐγένετο instead. Every count machine-verified against the SBLGNT.

  2. The Prologue is not a preface but an epistemic gift: it hands the reader information no character in the book will ever possess. [T]/[I] Surfaced by: Positional Necessity Check, Genre, Narrator’s Comment, Author’s Purpose. Every “Where are you from?” in chapters 7–9, every misunderstanding, every ironic statement by an opponent works only because 1:1–18 has already answered the question. The Prologue is the mechanism of Johannine irony, not merely its overture.

  3. The Prologue is already forensic — it files the brief before the trial opens. [T] Surfaced by: Structure (the two Baptist insertions at vv.6–8 and 15 interrupting a hymnic flow), Repetition (μαρτυρ- four times in thirteen verses of a very compressed unit), Vocabulary, Genre. A witness is introduced and his function twice defined (vv.7, 8); the charge is stated (v11, “his own did not receive him”); the verdict available to the reader is named (v12). John will not let his reader meet the Word except through testimony.

  4. χάρις occurs in John only here — 1:14, 16 (twice), 17 — and never again in the Gospel. [T] Verified: three verses, four tokens; zero occurrences in chapters 2–21. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Biblical Theology. Grace is the Prologue’s word, and its content is set by v17’s Moses/Christ pairing and by χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος (v16). A sermon that treats “grace” as a running Johannine theme is importing Paul.

  5. κόλπος occurs twice in John — 1:18 and 13:23 — and the pair grounds the book’s claim to reliable testimony. [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Tool 11 Move 4, Author’s Purpose. The Son who “makes God known” is εἰς τὸν κόλπον τοῦ πατρός; the disciple who claims to have written the book (21:24) was ἐν τῷ κόλπῳ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ. The one who exegetes the Father is in the Father’s bosom; the one who exegetes the Son was in his.

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. There is none within the book — this is the opening. But the Prologue has a canonical preceding movement, and it names it in its first three words: Ἐν ἀρχῇ is the LXX opening of Genesis, verbatim. [T] The preceding movement is the Torah, and the Prologue’s claim is that the story now beginning is continuous with the one that began “in the beginning”. The reader arriving at John from the canon is being told that this is not a new subject but the same one.

Necessity answer — why does this passage exist here, and not elsewhere? Two answers, and the second is the load-bearing one.

First: a Gospel whose whole burden is the question “Who are you?” and “Where are you from?” (1:19–22; 7:27–28, 41–42; 8:25; 9:29–30; 19:9 — verified) must answer that question for the reader before any character asks it, or the irony collapses. The Prologue exists here because the entire narrative depends on the reader knowing more than the characters. [I]

Second, and sharper: John is going to make a claim that could not possibly be established from within the narrative — that a man from Nazareth is God. No sign proves it (the opponents see the signs and reject them, 12:37). No argument compels it (ch. 5’s defence speech ends in a stoning attempt). No witness can produce it from below. So the claim is made from above, before the narrative begins, in the narrator’s own voice — and the rest of the book is not the demonstration of the claim but the record of what happened when the claim walked into the world. Position is meaning: had 1:1–18 been placed at the end as a doxological conclusion, the Gospel would read as an argument mounting to a verdict. Placed first, it reads as a testimony to a verdict already given. [I], high confidence.

Implication for this passage. The Prologue should not be preached as an introduction to what follows. It should be preached as the vantage-point from which everything that follows becomes intelligible — and, pastorally, as the place where a reader whose confession is under challenge is told the answer before being shown the argument.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 confirms the positional reading: the Genesis frame opened here is not resolved until 20:1–22 (first day, garden, gardener, breathing). The Prologue does not merely introduce the book; it opens a bracket that only the resurrection closes.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level. Stated by the book itself at 20:30–31: a selection of signs written that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and have life in his name. [T]

Passage-level. The Prologue’s contribution is to establish identity before narrative, and to establish it in three registers the rest of the book will use: ontological (the Word was God, v1), creational (all things through him, v3), and revelatory (he has made him known, v18). The unit also plants the book’s outcome-vocabulary in advance — believe, receive, life, light, glory, truth, children of God, name.

Note especially the verbal join to the purpose statement: v12’s πιστεύουσιν εἰς τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ (“believing into his name”) is the same construction as 20:31’s ζωὴν ἔχητε ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ (“have life in his name”). [T] The Prologue and the purpose statement use the same idiom, and the Gospel is bracketed by it.

The Prologue is also the only place in the book where the narrator speaks at this length without narrating anything. That is a genre-signal: the reader is being briefed, not told a story.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

See Phase 0.6 above. Summary: the Prologue exists first because John’s claim cannot be established from below, and because the book’s irony requires a reader who knows more than the characters.

Immediate context

Before: nothing — but the canonical “before” is Genesis 1, invoked verbatim.

After (1:19–34): the narrative opens with an interrogation — priests and Levites sent from Jerusalem to ask John “Who are you?” [T] The join is exact and is easily missed: the Prologue has just introduced John twice (vv.6–8, 15) and defined him negatively (“he was not the light”), and 1:19–21 has John define himself negatively three times (“I am not the Christ… I am not… No”). The Prologue’s characterisation of the Baptist is confirmed by the Baptist himself in the very next pericope. [T]

Section: 1:1–18 stands outside the narrative frame; 1:19 begins the book’s first great bracket, closing at 10:40–42 with the crowd’s verdict on this same witness (book-overview-john v0.2.0). So the Prologue’s introduction of John at vv.6–8, 15 is load-bearing for a structure that runs nine chapters.

Commonly read out of context? Yes — v12 (“gave the right to become children of God”) is regularly preached as a standalone conversion text, detached from v13’s flat denial that the birth is human in origin, and from v11’s prior statement that this is what happened when his own rejected him. And v9 is regularly pressed into inclusivist or universalist service; see Difficult Verses.

3. Structure

Two structural questions, and they should be handled separately because one is settled and one is not.

Settled: the micro-structures. These are visible in the Greek and are not disputed.

Staircase (anadiplosis) at v1 — the last word of each clause becomes the first of the next:

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος
       καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν
                        καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος

Chiasm across vv.1–2 — A (beginning / Word) · B (Word / God) · B′ (God / Word) · A′ (beginning / God):

A   Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος
  B   καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν
  B′  καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος
A′  οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν

Staircase again at vv.4–5 — ζωή → ζωή; φῶς → φῶς:

ἐν αὐτῷ ζωὴ ἦν
   καὶ ἡ ζωὴ ἦν τὸ φῶς τῶν ἀνθρώπων
                    καὶ τὸ φῶς ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ φαίνει

Triple κόσμος at v10 — in the world / the world through him / the world did not know him. The word tolls three times in one verse, and the third use reverses the first two. [T]

Contested: the macro-structure. Proposals fall into three families and the preacher should choose consciously rather than inherit one.

Proposal Shape Strength Weakness
Linear-thematic (adopted here) vv.1–5 the Word before creation · vv.6–8 the witness · vv.9–13 coming and reception · v14 incarnation · v15 the witness again · vv.16–18 what was received Follows the text’s own movement; explains the two Baptist insertions as deliberate interruptions Less elegant; no single centre
Chiastic, centred on vv.12–13 reception/new birth as the pivot Attractive symmetry (creation ↔︎ new creation; God unseen ↔︎ God made known) Requires forcing vv.6–8 and 15 to the margins; scholars centring it disagree about where the centre falls
Two-panel with hymn + prose insertions an underlying hymn (vv.1–5, 9–11, 14, 16–18) with prose about John added (vv.6–8, 15) Explains the metrical unevenness Compositional speculation, not textual observation — and the skill’s [T] boundary applies: the “underlying hymn” is a reconstruction, [S], not something the page shows

Judgement. The linear-thematic reading is preferred here because it is derivable from the page and because it makes the Baptist insertions meaningful rather than intrusive. But the chiastic proposal is not unreasonable, and a preacher who wants a centre should note that v14 has at least as good a claim as vv.12–13. Moderate confidence; the macro-structure of the Prologue is genuinely contested and the report should not pretend otherwise.

4. Linking Words

The Prologue is not connector-dense in the manner of Romans, but three connectives do real work and one is text-critically live.

v3 → v4, ὃ γέγονεν — the punctuation crux. Two options: - “…and without him was not any thing made that was made. In him was life” (ESV, NIV, most moderns — ὃ γέγονεν closes v3) - “…was not any thing made. That which has come into being — in him was life” (SBLGNT punctuation, and the reading of most Greek fathers — ὃ γέγονεν opens v4)

The second makes v4 say that created things have their life in him; the first makes v4 a fresh assertion about the Word. The oldest manuscripts have no punctuation at all, so this is an editorial decision, not a textual one. Moderate confidence that the ancient reading is right; high confidence that the preacher should know the choice is being made for them by their translation. Cross-referenced under Translations.

v13, οὐκ… οὐδὲ… οὐδὲ… ἀλλά — a triple negation resolved by a single adversative. The rhetorical shape is the theology: three human-origin candidates are excluded before the divine one is named. Do not abbreviate the list in preaching; the accumulation is the argument. [T]

v16 and v17, ὅτι… ὅτι — SBLGNT reads ὅτι at v16 (“because from his fullness…”), grounding v14’s “full of grace and truth” in the readers’ experience; v17’s ὅτι then grounds v16. So vv.16–17 are not a new topic but a two-stage explanation of “full of grace and truth”, with v15 as a parenthetical interruption. [T] The Textus Receptus reads καί at v16 (“and of his fulness”), which breaks the grounding chain — see Translations. A preacher using the KJV will not see the argument.

v17, no adversative. ESV and NIV supply “but” (“the law was given through Moses; but grace and truth came…”). There is no ἀλλά or δέ in the Greek — the two clauses are simply juxtaposed. [T], high confidence. This matters a great deal: the inserted “but” turns a comparison into an antithesis, and Marcionite readings of John have been built on less. See Translations and Difficult Verses.

5. Parallels

Unusually for John, this tool is fully live — the Prologue is the one sustained passage in the Gospel that uses Hebrew-style poetic structure.

  • Staircase parallelism at vv.1 and 4–5 (laid out under Structure). Each line picks up the previous line’s last term and advances it. This is a Semitic device, not a Greek one, and its presence at the head of the most “Hellenistic-sounding” passage in the NT is itself worth noting [I].
  • Antithetical parallelism at v11–12: he came to his own / his own did not receive him ‖ but as many as received him / he gave them authority. The antithesis is the Prologue’s turn.
  • Antithetical parallelism at v17: the law / through Moses / was given ‖ grace and truth / through Jesus Christ / came. Three-for-three correspondence — agent, mediator, verb. And the verbs are not synonymous: ἐδόθη (aorist passive, “was given”) against ἐγένετο (“came to be”) — the same verb that has run through the whole Prologue. [T] The law was given; grace and truth came into being — in a person.
  • Chiasm at vv.1–2 and, arguably, across the whole unit (contested; see Structure).

6. Narrator’s Comment

The whole passage is narrator’s comment — which is precisely its significance and is easily missed because there is no narrative for it to interrupt.

Three features worth naming:

  1. v15 is a parenthesis inside a parenthesis. SBLGNT prints it in brackets. The narrator breaks his own theological statement to insert the Baptist’s testimony in direct speech, in the present tense (μαρτυρεῖ, “bears witness” — not “bore”), with a perfect (κέκραγεν, “has cried out and the cry stands”). [T], high confidence. The Baptist’s witness is presented as still speaking.
  2. v18b is the narrator’s decoder for the entire book. ἐκεῖνος ἐξηγήσατο — “that one has exegeted him”. John will use decoder-asides throughout (2:21; 7:39; 11:51–52; 12:33; 21:19), and this is the first and the widest: the whole Gospel is the exegesis.
  3. “We” appears twice — ἐθεασάμεθα (v14) and ἡμεῖς πάντες ἐλάβομεν (v16). The narrator places himself and his readers inside the passage. This is the same first-person plural that returns at 21:24 (“we know that his testimony is true”). [T]

7. Vocabulary

Six terms, chosen because each materially affects the passage’s meaning. Full lexical treatment in the Original Languages extension; here the focus is on what the preacher must decide.

λόγος (vv.1, 14) [T] — high confidence on the range; moderate on the primary background. Verified: as a title for Christ, λόγος occurs in John only at 1:1 (×3) and 1:14. The word carries at least three streams: the Genesis “and God said” (creation by speech); the OT “word of the LORD” that comes to the prophets and does not return empty (Isa 55:11); and, in the Hellenistic register, the rational principle ordering the cosmos. The Prologue’s own signal is the first of these — Ἐν ἀρχῇ decides it [I]. Do not build a sermon on Stoic or Philonic background; note it and move on.

σκηνόω (v14) [T] — “pitched his tent, tabernacled”. A NT rarity and a Johannine hapax (verified: 1 occurrence). Cognate with σκηνή, the LXX word for the tabernacle. Coupled with δόξα in the same verse, the pairing is Exodus 40:34–35 — the tent, and the glory that fills it. High confidence that the tabernacle resonance is intended; the vocabulary pairing is too specific to be accidental.

μονογενής (vv.14, 18) [T] — high confidence. Verified: 4 occurrences in John (1:14, 18; 3:16, 18). Not “only-begotten” in the sense of generated — the word is μονο- + γένος (kind), “one of a kind, unique”, not μονο- + γεννάω (beget). The KJV “only begotten” imports a begetting the word does not carry, and the fourth-century Arian controversies pressed it in ways the lexeme does not require. Recommend a lexicon check before making this a sermon point — the etymological argument is now standard but not unanimous.

χάρις (vv.14, 16 ×2, 17) [T] — high confidence, and the finding is the distribution. Verified: χάρις occurs in John only in these three verses. Zero occurrences in chapters 2–21. Grace is not a Johannine theme; it is a Prologue theme. What the Gospel does instead is narrate it.

χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος (v16) — “grace in place of grace” or “grace upon grace”. ἀντί normally means “instead of / in exchange for”, which would give: one grace replacing another — most naturally the grace of v17’s law replaced by the grace of Christ. The “grace upon grace” rendering (grace piled on grace) is idiomatically possible but less natural for ἀντί. Moderate confidence, genuinely disputed; the choice changes v17’s force considerably. Flagged to Open Questions.

ἐξηγήσατο (v18) [T] — high confidence. Verified: a Johannine hapax. The verb from which “exegesis” comes: to lead out, expound, narrate in full. The clause is emphatic in Greek — ἐκεῖνος (“that one, and no other”) — and the object is left unstated in the Greek (“he has expounded”), so the ESV’s supplied “him” is an interpretive addition, correct but supplied.

Proper-noun inventory (run per Tool 7c): Ἰωάννης (vv.6, 15), Μωϋσῆς (v17), Ἰησοῦς Χριστός (v17). Three names, and all three are load-bearing rather than scenery. - Ἰωάννης — canonical freight: the name is Hebrew Yôḥānān, “YHWH has been gracious”, which sits with some point in a passage about χάρις. Uncertain whether John intends the etymology; flagged, not asserted. - Μωϋσῆς — the load-the-story rule applies and matters here. The canonical Moses of v17 is not merely “the law-giver”: he is the man who asked to see God’s glory and was refused the face but granted the goodness, and to whom the LORD proclaimed himself “abounding in ḥesed we-ʾemet” (Exod 33:18–34:7). v18’s “no one has ever seen God” is Exodus 33:20 almost verbatim. Read that way, v17 is not a polemic against Moses but the answer to Moses’s own unfulfilled request. [I], moderate–high. This is the single most under-preached move in the Prologue. - Ἰησοῦς Χριστός — the first appearance of the name in the Gospel, and it is withheld until v17. Seventeen verses about “the Word” before the name is given. [T] Structurally deliberate.

8. Translations

v17 — the supplied adversative. ESV: “the law was given through Moses; but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ.” NIV: “but grace and truth came…”. KJV: “but grace and truth came…”. NASB: “but grace and truth were realised…”. The Greek has no adversative particle at all. [T], high confidence. All four major versions supply it. The Greek juxtaposes two clauses without connecting them, which permits comparison or contrast but does not demand contrast. This is the most consequential translation decision in the passage and every English reader is receiving it pre-decided.

v16 — ὅτι or καί. SBLGNT and the critical text read ὅτι (“because”); the Textus Receptus reads καί (“and”). ESV “For from his fullness”; KJV “And of his fulness”. The critical reading makes v16 the ground of v14; the TR reading makes it a fresh statement. High confidence that ὅτι is original; the manuscript support is strong.

v3–4 — the punctuation of ὃ γέγονεν. ESV, NIV, NASB, KJV all close v3 with it. SBLGNT and the majority of the Greek fathers open v4 with it. See Linking Words. No English version in common use follows the patristic punctuation, which means a congregation cannot hear the option without being told.

v5 — κατέλαβεν. ESV “has not overcome”; KJV “comprehended it not”; NASB “did not comprehend”; NIV “has not overcome”. The verb genuinely carries both — to seize/overpower, and to grasp/understand. High confidence that the ambiguity is real; moderate that John intends both. John’s characteristic double meanings (ἄνωθεν, ὕδωρ ζῶν, ὑψόω) make deliberate ambiguity plausible here. The KJV/NASB tradition preserves the sense the moderns have dropped.

v9 — the dangling participle. ἐρχόμενον εἰς τὸν κόσμον can modify “everyone” (“every man that cometh into the world”, KJV) or “the light” (“the true light… was coming into the world”, ESV/NIV/NASB). Greek permits both. The moderns are probably right — John uses “coming into the world” of Jesus elsewhere (3:19; 11:27; 12:46; 16:28) — but the KJV reading has a long history. Moderate–high confidence for the modern reading.

Ancient versions check. Trigger met — the moderns substantively agree on v17’s adversative, and the rendering carries exegetical weight. - Vulgate v17: quia lex per Mosen data est, gratia et veritas per Iesum Christum facta est — no adversative supplied. Conceptually exact, verbally independent. Moderate confidence that Exod 33 is the specific referent; the fuller case rests on the σκηνόω/δόξα/χάρις-καὶ-ἀλήθεια cluster (see Tool 11).

9. Tone and Feel

Register: liturgical, weighted, unhurried — then, twice, interrupted.

The dominant device is repetition-with-advance (the staircases), which produces a processional feel: each clause takes a step and pauses. The vocabulary is almost entirely monosyllabic and abstract in Greek — was, Word, God, life, light, darkness — and the effect is of a statement being laid down rather than an argument being pressed.

Against that, two interruptions: vv.6–8 and v15. Both concern John. Both are syntactically abrupt (v6 begins Ἐγένετο ἄνθρωπος — a plain narrative opening dropped into a hymn; v15 is parenthetical and in direct speech). The tonal effect is deliberate: a voice keeps breaking in to say “there was a man”. [I], moderate–high. A reading that smooths the interruptions loses the passage’s texture.

The tonal climax is v14 — and it is achieved grammatically, not rhetorically. Nothing in the sentence is raised in volume; the shock is the single verb ἐγένετο applied, for the first time, to ὁ λόγος.

Soundtrack: unaccompanied plainchant for vv.1–5, interrupted twice by a speaking voice; the full choir does not enter until v14. Silence after v18.

For the preacher: this passage should not be preached briskly. Its own pacing is slow and cumulative, and a sermon that treats it as a dense doctrinal block to be unpacked at speed works against the text’s own rhetoric.

11. Quotation/Allusion

Independent candidate-finding (from the passage’s own vocabulary, before any source was consulted). The distinctive vocabulary is: in the beginning · was · all things came to be · life · light · darkness · witness · world · his own · children of God · born of God · Word · flesh · tabernacled · glory · only-of-its-kind · full of grace and truth · law · Moses · no one has ever seen God · in the bosom · made known. That vocabulary points independently to Genesis 1 (beginning, creation by word, light/darkness separation, life), Exodus 33–34 and 40 (glory, tent, ḥesed we-ʾemet, “you cannot see my face”), Wisdom personified (Prov 8; agent of creation), and Isaiah’s word-theology (Isa 55:11). No formula-citation appears in the unit — the first is at 1:23.

Genesis 1:1–5 → John 1:1–5 (high confidence — verbal, structural and thematic) [T]

Source context. Genesis 1 opens with a formless, dark, watery void; God speaks; light comes to be; light is separated from darkness; and the sequence proceeds by divine speech (“and God said”) until humanity is made in the image. The passage is not a general statement about origins but the establishment of order by word, and its first named creature is light. The immediately following material (Gen 2–3) is the garden, the command, the serpent and the loss — so the reader who knows Genesis 1 knows that the light-and-darkness order does not hold.

Book usage. First use. There is no earlier Johannine use to track, but the source is not dropped: the Genesis frame returns at 20:1 (“the first day of the week… while it was still dark”), 20:15 (κηπουρός, “gardener” — a NT hapax), and 20:22 (ἐνεφύσησεν, the LXX verb of Gen 2:7 and the only NT occurrence). [T] — all verified. So the Prologue opens a bracket that the resurrection narrative closes, and Move 2’s finding here is forward-looking: this is the plant, ch. 20 is the payoff.

OT-to-OT. Genesis 1 is already read within the OT through Psalm 33:6 (“by the word of the LORD the heavens were made”) and Proverbs 8:22–31 (Wisdom present at the founding of the world). John’s “the Word” arrives with both in the air. Isa 55:10–11’s word that “shall not return empty” adds the sense of a word that accomplishes. [I], moderate.

What it adds. Three things. (a) The Gospel is framed as a new creation document from its first three words, which is why ch. 20’s Sunday, garden and breath are not decoration. (b) The rejection of vv.10–11 reads as a fall-narrative in miniature — the light comes into its own place and is not received — so the Prologue contains Genesis 1 and Genesis 3. (c) Most sharply: in Genesis, God’s word creates; in John, God’s Word is created into — ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο. The agent of Genesis 1:3 enters the category of Genesis 1’s products.

Exodus 33:18–34:7 and 40:34–35 → John 1:14–18 (moderate–high confidence — a cluster of four converging elements, no single verbal quotation)

Source context. Moses, after the golden calf, asks to see God’s glory (33:18). He is refused the face — “you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live” (33:20) — but granted the goodness: God places him in a cleft, passes by, and proclaims his name: “The LORD, the LORD, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness (ḥesed we-ʾemet)” (34:6). The narrative then moves to the covenant renewal and the construction of the tabernacle, and Exodus ends (40:34–35) with the glory filling the tent so that Moses could not enter.

Book usage. First use of the Exodus glory-material. Moses is named 11 times in John (verified) and the pattern is consistent: opponents invoke him as their authority, Jesus invokes him as a witness (5:45–47). This passage sets that up.

OT-to-OT. Exod 33–34 is the source of the OT’s most-repeated self-description of God (ḥesed we-ʾemet recurs at Num 14:18; Neh 9:17; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jon 4:2) — so citing it invokes a formula the canon has already made central. And Exod 40 is in conversation with Ezek 43 (the glory returning to the temple), which Second-Temple readers awaited.

The four converging elements. (1) σκηνόω (v14) — cognate with the LXX σκηνή, the tabernacle. (2) δόξα (v14 ×2) — the glory that filled it. (3) πλήρης χάριτος καὶ ἀληθείας (v14) — a natural Greek rendering of ḥesed we-ʾemet. (4) θεὸν οὐδεὶς ἑώρακεν πώποτε (v18) — Exod 33:20’s refusal, near-verbatim in sense. No one of these would carry the case; four in five verses does. [I], moderate–high.

What it adds. This is the finding that most changes how v17 should be preached. If Exodus 33–34 is in view, v17 is not an attack on Moses but the granting of Moses’s own request. Moses asked to see the glory and was refused the face; John says “we have seen his glory” (v14) and “the only God… has made him known” (v18). The law came through Moses; the ḥesed we-ʾemet that God proclaimed to Moses came into being in Jesus Christ. Read this way, v17 is continuity-with-surpassing, not repudiation — which is exactly what the absence of an adversative in the Greek permits (see Tool 8).

Wisdom traditions (Proverbs 8:22–31; Sirach 24) → John 1:1–3, 14 (moderate confidence — conceptual; and contested)

Move 1 only, per the significance rule. Wisdom in Prov 8 is present before creation, is God’s agent or companion in it, and delights in the human race. Sirach 24 has Wisdom seeking a dwelling-place and being told to “pitch your tent (κατασκήνωσον) in Jacob” — which is verbally close to John’s ἐσκήνωσεν. That parallel is genuinely striking, and it is also from a book John’s readers may or may not have known. The wisdom background is worth the preacher knowing and not worth the sermon leaning on: Ἐν ἀρχῇ decides the primary frame in favour of Genesis, and the Prologue’s Word is personal and incarnate in a way Wisdom never is. Recommend verification if this becomes a sermon point.

Addressee-differentiation check. The Prologue names two parties (vv.11–12: those who did not receive; those who did) without naming individuals. The Gospel then resolves individuals from each side one at a time — Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the man born blind, Martha, Thomas, Peter. The Prologue is where the differentiation is set up, so the check applies outward from this passage rather than inward.

12. Genre

Theological prologue in elevated prose with poetic features, hosting two prose insertions about a named historical individual. The passage is neither straight narrative (nothing happens until v6) nor straight hymn (the insertions and the argumentative ὅτι-chain resist it).

Reading rules. (a) The staircase and antithetical structures are Semitic poetic devices, so the unit should be read for cumulative advance, not for propositional density alone. (b) The abstractions (Word, life, light) are not metaphors to be decoded but titles and categories to be filled by the narrative that follows — John will show what “light” means in ch. 9. (c) The unit is written to be heard, and its repetitions are aural.

On the “hymn” question. Many treatments assume an underlying hymn later expanded. That is a compositional reconstruction, not an observation about the page, and per the [T] boundary it is [S]. It may be right; nothing in this report depends on it, and a sermon should not present it as a fact about the text.

13. Copycat

Largely N/A for vv.1–5 and 9–18 — no narrative characters, no actions to imitate. The one figure available is John the Baptist (vv.6–8, 15), and the passage’s treatment of him is instructive rather than exemplary in the usual sense.

Element Category
John’s existence and mission (v6, “sent from God”) Unique — a salvation-historical office; not a template
John’s witness function (vv.7, 8, 15) Normative pattern — the narrative commends it without comment, and 15:27 explicitly extends witness-bearing to the disciples. Imitate the function
John’s self-effacement (“he was not the light”) Normative pattern — corroborated by 3:30 and by the crowd’s verdict at 10:41
Receiving / not receiving (vv.11–12) The reader’s decision, not a character’s action — this is where the passage puts the hearer

The application-posture question. v12–13 is decisive here and cuts against a very common preaching instinct: the right to become children of God is given (ἔδωκεν), and the birth is emphatically not of blood, flesh-will or man-will but of God. The passage’s own grammar makes the human role receiving, not achieving. [T] A sermon that turns v12 into a decision-technique has contradicted v13.

14. Bible Timeline

The Prologue is unusual in that it stands outside the timeline before entering it. vv.1–2 are pre-creation; v3 is creation; vv.6–8 are the first century; v14 is the incarnation; vv.16–18 are the position of the “we” who have received.

Where the reader is. This side of the cross and resurrection, in the church age, in the same position as the “we” of vv.14 and 16 — those who have received from his fullness. Note that the Prologue’s “we have seen his glory” is spoken by eyewitnesses (cf. 21:24), so a contemporary congregation is included in v16’s “we have all received” more directly than in v14’s “we have seen”. That distinction is worth preserving in the sermon. [I]

Timeline-driven misreading to avoid. v17’s law/grace pairing invites a supersessionist flattening in which the OT becomes the era of law and the NT the era of grace. The Prologue itself resists this: the χάρις-καὶ-ἀλήθεια of v14 is the ḥesed we-ʾemet God proclaimed to Moses (Exod 34:6), so grace and truth were not absent from Sinai — they were given there and have now come into being in a person. See Difficult Verses.

15. Who Am I?

  • The Word: not a figure to identify with. The Prologue’s subject is Christ as the object of faith, not as a model. Any “be like the Word” move is a category error.
  • John the Baptist: partially identify — as witness (see Copycat). The NT authorises this at 15:27 and 1 John 1:1–3, which is the closest NT parallel to this passage and explicitly places the readers in the testimony chain.
  • “His own” who did not receive (v11): the honest identification for a congregation is here first, not with v12. The rejection is not attributed to outsiders — it happened among those with the strongest claim to belong.
  • “As many as received” (v12): the reader’s proper hope, but as recipients of a gift, not as achievers of a status.
  • The “we” of vv.14, 16: eyewitnesses at v14; all believers at v16.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. That the reader should know who is speaking before hearing him speak, and should therefore receive rather than assess. The Prologue does not argue the reader into a conclusion; it tells them the conclusion and then hands them a narrative. The intended response is recognition — and, in a book whose readers were being expelled for confessing, confidence.

Stage 2 — worldview before behaviour. There is no imperative in the passage. Not one. [T] Whatever application follows must therefore be worldview-shaped, not command-shaped, and a sermon that manufactures three things to do has not preached this text.

How it tells us to think: - That the universe has a word-shaped origin — reality is spoken, therefore personal, therefore addressed to us. - That the deepest problem is not ignorance but non-reception (v11) — the world did not know him (v10) and his own did not receive him (v11), and the second is the graver. - That belonging to God is given, not achieved (vv.12–13). - That God is knowable but not by looking — no one has ever seen God; the seeing happens by hearing the one who exegetes him (v18).

Where behaviour follows: only downstream, and by implication — the receiving of v12, and the witness-bearing modelled by John.

Motivation. The Prologue’s motives are all indicative: he made everything; he came to his own; he became flesh; he has made God known; from his fullness we have all received.

Four audiences. - For me: which am I — the one who did not know (v10), the one who did not receive (v11), or the one who received (v12)? And note that v11 is the more uncomfortable option for a churchgoer, since “his own” are the insiders. - For a Christian friend: the friend whose faith feels like effort needs v13’s triple negation — not blood, not the will of the flesh, not the will of man. - For the church community: the “we” of v16 is corporate. The fullness is received together, and the testimony is a shared possession (21:24’s “we know”). - For an unbeliever: v18 is the point of contact — the claim is not that God has been proven but that he has been explained, by someone in a position to do it.

Prayer. You were in the beginning; we were not. All things came to be through you, and we did not know you. You came to your own, and we did not receive you. And still, of your fullness we have all received, grace in the place of grace. Explain the Father to us again.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • Anarthrous θεός in v1c. καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος — “God” lacks the article, “the Word” has it. This is standard Greek for identifying the predicate: the subject is ὁ λόγος, and the predicate nominative is fronted for emphasis. It does not mean “a god” (the indefinite reading), and it does not collapse the Word into the person of the Father (which the articular θεόν of v1b distinguishes). High confidence; this is well-established grammar. A preacher will meet the “a god” claim and should be ready.
  • Aspectual contrast, ἦν vs ἐγένετο. The imperfect ἦν presents continuous existence without reference to origin; the aorist ἐγένετο presents entry into existence. The contrast is the passage’s spine. High confidence.
  • Present φαίνει in v5. See above. High.
  • Perfect κέκραγεν in v15. “Has cried out” — the cry stands. With the present μαρτυρεῖ, the Baptist’s testimony is presented as ongoing at the time of writing. High.
  • Attributive participle ὁ ὢν (v18). “The one who is” — a present participle of εἰμί, and the same form as the LXX divine self-designation in Exod 3:14 (ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ὤν). Whether John intends the echo is uncertain, but the form is worth noticing in a Gospel that will make so much of ἐγώ εἰμι. Flagged, not asserted.
  • Triple negation with ἀλλά (v13). οὐκ… οὐδὲ… οὐδὲ… ἀλλά — rhetorically cumulative.
  • Asyndeton at v17. No connective between the two clauses. High confidence; see Tool 8.

Wordplay and sound-features

  • The staircase repetitions (vv.1, 4–5) are an aural device: λόγος/λόγος, θεόν/θεός, ζωή/ζωή, φῶς/φῶς. In an oral culture these are memory-hooks as much as rhetoric.
  • σάρξ at v13 and v14, four words apart. “Not of the will of the flesh… and the Word became flesh.” [T] The juxtaposition is almost certainly deliberate: human beings are not born of flesh; the Word was made flesh. Moderate–high confidence on the intent; the adjacency is certain.
  • χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος — a figura etymologica (the same noun in two cases either side of a preposition), which is a rhetorical flourish in its own right and should be preserved in reading aloud.

Recommended verification

  • The precise force of ἀντί in v16 (BDAG; NET notes).
  • Whether μονογενής should be pressed as far as “unique, not begotten” (BDAG; and note the older lexica differ).
  • Whether κατέλαβεν in v5 is deliberately double (a commentary that works the Greek — Carson, Keener, or Barrett).
  • The ὃ γέγονεν punctuation (Metzger’s Textual Commentary; NET notes).

Textual Variants

Two variants in this passage matter, and one of them is among the most significant in the Gospel.

1:18 — μονογενὴς θεός / ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός

  • The issue: does the verse read “the only God” or “the only Son”?
  • Manuscript evidence: μονογενὴς θεός is read by 𝔓⁶⁶, 𝔓⁷⁵, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus — the earliest and best witnesses, and geographically spread. ὁ μονογενὴς υἱός is read by the later majority, the Latin tradition (unigenitus Filius), and is the basis of the KJV. A third reading, ὁ μονογενὴς, is sparsely attested. Verified at edition level: SBLGNT prints μονογενὴς θεός and marks it as a variant point.
  • Most likely original: μονογενὴς θεός, on both external evidence (the earliest witnesses) and the harder-reading principle (a scribe is far likelier to smooth “only God” into the familiar “only Son”, especially given 3:16, than the reverse). High confidence.
  • Preaching implication: substantial. If original, v18 is a third predication of θεός of the Son, and the Prologue’s inclusio is θεός at v1, θεός at v18, and θεός at 20:28 — a triple frame rather than a double. Say it from the pulpit, because a congregation comparing ESV (“the only God”) with KJV (“the only begotten Son”) will see the difference and deserve an explanation. And note for the overview: the θεός-inclusio claim needs this qualification (already applied at v0.2.0).
  • Confidence: high on the reading; high on its significance.

1:13 — οἳ… ἐγεννήθησαν (plural) / ὃς… ἐγεννήθη (singular)

  • The issue: “who were born… of God” (of believers) or “who was born… of God” (of Christ — a reference to the virgin birth).
  • Manuscript evidence: the plural is read by all Greek manuscripts. The singular appears in some Old Latin witnesses and in a few patristic citations (Irenaeus, Tertullian).
  • Most likely original: the plural, overwhelmingly. High confidence.
  • Preaching implication: minimal for the sermon, but worth knowing, because the singular reading is occasionally advanced as a Johannine virgin-birth text and a hearer may have encountered the claim. John has no birth narrative; his incarnation statement is v14.
  • Confidence: high.

Not a variant, but often mistaken for one

The ὃ γέγονεν question (v3/v4) is a matter of punctuation, not of text. The earliest manuscripts are unpunctuated, so both readings render the same Greek. Handle it under Translations, not here. High confidence on the classification.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period of composition: late first century by the usual reckoning [S] — but note the [T] boundary: the text does not date itself, and nothing in this report depends on the date.
  • Location: unstated. Traditional association with Ephesus is [S] and, again, non-load-bearing.
  • Political context: not addressed in the passage.

What the original audience knew

  1. Genesis 1 by heart. Ἐν ἀρχῇ is the LXX’s opening. For any reader with a synagogue education, the first three words are a quotation, and the whole passage is heard as a claim about that text. [T] for the wording; [I] for the reception.
  2. What a tabernacle was. σκηνόω is not a poetic word for “lived”. The tent in the wilderness, the glory that filled it and the impossibility of entering when it did (Exod 40:34–35) are the frame, and the readers had it. [S], well attested.
  3. The Exodus 34 formula. ḥesed we-ʾemet is the OT’s most-repeated self-description of God. A reader hearing “full of grace and truth” of a man is hearing the divine name-formula transferred. [I], moderate–high.
  4. That “the Word” was contested vocabulary. In a first-century Greek-speaking Jewish setting, λόγος was in play in several registers at once — Genesis, Wisdom, Philo, Stoicism. John does not define it; he lets Ἐν ἀρχῇ define it and then, at v14, does something none of those registers had prepared anyone for. [S] for the background, [T] for John’s move.
  5. That “no one has ever seen God” was orthodoxy, not novelty. Exod 33:20 was the settled position. v18a is a concession, not a claim; the claim is v18b. [I], high.

What this changes about how we read

  • v14 was offensive, not merely mysterious. The scandal for a Jewish hearer is not “God has a Word” — that is unremarkable — but that the Word became flesh. For a hearer formed by Greek philosophical categories, the scandal is the same from the other direction: the rational principle does not acquire a body. The Prologue is designed to offend both of its likely audiences at exactly the same word. [I], moderate–high.
  • v17 was not read as anti-Jewish. A first-century Jewish Christian hearing “the law was given through Moses” would hear a statement of fact, and one they held dear. The adversative that turns it into a repudiation is a translator’s addition (Tool 8). Recovering that changes the sermon’s tone considerably.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: New Testament Gospel. The canonical reader arrives having read the entire OT and knowing how it ends — Malachi’s promised messenger, the second temple without the glory cloud, the unresolved exile.
  • What the canonical reader knows: Genesis 1’s creation by word. Exodus 33–34’s refused face and proclaimed name. Exodus 40’s glory filling the tent. Ezekiel 10’s glory departing the temple, and Ezekiel 43’s promise of its return. That last item is the one most often missed and it is the sharpest: the canonical reader knows the glory left and has not come back. v14 says where it went.
  • Canonical register: the wisdom literature is a live neighbour (Prov 8), and the Torah is the dominant one.

The first hearers

  • Who they were: a mixed readership — deeply OT-literate but needing Aramaic terms and Jewish customs explained (see the overview’s presenting situation). Plausibly a Greek-speaking, diaspora-facing community including both Jewish and Gentile believers. [I]
  • Their situation: a community for whom confessing Jesus as Christ carried the cost of expulsion from the synagogue (9:22; 12:42; 16:2 — [T]). Their confession had been formally adjudicated against.
  • What they brought: the whole OT; a settled conviction that God cannot be seen; an expectation that the glory would return to a temple; and a fresh, painful experience of being told by their own community that they did not belong.

Where the passage fits their world

  • v11 is their autobiography, told in advance and about someone else. “He came to his own, and his own did not receive him.” A community expelled by its own people is being shown that the same thing happened to him first, and that it did not indicate failure. [I], moderate–high.
  • v12’s ἐξουσία is the counter-ruling. They have been told they have no standing; the Prologue says the right to be children of God was given — by an authority that outranks the one that expelled them. [I], moderate.
  • v14 relocates the glory from a building to a person — pastorally decisive for people cut off from the institutions of worship.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο. The single most offensive clause in the passage on any first-century reading.
  • Surprising: that a Gospel begins before creation rather than with a genealogy or a baptism; and that the first named human being in the book is not Jesus but a witness.
  • Comforting: vv.12–13 — the birth is not of blood (not lineage), not of flesh-will, not of man-will. To a community whose ethnic and institutional belonging had just been revoked, the triple negation is the comfort, not a doctrinal aside. [I], moderate–high. This is the most pastorally under-read verse in the passage.
  • Disturbing: v11. If “his own” did not receive him, then insider status is no guarantee — which cuts against the hearer as much as against their opponents.

What we bring that they didn’t

  • A doctrinal reflex. We read the Prologue as a proof-text quarry for Christology. They read it as the opening of a book they needed.
  • Individualism. v16’s “we have all received” is corporate.
  • A settled canon of Christological vocabulary that makes v1 sound familiar. It was not familiar.
  • Post-Nicene ears. “Only begotten” carries fourth-century freight that μονογενής did not.

Implication for the sermon

Recovering the reception changes two things. First, v13’s triple negation should be preached as comfort, not as a doctrinal parenthesis — it is the answer to the FCF. Second, v17 should not be preached as a contrast with Judaism; the Greek supplies no adversative, and the original hearers were not being invited to despise Moses but to see his request granted.

Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge climactically.

1. Word of God

  • Where this passage sits: at the theme’s hinge — the point at which “the word of the LORD” ceases to be something that comes to a prophet and becomes someone who comes as a person.
  • What comes before: creation by speech (Gen 1); the word that comes to Abraham and the prophets; the words spoken and written at Sinai; the word that “shall not return empty” (Isa 55:11); wisdom personified (Prov 8).
  • What comes after: the apostolic testimony (1 John 1:1, “that which we have heard… concerning the word of life”); “his name is called The Word of God” (Rev 19:13).
  • What it contributes: it explains why the Gospel’s central conflict is about hearing rather than seeing — “my word finds no place in you” (8:37), “whoever hears my word… has eternal life” (5:24). If Christ is the Word, unbelief is a failure of reception, which is exactly v11’s diagnosis.
  • Confidence: high.

2. Temple / God’s presence

  • Where this passage sits: at the theme’s relocation — the glory that filled the tent (Exod 40) and departed the temple (Ezek 10) has taken up residence in a body.
  • What comes before: Eden as proto-temple; the tabernacle; Solomon’s temple; the departure of the glory; the second temple built without it; Ezekiel’s promise of return.
  • What comes after: “destroy this temple… he was speaking of the temple of his body” (2:21) — the very next chapter; “neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem” (4:21); the church as temple (1 Cor 3:16); “I saw no temple in the city” (Rev 21:22).
  • What it contributes: it supplies the reason σκηνόω is the verb. And it sets up John 2:13–22, which is otherwise a puzzlingly early temple-action: the Prologue has already said where the dwelling is, so ch. 2 is the announcement of a programme the Prologue has stated.
  • Confidence: high.

Confluence — and it is climactic convergence, not parallel

The two themes do not merely run alongside each other. They meet in a single clause: ὁ λόγος σὰρξ ἐγένετο καὶ ἐσκήνωσεν ἐν ἡμῖν. The Word (theme 1) tabernacles (theme 2). What the Word-theme was moving toward — a word finally embodied rather than merely spoken — is what the temple-theme needed: a dwelling that could not be destroyed, deserted or defiled. Neither theme reaches its end without the other, and v14 is where both arrive. This is the passage’s biblical-theological centre and it is the best available spine for a sermon. [I], high confidence.

Christological Reading

Type of connection. This is a NT passage, so the question is not how it points to Christ but how it presents him and which OT patterns it deploys. Two typological relationships are in view, and both should be tested rather than assumed.

Tabernacle → Christ (v14). Run against the four tests: (1) theological category — yes, the tabernacle is an established category, not an incidental detail. (2) NT precedent — yes, and explicitly, at John 2:21 and Heb 9:11. (3) escalation — yes: the tent was temporary, portable and enterable only by one man once a year; the Word’s dwelling is permanent and its glory is seen (“we have seen his glory”) rather than screened. (4) authorial pattern — yes: John himself signals it with σκηνόω + δόξα, and returns to it at 2:21. Passes all four → high confidence typology.

Moses → Christ (v17). (1) category — yes: covenant mediation. (2) NT precedent — yes (Heb 3:1–6; Deut 18:15 applied in Acts 3:22). (3) escalation — yes, but note carefully that the escalation is given → came to be, which is surpassing rather than negating. (4) authorial pattern — yes: John names Moses 11 times and makes him a witness for Jesus (5:45–47). Passes all four → high confidence.

How the passage points to Christ. Directly, and in four registers: he is God (v1); he is the agent of creation (v3); he is the tabernacling glory (v14); he is the exegesis of the Father (v18). The Prologue’s Christology is as high as any in the NT, and it is stated before a single act is narrated.

Trajectory. What the passage anticipates: it does not anticipate; it announces. What is anticipated is the reader’s response. What still remains: the “we have seen his glory” of v14 is partial — 17:24 asks that they may “see my glory” in a fuller sense still to come. Already/not-yet is present but muted here.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptation: faint but real, and it attaches to John the Baptist — “be a humble witness like John, pointing away from yourself”. That is not wrong (see Copycat) but it is not this passage’s burden, and a sermon that lands there has preached vv.6–8 and skipped vv.1–5 and 14–18.
  • The subtler temptation, and the one to guard: turning v12 into a technique. “Receive him” becomes a decision to be executed correctly, and v13 is quietly dropped because it undercuts the appeal. The passage’s own grammar makes reception the counterpart of a gift (ἔδωκεν, and the triple negation of v13).
  • The gospel grounding: everything the passage asks rests on what it first declares — he made all things, he came, he became flesh, he has made God known, and from his fullness we have all received.
  • Christ as hero, not example: unavoidable here. There is no imperative in the passage and no human achievement in it. The only human actions are not receiving (v11) and receiving (v12), and the second is enabled by a gift.

Confidence: high on both typological claims; high on the moralism analysis.

Difficult / Contested Verses

v9 — “the true light, which enlightens everyone”

  • Category: doctrinal / apologetic.
  • The difficulty: does this teach a universal inner illumination, or universal salvation, or a general revelation given to all?
  • Common mishandlings: inclusivist readings (“Christ enlightens everyone, so all religions lead to him”); and, from the other side, a nervous over-restriction that empties the verse.
  • Rhetorical function in context: v9 is transitional — it moves from the abstract light of vv.4–5 to the light’s arrival in the world at vv.10–11, and it is immediately followed by the statement that the world did not know him. The verse’s own context makes it an assertion about the light’s universal scope, not about universal reception, since the next two verses describe non-reception. That constrains the plausible readings considerably.
  • Main interpretive options: (a) universal general revelation; (b) the light shines on all without all receiving it — objective illumination, varied response; (c) “everyone” = everyone who is enlightened, i.e. all believers.
  • What honest exegesis surfaces: (b) has the strongest contextual support, given vv.10–11. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Suggested handling: address briefly; the immediate context does most of the work.

v13 — “not of blood… nor of the will of man”

  • Category: doctrinal (election / human agency); pastoral.
  • The difficulty: the verse appears to exclude human decision from the new birth, which lands hard in traditions that emphasise decision.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is an explanatory relative clause attached to v12’s “children of God” — its job is to specify the origin of the birth, not to describe the mechanism of conversion. It answers “where did these children come from?”, not “what must I do?”.
  • Main options: the standard Reformed and Arminian readings differ on whether v12’s “received” is prior to or consequent upon v13’s begetting.
  • What honest exegesis surfaces: the verse is emphatic and threefold about what the birth is not, and single about what it is — ἐκ θεοῦ. The text does not adjudicate the ordering question that divides the traditions; it insists on the source. [T] Do not oversimplify in either direction: the passage holds v12’s “as many as received” and v13’s “born of God” together without resolving the relation, and a sermon that flattens either half will be noticed.
  • Suggested handling: preach the source, name the debate briefly if the congregation is likely to be alert to it, and do not make the sermon’s weight rest on the ordering.

v17 — “the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ”

  • Category: doctrinal; weaponised; pastorally sensitive.
  • The difficulty: read with the supplied “but”, the verse sounds like law-versus-grace, Judaism-versus-Christianity, and has been used that way with real historical consequences.
  • Common mishandlings: supersessionist flattening; a Marcionite drift in which the OT becomes sub-Christian; and the milder error of treating “law” as a synonym for legalism.
  • Rhetorical function in context: v17 grounds v16 (“because the law was given…”), which grounds v14’s “full of grace and truth”. Its function is explanatory, not polemical — it explains where the fullness came from.
  • What honest exegesis surfaces: (a) there is no adversative in the Greek [T]; (b) χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια is the Exodus 34:6 formula, so grace and truth were not absent from Sinai but proclaimed there; (c) the verbs differ — the law was given (ἐδόθη), grace and truth came into being (ἐγένετο). The most defensible reading is escalation within continuity: what was given as covenant word has now come into being as a person. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Suggested handling: address this directly. Name the missing “but” from the pulpit. It takes thirty seconds, it is verifiable, and it inoculates against a reading with a very bad history.

v18 — “no one has ever seen God”

  • Category: apologetic.
  • The difficulty: the OT records people seeing God — Moses (Exod 33:11, “face to face”), Isaiah (Isa 6:1), Jacob (Gen 32:30), the elders (Exod 24:9–11).
  • What honest exegesis surfaces: the tension is within the OT already — Exod 33:11 and 33:20 sit fourteen verses apart. The distinction the OT itself makes is between mediated appearance and unmediated sight of the divine essence. v18 stands on the 33:20 side and answers it with the Son. High confidence that the tension is scriptural, not created by John.
  • Suggested handling: acknowledge; it is a good question and has a good answer.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Preaching the Prologue as an abstract doctrinal preface

  • What it looks like: a sermon that treats vv.1–18 as a systematic-theology lecture — the deity of Christ, the pre-existence of Christ, the incarnation — delivered as propositions with no reference to the narrative it opens.
  • Why it’s wrong: the passage’s function is positional. It exists to make chapters 2–21 legible, and its own genre is briefing, not treatise. Preached as abstraction it becomes the hardest passage in the Gospel and the least useful.
  • The corrective: preach it as the vantage-point. Show one place where the reader knows what a character does not — 7:27 (“we know where this man comes from”) is the cleanest example — and the Prologue’s purpose becomes obvious.

Pitfall: “But grace and truth came through Jesus Christ”

  • What it looks like: law against grace, Moses against Jesus, old covenant against new — with the adversative doing all the work.
  • Why it’s wrong: there is no adversative in the Greek [T], and the Vulgate does not supply one. χάρις καὶ ἀλήθεια is the formula God proclaimed to Moses at Exod 34:6, so the verse cannot mean that grace arrived for the first time in the incarnation.
  • The corrective: name the missing “but” out loud. Preach v17 as the granting of Moses’s own request (Exod 33:18) rather than as his demotion.

Pitfall: Turning v12 into a decision-technique

  • What it looks like: “receive him” as an act the hearer performs correctly in order to secure the status — with v13 skipped or hurried.
  • Why it’s wrong: v13’s triple negation exists precisely to exclude human origination, and the “right” of v12 is given (ἔδωκεν), not earned.
  • The corrective: preach vv.12–13 as one sentence, which they are. The comfort of v13 is the answer to the anxiety v12 can create.

Pitfall: “The light shines in the darkness and the darkness has not understood it”

  • What it looks like: the darkness as ignorance, and the passage as being about intellectual failure.
  • Why it’s wrong: κατέλαβεν carries both “grasp” and “overpower”, and the second is at least as well supported. The moderns’ “overcome” is likely right; the Gospel that follows is about hostility more than confusion (11:53; 12:37).
  • The corrective: hold both if you preach both, but do not let “did not understand” become the only sense — it makes the darkness sound merely slow.

Pitfall: “Only begotten” pressed as generation

  • What it looks like: a doctrinal excursus on eternal generation resting on the word μονογενής.
  • Why it’s wrong: the word is μονο- + γένος (“one of a kind”), not μονο- + γεννάω (“only begotten”). Eternal generation may be a good doctrine; this is not the word that establishes it.
  • The corrective: translate “the only one of his kind” and let the doctrine rest on better ground.

Pitfall: Skipping the Baptist

  • What it looks like: vv.6–8 and 15 treated as intrusions to be got past on the way back to the theology.
  • Why it’s wrong: they are the passage’s only narrative material, and the μαρτυρ- word-group they carry runs 47 times through the Gospel. The Prologue insists that the Word is known through testimony before it says anything about how he is received.
  • The corrective: let the interruptions interrupt. They are the passage’s argument about how the reader will come to know any of this.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. v3–4, ὃ γέγονεν. End of v3 (all moderns) or start of v4 (SBLGNT and most Greek fathers)? A punctuation decision, not a textual one, and no common English version offers the patristic option. Metzger; NET notes.
  2. v16, χάριν ἀντὶ χάριτος. “Grace in place of grace” (the normal force of ἀντί, giving a replacement sense that fits v17) or “grace upon grace” (idiomatic accumulation)? The choice materially changes v17. BDAG; NET notes. The highest-value lexical question in the passage.
  3. v5, κατέλαβεν. Deliberate double meaning, or has one sense been over-read? A commentary that works the Greek.
  4. v9, the dangling ἐρχόμενον. Modifying “everyone” (KJV) or “the light” (moderns)? Moderns probably right; worth confirming.
  5. How hard to press μονογενής. The “unique, not begotten” analysis is now standard but the older lexica differ. BDAG.
  6. The macro-structure. Linear-thematic, chiastic (and if so, centred where?), or hymn-plus-insertions? Genuinely contested; the report adopts the linear reading and flags the alternatives.
  7. Whether ὁ ὤν at v18 echoes Exod 3:14 LXX. Suggestive in a Gospel this invested in ἐγώ εἰμι; unverified.
  8. Whether ἐν ἀρχῇ (1:1) is deliberately set against ἀπ’ ἀρχῆς of the devil (8:44). Lexical fact certain; authorial intent inferred.
  9. The Sirach 24 parallel (Wisdom told to “pitch your tent in Jacob”) against John’s ἐσκήνωσεν. Verbally close; the question is whether John’s readers would have heard it.
