## The Passage

> **1** As he passed by, he saw a man blind from birth. **2** And his disciples asked him, "Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" **3** Jesus answered, "It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him. **4** We must work the works of him who sent me while it is day; night is coming, when no one can work. **5** As long as I am in the world, I am the light of the world." **6** Having said these things, he spit on the ground and made mud with the saliva. Then he anointed the man's eyes with the mud **7** and said to him, "Go, wash in the pool of Siloam" (which means Sent). So he went and washed and came back seeing.
>
> **8** The neighbours and those who had seen him before as a beggar were saying, "Is this not the man who used to sit and beg?" **9** Some said, "It is he." Others said, "No, but he is like him." He kept saying, "I am the man." **10** So they said to him, "Then how were your eyes opened?" **11** He answered, "The man called Jesus made mud and anointed my eyes and said to me, 'Go to Siloam and wash.' So I went and washed and received my sight." **12** They said to him, "Where is he?" He said, "I do not know."
>
> **13** They brought to the Pharisees the man who had formerly been blind. **14** Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes. **15** So the Pharisees again asked him how he had received his sight. And he said to them, "He put mud on my eyes, and I washed, and I see." **16** Some of the Pharisees said, "This man is not from God, for he does not keep the Sabbath." But others said, "How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?" And there was a division among them. **17** So they said again to the blind man, "What do you say about him, since he has opened your eyes?" He said, "He is a prophet."
>
> **18** The Jews did not believe that he had been blind and had received his sight, until they called the parents of the man who had received his sight **19** and asked them, "Is this your son, who you say was born blind? How then does he now see?" **20** His parents answered, "We know that this is our son and that he was born blind. **21** But how he now sees we do not know, nor do we know who opened his eyes. Ask him; he is of age. He will speak for himself." **22** (His parents said these things because they feared the Jews, for the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess Jesus to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue. **23** Therefore his parents said, "He is of age; ask him.")
>
> **24** So for the second time they called the man who had been blind and said to him, "Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner." **25** He answered, "Whether he is a sinner I do not know. One thing I do know, that though I was blind, now I see." **26** They said to him, "What did he do to you? How did he open your eyes?" **27** He answered them, "I have told you already, and you would not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? Do you also want to become his disciples?" **28** And they reviled him, saying, "You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses. **29** We know that God has spoken to Moses, but as for this man, we do not know where he comes from." **30** The man answered, "Why, this is an amazing thing! You do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes. **31** We know that God does not listen to sinners, but if anyone is a worshipper of God and does his will, God listens to him. **32** Never since the world began has it been heard that anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind. **33** If this man were not from God, he could do nothing." **34** They answered him, "You were born in utter sin, and would you teach us?" And they cast him out.
>
> **35** Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him he said, "Do you believe in the Son of Man?" **36** He answered, "And who is he, sir, that I may believe in him?" **37** Jesus said to him, "You have seen him, and it is he who is speaking to you." **38** He said, "Lord, I believe," and he worshipped him. **39** Jesus said, "For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." **40** Some of the Pharisees near him heard these things, and said to him, "Are we also blind?" **41** Jesus said to them, "If you were blind, you would have no guilt; but now that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains."

## Headline Findings

1. **The chapter runs two ladders in opposite directions, rung for rung, and the rungs are matched.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tone, Vocabulary. The man's estimate of Jesus climbs — "the man called Jesus" (v.11) → "he is a prophet" (v.17) → "if this man were not from God he could do nothing" (v.33) → "Lord, I believe," and he worshipped (v.38). The court's estimate falls in step — divided (v.16) → "this man is a sinner" (v.24) → "we do not know where he comes from" (v.29) → "you were born in utter sin," and they cast him out (v.34). Each climb costs him something, and each fall is delivered as a verdict. The chapter's structure *is* its argument, and a sermon that does not walk both ladders has not preached the chapter.

2. **Eleven "know" statements in forty-one verses, and the one that survives is the one that claims least.** `[T]` (English count verified; Greek οἶδα count inferred). Surfaced by: Repetition, Linking Words, Tone, Tool 16. The court and the parents assert "we know" three times (vv.20, 24, 29) and confess ignorance three times (vv.21 ×2, 29); the man confesses ignorance twice (vv.12, 25) and claims knowledge twice — "one thing I do know" (v.25) and, using their own formula against them, "we know that God does not listen to sinners" (v.31). Verse 30 hands their ignorance back as the argument: *you* do not know where he comes from, and yet he opened my eyes. Verse 41 then makes the *claim* to see the aggravating factor. The passage is about the epistemics of confession, not about courage.

3. **"You have seen him" (v.37) is the hinge in a chain the Prologue opened — and it is said to a man who had never seen anything.** `[T]` for the verbal chain; `[I]` for the hinge reading. Surfaced by: Tool 11 Move 4, Vocabulary (ἑώρακας), and the Prologue dig's own πώποτε thread. John 1:18 says *no one has ever seen God* (ἑώρακεν); 6:46 restricts seeing the Father to the one who is from God; 14:9 will say *whoever has seen me has seen the Father*. Between them stands 9:37, the first and only time in the Gospel that Jesus tells someone *"you have seen him"* (ἑώρακας αὐτόν). The Prologue's negative becomes the Farewell's positive by way of a beggar's eyes. **This extends the Prologue dig's four-stage chain (1:18 → 5:37 → 6:46 → 14:9), which does not include 9:37.**

4. **John slows the camera almost entirely onto the trial: the sign takes seven verses, the cross-examination twenty-seven — and Jesus is offstage for all of them.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Author's Purpose (narrative selectivity), Structure, Narrator's Comment, Original Audience. Jesus speaks at vv.3–5, acts at vv.6–7, and does not reappear until v.35. What John wants watched is not the miracle but the witness under examination — and the narrator interrupts at v.22 to say why that matters: confession got people put out of the synagogue. The chapter is the presenting situation dramatised, and the sign is its premise rather than its subject.

5. **"Give glory to God" (v.24) is not an invitation to praise; it is a judicial adjuration, and its canonical source is Joshua's charge to Achan.** `[T]` for the formula; `[S: logos]` confirmed. Surfaced by: Tool 7 (proper-noun/idiom check), Tool 11 Move 1, Tone, Original Audience. Joshua 7:19 (LXX δὸς δόξαν) opens a confession that ends in an execution. Beale and Carson's *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament* (Jn 9:1–41, p. 459) reads the words as "a solemn warning to tell the truth… with the implication that the person so exhorted has done something wrong"; Godet, Ryle (citing Calvin, Chemnitz, Beza, Alford, Trench), the ESV Study Bible and Howard's NAC *Joshua* concur. The tribunal is not asking the man to worship. It is putting him under oath as a condemned man and inviting him to incriminate his healer — and, unlike Achan, he has been glorifying God all along by telling the truth.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Chapter 8 has (a) made the claim "I am the light of the world" (8:12) and had it challenged *on evidential grounds* — "you are bearing witness about yourself; your testimony is not true" (8:13) — with Jesus invoking the two-witness rule of the Law in reply (8:17); (b) run the where-from question to a standstill ("you do not know where I come from," 8:14); (c) fought a paternity dispute in which the opponents claimed Abraham and denied being "born of sexual immorality" (8:39–41) while Jesus named their father as the devil; and (d) ended with stones raised and Jesus hidden and gone from the temple (8:59). `[T]`

**Necessity answer — four strands, and they interlock.** `[T]`/`[I]`

1. *The claim needs enacting.* 9:5 repeats 8:12 verbatim and then the light is given to a man who has never had any. The discourse supplies its own interpretation in advance — unusual in John, and it means the sign cannot be preached as a free-standing wonder. `[T]` (Independently reached; the sweep says the same. `[S: sweep]`)
2. *The evidential objection needs a second witness.* 8:13 objected that Jesus's testimony was self-referential; 8:17 cited the rule that two witnesses make a testimony true. Chapter 9 puts a second witness in the box and has him examined four times by a hostile court — and his testimony holds. **The chapter is the answer to 8:13, and it is answered in the very form the objection demanded.** `[I]`, *moderate–high*. This is the strongest positional finding of the run and it is not in the prior reports.
3. *The paternity quarrel needs its victim.* The birth-guilt category the opponents deflected from themselves at 8:41 they hurl at the man at 9:34 ("you were born in utter sin"). The disciples had already raised it at 9:2. Jesus refuses it at 9:3. Chapter 9 is where the abstract slur of chapter 8 lands on an actual person. `[T]`
4. *The expulsion needs a shepherd.* The chapter must sit before 10:1–21 because 9:34's casting-out is the crisis the shepherd discourse answers, and 10:21's back-reference to "the eyes of the blind" seals the join. `[T]` (Agrees with `[S: 7–10 dig]` and `[S: overview]`.)

**Why here and not elsewhere — the festal answer.** The Booths panel opened with the water-cry (7:37–39) and the light-claim (8:12), the feast's two great symbols. Chapter 9 fuses them into one act: the man is sent to *wash* in Siloam and comes back *seeing*. No other position in the Gospel would let a single sign discharge both halves of the feast. `[I]`, *moderate–high* (the identification of Siloam as the water-drawing rite's source is `[S]` — see Historical Background).

**Implication.** Preached as a standalone healing, the chapter becomes an inspirational story about a brave man. Preached from its position, it is the second witness in a trial that has been running since 1:19, giving evidence under adjuration, on the very ground the prosecution itself specified.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens strand 1. Isaiah 42:6–7 puts *light for the nations* and *opening blind eyes* in a single breath — so 9:5 followed by 9:6–7 is not two claims but one Isaianic commission enacted. And Isaiah 42:18–20's *blind servant* who "sees many things but does not observe them" supplies the other half of 9:39. The positional answer therefore has an Isaianic shape as well as a festal one.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** 20:31, read through the overview's forensic frame: the book is a brief laid before a jury, written so that a community whose confession is expensive knows whom they are confessing. `[S: overview]` / `[T]`

**Passage-level.** To exhibit the sign that enacts the light-claim, and — far more space — to exhibit the *witness the sign produces*: a man with no credentials, no theological training and no initial knowledge of who healed him, whose testimony survives four examinations, an adjuration, and expulsion, and who ends at worship. And, by the narrator's aside at v.22, to tell the reader that this is their own situation. `[I]`, *high*

**Narrative selectivity is the tell.** John hits the accelerator on the miracle (two verses of action, vv.6–7, with no crowd reaction, no acclamation, no note that Jesus was thanked) and the brake on the interrogation (vv.8–34, twenty-seven verses of dialogue). `[T]` He also removes his protagonist: **Jesus is offstage from v.8 to v.34** — as far as I can see the longest continuous stretch of narrative in the Gospel in which Jesus neither speaks nor acts (`[I]`, *moderate* — worth machine-checking against 18:15–27, where the denial scenes are interleaved with the hearing). Marianne Meye Thompson makes the same observation and adds that "the man continues to be described in ways that recall Jesus: being interrogated, the dispute about his identity, the need to give testimony about himself" (*The Gospel of John: A Theological Commentary*, p. 191). `[S: logos]`

The purpose of the removal is structural: the disciple must be seen to hold the line *while the Lord is absent*, which is precisely the readers' position. `[I]`, *moderate–high*

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above — four interlocking strands (enacted claim, second witness, paternity slur landed, expulsion answered), plus the festal fusion of water and light.*

**Immediately before (8:59).** Stones raised; "Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple." The light leaves the temple and finds a man who has never seen it. `[T]` The juxtaposition also sets up a rhyme the reader feels before naming it: the Word is driven out of the temple at 8:59, and the witness is driven out of the synagogue at 9:34. `[I]`, *moderate*

**Immediately after (10:1–21).** No time-marker, no place-change, no new audience: 10:1 continues the speech begun at 9:39 to the same Pharisees. The real break is 10:22 ("the Feast of Dedication… it was winter"). `[T]` Chapter and verse divisions actively mislead here; the preacher should say so, or at least read to 10:21.

**Section.** Within the Booths panel (7:2 → 10:21), the last and largest of its signs. Within the book, inside the forensic bracket 1:19 ↔ 10:40–42 that contains thirteen of the Gospel's seventeen σημεῖον references. `[S: overview]`

**Commonly read out of context.** Verse 3 is routinely quoted alone at funerals and in pastoral conversation about disability, detached from v.4's "we must work" and from a chapter in which the "works of God" turn out to be a court case that costs the man his community. Verses 39–41 are routinely detached from vv.1–38 and preached as a general maxim about spiritual pride.

### 3. Structure

Seven scenes and a verdict, with two characters (or two parties) on stage at a time. The chapter is the most fully dramatic unit in the Gospel.

| Verses | Scene | Function |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1–5 | Jesus and the disciples | The wrong question asked and refused; the light-claim restated |
| 6–7 | The sign | Clay, Siloam, sight. No dialogue, no reaction |
| 8–12 | The neighbours | Examination 1 — identity disputed; "I do not know" |
| 13–17 | The Pharisees | Examination 2 — the Sabbath disclosed; division; "he is a prophet" |
| 18–23 | The parents | Examination 3 — the family will not testify; the narrator explains why |
| 24–34 | The Pharisees again | Examination 4 — adjuration, the man's syllogism, expulsion |
| 35–38 | Jesus finds him | Confession and worship |
| 39–41 | Verdict | Judgement pronounced; the Pharisees convict themselves |

**Devices.**

*Bookend (inclusio).* v.2 "who sinned… that he was born blind?" ↔ v.34 "you were born in utter sin." `[T]` The disciples' premise and the Pharisees' verdict are the same theology. Jesus rejects it at v.3; the court affirms it at v.34 and expels the man on it. Everything between the two is bracketed by a doctrine of inherited guilt that the chapter's own protagonist disproves by being healed.

*Reversal-frame.* v.1 a man physically blind; v.41 men declared spiritually blind. The chapter opens with blindness that is nobody's fault and closes with blindness that is entirely culpable, and the difference between them is a claim to see.

*The two ladders* (Headline Finding 1) — the confession climbing (vv.11, 17, 33, 38) against the verdict falling (vv.16, 24, 29, 34). The two are interleaved scene by scene, not sequential. `[T]`

*Withheld disclosure.* The Sabbath is not mentioned until v.14, after the reader has already enjoyed the healing. John does the identical thing at 5:9. `[T]` The reader is made to like the sign before being told it is illegal.

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative, so the connectors are sparse — but three of them carry the chapter.

**v.3 — ἀλλ' ἵνα, "but that."** The pivot of the whole opening and a genuine crux (see Tool 8 and Difficult Verses). As punctuated in ESV/NASB/KJV, the ἵνα is a purpose clause governing the blindness: he was born blind *in order that* the works of God might be displayed. On an alternative punctuation the clause runs forward into v.4: "…but in order that the works of God might be displayed in him, we must work the works of him who sent me." `[T]` for the two options; the choice is not decidable on the Greek alone, since John elsewhere uses elliptical ἀλλ' ἵνα (cf. 1:8; 13:18; 15:25). *Moderate confidence that both are grammatically available; no confident verdict offered.* The parallel at Exodus 4:11–12 (Tool 11) leans, without settling, toward the forward reading.

**v.4 — "while it is day; night is coming."** A temporal frame, not a connector proper, but it governs the sign's urgency and plants a word the Gospel will cash: νύξ. See Move 4.

**vv.31–33 — the man's syllogism, and it is formally valid.** `[T]`
> *Major premise* (v.31, borrowed from his examiners' own theology): God does not listen to sinners, but listens to the God-fearing who do his will.
> *Minor premise* (v.32, from unprecedented fact): never since the world began has anyone opened the eyes of a man born blind.
> *Conclusion* (v.33, second-class conditional): if this man were not from God, he could do nothing.

The beggar out-argues the theologians on their own premises, which is why v.34 answers with an insult rather than a rebuttal. Note that the conclusion is stated as a contrary-to-fact conditional — the most cautious form available. He does not say "he is from God"; he says the alternative is impossible.

**v.41 — εἰ … νῦν δέ.** "If you were blind, you would have no guilt; **but now** that you say, 'We see,' your guilt remains." A contrary-to-fact conditional followed by the adversative pivot that delivers the chapter's verdict. `[T]` The νῦν δέ is doing all the work: the guilt is not in the blindness but in the claim.

### 5. Parallels

Largely **N/A** — Johannine narrative prose, not poetry. One construction earns the tool:

**v.39 — antithetical, and chiastic in the Greek.** ἵνα οἱ μὴ βλέποντες βλέπωσιν καὶ οἱ βλέποντες τυφλοὶ γένωνται — "that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind." `[T]` The pairing is exact and inverted, and it is the chapter's thesis compressed into one line. Verse 41 then breaks the symmetry deliberately: the antithesis had implied two groups, but v.41 shows there is only one culpable position, and it is the second. Read the pair together or the sermon will make v.39 sound like a balanced observation rather than a sentence.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Rich — four intrusions, and they are the interpretive spine of the chapter.

**v.7 — "(which means Sent)."** A decoder aside. John translates Σιλωάμ as ἀπεσταλμένος, the participle he uses of Jesus throughout, and he has just had Jesus say "him who sent me" three verses earlier (v.4). `[T]` The gloss is John's own hand pointing at the sending-Christology. Peter Leithart presses the observation further: John does not gloss Bethsaida, Cana, Galilee or Capernaum — "Siloam is unique. John wants us…" (*Deep Exegesis*, p. 99). `[S: logos]` *Qualification:* John does gloss Gabbatha and Golgotha (19:13, 17), so the claim is properly about ordinary place names, not all names.

**v.14 — "Now it was a Sabbath day when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes."** Withheld until after the healing. `[T]` And note what the narrator specifies as the offence: not the healing but **the making of the mud**. See Historical Background.

**v.18 — "The Jews did not believe that he had been blind and had received his sight."** Inside information on the court's disposition. `[T]` The reader is told the examination is not a search for facts.

**vv.22–23 — the long parenthesis.** The parents' motive (fear), the policy (expulsion for confessing Jesus as Christ), and a repetition of their evasion. `[T]` This is the verse where the narrator's world touches the story's world, and it is the single most important sentence in the chapter for understanding why John told it. It is also the first of exactly three ἀποσυνάγωγος notices in the Gospel (9:22; 12:42; 16:2 — verified `[S: sweep]`), and the only one attached to a particular family.

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory** (Tool 7c, Question 0). *Siloam · Jesus · Moses · the Christ · the Son of Man · the Pharisees · the Jews · God.* No place names besides Siloam; no numbers. Triage: **Siloam** and **Moses** carry canonical freight and receive the full check; the rest are routine or are treated under Tool 11.

#### Siloam — the full check `[T]` + `[S: logos]`

*Etymology.* Hebrew שִׁלֹחַ *šilōaḥ* is a passive form of שָׁלַח *šālaḥ*, "to send" — "sent" or "conducted" (ISBE, Masterman, "Siloam, Siloah, Shelah, Shiloah," p. 2791). Carson (*The Gospel according to John*, PNTC, p. 361) and Bruce (*The Gospel of John*, p. 208) both give the derivation and both make the point John makes: it "speaks of Jesus, the sent one (Greek *apestalmenos*) of God." *High confidence.*

*First canonical occurrence and major association.* Isaiah 8:6 — "because this people has refused the waters of Shiloah that flow gently, and rejoice over Rezin and the son of Remaliah." The LXX renders the name Σιλωάμ at Isa 8:6. Also Nehemiah 3:15 (the Pool of Shelah) and, for the engineering, 2 Kings 20:20 / 2 Chronicles 32:30 (Hezekiah's tunnel).

*Load-the-story rule applied to Isaiah 8.* Ahaz's Judah, threatened by the Syro-Ephraimite coalition, refuses the modest, quiet water God provides in favour of the Euphrates — Assyria — which will then flood them "up to the neck" (8:7–8). NIDOTTE 4:1229 makes the point of the metaphor exactly: the citizens rejected what "appeared insignificant and ineffectual — the protecting power of God." The passage runs straight on into darkness (8:22) and then into "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (9:2).

*Verdict, with the honest caveat.* Three things must be kept apart. (a) The **etymology** is certain and John supplies it himself. (b) The **theological reading** — a people who refused Shiloah's waters now refuse the one sent to them, while a beggar is washed in them and sees — is made by mainstream interpreters (e.g. *A Study Commentary on John* Vol. 1, p. 371: "in Isaiah 8:6 the Jews reject the waters of Shiloah; here they reject Jesus"). (c) The **topographical identification** of Isaiah's *waters of Shiloah* (a channel or aqueduct) with John's *pool of Siloam* is disputed: the *Zondervan Encyclopedia of the Bible* 5:480 warns that "one should not assume that the passage refers specifically to the Pool of Siloam mentioned in Jn. 9:7," and the Holman dictionary (p. 1503) calls the two "easily confused… through similarity of spelling." **Preach (a) confidently, (b) as a resonance, and do not stake anything on (c).** *Overall: moderate–high on the allusion, high on the etymology.* `[revised: logos-research]`

*A claim in the library to resist.* Several popular-level works (Towns, *John: Believe and Live*, p. 89; and Logos's own AI synopsis repeating him) assert that the LXX renders the messianic "Shiloh" of Genesis 49:10 as Σιλωάμ, and build a Shiloh–Shiloah–Siloam identity on it. Genesis 49:10's שִׁילֹה is not the same word, and LXX Gen 49:10 does not read Σιλωάμ. **Do not use this.** `[S: logos — flagged as unreliable]`

#### Moses (vv.28–29) `[T]`/`[I]`

"We are disciples of Moses. We know that God has spoken to Moses." The load-the-story rule points to Exodus 33: Moses is the man who asked to see God's glory and was told "you cannot see my face." The Prologue's "no one has ever seen God" (1:18) is Exodus 33:20 in substance (`[S: prologue dig]`, *moderate–high*). So the boast of v.29 — *God spoke to Moses* — is precisely accurate and precisely insufficient: Moses **heard**. The beggar, nine verses later, is told "**you have seen him**" (v.37). The Mosaic economy is one of speech; what the light of the world gives is sight. `[I]`, *moderate–high*. This is not in the prior reports and it is preachable.

The boast also walks into 5:45–47 — see Move 4.

### 8. Translations

**v.41 — ἁμαρτία rendered "guilt" (ESV) against "sin" (NASB, KJV). The most consequential rendering in the chapter.** `[T]`

> ESV: "If you were blind, you would have no **guilt**… your **guilt** remains."
> NASB: "…you would have no **sin**… your **sin** remains."
> KJV: "…ye should have no **sin**… your **sin** remaineth."
> Vulgate: *si caeci essetis, non haberetis **peccatum*** (*moderate* confidence on my recall of the Latin).

ESV footnotes the Greek, but the footnote does not repair the damage: the chapter has been running a sin-word from v.2 ("who **sinned**?") through v.3, v.16, v.24, v.25, v.31 and v.34 ("born in utter **sin**"), and v.41 is the term's return in Jesus's own mouth as the closing verdict. Rendering it "guilt" at the one place where the inclusio closes hides the closure. **Recommendation: read v.41 as "sin" from the pulpit and say why.** *High confidence that the point stands; the ESV rendering is defensible as sense and indefensible as a preserved thread.*

**v.3 — punctuation, not vocabulary.** ESV, NASB and KJV all attach the ἵνα clause to the blindness. The alternative (full stop after "or his parents"; ἀλλ' ἵνα running forward to v.4) is a punctuation decision, not a textual one, and no major English version prints it. The preacher should know it exists — it materially changes what the sermon says about God and disability — and should not present the ESV punctuation as though the Greek settled it. *See Difficult Verses.*

**v.4 — "We must work… him who sent me."** The mixed first-person plural / first-person singular is the harder and better-attested reading (ESV, NASB); KJV has "**I** must work the works of him that sent me," smoothing it. `[T]` The mixed reading is exegetically live: Jesus enrols the disciples in the works before performing one alone. Do not lose it.

**v.35 — "Son of Man" (ESV, NASB, critical text) against "Son of God" (KJV, Byzantine).** A textual matter, not a translation philosophy one; see Textual Variants.

**Ancient versions check.** Run, and it yields one result worth reporting: **at v.41 the Vulgate's *peccatum* sides with NASB/KJV against the ESV's "guilt"**, so the ancient tradition preserves the sin-thread the ESV breaks. *Moderate confidence on the Vulgate wording; the direction of the finding is secure because the Greek is not in doubt.* Elsewhere the ancient versions raise nothing exegetically weighty. As a NT passage the Hebrew/Greek triage does not apply.

**Pulpit edition note.** Pulpit text matches report default (ESV Anglicised); no divergence affects any finding here — except that the "guilt" rendering at v.41 is present in the Anglicised edition too, so the pulpit will hear it unless the preacher intervenes.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Three registers, sharply cut.**

*Comic, and genuinely so, through the middle.* The neighbours bickering over whether it is even the same man (v.9); the Pharisees asking the same question four times and getting shorter answers each time; the parents' twice-repeated "he is of age; ask him"; and above all v.27 — "I have told you already, and you would not listen. Why do you want to hear it again? **Do you also want to become his disciples?**" That is dry insolence from a man who was begging that morning, and it should be preached as funny. The comedy is not decoration; it is the deflation of an authority that has lost the argument.

*Pathos, undercut with fear, at the parents.* Not comic at all. They know their son. They say so. And then they hand him over. The narrator does not condemn them — he explains them (v.22). A congregation under any kind of social pressure will recognise them, and a preacher who mocks them has misread the tone.

*Cold, at the end.* The comedy stops the instant Jesus reappears at v.35. The register of vv.35–38 is tender (he *heard*, he *found*, he *spoke*); the register of vv.39–41 is judicial and final. "Your guilt remains" is the last word and there is no softening after it.

**Devices carrying the tone.** Repetition-with-shortening (the man's testimony contracts each time it is demanded: full at v.11, terse at v.15, refused at v.27); accumulation of interrogatives; and the abrupt scene-cuts, which give the chapter a stage-play pace.

**Soundtrack.** A scherzo that stops mid-bar. Or: courtroom drama with a farcical second act and a very quiet ending.

**Idea-level repetition: the deposition that shrinks.** The healing is retold three times and gets shorter each time — v.11 in full (name, mud, anointing, instruction, obedience, result), v.15 in eight words ("He put mud on my eyes, and I washed, and I see"), and at v.27 refused outright. `[T]` This is forensically exact: a witness whose account is consistent under repeated examination has nothing to embroider, and the shrinkage is the mark of truthfulness, not of impatience. The preacher who notices this has a way into the chapter that no amount of commentary supplies.

**Pattern-break absence — reported with its established pattern, per the admissibility gate.** πατήρ occurs 136× across John and is **absent from exactly two chapters: 9 and 19** (verified `[S: sweep]`). Against a mean of roughly seven per chapter, that absence is noticeable rather than guessed. Both chapters are formal interrogations ending in expulsion or execution. In a chapter that turns on parentage — *his parents* (γονεῖς) five times, *born* five times, a paternity slur at v.34 — the Gospel's characteristic word for Father does not appear, and even Jesus says "him who sent me" (v.4) rather than "my Father." *Weight: moderate.* What it means is properly open; flag, do not preach hard.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**No formal citations.** No γέγραπται, no fulfilment formula. Everything here is verbal or conceptual allusion, and Move 4 carries the weight.

**Independent candidate-finding, from the passage's own vocabulary before any source was opened:** *blind from birth · who sinned · works of God · sent me · light of the world · spat, made mud (πηλός), anointed · Siloam · washed · opened the eyes · Sabbath · a prophet · put out of the synagogue · give glory to God · disciples of Moses · God does not listen to sinners · never since the world began · born in utter sin · cast out · Son of Man · you have seen him · worshipped · for judgment I came · those who see may become blind.*

These point independently to: **Isaiah 42 and 35** (light for the nations; opening blind eyes; and the blind servant), **Isaiah 6:9–10 and 8:6** (seeing without perceiving; the refused waters), **Exodus 4:11** (who makes the blind?), **Genesis 2:7 with Job 10:9 / Isaiah 64:8** (forming from clay), **Ezekiel 18 / Jeremiah 31:29–30** (the repudiated inherited-guilt proverb), **Joshua 7:19** (give glory to God), **Deuteronomy 18:15** (a prophet), and **Daniel 7:13** (Son of Man).

**Isaiah 42:6–7 with 42:18–20 → John 9:5–7 and 9:39–41** *(moderate–high, conceptual with strong thematic volume)* `[I]`

*Source context.* Isaiah 42:6–7 is the commission of the first Servant Song: "I have given you as a covenant for the people, **a light for the nations, to open the eyes that are blind**, to bring out the prisoners from the dungeon." Light and eye-opening are a single commission, not two images. Then, in the same chapter, the movement turns: "Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind, that you may see! **Who is blind but my servant?**… He sees many things, but does not observe them" (42:18–20). Isaiah 42 therefore contains *both halves of John 9:39* — the Servant who opens blind eyes, and the people who see many things and observe nothing.

*Book usage (Move 2).* Isaiah is a confirmed live source in John — 1:23 (Isa 40:3), 6:45 (Isa 54:13), 12:38 (Isa 53:1), 12:40 (Isa 6:10), with 12:41's narratorial "Isaiah saw his glory and spoke of him." `[S: overview]` **Decisively for this passage: John's only other explicit engagement with blindness is the Isaiah 6:10 citation at 12:40 — "he has blinded their eyes… lest they see with their eyes."** So chapter 9 dramatises what chapter 12 will cite as Scripture. The narrative comes first and the warrant follows; and the verdict of 9:39 is, three chapters later, given a prophetic signature. `[I]`, *moderate–high*. **This is the passage's strongest Move 2 finding and neither the sweep nor the section dig states it in this form.**

*OT-to-OT (Move 3).* Isaiah's own book has already put these texts in conversation. Isaiah 6:9–10 (a commission to a people who will look and not see), 8:6 (the refused waters of Shiloah), 8:22–9:2 (darkness, then a great light), and 42:6–7 / 18–20 (the Servant who opens eyes; the servant who is blind) belong to one sustained Isaianic argument in which blindness is both judgement and the thing the Servant is sent to undo. John does not construct this pairing; he inherits it. `[I]`, *moderate*

*What it adds.* John 9 is not a healing story with a spiritual application appended. It is the Isaianic Servant's commission enacted inside the Isaianic verdict — sight given to one who never had it, blindness confirmed on those who insist they have it — staged at a pool whose name Isaiah used for the provision this people refused. The two halves of v.39 are the two halves of Isaiah 42.

**Exodus 4:11 → John 9:2–3** *(moderate–high, conceptual with a structural match)* `[I]`

*Source context.* At the burning bush Moses objects that he is "slow of speech and of tongue." The LORD answers: "**Who has made man's mouth? Who makes him mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Is it not I, the LORD?**" (Exod 4:11). And immediately: "Now therefore go, and I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall speak" (4:12). The context is a **commissioning**: a question about sensory disability arises inside a call narrative, the answer relocates causation in God's making, and the very next word is *go and work*.

*Structural match.* John 9:2–4 executes the identical two-step. A question about the cause of blindness (v.2) → causation relocated away from human sin and into God's purpose (v.3) → "**we must work** the works of him who sent me while it is day" (v.4). Both passages refuse the retributive question and answer with a commission.

*Book usage (Move 2).* Exodus is live in John at the institutional level (Passover, manna, the serpent, ἐγώ εἰμι), though Exodus 4 has not appeared before. *Moderate.*

*What it adds.* Two things. First, it supplies the theological register of v.3: not "God caused this in order to show off," but "the origin of every faculty is God's, and what follows from that is work, not diagnosis." Second — and this is a real contribution to the punctuation crux — **the shape of Exodus 4:11–12 leans toward reading the ἵνα forward into v.4**, since the source pattern moves from *God's making* to *therefore go and work* rather than terminating in an explanation of the disability. It does not settle the question, but it is evidence, and it is evidence the theodicy debate rarely brings.

**Joshua 7:19 → John 9:24** *(high, on external confirmation; a cold allusion carried by a distinctive idiom)* `[T]` + `[S: logos]`

*Source context.* Israel is defeated at Ai; the lot has fallen on Achan; Joshua says, "My son, **give glory to the LORD God of Israel and give praise to him**. And tell me now what you have done" (Josh 7:19, LXX δὸς δόξαν). Achan confesses; he and his household are stoned and burned. The formula's function in its own passage is a solemn adjuration opening a confession that ends in execution. The Hebrew *tôdâ* covers both praise and confession, which is why the two are one act. (ESV Study Bible, Josh 7, p. 403; Howard, *Joshua*, NAC 5:196–97.)

*Book usage (Move 2).* Joshua is not otherwise a live source in John. This is a cold allusion, and it survives on the distinctiveness of the idiom rather than on the book's habits — exactly the case the clustering principle says needs external support. It now has it.

*OT-to-OT (Move 3).* The formula recurs at Jeremiah 13:16 and 1 Samuel 6:5, so it is a standing Israelite oath-form rather than a Joshua-specific phrase; but Joshua 7 is its narrative paradigm and the one every commentator reaches for.

*External check.* `[revised: logos-research]` Beale and Carson, *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament* (Jn 9:1–41, p. 459): the words constitute "a solemn warning to tell the truth… and to make a confession, with the implication that the person so exhorted has done something wrong," and echo Josh 7:19. Godet (*Commentary on the Gospel of St John* 1:476) — "derived from Josh. 7:19." Ryle (*Expository Thoughts on John* 2:152) lists Calvin, Chemnitz, Beza, Alford and Trench as taking it as a solemn adjuration parallel to Joshua's words. **A dissent worth knowing:** Hendriksen (*John* 2:87) notes that "one can glorify God in more ways than one" and resists making the Achan parallel exclusive; the *Believer's Bible Commentary* (p. 1521) allows a second sense (give God, not Jesus, the credit for the miracle). The oath reading is the majority and the better one, but it is not unanimous. *High confidence in the allusion; moderate that it excludes the "give God the credit, not Jesus" overtone — quite possibly both are meant, which would be characteristically Johannine.*

*What it adds.* The court is not asking for doxology. It is administering an oath to a man it has already convicted, and inviting him to save himself by incriminating his healer. The Achan background makes the scene lethal rather than merely officious. And the contrast is exact and preachable: Achan gave glory to God by confessing a theft; **this man has been giving glory to God from v.11 onwards by refusing to say anything but the truth**, and it is the truth that gets him expelled.

**Genesis 2:7 (with Job 10:9; Isaiah 64:8) → John 9:6** *(moderate)* `[I]`

*Source context.* God forms the man from the dust of the ground and breathes into him. *Verbal caution, stated plainly:* LXX Genesis 2:7 has χοῦν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς, **not** πηλός. The verbal case for a direct Gen 2:7 echo is therefore weak. The conceptual case is stronger because πηλός is precisely the word for God's forming of humans elsewhere — Job 10:9 (ὡς πηλόν) and Isaiah 64:8 (the potter and the clay).

*Book usage (Move 2).* Genesis creation is a confirmed live source, bracketing the Gospel: 1:1–5 (Gen 1:1–5) and 20:22 (ἐμφυσάω, the LXX verb of Gen 2:7 — the only NT use), with the κῆπος cluster at 18:1; 19:41; 20:15. `[S: overview]` A creation-act at the Gospel's midpoint sits inside a bracket the book itself has built. *Moderate–high on the bracket; moderate on this instance.*

*What it adds.* The defect is **congenital**. Jesus is not restoring lost sight; he is supplying what was never there, and v.32 says so ("never since the world began"). Read against the Genesis bracket, the mud is not medicine but material: the Word by whom all things were made (1:3) finishing a piece of creation. `[I]`, *moderate*. (The sweep reaches the same conclusion independently `[S: sweep]`.)

**Ezekiel 18 / Jeremiah 31:29–30 → John 9:2** *(moderate, conceptual — Move 1 only)* `[I]`

*Source context.* Both prophets explicitly repudiate the sour-grapes proverb: "the soul who sins shall die… the son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father" (Ezek 18:4, 20); "everyone shall die for his own iniquity" (Jer 31:30, inside the new-covenant oracle). *Book usage:* not applicable. *OT-to-OT:* no pairing in this passage.

*What it adds, with the necessary honesty.* The disciples are not inventing their question — Exodus 20:5 does speak of visiting iniquity to the third and fourth generation, and the canon holds a real tension that the prophets adjudicate. Jesus does not resolve the tension in the abstract; he **refuses both horns in this case** ("neither this man nor his parents"), which is narrower and more useful than a general doctrine. The preacher should say so: v.3 is a ruling on one man, not a theory of suffering.

### 12. Genre

Gospel narrative; specifically a **sign-narrative that expands into a forensic interrogation cycle and closes with a judicial pronouncement**. The interrogation follows a recognisable juridical shape: summons, examination, re-examination, examination of the witness's family, adjuration under oath, verdict, expulsion.

Reading rules. (a) Signs in John are *exhibits entered into evidence*, not proofs — 20:30–31 defines them as a selection aimed at belief, and the Gospel itself shows sign-fed crowds defecting (6:26, 66). `[S: overview]` (b) The sub-genre means the chapter should be preached with its procedural rhythm intact; flattening the four examinations into "the Pharisees questioned him" destroys the form that carries the meaning. (c) The chapter is dramatic in structure — seven scenes, two parties at a time — and reads aloud extremely well. That is a homiletical asset.

### 13. Copycat

| Character | Category | Note |
|-----------|----------|------|
| **Jesus** | Unique | v.32 says so explicitly: unprecedented since the world began. Not a method to copy |
| **The disciples** | Negative example | The question of v.2 is the wrong question, asked about a man standing in front of them. It is the chapter's first mistake and the most familiar one |
| **The man** | Normative pattern — *with a specific qualification* | See below |
| **The parents** | Negative example, drawn with sympathy | The narrator explains rather than condemns (v.22). Preach them as a warning, not a target |
| **The Pharisees** | Negative example | But see Preaching Pitfalls: the congregation's instinct to file them under "other people" is what v.41 forbids |

**The qualification on the man.** The sweep says he "**is** a normative pattern… His courage under interrogation is the model John holds up for a community facing expulsion" `[S: sweep]`, and the section dig adds that his boldness is "response to grace, not native courage" `[S: 7–10 dig]`. Both are right, but the overview's Trap 8 puts the sharper point: the man "has no credentials **and no confidence**: he begins 'I do not know' (9:12, 25) and ends at worship." `[S: overview]` **There is a mild tension here between the sweep and the overview, and it should be resolved in the overview's favour**: what is imitable is not his nerve but his refusal to claim more than he knows. His testimony is exactly as long as his knowledge and not a word longer. A sermon that preaches "be bold like the blind man" has imported a virtue the text does not name; a sermon that preaches "say what you actually know, and keep saying it" has preached what is there.

**Manner-of-the-act check.** Jesus initiates entirely. The man does not ask (contrast 5:6, "do you want to be healed?"), does not believe first, does not know who healed him even after he can see (v.12), and does not seek Jesus out afterwards — **Jesus finds him** (v.35). Sight precedes faith throughout; faith never produces sight. `[T]` The application posture is therefore receptive, and reversing the order — "believe, and you will see" — inverts the chapter.

---

### 14. Bible Timeline

Standard NT location: within the narrative, pre-cross and pre-Pentecost; the reader stands after both. Two features deserve more than the standard note.

**The narrator's world intrudes into the story's world at v.22.** The expulsion policy is reported as already agreed, and it is the readers' policy as much as the characters'. `[T]` The reader is not looking back at a settled past; they are reading their own present in narrative form. This is unusual even for John and it is why the chapter lands as pastoral rather than historical.

**The judgement of v.39 is inaugurated, not final.** "Now is the judgement of this world" (12:31) is proleptic, and 12:48 defers the last word to the last day. So "your guilt remains" (v.41) is a present verdict on a present posture, not a sentence beyond appeal — a distinction the preacher must hold, both to keep the warning sharp and to keep it from becoming despair.

---

### 15. Who Am I?

The chapter is unusual in that the reader's identification **moves**, and the movement is the point.

- **We begin as the disciples** — looking at another person's suffering and turning it into a theological question about causes. `[I]`, *high*. Most hearers arrive here.
- **We pass through the parents** — knowing something true about Jesus and calculating what saying it will cost. `[I]`, *high*. This is the chapter's most probable resting place for a comfortable congregation, and the narrator's sympathy makes it safe to name.
- **We become the man born blind only at the point of "I do not know"** — and only if we are prepared to be put out. `[T]`/`[I]`
- **We are addressed as the Pharisees whenever we are certain we can see.** `[T]` — v.41 makes this unavoidable, and the overview's Trap 8 is right that the congregation's default identification with the blind man is precisely the position the chapter condemns.
- **Jesus is not an example here at all.** He is the light who gives sight, the sender who is himself Sent, and the one who goes looking for the man who has been thrown out. Christ is the subject of the chapter's verbs, not its model.

---

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** Believe into the Son of Man (v.35–38); go on saying what you know of him when saying it costs you something (vv.25, 27, 33); and stop claiming to see (v.41). The narrator's aside at v.22 tells us the middle of these is the pressing one for the first readers.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

*Worldview.* (a) Affliction is not a ledger to be read backwards. Verse 3 forbids the diagnostic question in this case, and the disciples' instinct — to explain someone's suffering by someone's sin — is named as a mistake before anything else happens in the chapter. (b) Sight is given, not achieved; the man contributes obedience to an instruction he does not understand. (c) Certainty about one's own sight is the one disqualifying condition the chapter names.

*Behaviour — stop.* Stop reading other people's suffering as a moral diagnosis. Stop saying "we know" about Jesus on the strength of a system rather than an encounter. Stop — if you have any authority in a church — casting people out for confessing more than your framework allows.

*Behaviour — start.* Say what you do know, and no more (v.25 is a model of testimony precisely because it is so short). Expect to be found: the man does not go looking for Jesus after his expulsion; Jesus goes looking for him.

*Motivation, not moralism.* The ground of all of this is v.35: **"Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him…"** The obedience the chapter asks for rests on the fact that the one who opens eyes also comes looking when the cost falls due. That is the gospel motive here, and it is what keeps "confess him whatever it costs" from being a demand for nerve.

**Four audiences.**
- *For me.* What am I certain I can see? Where is my "we know" doing the work my "I do not know" ought to be doing?
- *For a Christian friend.* The one who has lost a church, a family, or a standing for confessing Christ is the one this chapter was written for: Jesus heard that they had cast him out.
- *For the church community.* We are capable of being the tribunal. A church that requires people to say more than they know, or that expels those who say too much, is in the position of vv.24–34 — and note that the tribunal's motive at v.24 is presented as religious ("give glory to God").
- *For an unbeliever.* The chapter's honest offer is not a system to accept but a question to answer: "Do you believe in the Son of Man?" — and the man's reply ("who is he, sir, that I may believe in him?") is a legitimate place to stand.

**Prayer.** *Lord, we did not choose the dark and we cannot choose the light; you found us in it. Keep us from the certainty that blinds. Give us enough courage to say only what is true, and let us be found by you when it costs us. You have opened our eyes: Lord, we believe.*

### Textual Variants

`[revised: logos-research]` — checked against the Logos Exegetical Guide and *The Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible* (LTNB) at John 9:35–41. LTNB records **no variant note anywhere in 9:1–34**, so the interrogation narrative is textually quiet. Two variants matter, and both fall in the closing scene.

#### 9:35 — "Son of Man" or "Son of God"?

- **The issue:** τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου (ESV, NASB, critical text) against τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ θεοῦ (KJV, Byzantine).
- **Manuscript evidence:** LTNB — "Most early manuscripts have 'Son of Man,' but at least one early manuscript and later witnesses related to it have 'Son of God.'"
- **Most likely original:** "Son of Man." LTNB: "Many textual critics (**Metzger, Comfort, Omanson**) consider it to be less likely for scribes to change 'Son of God' into 'Son of Man,' thus see 'Man' as the more likely reading. Others (e.g. **Alford**) prefer the reading 'Son of God.'"
- **Preaching implication:** preach the Johannine "Son of Man," and note that it matters — the Son of Man is the Danielic figure to whom judgement is given (Dan 7:13–14; cf. John 5:27), which is exactly the title v.39's verdict requires. A congregation with a KJV will hear "Son of God"; a one-line note is enough.
- **Confidence:** *high*, and now attributable to named critics rather than to this report's reasoning. (The section dig reached the same verdict independently `[S: 7–10 dig]`.)

#### 9:38–39a — is the confession and the worship original?

- **The issue:** LTNB — "**A few early manuscripts omit all of John 9:38 and the beginning of verse 39, 'And Jesus said.'**" That is: ὁ δὲ ἔφη· Πιστεύω, κύριε· καὶ προσεκύνησεν αὐτῷ — the whole of "He said, 'Lord, I believe,' and he worshipped him."
- **Most likely original:** genuinely contested. The omission has early support and the shorter reading is normally preferred; on the other hand, an accidental omission by homoeoteleuton is easy to posit here, and the sentence is not the sort scribes usually add. **I offer no verdict.** *Uncertain — consider verifying* against Metzger's *Textual Commentary* and the NA28 apparatus before a sermon rests weight on it.
- **Preaching implication — and it is real.** Two findings in this report lean on v.38: the "only worship of Jesus in the Gospel" observation (Tool 7) and the 4:23–24 → 9:38 internal echo. Both should be stated with the variant acknowledged, or stated more lightly. Note also that the man's *confession* survives in every reading by way of v.36–37 and the chapter's whole trajectory; what is at risk is the word "worshipped," not the belief.
- **Confidence:** the existence of the variant is *high* (LTNB, checked); the verdict is *open*.
- **Note for the record:** **none of the prior John reports flags this variant**, and the section dig and the sweep both cite 9:38 as a load-bearing datum. This is the most useful correction this run produces.

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time period:** within the narrative, the Feast of Booths (7:2) and the days following, before the Dedication (10:22) — so autumn. The Gospel's own composition setting is not stated on the page.
- **Location:** Jerusalem. The Pool of Siloam sits at the southern tip of the City of David, fed from the Gihon spring through Hezekiah's tunnel (2 Kgs 20:20; 2 Chr 32:30). The first-century stepped pool was uncovered in 2005 by E. Shukron and R. Reich (*Expositor's Bible Commentary*, rev. edn, 4:485, at Neh 3:14) `[S: logos]` — worth knowing, because until then the site shown to pilgrims was the smaller Byzantine pool. *High confidence on the excavation; the older commentaries in the library predate it.*
- **Political/religious context:** Pharisaic authority over synagogue membership; the Sabbath as the live legal flashpoint (as in ch. 5).

#### What the original audience knew

1. **Siloam was where the feast's water came from.** The Booths water-libation — a daily procession drawing water from Siloam and pouring it at the altar, peaking on the last great day — is the backdrop to 7:37, and it makes 9:7 a festal act rather than an arbitrary errand. **Flag: the rite is described in the Mishnah (Sukkah 4–5), a later source; first-century detail is `[S]`, *moderate confidence*.** `[S: 7–10 dig]` concurs.
2. **The legal offence was the mud, not the healing.** The narrator specifies "when Jesus **made the mud**" (v.14). Kneading is among the prohibited Sabbath labours in the later rabbinic list (m. Shabbat 7:2), and anointing eyes on the Sabbath was disputed. `[S]`, *moderate — later material, cite with care.* But the narrator's own emphasis is `[T]`: he does not say "when Jesus healed him."
3. **Bethesda and Siloam were the two great pools of Jerusalem**, and John narrates a Sabbath healing at each (5:1–9; 9:1–7). Urban von Wahlde notes that they were the two largest *miqvaʾot* for the city (*The Gospel and Letters of John* 2:424) `[S: logos]` — which, if right, adds a purification register to both. *Moderate; the source's wider reconstruction is source-critical and need not be adopted.*
4. **Expulsion from the synagogue was social, not merely religious.** The synagogue was the centre of community life; exclusion cut a person off from a web of relationships, trade and welfare, not from a weekly meeting. *High confidence on the general point.*
5. **Begging was the ordinary economic outcome of congenital blindness**, which is why v.8 identifies him by his pitch rather than his name. He is never named. `[T]`

#### On the *Birkat ha-Minim* reconstruction

The standard move is to read 9:22 as reflecting a formal late-first-century expulsion decree and to date the Gospel accordingly. The overview's ruling — **keep the pressure, drop the dating** — is right, and it now has support: C. K. Barrett notes that the reference is to "marking out Jewish Christians and excluding them from the synagogue community," and that "it hardly amounts to the 'cursing' mentioned in Justin, *Trypho* 16, which is probably the result of a sharpening of feeling between Jews and Christians in the province of Asia" at a later stage (*The Gospel according to St John*, p. 353). `[S: logos]` The text shows a policy and a fear; it does not name a decree or a liturgy. `[T]` for the pressure; `[S]` and disputed for the setting.

#### What this changes about how we read

- The errand to Siloam is not incidental logistics but a festal and Christological signpost — and the man is sent to the very water the feast had just been pouring out.
- The court's outrage is procedurally specific. They are not shocked that a man can see; they are prosecuting a kneading offence. That is exactly the kind of detail that makes vv.24–34 feel true rather than cartoonish, and it should be given to the congregation.

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section:** New Testament Gospel, read by those who know the whole Hebrew canon.
- **What the canonical reader knows:** Isaiah's blind-eyes promises and blind-servant indictment; the Shiloah of Isaiah 8; Moses at Sinai denied the face of God; Joshua's charge to Achan; the prophets' repudiation of inherited guilt; the Sabbath law.
- **Canonical register:** Isaiah above all. A reader with Isaiah in their ears hears 9:5–7 and 9:39 as one Isaianic sentence.

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were:** readers deeply literate in the Scriptures but not at home in Palestinian Aramaic or Jerusalem topography — which is why John translates Siloam and not Bethsaida. `[S: overview]` / `[T]`
- **Their situation:** a community for whom confessing Jesus as Christ carried the concrete penalty the narrator names at v.22.
- **What they brought:** the assumption that religious authority and access to God ran through the synagogue; the retributive instinct the disciples voice at v.2; and, for Jewish believers, an actual family with an actual opinion about what they had done.

#### Where the passage fits their world

They would have recognised, immediately and painfully, that they were watching their own story: a person who says the true thing about Jesus and loses their place for it, and a family who will not back them up. They would also have heard the adjuration of v.24 as a courtroom formula, not a pious phrase.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking:** that a beggar out-argues the theologians, and that his argument uses their own major premise. And that Jesus flatly refuses the sin-question at v.3, when every available theology said otherwise.
- **Surprising:** that the man's parents evade, and that the narrator lets them off with an explanation rather than a rebuke.
- **Comforting:** v.35 — the only line in the chapter that shows the initiative running the other way. *He heard that they had cast him out, and he found him.* For a reader who has been expelled, this is the sentence.
- **Disturbing:** v.41. The chapter refuses to let the reader occupy the position of the man born blind by right.

#### What we bring that they didn't

- A therapeutic reading of the miracle ("what is my blindness?") that turns a historical sign into a metaphor for personal growth.
- Individualism, which makes the expulsion sound like a bruised friendship rather than the loss of a world.
- A modern disability discourse which — rightly sensitive — can make v.3 sound like instrumentalisation, and which the preacher must meet head-on rather than avoid.
- Two millennia of Christian anti-Judaism, which makes "the Jews" in vv.18 and 22 a phrase that cannot be read out without comment.

Recovering the reception moves the sermon's centre of gravity from the miracle to the deposition, and from courage to honesty. It also identifies the congregation's likeliest seat: not the blind man and not the Pharisees, but the parents.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

#### 1. Light and sight — creation, Servant, judgement

- **Where this passage sits:** at the junction of two trajectories that the OT had already joined. Creation light (Gen 1:3) is the first thing God makes; the Servant is given "as a light for the nations, to open the eyes that are blind" (Isa 42:6–7); and blindness is simultaneously the judgement Isaiah is commissioned to inflict (Isa 6:9–10).
- **Before:** Genesis 1; Isaiah 6, 8–9, 35, 42.
- **After:** John 12:40 (Isa 6:10 cited as the verdict); 12:46; and finally the city that needs no sun because the Lamb is its lamp, where "night will be no more" (Rev 21:23; 22:5).
- **What it contributes:** the chapter is not about the eyes of one man. It is the point in the Gospel where the Servant's commission and Isaiah's verdict are performed simultaneously on the same stage — which is why v.39 can state both in one sentence.
- **Confidence:** *high* on the theme; *moderate–high* on the specific Isaiah 42 linkage.

#### 2. Sin, birth and the repudiation of the ledger

- **Where this passage sits:** on the far side of Ezekiel 18 and Jeremiah 31, and inside John's own "born from above" argument (3:3–8).
- **Before:** Exodus 20:5 (visiting iniquity) held in tension with Ezekiel 18:20 and Jeremiah 31:30 (each dies for his own sin).
- **After:** the Gospel's answer is not a better theodicy but a new birth — and the chapter's opponents, who deploy birth-guilt as a weapon (v.34), are the ones Jesus has told must be born again (3:3).
- **What it contributes:** it keeps v.3 from becoming a general doctrine. Jesus is not resolving the problem of suffering; he is refusing a diagnosis and issuing a commission.
- **Confidence:** *high*.

#### 3. Gathering and expulsion (opening into ch. 10)

- **Where this passage sits:** as the crisis that Ezekiel 34's shepherd oracle answers. Shepherds who scatter the flock are indicted; the LORD says "I myself will search for my sheep… I will rescue them" (Ezek 34:11–12).
- **After:** 10:1–18 immediately; then 10:16 ("other sheep… one flock, one shepherd"), 11:52 ("gather into one"), and 17:21.
- **What it contributes:** v.35 is not merely a kind postscript. It is the first act of the shepherd whose discourse begins six verses later.
- **Confidence:** *high* (already established `[S: 7–10 dig]` / `[S: overview]`).

**Confluence: (c) climactic convergence.** These are not three parallel tracks. The light who gives sight is the one who refuses the ledger and then goes out after the man the ledger has condemned. Isaiah 42's Servant-light, Ezekiel 34's searching shepherd, and John's own new-birth argument meet in a single sentence: *Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him…* (v.35). That convergence is the preaching gold of the chapter.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection.** A NT passage in which Christ is explicit; the OT work is **fulfilment** (the Isaianic Servant's commission to open blind eyes and be a light) and **enacted claim** (8:12 → 9:5–7). Typology is thin here because Christ is the actor, not the antitype.

**How the passage presents him.**

- **The light of the world** — a title claimed twice in two chapters and then demonstrated on the one category of person for whom light has never existed.
- **The Sent One** — and the pool is named after him before he arrives. John's gloss at v.7 makes the errand a small parable of the whole Christology: the man is healed by going to "Sent" and washing.
- **The Son of Man** — the Danielic figure to whom judgement is given (Dan 7:13–14; John 5:27), which is why the confession of v.38 and the verdict of v.39 belong in the same breath.
- **The one who is himself seen** — v.37, standing between the Prologue's "no one has ever seen God" and the Farewell's "whoever has seen me has seen the Father."
- **The one who goes and finds** — v.35, the chapter's decisive verb, and the one on which any Christ-centred sermon here should turn.

**Trajectory.** What the chapter anticipates: the shepherd who gathers (10:1–18), the blinding verdict given its scriptural warrant (12:40), and the night that is coming (v.4 → 13:30). What remains not-yet: v.39's judgement is inaugurated; the last day still holds the last word (12:48).

**Moralism check.**

- *The "be like X" temptation here* is unusually strong and unusually specific: **be brave like the blind man**. It is strong because the man really is admirable and really does grow, and because the chapter's comedy invites the congregation to enjoy his cheek.
- *Why it fails.* He does nothing brave in the ordinary sense. He tells the truth, twice says he does not know, and is thrown out. His confession is produced *by* being found, not by resolve — and it ends in worship, which is the posture of someone who has been acted upon.
- *The gospel grounding.* Sight precedes faith at every point in the chapter; Jesus initiates at v.1 and again at v.35. The imperative the chapter supports is not "be courageous" but "say what you know, and be found."
- *Christ as hero.* The verbs that matter are all his: he *saw* (v.1), he *made* (v.6), he *sent* (v.7), he *heard* (v.35), he *found* (v.35), he *came for judgment* (v.39).

**Confidence:** *high* on the fulfilment reading; *moderate–high* on Isaiah 42 as the specific frame.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 9:2–3 — "who sinned…?" / "that the works of God might be displayed in him"

- **Category:** pastoral and doctrinal, with an apologetic edge. **The hardest verse in the chapter, by a distance.**
- **The difficulty:** on the standard punctuation, God appears to have made a man blind for forty-odd years in order to stage a miracle. Every disabled person in the congregation will hear that, and so will their family.
- **Common mishandlings:** (a) "God made him blind just to do a miracle" — stated cheerfully; (b) the opposite over-correction, which so softens v.3 that the sovereignty language disappears; (c) retreating into "we can't know."
- **Rhetorical function in context:** v.3 is a *refusal*, and its work in the passage is to clear the ground for v.4's commission. Jesus does not answer the question asked; he replaces it. Whatever else is decided, the verse's function is to move from diagnosis to work.
- **The main interpretive options:**
  1. **Purpose (standard punctuation).** The blindness was ordained so that God's works might be displayed. Requires, and can bear, a robust doctrine of God's sovereign purposing of hard providences.
  2. **Elliptical ἀλλ' ἵνα, running forward to v.4.** "Neither this man sinned nor his parents. But in order that the works of God might be displayed in him, we must work the works of him who sent me…" Grammatically available (John uses elliptical ἀλλ' ἵνα at 1:8; 13:18; 15:25), printed by no major English version, and much more comfortable theologically — which is itself a reason for suspicion.
  3. **Purpose without retrospective causation** — reading ἵνα as marking the outcome God intends *now* rather than the reason he acted *then*. A middle path, and probably where most careful preachers land.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** the Greek permits more than one punctuation and cannot settle the question by itself; the structural parallel with Exodus 4:11–12 (cause relocated in God, followed immediately by *now go and work*) mildly favours reading v.3 as clearing the ground for v.4 rather than as a theory of the blindness. `[I]`, *moderate*. **Logos check:** a Study Assistant query on this crux returned only general reasoning with no library citations, so the report is not able to name which contemporary scholars hold which position. *Flag as an Open Question.*
- **Suggested handling:** address it directly and early; state that the question the disciples asked is the one Jesus refuses; be honest that the punctuation is contested; and land on v.4 rather than on a theodicy. Say plainly that the chapter does not present the man's blindness as a punishment and does not offer his forty years as a lesson to anyone else about theirs.

#### 9:22 — the synagogue expulsion

- **Category:** apologetic / historical.
- **The difficulty:** a formal policy of excommunication for confessing Jesus as Christ during Jesus's own ministry is historically awkward, which is why the *Birkat ha-Minim* hypothesis exists and why many treat v.22 as the narrator's own setting projected back.
- **Handling:** the overview's ruling is right and now has Barrett behind it (see Historical Background) — keep the pressure, drop the dating. The preacher does not need to adjudicate; a sentence acknowledging that the narrator is telling us something about his readers' world as well as the story's is enough, and it is exegetically useful rather than defensive.

#### 9:31 — "God does not listen to sinners"

- **Category:** doctrinal; commonly misused.
- **The difficulty:** the sentence is flatly at odds with the tax collector of Luke 18:13 and with the gospel — and it is regularly quoted from pulpits as though it were a Johannine doctrine.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** these are **the healed man's words, not the narrator's and not Jesus's**, and he is deploying his opponents' own theology *ad hominem* to win an argument. The narrator does not endorse it. `[T]`, *high*.
- **Handling:** if the verse is read aloud, say whose it is. The chapter's own counter-example is the man himself.

#### 9:39–41 — judicial blinding

- **Category:** doctrinal (election, hardening).
- **The difficulty:** "those who see may become blind" reads as divine hardening, and v.41 as culpability — in consecutive sentences.
- **Handling:** hold both without resolving. The text does. Note that 12:40 will cite Isaiah 6:10 in the divine-agency direction and 12:42–43 will immediately give a human-agency explanation ("they loved the glory that comes from man"), so John himself puts the two side by side without arbitration. Do not use this passage to settle a systematic debate; the chapter will not carry it.

#### The pastoral landmine: preaching this chapter with blind or disabled people present

- Assume there are. The chapter uses blindness as a metaphor for culpable unbelief in vv.39–41 while narrating a real blind man in vv.1–38, and the slide from one to the other is easy and wounding.
- Two disciplines. First, be explicit that v.3 denies the diagnostic reading of *this* man's blindness, and do not then reintroduce it by implication. Second, when moving to the metaphor, mark the move: the man born blind is the chapter's model of sight, not its illustration of sin. Verse 41 is addressed to people with functioning eyes.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Using the chapter to flatter the congregation

- **What it looks like:** "The experts missed it; the ordinary believer saw. Who are the Pharisees in our day?" — with the hearers comfortably cast as the man born blind.
- **Why it's wrong:** v.41 makes the *claim to see* the aggravating factor. Any group that hears this chapter and concludes that it can see has walked into the position the chapter condemns. And the man's qualification for seeing is not insight but the two words "I do not know."
- **The corrective:** aim the irony at the hearer, not past them. The question is not "who are the Pharisees today?" but "what am I certain I can see?" (The overview names this as Trap 8; it exists because of this chapter.)

### Pitfall 2: Preaching "be brave like the blind man"

- **What it looks like:** a courage sermon. Stand firm under pressure; don't cave like the parents.
- **Why it's wrong:** the man's boldness is a *product* of the sign, not a precondition of it. He is found, not resolute; he ends worshipping, not standing tall. And the chapter's own model of testimony is a man saying less than he is asked to say, not more.
- **The corrective:** preach honesty rather than nerve — "say what you actually know, and keep saying it" — and ground the call in v.35, where Jesus goes looking for the one who paid the price.

### Pitfall 3: Instrumentalising the man's blindness at v.3

- **What it looks like:** "God made him blind so he could be healed and God could be glorified," delivered briskly, with no acknowledgement that this is contested or that it is agonising.
- **Why it's wrong:** the punctuation is genuinely disputed; the verse's function in context is to refuse the diagnostic question and commission work; and the sentence as usually preached says more than the passage will bear about anyone else's suffering.
- **The corrective:** name the crux, land on v.4, and state explicitly that the chapter forbids reading another person's affliction as a verdict.

### Pitfall 4: Preaching chapter 9 without chapter 10

- **What it looks like:** the sermon ends at v.41 with a warning about spiritual pride, and the shepherd discourse is a separate week with no connection made.
- **Why it's wrong:** 10:1 continues the same speech to the same audience with no break of time, place or hearer; 10:21 refers back to "the eyes of the blind." The false shepherds have just thrown a sheep out, and Jesus responds by describing the shepherd who leads his own out and dies for them.
- **The corrective:** at minimum, read to 10:21 in the service, or close the sermon by pointing over the chapter break. The expulsion of v.34 has an answer, and it is six verses away.

### Pitfall 5: Reading "the Jews" flat at vv.18 and 22

- **What it looks like:** letting the phrase stand unexplained, so that the congregation hears an ethnic villain.
- **Why it's wrong:** the referent in this chapter is the Judean authorities conducting the examination — the same body called "the Pharisees" at vv.13, 15, 16, 40 — not the people. The man, his parents, and Jesus are all Jews. And 4:22 stands: "salvation is from the Jews."
- **The corrective:** translate by context from the pulpit ("the authorities"), and say once, clearly, why. This trap has the worst history attached to it and addressing it is not optional.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **The v.3 punctuation.** The single most consequential open question in the chapter. A Logos Study Assistant query returned no library citations, so I cannot name who holds what. **Worth a targeted commentary check** — Carson (PNTC), Michaels (NICNT) and Keener would each be worth reading before the sermon; the NET Bible translator note on 9:3 is the quickest route.
2. **The 9:38–39a omission.** Verdict genuinely open; check Metzger's *Textual Commentary* and the NA28 apparatus. Two findings in this report (the *proskyneō* observation and the 4:23–24 echo) rest on it.
3. **The γεννάω count.** This report finds five English "born"s (vv.2, 19, 20, 32, 34); the sweep records a verified Greek count of four (omitting v.32). One of the two needs correcting. *Machine-checkable against the SBLGNT.*
4. **The Greek counts in Tool 10** — τυφλός, οἶδα, ἀνοίγω, ἁμαρτ- — are English-derived. Confirmable in minutes with a Greek text; nothing in the argument turns on the exact figures, but the report should not present them as verified.
5. **Is 9:8–34 really the Gospel's longest Jesus-absent stretch?** Claimed at *moderate*; the rival is 18:15–27. Checkable.
6. **ἐγώ εἰμι at 9:9.** The minority reading (Brodie; Shafer 2005; the Concordia commentary) is named but not assessed. If the preacher is drawn to it, read Shafer before using it.
7. **The Isaiah 8:6 topography.** The name-link is certain; the identification of Isaiah's channel with John's pool is disputed (*ZEB* 5:480). Do not stake weight on the physical identity of the two.
8. **ἀποσυνάγωγος as a Johannine coinage.** Asserted at *moderate*; a BDAG entry would settle whether the word is otherwise attested.

## Book-Overview Tensions

The overview is confirmed at every load-bearing point — the forensic frame, the presenting situation, the sending-Christology, Trap 8, the 9/10 seam, the *aposynagōgos* thread. Four items are **extensions** rather than tensions, and one is a genuine correction.

**Extensions — recommend adding to the overview:**

1. **9:37 belongs in the seeing-God chain.** The Prologue dig records 1:18 → 5:37 → 6:46 → 14:9. Add 9:37 (ἑώρακας) as the narrated hinge — the only place in the Gospel where Jesus tells a person "you have seen him," said to a man born blind.
2. **5:45–47 → 9:28–29 belongs in the echo table.** "We are disciples of Moses" is the boast that 5:45's "there is one who accuses you: Moses" has already answered. Verified verbally and thematically, and absent from the overview, the sweep and the section dig.
3. **8:41 → 9:34 belongs in the echo table.** "We were not born of sexual immorality" → "you were born in utter sin." The birth-guilt slur is deflected in ch. 8 and thrown in ch. 9.
4. **Addressee-differentiation refinement.** The overview lists the man born blind (9:38) as a resolution of 1:12. John 9 appears to be the only pericope in which *both* sides of 1:11–12 resolve in a single scene — 9:38 for the one who received, 9:41 for those who did not. *Moderate–high.*

**Correction — to the sweep, not the overview:**

5. **The sweep's γεννάω count for John 9 (4×, at vv.2, 19, 20, 34) appears to omit 9:32's γεγεννημένου.** Since the overview cites the ch.3/ch.9 γεννάω thread as a verified finding, the count should be re-run before it is quoted again. The *thread* is unaffected; the *figure* may not be.

**A tension between two prior reports, surfaced not resolved:**

6. **The sweep says the man "is a normative pattern… his courage under interrogation is the model John holds up"; the overview's Trap 8 says he "has no credentials and no confidence."** Both are defensible readings of the same character, but they will produce different sermons. This report sides with the overview and records the disagreement rather than silently harmonising it.
