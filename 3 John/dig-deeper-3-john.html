The Passage

1 The elder to the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth.

2 Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul. 3 For I rejoiced greatly when the brothers came and testified to your truth, as indeed you are walking in the truth. 4 I have no greater joy than to hear that my children are walking in the truth.

5 Beloved, it is a faithful thing you do in all your efforts for these brothers, strangers as they are, 6 who testified to your love before the church. You will do well to send them on their journey in a manner worthy of God. 7 For they have gone out for the sake of the name, accepting nothing from the Gentiles. 8 Therefore we ought to support people like these, that we may be fellow workers for the truth.

9 I have written something to the church, but Diotrephes, who likes to put himself first, does not acknowledge our authority. 10 So if I come, I will bring up what he is doing, talking wicked nonsense against us. And not content with that, he refuses to welcome the brothers, and also stops those who want to and puts them out of the church.

11 Beloved, do not imitate evil but imitate good. Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God. 12 Demetrius has received a good testimony from everyone, and from the truth itself. We also add our testimony, and you know that our testimony is true.

13 I had much to write to you, but I would rather not write with pen and ink. 14 I hope to see you soon, and we will talk face to face.

15 Peace be to you. The friends greet you. Greet the friends, each by name.

ESV footnote at v. 3: “brothers” — Greek adelphoi, which may refer to brothers and sisters; also vv. 5, 10. Versification note: the KJV runs vv. 14–15 together as a single v. 14, so the KJV has fourteen verses to the ESV’s fifteen.

Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

  1. The elder does not apply 2 John 10 to Diotrephes — and the restraint is the point. [T] Surfaced by: Phase 0.6, Tool 9 (Tone), Tool 4 (the mood of v. 10), Difficult Verses. The man who wrote “do not receive him into a house, and do not say ‘rejoice’ to him” is here confronted by a man who has refused to receive him — and his entire response is “if I come (ἐὰν ἔλθω, a contingent third-class condition), I will call attention to (ὑπομνήσω, ‘remind’) what he is doing.” No excommunication. No “antichrist.” No application of his own rule. Because Diotrephes has failed no christological test, and the rule’s author respects the rule’s scope. This is the strongest single answer in Scripture to the weaponisation of 2 John 10–11.

  2. 3 John is the only book in the New Testament that never names Jesus or Christ — and it calls him “the Name.” [T] Surfaced by: Tool 7 (proper-noun inventory and pattern-break absence), Tool 11, Christological Reading. ὄνομα occurs twice: the brothers “have gone out for the sake of the Name” (v. 7, absolute, unnamed), and the letter’s final clause is “greet the friends, each by name” (v. 15). The Name too well known to need saying, and the friends each worth naming — the letter’s first and last theological words. High confidence on the data.

  3. κατ’ ὄνομα occurs in only two places in the Greek New Testament: John 10:3 and 3 John 15. [T]/[S: logos] Surfaced by: Tool 11 (independent candidate-finding, then the full triad), Tool 7, Tool 16. In John 10:3 the Good Shepherd “calls his own sheep by name”; in 3 John 15 Gaius is told to greet the friends by name. Robertson makes the connection explicitly. The letter’s last instruction puts Gaius in the shepherd’s posture — set directly against Diotrephes, who casts out (ἐκβάλλει, v. 10). Moderate–high confidence.

  4. The λαμβάνω hinge at vv. 7–8 answers 2 John 10 across the diptych. [T]/[S: logos] verified Surfaced by: Tool 4 (the οὖν), Tool 7, Tool 11 Move 4, Phase 0.6. The missionaries take (λαμβάνοντες) nothing from the Gentiles (v. 7); therefore (οὖν) we ought to take them up (ὑπολαμβάνειν) (v. 8). And that is the same root 2 John 10 negated: μὴ λαμβάνετε. Five λαμβάνω forms run across the two letters (2Jn 4, 8, 10; 3Jn 7, 8), and every one of them is about who takes what from whom. This resolves Open Question 5 of the companion 2 John report at high confidence.

  5. Verse 6 is a polite request, not a commendation. [S: logos] Surfaced by: Tool 4, Tool 8, Tool 12 (genre), Original Languages. καλῶς ποιήσεις + aorist participle is “the normal way of saying ‘please’ in the papyri” and functions as “a gentle directive” (Yarbrough, BECNT, 371). The ESV’s “You will do well to send them on their journey” reads as praise for something done; the Greek is “please send them on.” The letter’s central practical ask has been hiding in plain sight as a compliment. High confidence.

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 2 John immediately precedes, and it left two things behind.

(i) A rule that is entirely negative. 2 John 10 rules on the traveller who does not bring the teaching: do not receive him, do not greet him. It says nothing whatever about the traveller who does. A congregation obeying 2 John alone has a fully specified procedure for refusal and none at all for reception. [T]

(ii) A vocabulary of taking. 2 John ran a λαμβάνω thread — we received a commandment (v. 4), you may receive a full reward (v. 8), do not receive him (v. 10) — and ended on the prohibition. [T]

Necessity answer. 3 John supplies the missing positive half, and supplies it in the same word. “They went out for the sake of the Name, taking (λαμβάνοντες) nothing from the Gentiles. Therefore we ought to take up (ὑπολαμβάνειν) such people” (vv. 7–8). [T]/[S: logos] verified The letter that follows a prohibition of receiving opens with an obligation to receive, and the obligation is stated as a compound of the prohibited verb. Read the two letters back to back and the second is not a change of subject; it is the completion of a sentence. High confidence.

Second layer — why the Diotrephes material sits here. 2 John’s rule, obeyed with the wrong object, produces exactly Diotrephes: a man who refuses to receive, and who puts out those who would. The letter exposing that counterfeit must follow the letter whose command it counterfeits, or the reader has no standard by which to see it as a counterfeit at all. And note what the text does not say: it lays no doctrinal charge against Diotrephes. His single named fault is φιλοπρωτεύων — he “likes to put himself first” (v. 9). Same behaviour, different motive, opposite verdict. [T]/[I] Moderate–high confidence; the canonical-order argument is an inference, not an authorial claim.

Third layer — the elder’s own restraint. The positional check surfaces something easy to miss. The elder had a rule for shutting doors, published one letter earlier. He does not use it here. Against a man who has refused the brothers, refused him, and expelled his supporters, his stated intention is to “call attention to” (ὑπομνήσω) the behaviour, in person, if he comes. The scope of 2 John 10 is being honoured by its own author. [T] See Headline Finding 1.

Implication. 3 John cannot be preached as a pleasant note about generosity that happens to contain a difficult man. Positionally it is the second ruling of a two-ruling book, and its function is to stop the first ruling from becoming a licence.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 (below) finds that the Johannine corpus explicitly cites Deuteronomy 19:15 at John 8:17, and that 3 John 12’s three-fold attestation of Demetrius meets that standard. That sharpens the positional answer: the elder is not merely asserting his authority against Diotrephes’ — he is putting Demetrius’s credentials on a legal footing Diotrephes cannot dismiss. The letter is doing church order, carefully, under pressure.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level (the two-letter book). The elder regulates the community’s welcome by truth and love; 3 John is the open-door panel. This confirms the overview [S: overview], and the text supports it independently: the letter’s load-bearing inference is the οὖν of v. 8 — therefore we ought to support such people — and everything before it builds the case and everything after it defends the case against the man obstructing it.

Letter-level. Three purposes, in descending order of prominence:

(a) To secure continued, concrete funding for the travelling mission. This is the practical business of the letter and it is stated as a request (v. 6, καλῶς ποιήσεις — “please send them on”) grounded in an obligation (v. 8, ὀφείλομεν — “we are under obligation”). The verb at v. 6 is προπέμπω, the technical term for a missionary send-off with provisions (cf. Rom 15:24; 1 Cor 16:6; Titus 3:13; Acts 15:3). [T]

(b) To credential Demetrius. Verse 12 is a formal letter of recommendation compressed into a sentence, with a legally sufficient number of witnesses (see Tool 11). Demetrius is very plausibly the letter’s bearer, in which case v. 12 is not a compliment appended to the letter but the letter’s own warrant. [I] Moderate confidence.

(c) To put Diotrephes on notice without acting against him. The elder tells Gaius what he will do, conditionally, in person. He does not instruct Gaius to do anything about Diotrephes at all. [T]

The letter’s most striking feature at book level. 3 John never names Jesus or Christ. In fifteen verses it says “God” three times (vv. 6, 11 ×2) and refers to Christ once, obliquely, as “the Name” (v. 7). This is unique in the New Testament. See Tool 7 (pattern-break absence) and the Christological Reading.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

See Phase 0.6 above. In summary: 2 John supplies a purely negative rule and a λαμβάνω vocabulary; 3 John supplies the positive obligation in a compound of the same verb (vv. 7–8), and exposes the over-correction the negative rule can license.

General contextual notes

Immediately before. 2 John — the closed-door panel. The two letters share an opening self-designation (“the elder”), a joy-report over children walking in the truth, and a near-identical closing formula. See Tool 11 Move 4.

Immediately after. Jude, and then Revelation. No demonstrable connection; the Johannine sequence ends here.

Internal context — this is the whole book. Fifteen verses, one papyrus sheet, and the elder says so (v. 13). As with 2 John, arguments from what 3 John does not say are unusually weak: what it omits, it may simply have had no room for. That caution bears directly on the temptation to reconstruct Diotrephes’ theology from silence.

Three churches, or one? ἐκκλησία occurs three times and it is not obvious they are all the same body: the brothers “testified to your love before the church” (v. 6 — plausibly the elder’s own sending congregation, where they reported back); “I have written something to the church” (v. 9); “puts them out of the church” (v. 10). If v. 6’s church is the elder’s and vv. 9–10’s is Gaius’s, the geography of the dispute changes. [I] Uncertain — flag, do not resolve. A sermon should not build on a reconstruction of who is where.

A widely misread text. Verse 2 (“I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health”) is among the most-quoted verses in prosperity preaching. The immediate context corrects it decisively: it is the conventional health-wish of a Greco-Roman letter, and its measure is Gaius’s soul — “as it goes well with your soul.” See Tool 8 and Preaching Pitfalls.

3. Structure

Verses Section Function

5–8 Gaius commended; the request “Please send them on”; the ground (v. 7) and the obligation (v. 8)
9–10 Diotrephes The obstruction: refuses the elder, refuses the brothers, expels the willing
11 The hinge “Do not imitate evil but imitate good” — the letter’s only imperative pair

Device 1 — a triptych of named men with v. 11 as the hinge. Gaius (the right host), Diotrephes (the wrong leader), Demetrius (the attested witness), with the two-ways command standing between the second and the third. The three portraits are arranged to be read against one another, and v. 11 tells the reader how. [T] High confidence. (Independently reached; the sweep says the same.)

Device 2 — ἀγαπητέ as a section marker, with one conspicuous gap. The vocative “Beloved” occurs three times, and each time it opens a unit: v. 2 (the health-wish and joy report), v. 5 (the Gaius section and the request), v. 11 (the hinge). [T]/[S: logos] verified It does not occur before vv. 9–10. The Diotrephes block is the one section of the letter the elder does not introduce by addressing Gaius affectionately. [I] Moderate confidence that the gap is deliberate; the data are certain, and the observation is worth a preacher’s notice — the warmth is switched off for exactly the length of the rebuke and switched back on at v. 11.

Device 3 — the closing inclusio of greeting. The letter opens by naming its recipient with affection and closes by commanding that everyone else be named too (v. 15). Between them stands a man who “likes to put himself first” (v. 9). [I]

4. Linking Words

  • v. 3 γάρ — “For I rejoiced greatly.” Grounds the prayer of v. 2: the elder can pray for Gaius’s bodily welfare to match his soul’s because he has had a report on the state of the soul. [T]
  • v. 5 ὃ ἐὰν ἐργάσῃ — “in whatever you do.” An indefinite relative clause: the commendation is deliberately open-ended, not tied to a specific past act.
  • v. 6 καλῶς ποιήσεις + προπέμψας — the letter’s request. Future indicative + aorist participle is the standard papyrological polite formula, “the normal way of saying ‘please’” (Yarbrough, BECNT, 371). [S: logos] Every English version renders it as a statement (“you will do well to…”), which turns a request into a compliment. High confidence.
  • v. 7 γάρ — the ground of the obligation: because they went out for the Name and take nothing from outsiders. Two reasons, and the second is the sharper: their refusal of pagan patronage is precisely what creates the church’s duty. [T]
  • v. 8 οὖν — the load-bearing connector of the letter. →therefore→ Everything before it is premise; everything after it is defence of the conclusion. “Therefore we ought (ὀφείλομεν — a word of debt) to support people like these.” [T]/[S: logos] verified
  • v. 9 ἀλλά — the adversative pivot. “I have written something to the church, but Diotrephes…”
  • v. 10 διὰ τοῦτο — “For this reason.” Points back to the obstruction as the cause of the visit’s agenda.
  • v. 10 ἐὰν ἔλθω — a third-class condition (ἐάν + aorist subjunctive): “if I come.” [S: logos] verified Contrast 2 John 10’s first-class εἰ + present indicative (“if anyone comes — and they will”). The deceiver’s arrival was assumed; the elder’s own is contingent. The man with authority is the one whose coming is uncertain. [T]/[I] A fine detail with real pastoral weight.
  • v. 10 οὔτε … καί … καί — a rising triad of Diotrephes’ actions: he does not receive, he stops those who would, he puts them out. Each step is worse than the last, and only the third involves other people’s consciences.
  • v. 11 ἀλλά — “do not imitate evil but imitate good,” followed by the grounding antithesis.
  • v. 13 ἀλλά / v. 14 δέ — the closing contrast: not with pen and ink, but face to face.

5. Parallels

v. 11b — antithetical parallelism, and as in 2 John 9 the asymmetry carries the meaning:

Whoever does good is from God; whoever does evil has not seen God.

A flat antithesis would give “is from God / is not from God.” Instead the negative half switches verbs: οὐχ ἑώρακεν τὸν θεόν — “has not seen God,” perfect tense of ὁράω. [T]/[S: logos] verified The evildoer’s problem is diagnosed as a failure of vision, not merely of parentage. This is thoroughly Johannine (John 1:18; 1 John 3:6; 4:20 — “he who does not love his brother whom he has seen cannot love God whom he has not seen”), and it lands on Diotrephes with precision: a man who cannot see people cannot have seen God. [I] Moderate–high confidence.

Otherwise N/A — occasional prose with no further parallel construction bearing on meaning.

N/A — an epistle, not narrative.

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory (run first). Gaius (v. 1), Diotrephes (v. 9), Demetrius (v. 12); the Gentiles (ἐθνικῶν, v. 7); God (vv. 6, 11 ×2); the Name (v. 7). Triage:

  • Gaius — one of the commonest names in the empire. Four NT Gaii (Acts 19:29; 20:4; Rom 16:23; 1 Cor 1:14); identification with any is impossible and unnecessary. Worth a single line, however: the Gaius of Rom 16:23 is described as “host (ξένος) to me and to the whole church” — the same role as ours. The coincidence of role is interesting; it is not evidence of identity. [I] Uncertain — do not build on it.
  • Diotrephes — “nourished by Zeus” (Διός + τρέφω). Otherwise unknown.
  • Demetrius — “belonging to Demeter.” The Ephesian silversmith of Acts 19:24 is almost certainly a different man.
  • Observation, low weight: both the men named after the letter’s protagonist are named for pagan deities, and the letter does not comment. In a Gentile-mission church, people carry the names they were born with; what sorts them is what they do with a door. [I] Uncertain — an observation, not a finding. Do not preach it as authorial design.

No canonical-history hyperlink fires on any of the three. [T]

Pattern-break absence (admissible — the pattern can be cited). 3 John never names Jesus or Christ. Every other book of the New Testament does; its own twin names “Jesus Christ” twice and “Christ” once in thirteen verses (2 John 3, 7, 9). The established pattern is citable at maximum strength, so the absence is noticeable rather than guessed. What it adds: the letter’s one reference to him is “the Name” (v. 7), absolute and articular — the designation that presumes he needs no naming. [T] on the absence; [I] on its significance. Weight: moderate–high — this is the strongest admissible absence-argument I have seen in a short NT book, but it should be preached as an observation about how the letter speaks, not as a claim about why.

ἀλήθεια, “truth” — 6×, vv. 1, 3 (×2), 4, 8, 12. [T]

The distributional contrast with 2 John. In 2 John, truth occurs five times and all five fall in vv. 1–4; the word never appears in the second half of the letter. In 3 John, truth occurs six times and they are spread from the first verse to the twelfth — through the greeting, the joy report, the support ethic, and the commendation. [T] High confidence — verified by inspection of the Greek in both letters.

The sweep’s formulation — truth is “doctrinal in 2 John, embodied in 3 John” — is right, and the distribution is the evidence for it. In 2 John truth is stated and then spent; in 3 John truth is worked, and it keeps turning up wherever work is being done. Note especially the grammatical shift: one becomes a “fellow worker with the truth” (v. 8, συνεργοὶ τῇ ἀληθείᾳ, dative), and Demetrius is attested “by the truth itself” (v. 12, ὑπὸ αὐτῆς τῆς ἀληθείας, a genitive of agency). [S: logos] verified Truth has become a colleague and a witness — very nearly a person. High confidence.

ὄνομα, “name” — 2×, vv. 7 and 15. [T]/[S: logos] verified

v. 7: ὑπὲρ γὰρ τοῦ ὀνόματος ἐξῆλθον — “they went out for the sake of the Name” v. 15: ἀσπάζου τοὺς φίλους κατ’ ὄνομα — “greet the friends, each by name”

The absolute “the Name” (cf. Acts 5:41) is Christ, unnamed because unmistakable. The letter’s last clause requires that everyone else be named individually. One Name too great to say; a congregation of names each worth saying. High confidence on the data; the pairing is [I] and, in my judgement, the letter’s finest touch.

ἀγαπητός / ἀγαπάω / ἀγάπη — 6× (vv. 1 ×2, 2, 5, 6, 11). Three of these are the vocative ἀγαπητέ marking structural units (Tool 3). [T]/[S: logos] verified

μαρτυρέω / μαρτυρία — 6×, vv. 3, 6, 12 (×4). [T]/[S: logos] verified The witness-thread, thoroughly Johannine and quasi-legal. Note the concentration: four of the six fall in v. 12 alone. High confidence.

ἐπιδέχομαι, “receive, welcome” — 2×, vv. 9, 10. [S: logos] verified Diotrephes twice fails to receive: he “does not receive us” (v. 9) and “does not receive the brothers” (v. 10). The two refusals are the same verb, and the ESV obscures this by rendering the first interpretively (“does not acknowledge our authority”) — see Tool 8. High confidence.

λαμβάνω — 2×, vv. 7, 8 (μηδὲν λαμβάνοντες; ὑπολαμβάνειν). [S: logos] verified See Headline Finding 4 and Phase 0.6. High confidence.

προπέμπω (v. 6), “send on one’s way.” [S: logos] verified A technical term for a missionary send-off with provisions, escort, and money (Rom 15:24; 1 Cor 16:6, 11; 2 Cor 1:16; Titus 3:13; Acts 15:3; 20:38; 21:5). Not a warm farewell — a funded departure. High confidence.

ἀξίως τοῦ θεοῦ (v. 6), “in a manner worthy of God.” The standard is not the missionaries’ need or the church’s capacity but God’s own worth. Cf. 1 Thess 2:12; Col 1:10. [T]

ξένους (v. 5), “strangers.” Logos tags the καί of v. 5 as concessive/emphatic and the phrase as a double accusative — i.e. the brothers are the strangers (“these brothers, strangers as they are”), not a second group. [S: logos] verified That settles a real translation question (see Tool 8) and sharpens the point: Gaius is funding men he has never met. High confidence.

φιλοπρωτεύων (v. 9), “who likes to put himself first.” A NT hapax; Logos glosses “to desire to be first, to desire to order others.” [S: logos] This is the entire diagnosis of Diotrephes. No doctrinal charge is laid anywhere in the letter. High confidence, and load-bearing for the pitfall work.

φλυαρέω (v. 10), “talking wicked nonsense.” Also a NT hapax; LSJ “talk nonsense, play the fool.” [S: logos] The elder is contemptuous as well as indignant — he does not dignify the charges by stating them.

ἐκβάλλω (v. 10), “puts them out.” [S: logos] verified See Tool 11 — the same verb as the expulsion of the man born blind (John 9:34–35).

ἀγαθοποιέω / κακοποιέω (v. 11). Compound verbs, “do good” / “do evil”; the vocabulary of the wisdom two-ways tradition (cf. Ps 34:14 LXX; 1 Pet 2:15, 20; 3:6, 11, 17). [T]

φίλοι (v. 15 ×2), “the friends.” [S: logos] verified The only place in the Johannine letters where believers are so designated. See Tool 11.

8. Translations

v. 2 — “I pray that all may go well with you” (ESV, NASB) vs “I wish above all things that thou mayest prosper” (KJV). The Greek is περὶ πάντων εὔχομαί σε εὐοδοῦσθαι. The moderns take περὶ πάντων adverbially with the verb — “in all respects, I pray that you may prosper.” The KJV construes it as “above all things,” which makes Gaius’s material prosperity the elder’s chief concern. This is a translation-driven error with a long history, and it is the engine of the prosperity misuse of the verse. The modern rendering is right. High confidence; flag it from the pulpit if anyone in the congregation reads a KJV.

v. 5 — “these brothers, strangers as they are” (ESV) vs “to the brethren, and to strangers” (KJV). The KJV reads two groups; the ESV and NASB read one. The Logos syntax tagging supports the ESV: the καί is concessive/emphatic and ξένους is a double accusative predicated of the brothers. [S: logos] verified The ESV is right, and the point matters — Gaius’s hospitality is to fellow-believers he has never met, which is the precise shape of the obligation being urged.

v. 6 — “You will do well to send them on their journey.” All English versions render καλῶς ποιήσεις as a statement of approval. It is the papyrological “please.” See Headline Finding 5. No English version conveys this, and a preacher who wants the congregation to hear the letter’s actual ask should gloss it.

v. 9 — “does not acknowledge our authority” (ESV) / “does not accept what we say” (NASB) / “receiveth us not” (KJV). The Greek is οὐκ ἐπιδέχεται ἡμᾶς — literally “does not receive us,” the same verb used of the brothers one verse later. [S: logos] verified

The KJV is here the more useful rendering, and the point is structural rather than stylistic. The ESV and NASB interpret the first ἐπιδέχεται and translate the second, with the result that an English reader cannot see that Diotrephes’ two refusals are the same act: he does not receive the elder, and he does not receive the brothers. That verbal identity is precisely what makes vv. 9–10 the mirror of 2 John 10 — and the modern versions break it. High confidence. This is the most consequential translation finding in the letter.

v. 10 — “talking wicked nonsense against us” (ESV) / “prating against us with malicious words” (KJV). Both catch the contempt; the ESV is crisper.

v. 11 — “has not seen God.” All versions agree, and correctly: ἑώρακεν is a perfect of ὁράω, not a verb of origin. See Tool 5.

vv. 13–15 — versification. The ESV has fifteen verses; the KJV has fourteen, running “Peace be to thee. Our friends salute thee. Greet the friends by name” into v. 14. Worth a word if the congregation is following in mixed versions.

Ancient versions check. Trigger met: ESV, NASB, and KJV substantively agree on v. 2’s health-wish (a rendering of real exegetical weight, given the misuse), and the Latin tradition agrees with them — the Vulgate’s “prospere te ingredi et valere sicut prospere agit anima tua” is the same conventional wish, measured by the soul. No split; the modern reading is not a modern invention. Moderate confidence on the Vulgate wording — recall, not verified.

The check does surface something at v. 9, though as a literal-versus-interpretive split rather than a text-critical one: the Latin keeps recipit at v. 9 and v. 10 alike, preserving the repeated verb that the ESV and NASB break. The older translational instinct — Latin and KJV together — is to render the word; the modern instinct is to render the sense. Here the word carried the argument. [I] Moderate confidence on the Vulgate wording; the English data are certain.

Pulpit edition note. Pulpit text matches report default (ESV Anglicised). No finding depends on a divergence — but note that the ESV’s v. 9 rendering, which the congregation will hear, is the one that hides the ἐπιδέχομαι link.

9. Tone and Feel

Warm → hot → warm, with the warmth switched off precisely for the length of the rebuke.

  • Affection, and a lot of it. ἀγαπητέ three times as direct address; “whom I love in truth” (v. 1); “my children” (v. 4); “I have no greater joy.” The letter is markedly warmer on its surface than 2 John. [T]
  • Real heat at vv. 9–10. φλυαρέω (“talking nonsense”) is a contemptuous word, and the accumulation οὔτε … καί … καί builds a charge sheet. The elder is angry, and a sermon that reads vv. 9–10 in the same register as vv. 5–8 has flattened the text. [T]
  • The vocative gap. The rebuke is the one block not opened with “Beloved,” and “Beloved” returns at v. 11 the moment the rebuke ends. [I] Moderate — but a genuine tonal signal.
  • Restraint at the point of maximum provocation. ὑπομνήσω — “I will remind him,” “call attention to what he is doing.” Future indicative, contingent on a visit that may not happen. No sentence is passed. [T] See Headline Finding 1.
  • Peace as the last word but one. Εἰρήνη σοι (v. 15) is the letter’s only occurrence of “peace,” and it lands immediately after the two-ways antithesis and Demetrius’s commendation. [S: logos] verified

Soundtrack: the same solo cello as 2 John, but this time with a genuine forte passage in the middle — and then, unusually, it does not stay loud. The last bars are quiet and name every player.

11. Quotation/Allusion

Independent candidate-finding (before testing any candidate a source named). From the letter’s own distinctive vocabulary — for the sake of the Name, fellow workers for the truth, puts them out of the church, do not imitate evil but imitate good, has not seen God, our testimony is true, the friends, by name — the candidates are: (a) OT Name-theology; (b) the Johannine expulsion (ἐκβάλλω / ἀποσυνάγωγος) motif; (c) the wisdom two-ways idiom (Ps 34:14); (d) the Gospel’s colophon formula (John 21:24); (e) the Deuteronomic witness rule; (f) John 15’s “friends”; (g) John 10:3’s κατ’ ὄνομα. No Old Testament citation. The letter quotes no OT text and uses no citation formula — confirming the overview and the sweep. [T] But the nil return needs one qualification this run has established: the Deuteronomic two-or-three-witnesses rule is operative at v. 12, and the Johannine corpus cites it explicitly elsewhere (John 8:17). John 10:3 → 3 John 15 (moderate–high confidence — distinctive verbal allusion)

Source context. John 10:1–5, the Good Shepherd discourse’s opening parable. The shepherd enters by the door, “the gatekeeper opens to him, the sheep hear his voice, and he calls his own sheep by name (φωνεῖ κατ’ ὄνομα) and leads them out.” What surrounds it matters: the discourse follows immediately on John 9, where the man born blind has just been cast out (ἐξέβαλον) by the authorities, and Jesus finds him. The shepherd who calls by name is introduced as the direct alternative to the leaders who expel. The function of 10:3 within its own passage is to distinguish the true shepherd from the thief and the hireling by his individual knowledge of the sheep.

Book usage (Move 2, tracked across the Johannine corpus). The shepherd material is a live Johannine source: John 10 in full, and John 21:15–17 (feed my sheep). The elder’s letter has already used the family metaphor (“my children,” v. 4) and is throughout concerned with the care of individuals in transit. [I]

OT-to-OT: N/A here.

What it adds. κατ’ ὄνομα occurs in only two places in the Greek New Testament — John 10:3 and 3 John 15. [S: logos] Robertson makes the connection explicitly: John “knew the friends in the church as the good shepherd calls his sheep by name.” That gives the allusion a Volume score most conceptual echoes never reach, and it converts the letter’s last clause from a courtesy into a commission: do what the Shepherd does. And it lands as the exact answer to Diotrephes, four verses earlier, who casts out (ἐκβάλλει) — the shepherd’s opposite, in the same discourse’s own terms.

Hays: Availability high · Volume high (a two-occurrence phrase) · Recurrence high (shepherd material is a live Johannine source) · Thematic coherence very high · Historical plausibility high · History of interpretation positive (Robertson) · Satisfaction high. Verdict: moderate–high, and an addition to the book-overview’s intertextual map.

John 9:34–35 (the ἀποσυνάγωγος motif) → 3 John 10 (moderate confidence for the verbal link; the ironic reading is uncorroborated)

Source context. John 9. The man born blind is interrogated, confesses “he is a prophet,” then “one thing I know, that though I was blind, now I see,” and finally the authorities “cast him out” (ἐξέβαλον αὐτὸν ἔξω, 9:34). Verse 35: “Jesus heard that they had cast him out, and having found him he said, ‘Do you believe in the Son of Man?’” The expulsion is anticipated at 9:22 (“the Jews had already agreed that if anyone should confess him to be Christ, he was to be put out of the synagogue”) and the theme recurs at 12:42 and 16:2. In its own passage, the expulsion is the price of confession — and the moment at which Jesus seeks the man out.

Book usage. ἀποσυνάγωγος is a distinctively Johannine word (John 9:22; 12:42; 16:2 — nowhere else in the NT), and the expulsion motif is one of the Fourth Gospel’s structuring concerns. [T]

What it adds. Diotrephes ἐκ τῆς ἐκκλησίας ἐκβάλλει — he puts believers out of the church, using the verb the Gospel uses for putting a believer out of the synagogue. The Logos pass confirms that commentators recognise the verbal correspondence and the excommunication sense (Black, Biblical Theology, 242; Dunn, “Excommunication,” NIDB 2:364; Robertson notes ἐκβάλλω at John 2:15 and 9:34f.). [S: logos]

But the honest verdict is split. The lexical link is attested. The ironic reading I want to draw from it — that the Johannine community has begun doing to its own what was done to it, and that Diotrephes is therefore standing where the synagogue stood — the Logos library explicitly does not support: “commentators treat these as parallel examples of exclusionary practice rather than as thematically interconnected passages,” and “they don’t systematically argue that Diotrephes’s behavior represents the Johannine community itself adopting the very exclusionary practices from which it had suffered.” [S: logos] So: report the verbal link at moderate, and report the irony as a preacher’s inference at moderate, uncorroborated — a legitimate homiletical observation, not a demonstrated authorial design. [I]

Deuteronomy 19:15 (via John 8:17) → 3 John 12 (moderate–high confidence — legal standard invoked, not quoted)

Source context (Move 1). Deuteronomy 19:15 sits in the section on judicial procedure (19:15–21), immediately after the cities of refuge and the boundary-marker law: “A single witness shall not suffice against a person for any crime or for any wrong… only on the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses shall a charge be established.” What follows (vv. 16–21) is the law of the malicious witness, who is to receive the penalty he sought for his brother. The setting is thus not abstract: it concerns the establishing of a person’s standing against accusation, and the danger of a lying accuser.

Book usage (Move 2). The Johannine corpus is demonstrably in conversation with this rule. John 8:17 cites it explicitly — “in your Law it is written that the testimony of two people is true” — and John 5:31–37 works the same standard at length (the Baptist, the works, the Father). 1 John 5:6–8 supplies a further triad (Spirit, water, blood). [T] This is one of the few OT texts the Johannine writings quote, which raises the prior for its being operative here considerably.

OT-to-OT: N/A.

What it adds. Verse 12 supplies exactly three witnesses for Demetrius: everyone (ὑπὸ πάντων), the truth itself (ὑπὸ αὐτῆς τῆς ἀληθείας), and we (καὶ ἡμεῖς δὲ μαρτυροῦμεν). [S: logos] verified Brendsel notes the conformity to the Deuteronomic requirement, which John treats as of abiding authority, citing John 8:17 and 1 John 5:7. [S: logos] The move is tactical as well as theological: against a man who dismisses the elder’s authority, Demetrius is credentialed not by the elder’s say-so but by a standard drawn from the Law — which Diotrephes cannot wave away without waving away Scripture. And the surrounding Deuteronomic context adds a sting: 19:16–21 concerns the malicious witness, and Diotrephes has just been described as “talking wicked nonsense against us.” [I] The Deut 19:16–21 resonance is my inference; treat as moderate.

John 21:24 → 3 John 12 (high confidence — near-verbatim formula)

Source context. The closing colophon of the Fourth Gospel: “This is the disciple who is bearing witness about these things, and who has written these things, and we know that his testimony is true” (καὶ οἴδαμεν ὅτι ἀληθὴς αὐτοῦ ἡ μαρτυρία ἐστίν). Cf. John 19:35 at the cross. Its function in its own passage is to authenticate the entire Gospel.

What it adds. 3 John 12 reads καὶ οἶδας ὅτι ἡ μαρτυρία ἡμῶν ἀληθής ἐστιν — the same clause with “you know” for “we know” and “our” for “his.” [S: logos] verified The elder applies to Demetrius the very formula that authenticates the Gospel of John. That is a remarkable weight of attestation for a man who gets one verse, and it strongly supports reading v. 12 as a formal letter of recommendation rather than a passing compliment. High confidence on the verbal parallel; moderate–high on the significance.

John 15:13–15 → 3 John 15 (moderate–high confidence)

Source context. The vine discourse: “Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends (φίλων)… No longer do I call you servants… but I have called you friends (φίλους), for all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you.” Friendship is redefined by disclosure and by self-giving.

What it adds. “The friends greet you. Greet the friends, each by name” is the only place in the Johannine letters where believers are designated οἱ φίλοι. [S: logos] verified Myers argues the term draws on the Gospel tradition, and that closing with it reminds Gaius that to side with the elder is to remain a friend of the community — over against Diotrephes, who displays none of the self-giving love that constitutes Jesus’ friendship. [S: logos] Moderate–high; a good closing note for a sermon, and it should be attributed as an echo rather than asserted as a quotation.

Psalm 34:14 / the two-ways idiom → 3 John 11 (moderate confidence — idiom-participation, not allusion)

Source context (Move 1). Psalm 34 (33 LXX) is a wisdom-instruction psalm: “Come, O children, listen to me, I will teach you the fear of the LORD… Keep your tongue from evil… Turn away from evil and do good” (v. 14, LXX ἔκκλινον ἀπὸ κακοῦ καὶ ποίησον ἀγαθόν). Addressed to “children,” it teaches a two-ways ethic to those who “desire life.”

What it adds. The verbal overlap with 3 John 11 is at the level of common words (ἀγαθόν, κακόν) rather than distinctive phrasing; the elder uses compounds (ἀγαθοποιέω, κακοποιέω) where the LXX has a verb plus object. Verdict: participation in a well-worn wisdom idiom, not a citation. [I] The resonance worth noting is the setting: a teacher addressing “children” (cf. v. 4) with a two-ways instruction. Moderate confidence; do not present as an allusion.

OT Name-theology → 3 John 7 (moderate confidence — conceptual)

The absolute “the Name” belongs to a deep OT current: the Name placed at the chosen place (Deut 12:5, 11), the Name profaned among the nations and vindicated for its own sake (Ezek 36:20–23), and the recurring covenant formula “for my name’s sake” (Ps 25:11; 79:9; Isa 48:9–11; Ezek 20:9, 14, 22). The nearest NT parallel is Acts 5:41 (rejoicing to suffer ἀτιμασθῆναι ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος). [I]

What it adds. In the OT idiom, it is God who acts “for his name’s sake.” In 3 John 7, it is the missionaries who go out for the sake of the Name. They have taken up God’s own stated motive as their travel warrant. [I] Moderate confidence — the idiom is too common for a specific allusion, but the transfer of subject is a real observation and preaches well.

12. Genre

A genuine Greco-Roman private letter — the most conventional in form of any NT book. It has a named individual recipient, the standard health-wish (v. 2, the περὶ πάντων … ὑγιαίνειν formula found throughout the papyri), a body of business, travel plans, and greetings. [S]

Two sub-genres embedded, and both matter:

(a) A letter of recommendation. Verse 12 performs the function of the standard ancient commendatory letter (cf. Rom 16:1–2; 2 Cor 3:1; Acts 18:27) — the named person, the attestation, the vouching authority. If Demetrius carried this letter, v. 12 is his passport. [I] Moderate–high.

(b) A polite request. Verse 6’s καλῶς ποιήσεις + participle is the papyri’s standard “please.” [S: logos]

Reading rules. (i) Read it as correspondence into a live dispute, not as systematic ecclesiology — though it witnesses to a genuine hinge in church order. (ii) The conventional elements (the health-wish especially) are conventions and must not be mined for theology; this is the single most important genre point for preaching 3 John, and the whole prosperity misuse ignores it. (iii) As with 2 John, the author says the sheet ran out (v. 13), so arguments from silence — above all about Diotrephes’ theology — are weak.

13. Copycat

Applies — and the text licenses it explicitly. Verse 11 commands imitation: μὴ μιμοῦ τὸ κακὸν ἀλλὰ τὸ ἀγαθόν. This is one of the clearest copycat authorisations in the NT, and it is placed by the author himself between the negative and positive examples.

The moralism guard is in the text. Verse 11b does not say “and those who do good will be rewarded.” It says “whoever does good is from God.” The imitation is evidence of origin, not an achievement that produces it. A sermon that stops at “be a Gaius, not a Diotrephes” has preached the imperative and dropped the indicative the author supplied one clause later.

14. Bible Timeline

Church age, this side of the cross and Pentecost, and — more precisely than most NT texts — at a datable institutional moment: the apostolic itinerant economy in transition. A network held together by travelling workers and personal hospitality is meeting a settled local leadership that resists external oversight. Diotrephes “does not receive us”; the elder’s recourse is a visit, not a canon.

Timeline cautions for the preacher.

  1. Do not read modern church polity back into vv. 9–10. Diotrephes is not a bishop in any later sense, and the elder is not a denominational authority. What the text shows is friction at a hinge, not a settled structure being violated. [I] Moderate confidence on the church-order reconstruction — the sweep flags this too, and it deserves the flag.

  2. Do not read modern mission funding back into vv. 7–8 without translation. “Accepting nothing from the Gentiles” reflects a patronage economy in which taking money created obligation and status-dependence. The principle (keep the mission’s mouth free) transfers; the mechanism does not automatically.

  3. “The Name” (v. 7) is post-resurrection shorthand. It presupposes a community for whom one name needs no qualifier — which is itself a datum about where on the timeline we are standing.

15. Who Am I?

Direct NT epistolary address to a named individual, so the tool is short but not empty.

  • Gaius — the identification point, and it is unusually direct. He is a lay host with money, a house, and a decision to make about strangers. Most hearers are more like Gaius than like anyone else in the NT. [T]
  • Diotrephes — a warning, and v. 11 says so. But note whom the warning is for: it is addressed to Gaius, not to Diotrephes. The elder does not write to the offender; he writes to the man who might be tempted to imitate him. Preachers should note the direction of the arrow — this text lands on the congregation’s own capacity to shut people out, not on an absent villain. [I] Moderate–high; this is the corrective to Preaching Trap 4.
  • Demetrius — the commended worker; the one who needs the church’s welcome and gets it.
  • The brothers — those we support; not those we are, unless we go.
  • The elder — not the preacher. His authority is apostolic; his restraint, however, is imitable and is the more useful model.
  • Christ — not a character in the letter and not named in it. He is “the Name” for whose sake the whole traffic exists, and (via John 10:3) the Shepherd whose practice Gaius is told to copy in v. 15.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. That Gaius continue and increase his concrete support of the travelling missionaries — housing, provisioning, funding them onward in a manner worthy of God — and that he not be drawn into imitating Diotrephes’ obstruction; and that he receive Demetrius on the strength of a properly attested commendation.

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.

Worldview. Three convictions the letter assumes: (i) Money and hospitality are theology. To fund a missionary is to become a “fellow worker with the truth” (v. 8) — not an assistant to workers, a co-worker with truth itself. The supporter is not in a lesser category than the sender. (ii) The mission’s independence creates the church’s obligation. Verse 7 is the ground of verse 8. Precisely because the workers will not take from outsiders, the church must take them up. (iii) A leader’s besetting sin may be entirely non-doctrinal. Diotrephes is orthodox as far as the letter tells us. What disqualifies him is φιλοπρωτεύων. Doctrinal soundness does not immunise a church against a man who wants to be first.

Behaviour. Start: funding and provisioning gospel workers deliberately, “in a manner worthy of God” — a standard set by God’s worth, not by the budget or the need. Greeting believers individually and by name (v. 15), which is the letter’s last command and the least abstract thing in it. Stop: obstructing, or acquiescing in the obstruction of, people whose only offence is that someone else did not choose them. And — reading v. 11 as it is addressed — stop imitating the obstructor.

Motivation. Not duty and not guilt. The elder’s stated motives are joy (“I have no greater joy,” v. 4), partnership (“that we may be fellow workers for the truth,” v. 8), and origin (“whoever does good is from God,” v. 11). The good is evidence, not currency.

Four audiences.

  • For me. Whose ministry am I actually funding, and how would anyone know? And, from v. 11 addressed to Gaius: where am I quietly imitating someone’s territorialism because it protects something of mine?
  • For a Christian friend. For the person who has been put out — sidelined, frozen out, quietly excluded by a leader who liked being first: this letter says the elder saw it, named it, and intends to raise it. The New Testament contains a man like that, on the record, and the apostle’s sympathy is not with him.
  • For the church community. Two corporate questions. Who decides which workers we support, and on what evidence (v. 12’s three witnesses is a standard, not a formality)? And is there anyone here whom we have made it hard to belong to us?
  • For an unbeliever. The letter shows a first-century church arguing about money, authority, and hospitality, and losing its temper — and it is in the Bible. The New Testament does not describe an idealised church; it describes this one, and asks it to do better.

Prayer. Father, thank you for those who go out for the sake of the Name and take nothing from the world. Make us fellow workers with the truth — open-handed, generous, and glad. Keep us from loving to be first. And teach us to greet your people the way your Son calls his sheep: one by one, each by name.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • v. 4 μειζοτέραν — a double comparative (μείζων already comparative, plus -τερος). Colloquial and emphatic: “no greater joy at all.” (Moderate–high; the sweep notes it too.)
  • v. 5 ὃ ἐὰν ἐργάσῃ — indefinite relative with subjunctive: “whatever you may do.” The commendation is open-ended by design.
  • v. 6 ἐνώπιον ἐκκλησίας — anarthrous. “Before a church” / “before the assembly.” Minor, but it slightly loosens the identification of which congregation heard the report. [S: logos] verified
  • v. 8 the οὖν — the letter’s structural inference; everything hangs on it.
  • v. 10 the contingent ἐάν against 2 John 10’s assumed εἰ + indicative. A deliberate-looking asymmetry: the false teacher’s arrival was certain, the apostle’s is not.
  • v. 11 present imperatives (μὴ μιμοῦ … μιμοῦ): settled disposition, not a single act.
  • v. 12 three agents of attestation — ὑπὸ πάντων, ὑπὸ αὐτῆς τῆς ἀληθείας, καὶ ἡμεῖς. See Tool 11.
  • v. 14 λαλήσομεν — future indicative (“we will speak”), against 2 John 12’s aorist infinitive λαλῆσαι. 3 John’s closing is fractionally more confident of the meeting.

Wordplay, repetition, and sound-features in the original

The λαμβάνω pivot across the οὖν (vv. 7–8). μηδὲν λαμβάνοντες ἀπὸ τῶν ἐθνικῶν … ἡμεῖς οὖν ὀφείλομεν ὑπολαμβάνειν τοὺς τοιούτους. [T] The same root on both sides of the inference. Verified in Logos. High.

The ὄνομα frame (vv. 7, 15). τοῦ ὀνόματος … κατ’ ὄνομα. [T] High on the data.

The ἐπιδέχομαι pair (vv. 9, 10). [T] High.

No paronomasia or sound-play beyond these; the letter’s artistry is in repetition and placement rather than in puns.

Textual Variants

None in 3 John is doctrinally load-bearing; the letter’s text is unusually stable. Four are worth the preacher’s awareness.

v. 4 — χαράν / χάριν

  • The issue: “no greater joy” (χαράν) or “no greater grace/favour” (χάριν)?
  • Manuscript evidence: divided; χαράν is the majority reading and is followed by all major modern versions.
  • Most likely original: χαράν. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Preaching implication: slight, but note the joy-thread would lose one of its two members on the alternative reading.

v. 5 — καὶ τοῦτο ξένους / καὶ εἰς τοὺς ξένους

  • The issue: are “the brothers” and “strangers” one group (καὶ τοῦτο ξένους, “and these strangers”) or two (καὶ εἰς τοὺς ξένους, “and to the strangers”)?
  • Manuscript evidence: the first is the harder reading and is followed by the critical text; the second looks like a smoothing, and is behind the KJV’s “to the brethren, and to strangers.”
  • Most likely original: one group. [S: logos] verified — the Logos syntax tagging construes ξένους as a double accusative. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Preaching implication: real. Gaius is funding brothers he has never met, not two separate charities.

v. 8 — ὑπολαμβάνειν / ἀπολαμβάνειν

  • The issue: “take up, support” (ὑπολαμβάνειν, critical text) or “receive, welcome” (ἀπολαμβάνειν, behind the KJV’s “receive such”).
  • Manuscript evidence: ὑπολαμβάνειν is better attested. [S: logos] verified
  • Most likely original: ὑπολαμβάνειν. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Preaching implication: none for the cross-letter finding — both readings are λαμβάνω compounds, so the link back to 2 John 10’s μὴ λαμβάνετε stands on either. That is a rare and welcome situation: the finding is variant-proof.

v. 9 — ἔγραψά τι / ἔγραψα

  • The issue: “I have written something to the church” or simply “I have written to the church.”
  • Most likely original: with τι. [S: logos] verified Moderate confidence.
  • Preaching implication: the vagueness of “something” is a small argument against identifying the letter with 2 John — an elder referring to a letter his reader has in hand would more likely name it. Do not build on this either way; the identification remains open. See Open Questions.

Versification (vv. 14–15). Not a variant but a division: the KJV has fourteen verses, the critical text and modern versions fifteen. [T]

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period: late first century, at the tail of the apostolic era. [S]
  • Location: a network of house churches, by tradition in the province of Asia; the letter names no place. [S]
  • Political context: entirely absent. As with 2 John, the crisis is internal — a striking fact for a first-century document, and one worth noting: the New Testament’s most vivid portrait of a church conflict has no persecutor in it.

What the original audience knew

(i) A travelling worker’s life depended on προπέμπω. To “send someone on their way” was a definite package — food for the road, money, sometimes an escort to the next town, and a letter of introduction. [S] Verse 6 is asking for that package, and v. 8’s ὀφείλομεν frames it as a debt the church owes. Without it the mission simply stopped.

(ii) Taking money created obligation. In a patronage economy, a benefactor’s gift bought a claim on the recipient’s loyalty and public honour. [S]The brothers’ refusal to accept anything “from the Gentiles” (v. 7) was therefore not fastidiousness but strategy: it kept the message free of a patron’s expectations — and it is exactly why the churches had to carry the cost instead. High confidence on the patronage dynamic; moderate on this being the missionaries’ conscious rationale.

(iii) Letters of recommendation were the credentialing system. With no institutions, no ordination registers, and no way to verify a stranger, a letter from a known figure was the only currency of trust (cf. Rom 16:1–2; 2 Cor 3:1; Acts 18:27). [S] Verse 12 is one. And the fact that Diotrephes will not accept the elder’s letters is precisely what makes the dispute a crisis rather than a disagreement.

(iv) Expulsion from an assembly was social, not merely religious. In a house-church network the ἐκκλησία was also the hospitality network, the trade contacts, and much of the social world. To be “put out” (v. 10) was to lose more than a seat. [S]/[I]

(v) The conventional health-wish. The formula behind v. 2 (a wish for the recipient’s εὐοδία and ὑγίεια) is so standard in the papyri that its absence would be more remarkable than its presence. [S] High confidence — this is well attested and is the decisive datum against the prosperity reading.

(vi) Diotrephes and the shape of emerging church order. The standard reconstruction is that he represents rising local leadership resisting the older itinerant-apostolic network — a genuine hinge on the road to settled monepiscopacy. [S] Moderate confidence — the text supports the friction; the developmental narrative around it is a scholarly reconstruction and should be flagged as such, not preached as background fact.

What this changes about how we read

Verse 2 stops being a promise the moment you know it is a convention. Almost the entire prosperity misuse rests on not knowing that ancient letters routinely opened this way.

Verses 9–10 stop being a personality clash the moment you know what a letter of recommendation was for. Diotrephes is not being difficult about a guest; he is refusing to honour the credentialing system on which the whole mission ran, and expelling those who would.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: the last of the Johannine letters, at the far end of the NT canon.
  • What the canonical reader knows: the Fourth Gospel (the Good Shepherd who calls by name; the man cast out of the synagogue; “I have called you friends”; the colophon “we know that his testimony is true”), 1 John, and — immediately preceding — 2 John’s closed-door ruling. Every one of those is live in this letter.
  • Canonical register: wholly Johannine, and unusually so: this letter’s only substantial intertextual conversation is with the Gospel of John.

The first hearers

  • Who they were: primarily one man, Gaius, and behind him a house church; and — since letters were read aloud — plausibly a room.
  • Their situation: a support network under strain, with a leader in it refusing to co-operate and punishing those who did.
  • What they brought: an honour-shame instinct that read hospitality as public alignment; a patronage instinct that read money as obligation; and — for anyone who had been on the receiving end of Diotrephes — a live fear of being next.

Where the passage fits their world

Gaius is being asked to keep doing something that has already cost him, in a network where a prominent man is punishing exactly that behaviour. The letter’s warmth is not decoration; it is what makes the request bearable. And v. 11’s “do not imitate evil” is not an abstraction — it names the temptation of a man watching someone else’s obstruction succeed.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: a Christian leader expelling believers from the church for showing hospitality (v. 10). In a network whose whole infrastructure was welcome, this is the deepest possible betrayal of the system.
  • Surprising: how little the elder does about it. No sentence, no counter-expulsion, no doctrinal charge — a reminder, in person, if he comes.
  • Comforting: being named, attested, and prayed for; and the assurance that the good done is “from God” (v. 11) rather than self-generated. Also the closing “Peace be to you” — the letter’s one use of the word, arriving after the storm.
  • Disturbing: v. 11b. If doing evil means one “has not seen God,” then a church-leadership behaviour has been given a spiritual diagnosis, and any hearer inclined to Diotrephes’ instincts has just been implicated.

Implication for the sermon

Recover two things: that προπέμπω meant money, and that v. 11’s warning is addressed to the good man, not the bad one. The first makes the letter concrete; the second stops it becoming a sermon about somebody else.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. The Name

  • Where this passage sits: at the point where “the Name” has become the mission’s whole warrant. The OT trajectory runs from the Name placed at the sanctuary (Deut 12:5, 11), through the Name profaned among the nations and vindicated for its own sake (Ezek 36:20–23), to the apostolic “for the sake of the Name” (Acts 5:41) and here.
  • What it contributes: the decisive move is one of subject. In the OT it is God who acts for his Name’s sake; in 3 John 7 it is the missionaries who go out for it. They have adopted God’s own motive as their itinerary, and the church’s funding of them is participation in that motive. [I] Moderate–high.

2. Shepherd and flock

  • Where this passage sits: at the pastoral end of the Johannine shepherd material (John 10; 21:15–17). The letter contrasts a man who casts out (v. 10, ἐκβάλλει — the verb of the synagogue’s expulsion in John 9:34) with an instruction to greet the friends by name (v. 15, κατ’ ὄνομα — the phrase of John 10:3).
  • What it contributes: it turns the letter’s final line into a christological instruction. Gaius is not being asked to be polite; he is being asked to do, at his own small scale, what the Good Shepherd does. [I]/[S: logos] Moderate–high.

3. Truth as agent

  • Where this passage sits: at the far end of the Johannine development of ἀλήθεια. In the Gospel, truth is what Christ is and gives (John 1:14, 17; 14:6); in 2 John it is what is confessed and abided in; here it is what one co-works with (v. 8, dative) and what testifies (v. 12, ὑπὸ αὐτῆς τῆς ἀληθείας).
  • What it contributes: truth in this letter is nearly personified — a colleague and a witness. That is what stops the support ethic being mere charity: to fund a missionary is to join a project the truth is already running. [T]/[I] Moderate–high.

Confluence

The relation is (c) climactic convergence, and it converges on the letter’s last verse. The Name that needs no naming (v. 7) is the Shepherd who names his own (John 10:3), and the instruction to greet the friends by name (v. 15) is where the Name-theme and the shepherd-theme meet in a single act a householder can perform. The truth-as-agent theme supplies the reason it is not sentimentality: naming people is co-working with the truth about them. [I]

Christological Reading

Type of connection: for a NT passage, the live question is what Christological logic is operative. Here it is unusual, and worth stating carefully: 3 John’s Christology is entirely by reference. The letter never names Jesus or Christ (Tool 7). It has no confession, no title, no doctrinal statement. What it has is “the Name” (v. 7), “God” (vv. 6, 11), and — through two distinctive verbal links to the Fourth Gospel — the Shepherd’s practice and the Gospel’s own attestation formula.

How the letter presents Christ.

  • As “the Name” (v. 7). The absolute usage presumes a community for whom no further specification is needed. Christ is presupposed, and the entire missionary economy the letter is defending exists ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος. [T]
  • As the Shepherd, by allusion (v. 15 ← John 10:3). κατ’ ὄνομα occurs in only two NT places. [S: logos] The letter closes by asking a layman to do what the Good Shepherd does — over against Diotrephes, who does what the men who expelled the blind man did (v. 10 ← John 9:34). The letter’s two Christological anchors are a shepherd verb and an expulsion verb, and they are the letter’s contrast in miniature. [I] Moderate–high.
  • As friend, by allusion (v. 15 ← John 15:13–15). The believers are οἱ φίλοι, which in Johannine usage is Jesus’ own re-designation of his disciples. [S: logos] Moderate–high.
  • Behind the attestation (v. 12 ← John 21:24). The formula that authenticates the Gospel authenticates Demetrius. [S: logos]

Trajectory.

  • What the passage anticipates: a face-to-face meeting (v. 14) and peace (v. 15) — again, as in 2 John, a small eschatology in a domestic key.
  • What Christ fulfils: he is the Name for whom they go; the Shepherd whose naming Gaius imitates; the friend whose friendship constitutes the community.
  • What remains: the workers are still travelling, still needing funds; Diotrephes is still in post.

Moralism check.

  • The “be like X” temptation, and it is acute here: “Be a Gaius, not a Diotrephes.” This letter is the easiest in the NT to preach as a character study with hospitality tips, and v. 11’s explicit “imitate” makes the temptation almost respectable.
  • The gospel grounding is supplied by the author: “Whoever does good is from God” (v. 11b). The good is diagnostic of origin, not productive of it. Preach v. 11a without v. 11b and the sermon is moralism with a proof-text.
  • Christ as hero, not example: the letter’s own final move is to hand Gaius the Shepherd’s practice (v. 15). The right shape is therefore not “be like Gaius” but “the Shepherd who calls his own by name has given you his people to name” — which makes Christ the agent and Gaius the instrument.

Confidence: moderate–high on the John 10:3 and John 15 links (both attested); high on “the Name” and on the John 21:24 formula; moderate, uncorroborated on the John 9:34 irony (see Tool 11).

Difficult / Contested Verses

v. 2 — “that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health”

  • Category: weaponised
  • The difficulty: one of the two or three most-abused verses in prosperity preaching, and the KJV’s “above all things” makes it worse.
  • Rhetorical function in context: a conventional epistolary health-wish, occupying the slot every papyrus letter of the period fills the same way.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: περὶ πάντων goes with the verb (“in every respect I pray”), not as a ranking of the elder’s priorities; the measure of the wish is Gaius’s soul (“as it goes well with your soul”), which makes spiritual health the known quantity and bodily health the thing wished for. It is a kind word, not a promise. High confidence.
  • Suggested handling: deal with it early and briefly, name the convention, and move on. Do not build the sermon here — but do not skip it either, because someone in the room has heard it misused.

vv. 9–10 — Diotrephes

  • Category: pastoral · doctrinal (church order) · weaponised
  • The difficulty: a leader behaving badly, and a text that will land hard on anyone who has been on the receiving end — and can be weaponised by anyone who wants a proof-text against a leader they dislike.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the negative panel of the triptych, set up to be read against Gaius (before) and Demetrius (after), with v. 11 telling the reader what to do with it.
  • Main interpretive options: (a) a personal power-grab with no doctrinal dimension (what the text says); (b) an unstated doctrinal agenda behind the refusal (a reconstruction); (c) a legitimate local leader resisting an over-reaching itinerant (a minority revisionist reading).
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the text lays exactly one charge, φιλοπρωτεύων, and no doctrinal charge whatever. Resist importing one — the letter’s whole force depends on Diotrephes being orthodox and wrong. Equally, resist rehabilitating him: he refuses the brothers, obstructs the willing, and expels them. High confidence.
  • Suggested handling: this is where the pastoral care is needed. Some hearers have been Diotrephes’ victims; a few have been Diotrephes. Preach the text’s restraint as well as its indictment: the elder names the behaviour, intends to address it face to face, and does not apply 2 John 10 to him.

v. 7 — “accepting nothing from the Gentiles”

  • Category: doctrinal / practical (mission funding)
  • The difficulty: does this forbid Christian ministries taking money from non-Christians — government grants, secular trusts, non-believing donors?
  • Rhetorical function in context: a ground for the church’s obligation (v. 8), not a rule for the missionaries. The elder is not legislating fundraising policy; he is explaining why the bill falls to the church.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the concern is patronage — money that buys a claim on the message. Whether a given modern gift does that is a prudential question the verse does not settle. Moderate confidence.
  • Suggested handling: name the principle (keep the message unpurchased), decline to legislate the application, and turn it back where the elder turns it: onto the church’s own duty.

v. 11b — “whoever does evil has not seen God”

  • Category: doctrinal (assurance) · pastoral
  • The difficulty: taken alone it appears to make any evildoing proof of unregeneracy, which will trouble tender consciences and comfort no one.
  • Rhetorical function in context: the ground of the imitation command, in the Johannine idiom of settled patterns (cf. 1 John 3:6–10). It sorts Gaius from Diotrephes theologically, not psychologically.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the participles are present and characterising (“the one who habitually does good / evil”), and the verb of the negative half is ὁράω — a failure of vision, which in John is a failure of recognition rather than a moral scorecard. This is a description of a settled direction, not an audit of a bad week. Moderate–high confidence.
  • Suggested handling: one clear sentence protecting the anxious hearer, without blunting the warning to the complacent one.

v. 9 — “I have written something to the church”

  • Category: interpretive
  • The difficulty: is this 2 John, a lost letter, or 1 John?
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: genuinely undecidable on present evidence; the indefinite τι slightly favours a letter the reader does not have to hand. Uncertain.
  • Suggested handling: do not mention it unless asked; certainly do not build the sermon’s framing on the identification.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Preaching Diotrephes instead of preaching to Gaius

  • What it looks like: a character study of a difficult man, with the congregation invited to recognise him — usually in someone else, sometimes in a leader the preacher has in mind.
  • Why it’s wrong: the letter is addressed to Gaius, and v. 11’s “do not imitate evil” is spoken to the good man. The elder does not write to Diotrephes at all. The arrow points at the hearer’s own capacity to shut people out, not at an absent villain.
  • The corrective: preach v. 11 as it is addressed. Ask the congregation where they are tempted to protect a position by controlling who gets in.

Pitfall 2: Prosperity misuse of v. 2

  • What it looks like: “God’s will is that you prosper and be in health” — often with the KJV’s “above all things.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it is the conventional health-wish that opened virtually every letter of the period, and its measure is the soul’s prosperity, already assumed. “Above all things” is a KJV construal of περὶ πάντων that the Greek does not require.
  • The corrective: name the convention in a sentence. It costs nothing and inoculates the congregation.

Pitfall 3: “Send them on their way” as a compliment

  • What it looks like: “The elder commends Gaius for his generosity” — with v. 6 as the evidence.
  • Why it’s wrong: καλῶς ποιήσεις + participle is the papyri’s “please.” Verse 6 is the letter’s ask, not its applause. A sermon that reads it as commendation preaches the letter without its request.
  • The corrective: render it as a request from the pulpit (“please send them on their way, in a manner worthy of God”) and let the congregation feel that they are being asked for something.

Pitfall 4: Losing the ἐπιδέχομαι link because the ESV interprets it

  • What it looks like: treating v. 9 (“does not acknowledge our authority”) as a matter of church politics and v. 10 (“refuses to welcome the brothers”) as a matter of hospitality — two separate faults.
  • Why it’s wrong: they are the same verb and the same act, widening. Diotrephes does not receive the elder; Diotrephes does not receive the brothers. The one refusal is the other.
  • The corrective: say so. “Does not receive us… does not receive the brothers.” It takes one sentence and it is what makes vv. 9–10 the mirror of 2 John 10.

Pitfall 5: Moralism — “be a Gaius, not a Diotrephes”

  • What it looks like: the sermon lands on hospitality tips and a warning against being controlling.
  • Why it’s wrong: the author supplies his own guard one clause later — “whoever does good is from God.” The good is evidence of origin, not an achievement. And the letter’s final instruction (v. 15) is a shepherd’s act, given to a layman because the Shepherd names his own.
  • The corrective: ground the imitation in v. 11b, and close where the letter closes — with the Name that needs no saying and the friends who each need naming.

Pitfall 6: Reading 3 John without 2 John

  • What it looks like: “3 John: be welcoming.” Full stop.
  • Why it’s wrong: the same act — refusing to receive a traveller — is obedience in 2 John 10 and sin in 3 John 10, and only the confession sorts them. Read alone, 3 John licenses an indiscriminate welcome its twin forbids. (Book-overview Trap 6.)
  • The corrective: at minimum, frame the sermon with its twin. The λαμβάνω link at vv. 7–8 gives you the bridge in the text itself.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. The John 9:34 irony. The verbal link between 3 John 10’s ἐκβάλλει and the Fourth Gospel’s expulsion motif is attested (Black; Dunn, NIDB 2:364; Robertson). The reading I draw from it — that the Johannine community is now doing to its own what was done to it — is explicitly not developed in the commentaries the Logos pass surfaced. [S: logos] Present it as a preacher’s observation, not as a demonstrated authorial design. Recommended: Yarbrough (BECNT) or Brown (Anchor) on v. 10 before pressing it.

  2. How many churches? ἐκκλησία at vv. 6, 9, 10 may denote one congregation or two (the elder’s sending church at v. 6; Gaius’s or Diotrephes’ at vv. 9–10). The geography of the dispute changes with the answer. Uncertain — do not build on a reconstruction.

  3. “I have written something to the church” (v. 9) — 2 John, 1 John, or a lost letter? The indefinite τι slightly favours a letter Gaius does not have. Uncertain; carried over unresolved from the sweep and the overview.

  4. The Vulgate at vv. 2 and 9. My ancient-versions notes rest on unverified recall. Recommended: check a Vulgate text or the NET notes if the v. 9 point is preached.

  5. Whether Demetrius carried the letter. Plausible and widely assumed, and it makes v. 12 into the letter’s own warrant. But it is an inference. Moderate confidence; flag if it becomes load-bearing in the sermon.

  6. The Diotrephes-as-emerging-monepiscopate reconstruction. Useful background, but a scholarly narrative rather than a datum. Moderate confidence; do not preach as fact.

  7. The φιλοπρωτεύων / φίλοι stem-echo (vv. 9, 15). Noted in Move 4 and deliberately not pressed — φιλ- is far too common a stem to bear allusive weight. Recorded so a later reader knows it was considered and declined.

Book-Overview Tensions

The exegesis confirms the overview throughout and contradicts it nowhere. Six places where it extends, sharpens, or corrects the overview or the sweep, offered for the user’s decision.

  1. Two strong new entries for the intertextual map. John 10:3 → 3 John 15 (κατ’ ὄνομα, a two-occurrence NT phrase; Robertson makes the link) at moderate–high; and John 15:13–15 → 3 John 15 (οἱ φίλοι; Myers) at moderate–high. Neither appears in the overview or the sweep, and the first is, in my judgement, the best allusion in either letter.

  2. The “no OT citation” finding needs one qualification. It remains true of quotation. But Deuteronomy 19:15 is operative at v. 12 — three witnesses for Demetrius — and the Johannine corpus cites that rule explicitly at John 8:17 (Brendsel, in Beale & Gladd, Connecting Scripture NT, 488). [S: logos] Proposal: keep “zero OT citation” and add a line: the Deuteronomic witness rule is invoked without being quoted, on the warrant of John 8:17.

  3. The overview’s 2Jn 10 ↔︎ 3Jn 10 “Mirror” row should be re-described. It is a mirror of the act and the semantic field, not of a single verb: 2 John 10 has λαμβάνω, 3 John 9–10 has ἐπιδέχομαι. The λαμβάνω link runs instead to 3 John 8 (ὑπολαμβάνειν) — which is a better finding, because it makes the second letter answer the first in the first letter’s own word. Proposal: split the row into two — a conceptual mirror (2Jn 10 ↔︎ 3Jn 10) at high, and a lexical resolution (2Jn 10 ↔︎ 3Jn 8) at high.

  4. A new macro-argument candidate: the elder does not apply his own rule to Diotrephes. This is the strongest available answer to the overview’s Preaching Trap 1 (weaponising 2Jn 10–11) and it sharpens Trap 7 (vigilance that never audits itself) by supplying the author’s own worked example of restraint. Proposal: add to the Cross-Pericope Macro-Arguments table, anchored at 2Jn 10–11 and 3Jn 9–10, at strong.

  5. A new echo-table row: the last imperative of each letter is a greeting-command. 2 John ends its commands with χαίρειν … μὴ λέγετε; 3 John ends its commands with ἀσπάζου … κατ’ ὄνομα. Proposal: add as an early↔︎late row at moderate–high, alongside the χαίρω-thread row proposed in the 2 John report.

  6. Two translation points worth recording in the overview’s preaching-traps section. (i) The ESV’s interpretive “does not acknowledge our authority” at v. 9 breaks the ἐπιδέχομαι thread the argument runs on. (ii) v. 6’s καλῶς ποιήσεις is the papyri’s “please,” so the letter’s central ask reads in English as a compliment (Yarbrough, BECNT, 371). Both are pulpit-relevant and neither is in the overview or the sweep.
