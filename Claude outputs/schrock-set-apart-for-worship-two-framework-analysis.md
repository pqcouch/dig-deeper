# Set Apart for Worship — Two-Framework Assessment

*David Schrock's* Christ Over All *essay on the Fourth Commandment, read against John Sailhamer's text/event distinction (with Schnittjer and Postell) and the Proclamation Trust framework of Cooper, Davies and Styles.*

## 1. Overview

**The article.** David Schrock, "Set Apart for Worship: Ten Words about the Fourth Commandment", *Christ Over All* (longform), 2026. About 10,500 words, with 54 footnotes. It is a review-essay, not a sermon. It frames a pastoral question, argues a thesis against a named book, and then comes back to the pastoral question. The structure is:

- **The hook**: an email asking whether mental health is ever a legitimate reason not to gather with the church, set against a murder case in the news.
- **The target**: Beau Landers, *Christ Will Give You Rest* (NSBT, Apollos 2026). Landers concludes that "the Bible does not mandate Sunday gatherings" and that the OT Sabbath was a day of rest, not of worship.
- **The thesis**: "the Fourth Commandment, in its original literary and covenantal context, commanded worship". Put more sharply, it "requires corporate worship. This is its liturgical purpose."
- **Ten arguments**: (1) Sinai as a worship setting; (2) the Sabbath's place among the God-ward words; (3) "remember"; (4) "keep holy" and the kingdom of priests; (5) work and rest, with *šmr* as priestly guarding; (6) the Sabbatical calendar of Exodus 23; (7) Deuteronomy 14:22–16:17 as Moses' exposition of the Fourth Word; (8) the Sabbath sacrifices of Numbers 28; (9) Exodus 16; (10) Exodus 31:12–17, in five sub-points ending with God "refreshed" by worship.
- **The conclusion**: mental health is never a legitimate excuse. "The answer has to be a resolute, 'NO.'"

**The character of the article in one sentence.** Its instinct is right and it is well worth arguing: the Sabbath is holy time belonging to the LORD, and it is bound up with the worship of Israel. But it proves this mostly by the wrong texts. Its best evidence is left in footnotes, and its pastoral conclusion goes further than the exegesis will carry.

**A note on the frameworks.** The article is a Torah argument, so it is on Sailhamer's home ground, and both frameworks apply without adjustment. Schrock himself works within Progressive Covenantalism and uses Richard Lints's textual/epochal/canonical horizons. That is a salvation-historical frame, much closer to the PT approach than to Sailhamer's, and the difference turns out to matter (§4).

**What has not been checked.** Only Schrock's account of Landers is available here. Nothing below should be taken as a judgement on Landers's book itself.

**Texts checked.** Every Hebrew and Greek claim below was checked in the `_texts/` corpus: WLC (the BHS text) and Swete's LXX. That covers Gen 2:15; 3:23–24; 26:5; Exod 3:12; 16; 20:8–11, 24; 23:10–19; 25–31 (speech formulas); 31:12–17; 35:1–3; Lev 19:3, 30; 23:2–4, 37–38; 26:2; Num 15:30–41; Deut 5:12–15; 15:15, 19–20; 16:12; 2 Sam 16:14; Neh 10:34; 2 Chr 36:21; Isa 56:6–7; 66:23; Ezek 46:1–3; Ps 92:1. The Decalogue synoptic dig (`Torah/dig-deeper-decalogue-exodus-20-deuteronomy-5.md`) was also consulted, and several of its findings bear directly on this article.

## 2. The Sailhamer analysis

*with Schnittjer's narrative categories and Postell on compositional seams*

### 2.1 Opening summary

The article is more text-centred than most evangelical writing on the Sabbath, and it says so on purpose. It insists that "more work needs to be done at the textual horizon" before any canonical move is made, and it faults Landers for skimming Exodus 20 "to get to other passages". Sailhamer would applaud the principle.

The practice is another matter. It is a **thematic synthesis across the Torah** (Lev 23, Num 28–29, Deut 14–16, Exod 16, 31, 33), held together by an idea, *worship*, more than by the author's own verbal and structural signals. In three places it becomes **imaginative reconstruction**: the Israelites gazing at the glory from their tents (point 9), the stick-gatherer building a rival altar-fire (point 10.4), and God refreshed by his people's praise (point 10.5). There is an irony too. Of the ten arguments, only points 3–5 work with the words of Exodus 20:8–11, and even those move quickly to Genesis 2, Leviticus 23 and Deuteronomy 5. The article does to Exodus 20 what it accuses Landers of doing.

At the same time, some of the article's micro-observations are exactly the kind Sailhamer trained his students to make. And its strongest evidence is textual evidence it never puts to work (§2.4).

### 2.2 What is working at the text level

**Exodus 3:12 as the book's frame.** *taʿabdûn ʾet-hāʾĕlōhîm ʿal hāhār hazzeh*, "you shall serve God on this mountain". The repeated "let my people go, that they may serve me" is Exodus's own leading idea, and reading Sinai through it is sound compositional reading. So is the note that Moses refuses to leave the flocks behind (10:24–26). Qualification: 10:26 says Israel does not yet know "with what we must serve", meaning which sacrifices. It does not say that the Ten Words are "for ordering Israel's worship". The article's inference there goes beyond the verse.

**The זכר and ברך links inside the Sinai speech (point 3).** This is the article's best observation. The Sabbath word begins *zākôr* (20:8) and ends *bērak YHWH ʾet-yôm haššabbāt* (20:11). A few verses later the altar law has *bəkol-hammāqôm ʾăšer ʾazkîr ʾet-šəmî… ûbēraktîkā*, "in every place where I cause my name to be remembered… I will bless you" (20:24). Two verbs, remembering and blessing, recur within one speech unit (20:1–26). That is an authorial connection, not a thematic one, and Schnittjer would call it a real verbal link. Two cautions. First, *ʾazkîr* is Hiphil and God is its subject: God causes remembrance at the altar, while Israel is told to remember the day. Second, **the link exists only in Hebrew.** Swete has *Mnēsthēti* at 20:8 and *epenomasō to onoma mou* ("where I shall name my name") at 20:24, so the Greek reader could not hear it. A Hebrew-only echo is still an echo, but it cannot bear the weight of "remembrance is defined as worshipping God".

**The splitting of עבד and שמר after Eden (point 5).** Gen 2:15 has *ləʿobdāh ûləšomrāh*. In 3:23 Adam is sent out *laʿăbōd ʾet-hāʾădāmâ*, to work the ground, with *šmr* missing. In 3:24 the cherubim take over *lišmōr ʾet-derek ʿēṣ haḥayyîm*. Schrock says he holds this "loosely", which is right, but the observation is careful and it is textual. It also lines him up with Sailhamer himself, who reads 2:15 as "to worship and to obey". Sailhamer notes that the feminine suffixes cannot refer to the masculine *gan*. The Greek, *ergazesthai auton kai phylassein*, has smoothed the difficulty to a masculine.

**Leviticus 23:3, *miqrāʾ-qōdeš*.** The weekly Sabbath is called a "holy convocation". This is the single strongest datum in the article for its thesis, and it is used correctly. (The article's note that the phrase stands "epexegetically" to "solemn rest" is garbled, but the observation stands.)

**"My Sabbaths" (point 10.1).** *ʾet-šabbətōtay tišmōrû* (Exod 31:13): the Sabbaths are the LORD's before they are Israel's. This is right, and the parallel with the Noahic sign (Gen 9) is a fair compositional parallel, since both are covenant *ʾôt*.

**Exodus 35:1–3 opening the construction (footnote 52).** The Sabbath command heads the building of the tabernacle. This is a structural observation, and it leads straight to the article's biggest missed opportunity (§2.4).

### 2.3 Where event-centred and structure-forcing moves appear

**(a) Exodus 16: the tents and the glory.** The article says that on the seventh day Moses was "restricting the people to their tents", and that there "they could do nothing but look out from their tent and behold the glory of God". It then adds Exodus 33:8–10 and concludes: "it doesn't take much imagination to see how this worship would have been commonplace".

Sailhamer would say that imagination is exactly the problem. The Hebrew of 16:29 is *šəbû ʾîš taḥtāyw ʾal-yēṣēʾ ʾîš mimməqōmô*, "stay each man where he is; let no one go out from his place". There is no tent. *ʾōhel* occurs in Exodus 16 only at 16:16, about gathering. The Greek does read *kathēsesthe hekastos eis tous oikous hymōn*, "sit each in your houses", which gives the domestic setting some support. But neither text says anything about glory on that day. The glory appears at 16:10 before the manna is given, and the text never ties it to the seventh day. Exodus 33:7–11 comes after the golden calf, at a tent pitched outside the camp, and it belongs to a different stage of the narrative. The text's own report of what the people did is four words: *wayyišbətû hāʿām bayyôm haššəbiʿî*, "so the people rested on the seventh day" (16:30).

What the text does offer is better. Exodus 16 frames the Sabbath as a test of torah before Sinai: *ləmaʿan ʾănassennû hăyēlēk bətôrātî ʾim-lōʾ*, "that I may test them, whether they will walk in my law or not" (16:4). And when some go out on the seventh day, the LORD says *ʿad-ʾānâ mēʾantem lišmōr miṣwōtay wətôrōtāy*, "how long do you refuse to keep my commandments and my laws?" (16:28). That pairing echoes Genesis 26:5, where Abraham *wayyišmōr mišmartî miṣwōtay ḥuqqôtay wətôrōtāy*, "kept my charge, my commandments, my statutes and my laws". **Abraham kept the torah without Sinai. Israel, at the first Sabbath, does not.** This is one of the linchpins of Sailhamer's reading of the Pentateuch (*The Meaning of the Pentateuch*, 2009). It makes the first Sabbath a test of trusting obedience, keeping God's word by gathering a double portion and resting, and not an occasion of liturgy.

**(b) *šmr* as priestly "guarding" (point 5).** The article says Deuteronomy 5:12 "changes the word and the verb tense to stress the commandment", and that *šmr* "reinforces the priestly nature of the commandment". The first claim is wrong. *zākôr* (Exod 20:8) and *šāmôr* (Deut 5:12) are both infinitive absolutes used as commands. The form is the same and only the verb differs, as the Decalogue dig records. Two further points weaken the second claim. Exodus itself already uses *šmr* of the Sabbath (31:13, 14, 16), so *šmr* is not a Deuteronomic strengthening. And **Deuteronomy has not dropped "remember" at all. It has moved it**, from the day to the slavery: *wəzākartā kî-ʿebed hāyîtā*, "you shall remember that you were a slave" (5:15). With *miṣwōt* or *tôrōt* as its object, *šmr* is the ordinary Torah verb for obeying (Gen 26:5; Exod 16:28). It carries a priestly sense only when it is paired with *ʿbd* in a sanctuary context (Num 3:7–8; 18:7). On its own it does not.

**(c) The stick-gatherer (point 10.4).** The article says the man "was presumably doing so to stoke the fire at his house, which was a rejection of the tabernacle's altar", and that he "died like Nadab and Abihu". None of this is in Numbers 15. The text says *məqōšēš ʿēṣîm*, "gathering wood", with no fire and no altar. Exodus 35:3 does forbid kindling a fire, and it is fair to link the two passages. But the rival-altar reading is reconstruction.

The placement tells us why the man died. Moses sets the incident **immediately after** the law of the "high-handed" sin: *wəhannepeš ʾăšer-taʿăśeh bəyād rāmâ… ʾet-YHWH hûʾ məgaddēp… kî dəbar-YHWH bāzâ*, "the person who does anything with a high hand… reviles the LORD… because he has despised the word of the LORD" (15:30–31). So law, narrative and law form a single unit. The stick-gatherer is Moses' example of despising the word, not an example of false worship. The tassel law then follows (15:37–41), with *ûzəkartem ʾet-kol-miṣwōt YHWH waʿăśîtem ʾōtām*, "that you may remember all the commandments of the LORD and do them" (15:39). The article rightly sees the tassels as priestly in colour (*təkēlet*). But it misses that *zkr* returns here, and that in this passage *remembering means doing*. That cuts across point 3, which defined remembrance as worship.

**(d) "Refreshed" (point 10.5), the article's own "most important argument".** In Exodus 31:17 God *šābat wayyinnāpaš*, "rested and was refreshed". The article develops this through the noun *nepeš*, the "pleasing aroma" of sacrifice, and Psalm 22:3, and concludes that God is "wonderfully enlivened by the communion of his faithful saints who turn their attention to him in worship".

The verb occurs in the Niphal **only three times in the Hebrew Bible**: Exodus 23:12, Exodus 31:17 and 2 Samuel 16:14 (lemma index, verified). The article cites 2 Samuel 16:14, and it spends a whole section on Exodus 23:10–19, but it never mentions **Exodus 23:12**. That verse is the Torah's other Sabbath use of the word:

> *šēšet yāmîm taʿăśeh maʿăśêkā ûbayyôm haššəbîʿî tišbōt ləmaʿan yānûaḥ šôrkā waḥămōrekā wəyinnāpēš ben-ʾămātkā wəhaggēr.*
> "Six days you shall do your work, but on the seventh day you shall rest, that your ox and your donkey may have rest, *and the son of your servant woman, and the alien, may be refreshed*."

In the Book of the Covenant, then, the one who "catches his breath" on the Sabbath is the slave-girl's son and the stranger. When Exodus 31:17 then says God *wayyinnāpaš*, the anthropomorphism is deliberately one of solidarity: **the LORD rested the way the weakest labourer in Israel rests.** The text contains no idea of God being refreshed by praise. Psalm 22:3 and the "pleasing aroma" texts use different vocabulary altogether. Two further facts weaken the argument. Moving from the verb to the noun *nepeš* is the root-meaning move that James Barr warned against, although Schrock is cautious here. And **the Greek does not render the word at all**: 31:17 has *katepausen kai epausato*, "rested and ceased", and 23:12 has *anapausētai*. So the argument the article calls its most important rests on one Hebrew word, whose only other Sabbath use points the opposite way and which the Greek translators did not mark.

**(e) Exodus 23:10–19 and "the Sabbaths" as a system (points 6 and 10.2).** The article says Moses "moves from the Sabbath day (23:12) to the Sabbath year (23:11)". In fact the text runs the other way: year first (23:10–11), then day (23:12). More important, the two Sabbath clauses have their own purpose clauses, and both concern the poor and the dependent: *wəʾākəlû ʾebyōnê ʿammekā*, "that the poor of your people may eat" (23:11), and the rest and refreshment of animals, slave and alien (23:12). The feasts follow as a separate unit, "three times in the year" and for males (23:14–17). The text does not fold the weekly Sabbath into them.

Leviticus 23 makes the distinction explicit. After the whole festal calendar comes the summary: *ʾēlleh môʿădê YHWH… millbad šabbətōt YHWH*, "these are the appointed feasts of the LORD… **besides the Sabbaths of the LORD**" (23:37–38). The chapter also sets 23:3 apart structurally: the heading "these are the appointed feasts of the LORD" is given at 23:2 and repeated at 23:4, with the weekly Sabbath placed between the two. The plural *šabbətōt*, which point 10.2 reads as "the entire system of Sabbaths", is kept distinct from that system by Leviticus itself. Leviticus 23:3 adds one more detail the article passes over: the weekly convocation is *bəkōl môšəbōtêkem*, "in all your dwellings". **It is local, not at the altar.** That undercuts the altar-centred argument of point 3. But it actually supports what Schrock needs for the church, because a weekly holy assembly wherever God's people live is exactly what he wants to establish.

**(f) Deuteronomy 14:22–16:17 as "Moses' exposition of the Fourth Commandment" (point 7).** Stephen Kaufman's thesis that Deuteronomy 12–26 follows the order of the Decalogue, which Philpot and Redd take up, is a respectable scholarly hypothesis. But it is a hypothesis about arrangement, and it has to be tested against the text. *Šabbāt* does not occur anywhere in Deuteronomy outside 5:12, 14 and 15 (lemma index, verified). Some particulars are also wrong. Deuteronomy 15:20 ("eat it before the LORD… you and your household") belongs to the **firstling law** (15:19–23), not the release of slaves. And "the tithe is collected so that on the Sabbath the people will not need to do ordinary work" is not in the text. The tithe's own purpose is *ləmaʿan tilmad ləyirʾâ*, "that you may learn to fear the LORD your God always" (14:23).

The real textual link between Deuteronomy 5 and 15–16 is the one the article does not cite. The Sabbath motive clause *wəzākartā kî-ʿebed hāyîtā bəʾereṣ miṣrayim*, "you shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (5:15), comes back almost word for word at 15:15 (the release of slaves) and 16:12 (the Feast of Weeks). That is Deuteronomy's own signpost. It links the Sabbath to **release and mercy**, not primarily to liturgy.

**(g) Background brought in from outside.** Kline's two-copy reading of the tablets rests on ancient Near Eastern treaty practice, which is event-level background. The article's argument does not depend on it, since the God-ward words are grouped first whichever view of the tablets is taken. Reading the Fifth Commandment as "about worship" by way of Psalm 78 and Ephesians 6 is thematic association, not reading of the text. Leithart's list of capital crimes is a proof-list. None of these does serious harm, but none of them is the author's own signal.

**(h) The frame itself.** The article moves from Sinai to Sunday through Lints's "epochal horizon" and Progressive Covenantalism's "typological patterns" (footnote 7). Sailhamer does not use salvation history or traditional typology to link the Testaments. So on his terms the whole last movement of the argument (kingdom of priests → corrupted at the calf → restored in 1 Peter 2 → Lord's Day gathering) is framework, not text. That does not make it wrong. But it means the question of what the *Tanak itself* does with the Sabbath is never asked. That question is the subject of §2.4.

### 2.4 The deepest missed opportunity: Sabbath and sanctuary as one composition

The article sets out to prove that Moses tied the Sabbath to the worship of God. **Moses did, and he did it in the structure of the text.** The article has the evidence and leaves it in the footnotes.

**The seven speeches.** The tabernacle instructions are divided by the formula "and the LORD said/spoke to Moses" into **seven speeches**, at Exodus 25:1, 30:11, 30:17, 30:22, 30:34, 31:1 and 31:12 (verified). The seventh speech is the Sabbath (31:12–17), and it is grounded in creation: "in six days the LORD made… and on the seventh day he rested" (31:17). The golden calf follows (32–34). When construction begins, the Sabbath comes first (35:1–3). So the sanctuary is enclosed on both sides by the Sabbath, and the instructions for it are set out as a seven-day creation. This is widely recognised (Kearney, 1977). The Exodus book-overview already treats it as established. It is the clearest compositional statement in the Torah that **holy time and holy space are one thing**: the day God rested and the place God dwells.

**The pairing, twice, verbatim.** Leviticus says it outright, in identical words, at 19:30 and 26:2: *ʾet-šabbətōtay tišmōrû ûmiqdāšî tîrāʾû ʾănî YHWH*, "you shall keep my Sabbaths and reverence my sanctuary: I am the LORD". The second occurrence stands at the head of the covenant blessings and curses. The article cites these verses only in footnote 43, and only to show that the Sabbaths are God's. Yet they are the most direct textual answer in the Torah to Landers's claim that "the correlation between sabbath and worship does not exist in the context of the Bible".

**The Tanak's own reading of the Sabbath (Postell's canonical framing).** Sailhamer holds that the Prophets and the Writings are already reading the Torah forward, so no "epochal" bridge is needed. Taken in Tanak order:

- **Isaiah 56:6–7.** The foreigners *hannilwîm ʿal-YHWH ləšārətô*, "who join themselves to the LORD to minister to him", *kol-šōmēr šabbāt mēḥalləlô*, "every one who keeps the Sabbath and does not profane it", are brought "to my holy mountain", made joyful "in my house of prayer", and their offerings are accepted "on my altar". *šārat* is the verb for priestly service. **The prophet sees Sabbath-keeping outsiders brought into priestly ministry.** That is the "kingdom of priests" argument of point 4, reached by text and not by the detour through the golden calf.
- **Isaiah 66:23**, the book's closing promise: *ûmiddê šabbāt bəšabbattô yābôʾ kol-bāśār ləhištaḥăwōt ləpānay*, "from Sabbath to Sabbath all flesh shall come to worship before me". Isaiah is read here as one book, and it ends by joining the Sabbath to the worship of all nations. The article cites this verse only as an example of the plural.
- **Ezekiel 46:1–3.** The inner gate is shut "the six working days" and opened on the Sabbath, and *wəhištaḥăwû ʿam-hāʾāreṣ… baššabbātôt*, "the people of the land shall bow down at the entrance of that gate before the LORD on the Sabbaths". Again the article uses it only for the plural.
- **Psalm 92:1**: *mizmôr šîr ləyôm haššabbāt*, "a psalm, a song for the Sabbath day". In the Hebrew Psalter it is the only psalm with a day in its title. (The Greek adds day titles to several others.) The article quotes Landers's comment on Psalm 92, but never uses the title against him.

Taken together, this material answers Landers from the text and shows that the Tanak itself reads the Sabbath toward worship "before me" by "all flesh". It would also have made the article shorter. Points 3, 4, 7, 9 and 10.4–5 could all have gone. Sailhamer's usual result holds: staying with the author's own structure gives *more* of what the expositor wanted, not less.

### 2.5 Summary judgement (Sailhamer)

This is a writer with real text-centred instincts. He hears verbal repetitions (*zkr*/*brk* in Exod 20), watches what drops out of a sentence (*šmr* after Gen 3:23), and insists in principle that the text comes before the epoch. What he lacks is the discipline to **let the author's structure choose the evidence**. The argument begins from a theme, *worship*, and gathers texts that fit it. Where the texts do not quite fit, imagination supplies the rest (the tents, the altar-fire, the refreshment). Sailhamer's method would give him the seven-speech frame, the Sabbath–sanctuary pairing, the Genesis 26:5 / Exodus 16:28 test of torah, and Isaiah's closing verse. It would also take away most of his current arguments. His thesis would be stronger as a result.

## 3. The Proclamation Trust analysis

### 3.1 Opening summary

The essay is not a sermon, but it is exposition put to use: it asks a pastoral question, argues from a text, and lands an application. So the PT questions apply.

On **Cooper's** terms, it states the right order (original context before canonical context) better than most. But it does not always read "normally". Several arguments need a specialist route that an ordinary careful reader would not take. On **Davies'** terms, the essay's purpose was fixed before the text was heard. The question and its answer were both set by the email and by the polemic against Landers, and the Fourth Word's own stated purpose is never allowed to shape the application. On **Styles'** terms, the essay puts off the move to Christ ("more needs to be said in order to move from Sinai to today") and then lands its application anyway. That is the rectangle short-circuited, and the result is law without the King.

A seven-second sermon for the essay as written:

**Question?** Must a struggling saint gather with the church?

**Answer!** The Fourth Commandment commanded corporate worship.

**So what…** No excuse: gather.

A seven-second sermon for **Exodus 20:8–11 (with Deuteronomy 5:12–15)** as the text frames it:

**Question?** Now that Israel is no longer Pharaoh's, whose is its time?

**Answer!** The seventh day is *šabbāt laYHWH*, "a Sabbath to the LORD": his by creation (he rested, blessed and hallowed it) and his by redemption (you were a slave).

**So what…** Stop, and make sure everyone under your roof stops too, down to the slave, the stranger and the ox. Keep the day holy as the LORD's.

The two do not match. The gap between them is the subject of §3.3 and §3.4.

### 3.2 What is working well

**Cooper's order, clearly argued.** "If it is improper to apply Exodus 20:8–11 to Christians today without recognizing the covenantal differences… then it is also improper to jump from Exodus 20:8–11 to other passages of Scripture without putting in the work to understand what this commandment meant in its original context." That is Cooper's original context → canonical context → contemporary application, stated as a method and used as a criticism. It is the article's best methodological moment.

**Attention to placement.** Reading the Sabbath word inside Exodus 19–24, and beside the altar law of 20:22–26, is macro-level reading in Cooper's sense. The *zkr*/*brk* observation (§2.2) is micro-level reading of the same kind.

**A real pastoral question, pressed honestly.** The article does not let the question drift into abstraction. It asks what a pastor can actually say. It is right that a theology which leaves the pastor with "little more than sympathetic encouragement" has failed somewhere. Its warning about an "antinomian age" is fair.

**The corporate people of God.** The article's instinct that the Sabbath is public and communal, not private remembrance, is sound. So is its move from the kingdom of priests (Exod 19:6) to 1 Peter 2:5–9 and the assembly at Zion (Heb 12:22–24). That is a Styles-shaped vision: the King's people gathered before him.

### 3.3 Where the framework is not fully applied

**Cooper: "read normally, not weirdly".** Cooper's test is whether any careful reader could reproduce the reading. Four of the article's arguments fail it: the Israelites gazing at the glory from their tents, the stick-gatherer as a rival altar-builder, the Fifth Commandment as "about worship", and God "refreshed" by praise. Each needs a chain of inference that the text does not provide. The article admits as much: "there are lots of pieces to this puzzle, and some that are stronger than others". A reader who is not persuaded by the weak pieces may then discount the strong ones.

**Cooper: the Fourth Word's own words are under-read.** For an essay promising "a close reading of Exodus 20:8–11", it passes over the phrases in those verses that would help it most:

- *šabbāt laYHWH ʾĕlōhêkā*, "a Sabbath **to the LORD** your God" (20:10). This is the text's own statement that the day is directed Godward, and it is never used.
- The household list in 20:10 and Deuteronomy 5:14: son, daughter, male and female servant, livestock, sojourner. Deuteronomy adds the purpose, *ləmaʿan yānûaḥ ʿabdəkā waʾămātəkā kāmôkā*, "that your male servant and your female servant may rest **as well as you**".
- **One verb with two subjects.** In Exodus 20:11 God rests (*wayyānaḥ*). In Deuteronomy 5:14 the servant rests (*yānûaḥ*). This comes from the Decalogue dig, and it is the tidiest statement in either text of what the Sabbath is for.

The Fourth Word states its own purpose, and that purpose is *rest, to the LORD, for the whole household*. Worship is implied in "to the LORD" and "keep it holy", and made explicit in Leviticus 23:3. But "the Fourth Commandment requires corporate worship… this is its liturgical purpose" claims more than the Fourth Word says.

**The logical gap: association is not command.** The article shows convincingly that the Sabbath was *associated with* worship: priests offered the Sabbath sacrifices (Num 28:9–10), and the day was a convocation (Lev 23:3). It then concludes that the commandment *requires* each Israelite's corporate worship. That does not follow. Point 8 concedes the gap ("even if we accepted Landers' argument that the people of Israel did not worship the Lord on the Sabbath, the priests did!"), and point 6 concedes that "the Fourth Commandment only uses language that is suggestive of worship". The conclusion ("ten words… that prove") is stronger than the argument supports.

**Davies: "using the Bible".** The essay opens with a question and a target, and it has decided the answer before the text is read. Davies' first diagnosis fits: the text is gathered to serve a conclusion. The clearest sign is that the Fourth Word's own application, rest *given* to the weakest members of the household, points the other way from the essay's application, which uses the Sabbath to *require something of* the weakest member of the church.

**Davies: the "us now" is landed on an extreme.** The opening sets a church member's mental-health struggle next to a murder case, and it frames mental ill-health as a possible "shield" against justice. Davies insists that application be landed on real people, not stereotypes. A member who has stopped coming to church through anxiety or depression is badly served by being introduced in that company. It also biases the reader toward suspicion before any text has been opened.

**Davies: what carries the conclusion?** The article admits that it has not made the move from Sinai to today ("more needs to be said"). Yet it answers the pastoral question with a "resolute, 'NO'". The answer actually rests on Hebrews 10:25, and on the admission that "such commandments to worship God can be found in Scripture independent of the Sabbath". So the ten words do not carry the conclusion. Hebrews does. Hebrews 10:24–25 comes pre-applied: "let us consider how to stir up one another… not neglecting to meet together… but **encouraging one another**". Its purpose is mutual encouragement. On Davies' terms, the pastoral application for the struggling member's church comes from that purpose: the gathering exists for her, and so the church's first move is toward her.

**Styles: the rectangle short-circuited.** Styles' rule for OT texts is that the step through Christ must be neither bypassed nor rushed. The essay says openly that it bypasses it. Christ appears in a clause ("to gather with Jesus Christ on the Lord's Day, as his Spirit leads us in worship"), and in Hebrews 4:8–11 near the end. The pastoral answer is then given as a moral imperative without the King who gives the rest. Styles would call that the moralism risk: application as duty, not as gospel.

**Styles: the gospel was in the footnotes.** Footnote 40 says: "Importantly, this is how Jesus keeps the Sabbath. On the Sabbath, he heals the sick and gives rest to others". It cites Matthew 12, Mark 1, Luke 13 and John 5. Luke 13:16 is the sharpest of these: "ought not this woman, a daughter of Abraham whom Satan bound… be loosed from this bond **on the Sabbath day**?" Here the King makes the Sabbath the day the bound are loosed. That is exactly the gospel-shaped Sabbath the pastoral question needed, and the essay leaves it at the foot of the page.

**Styles: every Christian a preacher.** The gathering is itself proclamation: "as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (1 Cor 11:26). The essay could have used this to strengthen its case. For Styles, a member's absence matters because the church's gathered witness to the King is diminished, and not only because a command is broken.

### 3.4 The deepest missed opportunity (PT): the pastoral answer

The Fourth Word is the only commandment in either Decalogue that names the dependants of the household. It is the only one whose Deuteronomic motive is the memory of having been a slave. And it is the one Jesus kept by healing the bound. If the pastoral answer had been heard from this text, it would say something like this:

*The day is the LORD's, and he commands his people to keep it holy together. That command is not optional, and the church should say so. But the command is given for the weary. In Israel, the householder kept it by making sure the slave, the stranger and the ox could stop too. The church keeps it in the same way: it gathers, and it carries those who cannot yet come. So a pastor faced with a member who has stopped attending neither issues a free pass nor a flat "no". He takes the gathering to her: the word, the prayers, the fellowship and, where possible, the table. And he calls her back to the assembly as the place where the King gives rest, not as a test she is failing.*

That keeps everything Schrock is rightly concerned about: the gathering is commanded, Sunday matters, and sympathy alone is not enough. And every part of it comes from the text he chose.

### 3.5 Summary judgement (PT)

This is a capable, well-read theologian with a pastor's instinct. He is right that an individualised Sabbath is a thin Sabbath, and right that the church needs grounds for calling its members to gather. The PT framework would do three things for him. (1) Let Exodus 20:8–11's own stated purpose, "to the LORD… that your servant may rest as well as you", shape the application. (2) Make the Christ-step explicitly before applying, especially the Sabbath healings he himself cites. (3) Land the "us now" on the actual member in the email, not on a headline. None of these changes his conviction that the Lord's Day is for gathered worship. All of them would make the case more persuasive and more pastoral.

## 4. Where the two frameworks agree and diverge

**They agree** on three things. The article's core claim, that the Sabbath is holy time belonging to the LORD and bound up with Israel's worship, is textually defensible. Its best evidence is under-used and its weakest is over-used. And its pastoral conclusion goes beyond what its exegesis establishes.

**They diverge over the bridge from Sinai to Sunday.** A PT reader accepts in principle the article's Progressive-Covenantal route: a typological pattern (Sabbath, kingdom of priests) fulfilled in Christ and the new-covenant assembly. The PT critique is that the route is announced but not walked.

Sailhamer would press harder, and in a direction that cuts both ways.

- **For Schrock:** the Tanak already reads the Sabbath toward worship by all nations (Isa 56:6–7; 66:23). So the "worship" content of the Sabbath is not a later typological development. It is the authorially intended meaning, visible in the Torah's own Sabbath–sanctuary composition. On this point Sailhamer gives Schrock more than his own framework does.
- **Against Schrock:** Sailhamer reads the Pentateuch as setting the faith of Abraham, who "kept my torah" without Sinai (Gen 26:5), against the failure of Israel under the Sinai covenant. The first Sabbath (Exod 16), before Sinai, is a test of trusting obedience, and Israel fails it. On that reading the Sabbath's abiding meaning lies in the trust of Exodus 16 and the rest of Hebrews 4 ("we who have believed enter that rest"). It does not lie in carrying the Sinai stipulation over as a moral imperative. That is closer to Landers's conclusion about the *imperative*, even while it supports Schrock on the *content*. *(This is an extrapolation from Sailhamer's general reading of law and faith in the Pentateuch. I am not aware of a published treatment by Sailhamer of the Lord's Day question, and it should not be attributed to him as his view.)*

This is a real conflict, and it is worth keeping rather than resolving. It shows that "was the Sabbath for worship?" and "is Sunday gathering a moral imperative carried over from the Fourth Commandment?" are two different questions. The article treats them as one.

## 5. Additional scholarly contribution

**The seven speeches of Exodus 25–31.** P. J. Kearney, "Creation and Liturgy: The P Redaction of Ex 25–40", *ZAW* 89 (1977), is the standard statement. The seven-speech count was verified in the corpus for this assessment, but the citation details should be checked before being quoted.

**Deuteronomy and the Decalogue order.** Stephen A. Kaufman, "The Structure of the Deuteronomic Law", *Maarav* 1/2 (1978–79), is the source of the scheme Philpot and Redd follow. Scholars disagree over where the section boundaries fall, including whether 14:22–29 goes with the Fourth Word, and the article's own footnotes 29 and 38 show it wavering between outlines.

**Sailhamer on Gen 2:15 and Gen 26:5.** See *Genesis* (EBC, rev. 2008) on 2:15, "to worship and to obey"; *The Pentateuch as Narrative* (1992); *The Meaning of the Pentateuch* (2009) on Gen 26:5 and law versus faith. Page references are not given here and should be checked in Logos.

**The stick-gatherer and the tassels.** Milgrom (*Numbers*, JPS, 1990) treats Num 15:32–36 as an illustration of the preceding law of presumptuous sin, and discusses the *təkēlet* tassel as a mark of priestly holiness extended to the laity. That supports Schrock's tassel observation while undercutting his altar-fire reading. The attribution reflects Milgrom's well-known position, but it is unchecked here.

**Synagogue Sabbath assembly.** Acts 15:21 — "from ancient generations Moses has had in every city those who proclaim him, for he is read every Sabbath in the synagogues" — is first-century witness to exactly the local weekly assembly that Lev 23:3 (*bəkōl môšəbōtêkem*) anticipates. It would have strengthened the article more than any of points 8–10.

**The wider debate.** D. A. Carson (ed.), *From Sabbath to Lord's Day* (1982) is the main scholarly precursor of the "fulfilled Sabbath" position the article opposes. R. T. Beckwith and W. Stott, *This Is the Day* (1978), argue the opposite case from within Anglicanism. Neither is engaged in the article.

**On root meanings.** James Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961), is the classic warning against reading a noun's range (*nepeš*, "soul, life") into a verb (*nāpaš*, "catch breath").

## 6. Accuracy and reference checks

- **"Exodus 20:8 lacks an imperative… Deuteronomy 5:12 changes the word and the verb tense."** Both verbs are infinitive absolutes used as commands. The verb changes but the form does not. (The Greek has imperatives in both: *Mnēsthēti*, *Phylaxai*.)
- **"Moses moves from the Sabbath day (23:12) to the Sabbath year (23:11)."** The order is the reverse: year (23:10–11), then day (23:12).
- **"Restricting the people to their tents" (Exod 16:29).** The Hebrew has "in his place… from his place". The Greek has "in your houses". Neither has "tent".
- **Deut 15:20 as masters giving rest to slaves "so that they can eat before the Lord".** Deut 15:20 belongs to the firstling law (15:19–23), not the release of slaves (15:12–18).
- **Deut 14:22–16:17 as an exposition of the Sabbath.** *Šabbāt* does not occur there. In Deuteronomy it occurs only at 5:12, 14, 15.
- **"The tithe is collected so that on the Sabbath… the people will not need to do ordinary work."** This is not in the text. The stated purpose is "that you may learn to fear the LORD" (14:23).
- **"Holy convocation stands epexegetically to solemn rest… the first term is defined by the second."** This is confused. In Lev 23:3 *šabbat šabbātôn miqrāʾ-qōdeš* the terms stand in apposition.
- **"Exodus 10:26 tells us clearly that the words God reveals at Sinai are for ordering Israel's worship."** The verse concerns which animals are needed for sacrifice. The inference goes beyond it.
- **"Exodus 16 is the first place where God's glory is seen."** Correct for the phrase *kəbôd YHWH* (first at 16:7, 10). Earlier forms of the root in Exodus refer to God getting glory over Pharaoh (14:4, 17–18), not to a visible glory.
- **Footnote 43, "Lev. 19:13".** Should be 19:3 (verified: *ʾet-šabbətōtay tišmōrû*).
- **Footnote 46, "Num. 10:33".** Should be Neh 10:33 (Heb. 10:34, *haššabbātôt*). **"2 Chr. 36:2"** should be 36:21, which is about the land's Sabbaths.
- **Footnote 38, "Philpot, 'Ten Words and the.'"** A truncated short title.
- **"Joshua Philpot reminds us, Deuteronomy 6–26 expounds the Ten Words in order."** This is a hypothesis (Kaufman), presented as settled.
- **"God rested… and was refreshed."** The Hebrew is correct. The Greek (*katepausen kai epausato*) has no equivalent of "refreshed".
- **Landers's statements.** These are quoted from Schrock with page references and have not been checked against the book.

## 7. Critical assessment of this analysis

**It may be too hard on the thematic method.** Schrock's genre is a polemical review-essay, and he says openly that a whole-Bible Sabbath theology is beyond its scope. A synthesis across Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy is a legitimate way of reconstructing *what the Sabbath was in Israel*. The Sailhamer critique holds on its own terms (what did Moses intend in *this text*?), but it is not the only fair measure of the article.

**The Exod 23:12 / 31:17 argument is strong but not decisive.** Three occurrences are a small sample. The solidarity reading ("God rested as the slave-girl's son rests") is the most natural reading of the verbal link, but it is still a reading. An anthropomorphism can serve more than one purpose, and Schrock's broader point, that God delights in his people's worship, is true on other grounds (e.g. Zeph 3:17). The objection is to deriving that point from *wayyinnāpaš*.

**The Sailhamer divergence in §4 is extrapolated.** It is built from Sailhamer's reading of law and faith in general, not from anything he wrote about the Lord's Day. It is offered as a question for testing, not as his position.

**The pastoral critique should allow for Schrock's context.** He is answering a real email from a pastor, in a culture he sees as drifting toward treating church attendance as optional. His "resolute NO" is explicitly *not* "pastors… force ailing members to go to church", and he grounds it in the Spirit's enabling. §3.4 reframes his answer and does not reject it. His concern and the reframing can both stand.

**The Lindsay Clancy framing is judged on how it reads, not on intention.** It is possible the opening was meant only to set up a contrast between justice and therapy. Even so, a reader who has mental-health struggles is likely to take it the way §3.3 describes.

## Appendix: point 10.5 reworked

*A reworking of the article's "most important argument", kept in Schrock's voice and at about his length. It keeps his conclusion, that the Sabbath is Godward holy time, and replaces the refreshment-by-praise argument with what the vocabulary itself supports. For reference only.*

> **Fifth, the God who rested is the God who gives rest to the weakest.**
>
> Bringing this essay to a close, I land on what may be the most surprising word in the Sabbath texts. Exodus 31:17 says that on the seventh day the LORD "rested and was refreshed" — *šābat wayyinnāpaš*. The verb is rare: in the Niphal it occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible. One is David's weary company catching their breath at the Jordan (2 Sam 16:14). The other is in the Book of the Covenant, in the Sabbath law itself: "on the seventh day you shall rest, that your ox and your donkey may have rest, and the son of your servant woman, and the alien, *may be refreshed*" (Exod 23:12).
>
> Notice who catches their breath on the Sabbath in Exodus 23: the slave-girl's son and the stranger. So when Moses says in Exodus 31 that the LORD was "refreshed" on the seventh day, he chooses the very word he has already used of Israel's weakest labourers. The Almighty was not exhausted by creation. But Moses describes the divine rest in the language of the servant's rest, so that every Israelite householder who stopped work and let his servants stop would be doing, in his own yard, what God did at the beginning.
>
> That is why the Sabbath is "to the LORD" and holy. It is his day, patterned on his rest, and it belongs to him before it belongs to Israel: "you shall keep *my* Sabbaths" (31:13). It is also why the day was never private. Leviticus calls it a "holy convocation… in all your dwellings" (Lev 23:3), and twice pairs it with the sanctuary in a single breath: "you shall keep my Sabbaths and reverence my sanctuary" (Lev 19:30; 26:2). Holy time and holy space belong together, and the tabernacle instructions are built that way. Seven speeches, the seventh the Sabbath (Exod 25–31), and the building begun with the Sabbath (35:1–3).
>
> The prophets saw where this was going. Isaiah promises that foreigners "who keep the Sabbath and do not profane it" will be brought to the holy mountain "to minister to him" (Isa 56:6–7). His book closes with this: "from Sabbath to Sabbath, all flesh shall come to worship before me" (66:23). The Sabbath was a day of rest; and the rest was always meant to be taken in the presence of the LORD, among his people.
>
> So the pastoral question comes back with a different shape. The God who commands the day is the God who rested the way the slave rests, and who commands the strong to see that the weak can rest too. And when the Lord of the Sabbath came, this is what he did with it: "ought not this woman, a daughter of Abraham whom Satan bound… be loosed from this bond on the Sabbath day?" (Luke 13:16). The command to gather stands. But it is a command to come and be given rest. And it lays on the whole church the householder's duty: to bring that rest to those who cannot yet come.
