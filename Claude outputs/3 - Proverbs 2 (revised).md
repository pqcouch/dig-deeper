# Treasure Hidden to Be Found

Proverbs 2

Have you ever set up a treasure hunt for a child?

It takes ages. You hide the prize somewhere they’ll never think to look. You write the clues. You tuck the first one under a pillow, the next in the fridge, another behind the clock. Then you stand back and watch.

And what you see is a child working very hard. Running from room to room. Turning cushions over. Reading the clue again, frowning. Nobody is pretending. The search is real.

But something else is true at the same time. The child can’t earn that prize. It was bought before the hunt began. It was hidden by someone who wanted it found. Every clue was written by someone on the child’s side. And when the child gets stuck – they always do – the best thing they can do isn’t to try harder in silence. It’s to shout: “Dad! I can’t find the next one!”

Hold on to that picture. Proverbs 2 is a treasure hunt.

Our big question in this series is: how would we know if we were actually wise? The first answer was: we wouldn’t – not by looking at ourselves. Last time, from 1:1–7, we added: start by admitting that you’re the person this book is for – not the expert, but the simple. And I promised that chapter 2 would tell us where the gift of wisdom comes from.

Here it is, in verse 6:

> For the LORD gives wisdom.

So this week’s answer to our big question is: we wouldn’t know by checking ourselves. We would know by receiving.

That matters, because most of us go wrong in one of two ways. Some of us treat wisdom like an exam. If I read enough, pray enough and try hard enough, I’ll get there – and I’ll have earned it. Others go the opposite way: God gives it, so I’ll wait for it to arrive. One way makes us proud. The other makes us lazy. And both have one thing in common. We don’t ask.

Now picture the young man in this chapter. Verse 1 begins, “My son.” A father is talking to his son at the door of the family home, before the son heads out into the city. And the son has just heard something frightening. At the end of chapter 1, Wisdom stands in the street and says this about people who would not listen to her (1:28):

> Then they will call to me but I will not answer;  
> they will look for me but will not find me.

A closed door. A son hearing that might well ask: is it ever too late? How do I make sure that never happens to me? Chapter 2 is his father’s answer.

One more thing before we start. In Hebrew, Proverbs 2 is one long sentence – all twenty-two verses. Like a treasure hunt, each clue leads to the next. It goes like this: three “ifs”; then a “then”, with a “for”; then a second “then”; and finally, two rescues and a home. Let’s follow the clues.

## Three “ifs” (verses 1–4)

Verses 1 and 2:

> My son, if you accept my words  
> and store up my commands within you,  
> turning your ear to wisdom  
> and applying your heart to understanding,

The first “if” is: *receive what you’re given.* The father’s words are already there. The son must take hold of them and store them up – like putting something precious in a safe.

Verse 3:

> and if you call out for insight  
> and cry aloud for understanding,

The second “if” is: *call out for what you lack.* That’s a surprise. In a library the rule is “Silence, please!” In a classroom you put your hand up and wait. But here the father says: make a noise. Shout for it.

Verse 4:

> and if you look for it as for silver  
> and search for it as for hidden treasure,

The third “if” is: *dig for what you want.* Silver had to be mined. It was hard, dirty, slow work. The father wants his son to want wisdom as much as a miner wants silver.

Receive. Call out. Dig. Each one asks for more than the last. This isn’t a lazy faith. Notice, too, that the father isn’t barking orders. In Hebrew there isn’t a single command in the whole chapter. It’s all “if… then”: an invitation and a promise. But make no mistake – he is urging his son to act.

So which of the three do we skip? The middle one. Reading and digging feel like effort, so they feel useful. Asking feels like doing nothing. So we don’t ask.

James had learned from this chapter. He writes (1:5):

> If any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to him.

Like the child in the treasure hunt: when you’re stuck, shout.

## “Then”… “for” (verses 5–8)

Verse 5:

> then you will understand the fear of the LORD  
> and find the knowledge of God.

Here is what the son will find. Not a technique. Not a set of skills. He will find the fear of the LORD and the knowledge of God. The treasure at the end of this hunt is knowing God himself.

And now comes the most important little word in the chapter. Verse 6:

> For the LORD gives wisdom,  
> and from his mouth come knowledge and understanding.

“For.” Why can the father promise that the search will succeed? *Because the LORD gives wisdom.*

Notice which way round it is. The son doesn’t search in order to make God give. He searches because God gives. The effort isn’t a payment. It’s the way you receive a gift that’s already waiting for you.

That’s why verse 6 should never be pulled out on its own, like a verse on a fridge magnet. On its own, it’s a nice thought about a generous God. Left where it belongs, it’s a promise: *this* search will not be wasted.

And remember that closed door at the end of chapter 1? “They will *call* to me… they will not *find* me.” Now listen to chapter 2: “If you *call* out… then you will… *find*.” The same two words – call and find – with the opposite ending.

So if you have ever made a bad decision and decided that the door is now shut for you, this chapter is for you. Call out. You will find.

Verse 7:

> He holds victory in store for the upright,  
> he is a shield to those whose walk is blameless,

Did you hear the word “store”? In verse 1 the son was told to *store up* his father’s commands. Here in verse 7, God *holds in store*. Those are the only two times that word appears in the chapter, and we are meant to notice. The son stores up the words. God stores up the treasure. It’s like the parent who hid the prize before the hunt began. What the son needs was put aside for him before he ever started looking.

Verse 8:

> for he guards the course of the just  
> and protects the way of his faithful ones.

Count what God does in these three verses. He gives. He holds in store. He shields. He guards. He protects. Everything the son does comes with an “if”. Everything God does is certain.

## “Then” again (verses 9–11)

Verse 9:

> Then you will understand what is right and just and fair – every good path.

“Then you will understand” – the second time in the chapter. And did you recognise “right and just and fair”? We met those words last time, in 1:3, where they told us what the book is for. Those three words stand together in only two verses in the whole of Proverbs: 1:3 and here. What the book promised at the start, the search now delivers.

But look at the order. First, verse 5: you will know God. Then, verse 9: you will know what is right. God first; then other people. Wisdom isn’t, first of all, a set of life hacks for getting on with others. It starts with knowing God, and good living flows from that.

Verses 10 and 11:

> For wisdom will enter your heart,  
> and knowledge will be pleasant to your soul.  
> Discretion will protect you,  
> and understanding will guard you.

Wisdom moves in, and becomes the son’s bodyguard.

## Two rescues and a home (verses 12–22)

A bodyguard against what? Two dangers. Verse 12:

> Wisdom will save you from the ways of wicked men,  
> from men whose words are perverse,

And verse 16:

> It will save you also from the adulteress,  
> from the wayward wife with her seductive words,

In our Bibles those two verses begin slightly differently. In Hebrew they begin with exactly the same words: “to rescue you”. They are a matched pair. This is where the chapter turns.

The first danger is men whose words are twisted – like the gang in chapter 1 who try to talk the son into joining them. The second is a married woman who has broken faith with her husband, and she too uses words. Notice that both dangers are people, not ideas. And both come at the son through what they say. Just like the “Hi Mum” text, a false voice can sound very like a true one. Chapters 3 to 7 will come back to both of these voices. For now, the father simply names them.

Verse 17 says she

> has left the partner of her youth  
> and ignored the covenant she made before God.

She made her marriage promise in God’s sight. (The Hebrew could also mean “the covenant of her God”.) Either way, the heart of it is this: she walked away from a promise.

Then verse 19:

> None who go to her return  
> or attain the paths of life.

We need to be careful here. Proverbs describes where a road usually leads. It isn’t passing sentence on any person in this room. The same book says (28:13):

> He who conceals his sins does not prosper,  
> but whoever confesses and renounces them finds mercy.

If that road is part of your story, there is mercy.

The chapter ends in verses 20 to 22:

> Thus you will walk in the ways of good men  
> and keep to the paths of the righteous.  
> For the upright will live in the land,  
> and the blameless will remain in it;  
> but the wicked will be cut off from the land,  
> and the unfaithful will be torn from it.

That last word, “torn”, is rare. It’s found only four times in the Old Testament. One of them is Deuteronomy 28:63, where Moses warns Israel:

> You will be uprooted from the land you are entering to possess.

So this isn’t a promise that good people always get a comfortable life. It’s the language of the covenant – the binding agreement God made with Israel. God’s people lived in God’s land on God’s terms, and the land could be lost.

We aren’t Israelites waiting to enter Canaan. Hebrews 11:16 says that God’s people

> were longing for a better country – a heavenly one.

But the chapter’s point still stands. There is a home to be held, or lost.

## The treasure has a name

And that raises a problem. Verse 7: the treasure is stored up “for the upright”. Verse 21: “the upright will live in the land”. So who is upright? Not me. If the treasure is kept for the upright, then I’m locked out – back at the closed door of chapter 1.

Listen to Paul in 1 Corinthians 1:30:

> It is because of him that you are in Christ Jesus, who has become for us wisdom from God – that is, our righteousness, holiness and redemption.

Paul can’t say “wisdom” without straight away saying “righteousness”. Christ is the upright one, standing in our place. The treasure stored up for the upright has been given to people who were not upright. The “if” of Proverbs 2 has been met for us, by him, before we ever meet it.

And where is the treasure now? Colossians 2:3 says that in Christ

> are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.

Hidden treasure – the picture of verse 4. The hunt had a destination all along, and the destination is a person. Verse 5 promised “the knowledge of God”. And Paul says that God has made his light shine in our hearts

> to give us the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Christ. (2 Corinthians 4:6)

So the search isn’t called off. We still read, still dig, still ask. Paul puts it like this: “work out your salvation… *for* it is God who works in you” (Philippians 2:12–13). It’s the same “for” as verse 6. We work because God is at work.

## So, how would we know?

Think again of that son at his father’s door. He doesn’t have to be clever enough to beat every voice in the city. He has to keep his father’s words, call out, and dig – knowing that the LORD has already stored up what he needs.

It’s the same for us. So how would we know if we were actually wise? Not by checking ourselves. By receiving.

If you’re the hard-working type, hear this: your effort is real, but it isn’t a claim on God. If you’re the waiting type, hear this: “The LORD gives wisdom” is not a reason to stop digging. It’s the reason digging works. If you think the door has closed on you, hear this: call out, and you will find. And if you’re not yet a Christian, notice what the treasure is. It isn’t a better technique for life. It’s knowing God, in the face of Jesus Christ.

Let’s go back one last time to that treasure hunt. The prize was bought before the hunt began. It was hidden by someone who wanted it found. Every clue was written by someone on the child’s side. And the child who gets stuck and shouts for help isn’t failing the hunt. That’s exactly how it’s meant to work.

That’s Proverbs 2. The search is real, but it rests on a gift. And the One who hid the treasure is the One who gave his own Son, so that people who were not upright could find it.

So this week, let’s search harder, claim less – and ask. “For the LORD gives wisdom.”

Amen.
