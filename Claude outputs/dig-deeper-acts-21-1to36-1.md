## Headline Findings

**1. Luke's preface fails inside this pericope, and Luke says so with his own two words.** Luke 1:4 states the purpose of both volumes: *ἵνα ἐπιγνῷς περὶ ὧν **κατηχήθης** λόγων τὴν **ἀσφάλειαν*** — that you may know the certainty about the things you have been *instructed* in. **Both words recur here, and both fail.** At 21:21 the Jerusalem believers have been *κατηχήθησαν … περὶ σοῦ* — catechised about Paul — and what they were told is false. At 21:34 the tribune *οὐ δυνάμενος γνῶναι **τὸ ἀσφαλές** διὰ τὸν θόρυβον* — cannot get at the certainty because of the uproar. The passive *κατηχέω* + *περί* occurs twice in Luke–Acts: Luke 1:4 and Acts 21:21 `[T]`. *τὸ ἀσφαλές* is then sought twice more and never obtained — 22:30 (the tribune again) and 25:26 (*περὶ οὗ ἀσφαλές τι γράψαι τῷ κυρίῳ οὐκ ἔχω*, Festus). **The reader was given ἀσφάλεια in the preface and *ἀσφαλῶς* in the first sermon (2:36); from 21:34 onward the officials who must judge Paul cannot get it.** `[T]` on all four distributions; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the irony being designed. **Flagged for a Logos query** — this is the run's most distinctive claim and I do not know whether it is noticed at 21:34.

**2. Acts 21:23–27 is written in the technical vocabulary of Numbers 6 LXX.** Six items, one of them an NT hapax: *εὐχή* (Num 6:2, 5, 9, 12, 13, 18, 19, 21); *ἁγνίζω* (6:3 *ἁγνισθήσεται*); ***ἁγνισμός*** — **Num 6:5 *πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ*** against **Acts 21:26 *τῶν ἡμερῶν τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ***, the same three-word phrase, and the noun occurs once in the NT; *ξυράω* (6:9, 18, 19); *προσφορά / προσφέρω* (6:12–14); and **the seven days of Num 6:9** (*τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ἑβδόμῃ ξυρηθήσεται*) against **Acts 21:27** (*αἱ ἑπτὰ ἡμέραι*). **Luke rebuts the charge of *ἀποστασία ἀπὸ Μωϋσέως* (21:21) by narrating Paul's compliance in Moses' own words.** `[T]` on the lexical data; `[I]`, high, on the function. The overview's intertextual map has no Numbers entry at all.

**3. Luke has already shown Paul under a vow, so 21:23–26 is not out of character — and he did it unprompted.** *εὐχή* occurs three times in the NT and twice in Acts: **18:18** (*κειράμενος ἐν Κεγχρεαῖς τὴν κεφαλήν, εἶχεν γὰρ εὐχήν*) and 21:23 `[T]`. At Cenchreae nobody asked him; he shaved his head on his own initiative in Greece. **That materially changes the standing debate about whether Paul was right to comply at Jerusalem:** whatever else is true, Luke has not presented the rite as a compromise forced on a man who despised it. Name the debate; note that Luke has pre-empted its easiest form.

**4. The crowd that follows Paul is verbally the crowd that followed Jesus to the cross — and it is doing the opposite thing.** ***τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ* with *ἀκολουθέω* occurs twice in the New Testament**: Luke 23:27 (*Ἠκολούθει δὲ αὐτῷ πολὺ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ … αἳ ἐκόπτοντο καὶ ἐθρήνουν αὐτόν*) and Acts 21:36 (*ἠκολούθει γὰρ τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ … Αἶρε αὐτόν*) `[T]`, verified across the whole NT by accent-insensitive search. Same verb, same imperfect, same phrase — and the Gospel crowd mourns while the Acts crowd bays. **The parallel exists in order to invert.** Add *Αἶρε* (Luke 23:18 *Αἶρε τοῦτον*) and *ἐπεφώνουν* (Luke 23:21 *Σταύρου σταύρου*, of which Acts has 12:22, 21:34, 22:24) and the shape is settled. `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, high.

**5. Agabus's oracle fuses Luke's two forms of the passion prediction.** *παραδίδωμι εἰς χεῖρας* occurs three times in Luke–Acts: Luke 9:44 (*εἰς χεῖρας ἀνθρώπων*), Luke 24:7 (*εἰς χεῖρας ἀνθρώπων ἁμαρτωλῶν*), Acts 21:11 (*εἰς χεῖρας ἐθνῶν*) `[T]`. The *content* "to the Gentiles" comes from the other form, Luke 18:32 (*παραδοθήσεται τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*), which does not use *εἰς χεῖρας*. **Acts 21:11 is the only place in Luke–Acts where the two are combined**, and it is spoken about Paul. `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate–high.

**6. What the tribune does at 21:27–36 is what Jesus told the disciples would happen.** Luke 21:12: *ἐπιβαλοῦσιν ἐφ' ὑμᾶς **τὰς χεῖρας** αὐτῶν καὶ διώξουσιν, **παραδιδόντες** … ἀπαγομένους ἐπὶ **βασιλεῖς καὶ ἡγεμόνας** ἕνεκεν τοῦ **ὀνόματός** μου.* Acts 21 supplies each: *ἐπέβαλον ἐπ' αὐτὸν τὰς χεῖρας* (21:27), *παραδώσουσιν* (21:11), *ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ* (21:13) — and Felix, Festus and King Agrippa follow. `[T]` on the four lexical items; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the fulfilment being deliberate.

**7. Philip's four daughters are the narrated fulfilment of the Joel citation, reported without comment.** *θυγάτηρ* occurs three times in Acts: **2:17** (*αἱ θυγατέρες ὑμῶν προφητεύσουσιν*, in the Joel quotation), 7:21 (Pharaoh's daughter), and **21:9** (*θυγατέρες τέσσαρες παρθένοι προφητεύουσαι*) `[T]`. Luke lets the promise land in a subordinate clause in a travel narrative and says nothing about it. Note also that the host is **Philip**, last seen thirteen chapters earlier baptising a man the Law excluded from the assembly — and Paul is on his way to be accused of defiling the temple. `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, moderate, on the staging.

**8. Two seizures, and the second one saves him.** *ἐπιλαμβάνομαι* occurs twice in three verses: the crowd seizes Paul (21:30) and the tribune seizes Paul (21:33). And *ἐπιλαβόμενοι* + *ἕλκω* occurs twice in Acts — **16:19** (Philippi: Gentiles seize Paul over money and drag him to the magistrates) and **21:30** (Jerusalem: Jews seize Paul over the temple and drag him out of it) `[T]`. Rome is the danger at Philippi and the rescue at Jerusalem; the same two verbs carry both.

## Phase 0.5 — Book-Overview Active Layer

**Presenting situation.** The book exists to give a mixed church *ἀσφάλεια* that the Gentile mission is God's purpose and that it is safe to belong to this movement. **Bearing on this passage:** 21:20's *μυριάδες* of law-zealous Jewish believers and 21:25's citation of the Council decree are the two data on which that reassurance rests, and they are both here. So is the counter-evidence — a riot, an arrest, and a charge of apostasy. This pericope holds the book's central anxiety in one scene.

**Structural arc.** Seventh and last movement, **19:21–28:31, "The road to Rome"**. 21:15's *ἀνεβαίνομεν εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα* completes the resolve of 19:21 and 20:22; 21:33 begins the custody that does not end. **This pericope is the movement's hinge**: everything before it is Paul travelling freely, everything after it is Paul in Roman hands.

**Intertextual map.** The map records no source for Acts 21 at all, and warns that the later Pauline material is citation-light and should lean on Move 4. **Half right.** There is no citation formula here `[T]`. But Numbers 6 is present in force (Headline 2) and the Lukan Gospel is present in force (Headlines 4–6), and neither is in the map.

**Christological trajectory.** The overview's thread is a living, acting Christ whose commission passes outward, with the *acting subjects* of the main verbs being God, the Lord, the Spirit or the word. **Bearing on this passage:** the Spirit speaks three times (21:4, 21:11, and behind 20:23), God is the subject of 21:19's report (*ὧν ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός*), and the community's last word before the ascent is *τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω*. Paul, by contrast, is the grammatical *object* from v.30 to the end of the book: seized, dragged, bound, carried, followed.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Q1. What has to have happened before this passage for it to make sense?**

(a) **19:21** — the resolve to see Jerusalem and then Rome. (b) **20:22–23** — the Spirit's testimony that bonds await, which 21:4 and 21:11 execute; without it, 21:4's contrary counsel has nothing to contradict. (c) **Acts 15** — without the Council there is no decree for James to cite at 21:25 and no settled question for 21:21 to reopen. (d) **18:18** — Paul's own vow at Cenchreae, without which 21:23–26 looks like capitulation. (e) **20:4** — Trophimus the Ephesian is listed among the travelling party, which is the only reason 21:29's explanation works. Luke planted the name seventeen verses earlier and does not remind the reader that he did `[T]`.

**Q2. Why does this passage exist *here* rather than anywhere else?**

Because Luke has to get Paul from freedom into Roman custody, and he has chosen to do it in a way that answers three charges at once. The arrest could have been narrated in three verses. Instead Luke spends ten verses (21:17–26) establishing, **before** the riot, that Paul reported to the Jerusalem leadership, that the leadership glorified God for the Gentile mission, that the charge against Paul was hearsay, and that Paul submitted to a Mosaic rite in the temple at his own expense. **The accusation of 21:28 is refuted by the narrative before it is made.** That is why the scene is this long and why it sits here: the reader is to watch the riot knowing the charge is false.

There is a second answer, structural. This is the last place in Acts where the church of Jerusalem appears. James, the elders, and the *μυριάδες* are named, and then the book leaves them for good. Whatever Acts wishes to say about the relation of the Gentile mission to law-observant Jewish Christianity, it must say here or not at all.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

Two purposes, running against each other, which is why the pericope feels unsettled.

**The apologetic purpose** dominates 21:17–26. Luke is putting on record that Paul was received (*ἀσμένως ἀπεδέξαντο*, v.17), that the Jerusalem leadership *glorified God* for his Gentile work (v.20), that the accusation was known to be false by the men best placed to know (*ὧν κατήχηνται περὶ σοῦ οὐδέν ἐστιν*, v.24), and that Paul entered the temple to fulfil a Mosaic vow. Every element is a rebuttal of 21:28 and of the charges Paul will face in chapters 22–26.

**The theological purpose** dominates 21:1–14 and 21:27–36, and it is about the shape of obedience. The Spirit says what will happen; the disciples infer that it must not; Paul goes; the community concedes with the words of Gethsemane. Then the thing the Spirit said happens exactly.

The two purposes explain the pericope's oddest feature: **Luke never adjudicates 21:4.** He reports that disciples at Tyre told Paul *διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* not to go up, and he reports that Paul went, and he never says who was right. A writer with only the apologetic purpose would have removed 21:4. `[T]` on the non-adjudication; `[I]`, high, that it is deliberate.

### 2. Context

**Immediately before (20:17–38).** The Miletus farewell, which this pericope executes. 21:1's *ἀποσπασθέντας* answers 20:30's *ἀποσπᾶν* (same verb, opposite valence — what the wolves will do to the disciples is what the sea now does to Paul); 21:5's *θέντες τὰ γόνατα … προσευξάμενοι* and *προπεμπόντων* repeat 20:36–38 almost exactly; 21:11–13 executes 20:22–23. `[T]`.

**Immediately after (21:37–22:29).** The pericope's boundary is at v.36 and not v.40 because 21:37 begins a new scene: Paul speaks for the first time since v.13, and the whole apparatus of the defence speeches starts. But note the join: *ἀναβαθμός* occurs twice in the NT, at 21:35 and 21:40 `[T]`, so the steps he is carried up are the steps he then preaches from. **The place of his rescue becomes his pulpit.**

**Boundaries.** 21:1 opens with the sea and 21:36 closes with the mob; the unit is held together by a single movement — Miletus to the barracks — and by the *ἀναβαίνω* thread (21:12, 21:15, 21:31: they beg him not to *go up*; he *goes up*; word *goes up* to the tribune) `[T]`.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Movement | Marker |
|---|---|---|
| 1–6 | **Tyre: the first warning** | *Ὡς δὲ ἐγένετο ἀναχθῆναι* |
| 7–9 | **Caesarea: Philip's house** | *Ἡμεῖς δὲ τὸν πλοῦν διανύσαντες* |
| 10–14 | **Agabus, and the settled question** | *ἐπιμενόντων δέ* |
| 15–17 | **The ascent and the welcome** | *Μετὰ δὲ τὰς ἡμέρας ταύτας* |
| 18–26 | **James: the report, the problem, the rite** | *τῇ δὲ ἐπιούσῃ* |
| 27–30 | **The riot** | *Ὡς δὲ ἔμελλον αἱ ἑπτὰ ἡμέραι* |
| 31–36 | **The rescue and the arrest** | *ζητούντων τε αὐτὸν ἀποκτεῖναι* |

**The binding device is a countdown of days.** Seven at Tyre (v.4), one at Ptolemais (v.7), "the next day" (v.8), "many days" at Caesarea (v.10), "after these days" (v.15), "the following day" (v.18), "the next day" (v.26), seven days again (v.27). `[T]`. The narrative slows as it approaches Jerusalem and then, at v.27, the clock that has been running since v.26 runs out mid-sentence — *ὡς δὲ ἔμελλον αἱ ἑπτὰ ἡμέραι συντελεῖσθαι*, "when the seven days were about to be completed". **The rite is never finished.** Luke tells us the offering was to be made for each man (v.26) and then the days are interrupted. That unfinished rite is the pericope's structural point and it is easy to read past.

**The two halves are joined by the temple.** *ἱερόν* occurs four times in vv.26–30 (26, 28, 29, 30) and not at all in vv.1–25 `[T]`. Paul goes in (26), is seen in it (27), is accused of defiling it (28), is dragged out of it (30), and the doors shut behind him (30). **The last thing Acts says about the temple is that its doors were shut with Paul on the outside.**

### 4. Linking Words

- **γάρ (21:3, 29, 36)** — three explanatory asides, and all three explain something the reader would otherwise get wrong: why the ship stopped at Tyre, why the Asians assumed what they assumed, and why Paul had to be carried. The narrator intervenes only to prevent misunderstanding.
- **τε (21:20, 30, 31)** — Luke's連 connective, binding the report to the response and the riot to its consequences.
- **οὖν (21:22, 23)** — *τί οὖν ἐστιν; … τοῦτο οὖν ποίησον.* The two *οὖν*s carry James's whole argument: here is the problem, therefore do this. It is a practical proposal, not a doctrinal ruling, and the connectives say so.
- **δέ (21:25)** — *περὶ **δὲ** τῶν πεπιστευκότων ἐθνῶν.* The adversative marks the boundary James is careful not to cross: this proposal is for Paul, not for Gentile believers, whose position is already settled. **The *δέ* is doing more theological work than any word in the paragraph.**
- **πλήν** — absent, notably. Where the Miletus speech qualified (20:23), this pericope asserts.
- **ἵνα (21:24)** — the purpose of paying the expenses is *ἵνα ξυρήσονται* — that **they** may shave. The vow is theirs; Paul's part is the cost and the accompanying purification.

### 5. Parallels

**Within Acts — Philippi and Jerusalem.** *ἐπιλαβόμενοι* + *ἕλκω* occurs at **16:19** and **21:30** and nowhere else in Acts `[T]`. At Philippi the seizers are Gentiles whose income Paul has damaged; at Jerusalem they are Jews whose temple he is alleged to have defiled. Both scenes end with Roman intervention, and in both the Romans' first act is to restrain Paul. The pair frames the whole European mission.

**Within Acts — 11:18 and 21:14/20.** *ἡσυχάζω* occurs twice in Acts: **11:18** (*ἡσύχασαν καὶ ἐδόξασαν τὸν θεόν*, the circumcision party after Peter's Cornelius report) and **21:14** (*ἡσυχάσαμεν*) `[T]`. And 21:20 has *ἐδόξαζον τὸν θεόν*. **The two elements of 11:18 recur six verses apart in Acts 21**, the first of Paul's companions accepting God's will about Paul, the second of James's circle accepting God's work among the Gentiles. `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, moderate.

**Within Acts — the Council replayed.** *ἐξηγέομαι* occurs four times in Acts: 10:8, **15:12** (*ἐξηγουμένων ὅσα ἐποίησεν ὁ θεὸς … ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*), **15:14**, and **21:19** (*ἐξηγεῖτο … ὧν ἐποίησεν ὁ θεὸς ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*) `[T]`. 21:19 reproduces 15:12's construction — the verb, *ὅσα/ὧν ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός*, *ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*. Paul reports to James exactly as he reported to the Council, and six verses later James cites the Council's decree. **The scene is deliberately staged as a return to Acts 15.** Note also *ἀρχαῖος*: three in Acts, at **15:7**, **15:21** and **21:16** `[T]` — two of them in the Council speeches that set the terms 21:25 repeats.

**Within Luke–Acts — the passion.** See Headlines 4–6, and:

| Luke | Acts 21 | Shared |
|---|---|---|
| 23:27 *Ἠκολούθει … πολὺ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ* | 21:36 *ἠκολούθει … τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ* | The only two in the NT `[T]` |
| 23:18 *Αἶρε τοῦτον* | 21:36 *Αἶρε αὐτόν* (and 22:22) | `[T]` |
| 23:21 *ἐπεφώνουν* | 21:34 *ἐπεφώνουν* | 4 in the NT, 3 Lukan `[T]` |
| 23:26 *ἐπιλαβόμενοι* (Simon of Cyrene) | 21:30, 33 *ἐπιλαβόμενοι / ἐπελάβετο* | Contributory |
| 22:42 *μὴ τὸ θέλημά μου ἀλλὰ τὸ σὸν **γινέσθω*** | 21:14 *Τοῦ κυρίου τὸ **θέλημα γινέσθω*** | *θέλημα* + *γινέσθω*: only these two in Luke–Acts `[T]` |
| 9:44 / 24:7 *παραδοθῆναι εἰς χεῖρας* | 21:11 *παραδώσουσιν εἰς χεῖρας ἐθνῶν* | `[T]` |

**The Gethsemane line is spoken by the wrong people.** At Luke 22:42 Jesus says it about himself; at Acts 21:14 **Paul's companions say it about Paul, having failed to talk him out of going.** The submission is theirs, not his — he was never in doubt (21:13). That is a finer and more useful observation than the flat "Paul is like Jesus" reading. `[I]`, moderate–high.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Luke comments six times, and the pattern is instructive.

- **v.4** *οἵτινες τῷ Παύλῳ ἔλεγον **διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος** μὴ ἐπιβαίνειν.* The narrator vouches for the *medium* and not the *message* — he says the telling was through the Spirit and does not say Paul should have obeyed. This is the most carefully worded clause in the pericope.
- **v.8** *Φιλίππου τοῦ εὐαγγελιστοῦ ὄντος ἐκ τῶν ἑπτά.* An identification the reader needs after thirteen chapters, given without further comment. *εὐαγγελιστής* occurs three times in the NT and once in Acts `[T]`.
- **v.9** *θυγατέρες τέσσαρες παρθένοι προφητεύουσαι.* See Headline 7. Luke reports the fulfilment of the Joel promise and moves on within the same sentence.
- **v.14** *μὴ πειθομένου δὲ αὐτοῦ ἡσυχάσαμεν.* First person. The narrator includes himself among those who tried and failed to stop Paul.
- **v.29** *ἦσαν γὰρ προεωρακότες Τρόφιμον … ὃν **ἐνόμιζον** ὅτι εἰς τὸ ἱερὸν εἰσήγαγεν.* The narrator states flatly that the charge was a mistaken inference. **This is the only place in Acts where Luke explicitly labels an accusation against Paul as factually wrong**, and he does it in an aside `[T]`.
- **v.35** *συνέβη βαστάζεσθαι αὐτὸν ὑπὸ τῶν στρατιωτῶν διὰ τὴν βίαν τοῦ ὄχλου.* *συμβαίνω* signals the incidental; the detail is included because it is undignified and true.

**What the narrator does not do:** he does not evaluate James's proposal, does not say whether Paul was right to accept it, does not report whether the offering was made, and does not adjudicate 21:4. Four silences in twenty verses, all at the points a preacher most wants an answer.

### 7. Vocabulary

Counts against SBLGNT via the MorphGNT lemma index; surface searches run accent-insensitively over NFD-stripped text.

**ζηλωτής (21:20).** Three in Acts: **1:13** (Simon *ὁ ζηλωτής*), **21:20** (*πάντες ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου **ὑπάρχουσιν***), **22:3** (Paul: *ζηλωτὴς **ὑπάρχων** τοῦ θεοῦ καθὼς πάντες ὑμεῖς ἐστε σήμερον*) `[T]`. Note the same participle/verb in the last two, twenty verses apart: **Paul's next speech opens by claiming precisely the identity James has just attributed to the Jerusalem believers** — and extends it to the crowd that is trying to kill him. The genitives are the argument: zealous *for the law* (21:20), zealous *for God* (22:3).

**κατηχέω (21:21, 24).** Three in Acts: 18:25 (Apollos *κατηχημένος τὴν ὁδὸν τοῦ κυρίου*) and twice here — and Luke 1:4. See Headline 1. In 18:25 the catechesis is sound but incomplete; in 21:21 it is simply false. `[T]`.

**ἀποστασία (21:21).** Two in the NT, one in Acts. The technical noun for defection, and the charge is *ἀποστασίαν … ἀπὸ Μωϋσέως*. Neither NASB95 nor ESV keeps the noun (§8).

**ἁγνίζω / ἁγνισμός (21:24, 26).** *ἁγνίζω* three times in Acts: 21:24, 21:26, **24:18** — where Paul's defence before Felix is *εὗρόν με **ἡγνισμένον** ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ* `[T]`. *ἁγνισμός* is an NT hapax and a Numbers 6 technical term (Headline 2). **The rite James proposed becomes Paul's legal defence three chapters later.**

**προσφορά (21:26) and θόρυβος (21:34).** *προσφορά* twice in Acts: 21:26 and **24:17**. *θόρυβος* three times: 20:1, 21:34, **24:18**. So Acts 24:17–18 recites **three** items from this pericope — the offering, the purification, and the absence of a *θόρυβος* — in two verses `[T]`. **Paul's defence before Felix is a point-by-point account of Acts 21**, which is the strongest internal evidence that Luke composed the two scenes together.

**εὐχή (21:23).** Three in the NT, two in Acts: 18:18 and 21:23. See Headline 3. `[T]`.

**συγχέω / συγχύννω (21:27, 31).** Five in the NT, **all in Acts**: 2:6 (the Pentecost crowd *συνεχύθη*), 9:22 (Saul *συνέχυννεν* the Jews of Damascus), 19:32 (the Ephesian *ἐκκλησία συγκεχυμένη*), 21:27, 21:31 (*ὅλη συγχύννεται Ἰερουσαλήμ*) `[T]`. A wholly Lukan word, and its arc is worth the pulpit: the first confusion is Pentecost's, caused by everyone hearing their own language; the last is Jerusalem's, caused by nobody being able to hear anything (21:34). And 9:22 is Saul himself doing the confounding.

**κοινόω (21:28).** Three in Acts: 10:15, 11:9, 21:28 `[T]` — and *κοινός* at 10:14, 10:28, 11:8. **Eleven chapters separate the vision from the accusation.** Peter was told not to call *κοινόν* what God had cleansed; Paul is charged with having *κεκοίνωκεν* the holy place. The sweep has this; what it does not have is that the charge is stated by Luke, four sentences later, to be a mistaken inference (v.29).

**τόπος (21:28 ×2).** Eighteen in Acts. The relevant cluster is **6:13** (*κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου καὶ τοῦ νόμου* — against Stephen), **6:14** (*καταλύσει τὸν τόπον τοῦτον*), and 21:28 (*κατὰ τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ νόμου καὶ τοῦ τόπου τούτου* … *τὸν ἅγιον τόπον τοῦτον*) `[T]`. The charge against Paul is the charge against Stephen with one item added — *τοῦ λαοῦ* — and the witnesses at 6:13 are called *ψευδεῖς*. Luke does not repeat the word here; he supplies v.29 instead.

**βαστάζω (21:35).** Four in Acts: 3:2 (the lame man carried to the gate), **9:15** (*τοῦ **βαστάσαι** τὸ ὄνομά μου ἐνώπιον ἐθνῶν τε καὶ βασιλέων*), **15:10** (the yoke *οὔτε οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν οὔτε ἡμεῖς ἰσχύσαμεν **βαστάσαι***), 21:35 `[T]`. **The man commissioned to *carry* the name before Gentiles and kings is *carried* by Gentile soldiers, up the steps, on his way to speak before a king.** And the middle term is the yoke of the law, in the chapter whose decree v.25 cites. Three-way, and all `[T]`.

**σπεῖρα (21:31) and χιλίαρχος (21:31).** *σπεῖρα* three times in Acts: **10:1** (the Italian cohort — Cornelius), 21:31, **27:1** (the Augustan cohort — the voyage to Rome) `[T]`. Acts' three cohorts produce the first Gentile convert, the arrest, and the passage to Rome. *χιλίαρχος* occurs seventeen times in Acts and **21:31 is the first**; from here to 25:23 the tribune is a fixture. The Roman officer enters the story at the moment the Jewish city expels Paul.

**ἀκολουθέω (21:36).** Four in Acts: 12:8, 12:9 (Peter following the angel out of prison), 13:43, 21:36 `[T]`. **The last act of following in Acts is a mob following Paul to demand his death** — and its Gospel counterpart followed Jesus to Golgotha.

**λαός against ὄχλος (21:35–36).** *ὄχλος* at v.27, v.34, v.35; *λαός* at v.28, v.30, v.36. In consecutive clauses: *διὰ τὴν βίαν τοῦ **ὄχλου**, ἠκολούθει γὰρ τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ **λαοῦ***. In Luke–Acts *λαός* is characteristically the people of God (48 times in Acts) `[T]`. **Luke switches term at the last moment, so that the shout is raised not by a crowd but by *the people*.** And the Western text removes the *λαός* from both places (see Textual Variants) — the scribes felt the oddity and smoothed it, which is external evidence that it is odd.

**ἑπτά (21:4, 8, 27).** Three times in the pericope, in three senses: seven days at Tyre, "one of the seven", the seven days of the vow `[T]`. Probably not designed; worth noticing before building anything on it.

**Two lemma-collisions to avoid.** (a) *μυριάς* occurs twice in Acts — 19:19 (fifty thousand pieces of silver) and 21:20 (myriads of believers). Same lemma, entirely different function; **not** a chain. (b) *ἀναβαίνω* at 21:12, 21:15 and 21:31 is a real repetition, but 21:31's subject is a *report* going up to the tribune, not a person going up to Jerusalem; note it as wordplay at low confidence, not as a thread.

That is worth saying to a preacher once, as a standing caution, rather than re-discovering each week.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Vv. 1–17 are a slow, warm, domestic travelogue.** Wives and children on the beach; a night here, seven days there; a household with four daughters; an old disciple from Cyprus with a spare room. The first-person plural is continuous. It reads like a memoir.

**Vv. 18–26 change register entirely** — the vocabulary is official (*ἀπεδέξαντο*, *ἐξηγεῖτο*, *ἐδόξαζον*, *κρίναντες*), the sentences are longer, and the exchange is between representatives, not friends. This is minutes of a meeting.

**Vv. 27–36 are violent and fast.** Eleven finite verbs of action in ten verses. And the "we" **disappears at v.18 and does not return** until 27:1 `[T]` — Luke narrates the arrest from outside. Whatever the reason, the effect is that the reader loses the companion's-eye view at exactly the moment Paul is seized.

**The pericope's emotional centre is v.13, and it is a rebuke.** *Τί ποιεῖτε κλαίοντες καὶ **συνθρύπτοντές** μου τὴν καρδίαν;* — *συνθρύπτω* is an NT hapax, "to crush small, to pulverise". Paul is not comforted by their grief; he says it is breaking him. Preachers usually make v.13 heroic. Luke makes it costly: the man is being worn down by the love of his friends, and says so.

### 10. Repetition

- ***δέω*** ×4 in the pericope (11 ×2, 13, 33) — the chain from 20:22, now executed. Agabus *binds himself* to say it, Paul says he is ready to *be bound*, the tribune *binds* him.
- ***ἡμέρα*** ×8 (4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 15, 26, 27) — the countdown; see §3.
- ***ἀναβαίνω*** ×3 (12, 15, 31).
- ***ἱερόν*** ×4, all in vv.26–30; **the word does not occur in the first twenty-five verses** `[T]`.
- ***πᾶς*** ×7 (18, 20 ×2, 21, 22, 24, 27) — heavily concentrated in the James scene: *πάντες οἱ πρεσβύτεροι*, *πάντες ζηλωταί*, *πάντας Ἰουδαίους*, *πάντως*, *γνώσονται πάντες*, *πάντα τὸν ὄχλον*. The totalising vocabulary of the Miletus speech reappears, now in the mouths of others and in the service of a problem.
- ***παραλαμβάνω*** ×3 (24, 26, 32) — James says *take* these men; Paul *takes* them; the tribune *takes* soldiers. The same verb runs the proposal, the compliance and the rescue.
- ***κράζω*** ×2 (28, 36) — the riot's first and last sound.

### 11. Quotation / Allusion

**No citation formula occurs in the pericope** `[T]`. What it has instead is a sustained unmarked use of **Numbers 6**, a dense set of **intra-Lukan** echoes of the passion, and a heavy internal-echo load.

#### Move 1 — the sources in their own context

**Numbers 6:1–21 LXX (the law of the Nazirite).** Swete's text supplies: *εὔξηται **εὐχὴν** ἀφαγνίσασθαι ἁγνείαν Κυρίῳ* (v.2); *ἁγνισθήσεται* (v.3); ***πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ*** … *ἕως ἂν πληρωθῶσιν αἱ ἡμέραι* (v.5); *ἐὰν δέ τις ἀποθάνῃ ἐξάπινα ἐπ' αὐτῷ … **ξυρήσεται** τὴν κεφαλήν … **τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ἑβδόμῃ** ξυρηθήσεται* (v.9); *προσάξει … ἀμνόν* (v.12); *ᾗ ἂν ἡμέρᾳ **πληρώσῃ ἡμέρας εὐχῆς** αὐτοῦ, **προσοίσει*** (v.13); *ξυρήσεται ὁ ηὐγμένος … τὴν κεφαλὴν τῆς εὐχῆς αὐτοῦ* (v.18). The chapter's own logic: a vow is undertaken for a set number of days; if it is defiled the days are void and a seven-day purification with shaving and offerings begins; at completion the votary shaves at the sanctuary door and offerings are presented.

#### Move 3 — OT to OT

Numbers 6's vow is a *voluntary* holiness undertaken by a lay Israelite — the one provision by which someone outside the priesthood may take on priestly restrictions for a term. Its later career runs through Samson (Judg 13, involuntary and lifelong) and Samuel (1 Sam 1:11), i.e. from a temporary lay option to a lifelong prophetic calling. Acts 21 uses it in its original Numbers form and for its original purpose — a lay Israelite demonstrating covenant seriousness — which is exactly why it works as a public answer to 21:21.

### 12. Genre

Travel narrative with an embedded **prophetic sign-act** (21:11) and an embedded **deliberative consultation** (21:18–26), closing in a **riot-and-rescue** scene.

The sign-act is the formally interesting element. Agabus does what Isaiah (Isa 20), Jeremiah (Jer 13, 19, 27) and Ezekiel (Ezek 4–5, 12) did: he performs the oracle on his own body and then interprets it, with the messenger formula. Luke gives him the formula in its Septuagintal shape — ***Τάδε λέγει τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον***, where the prophets have *τάδε λέγει Κύριος*. `[T]` on the substitution; `[I]`, high, on its significance: **the Spirit is put in the place the formula reserves for the LORD.** That is a Christological/pneumatological datum of the first order, made in passing, and it is the strongest thing in the pericope for the doctrine of the Spirit.

**Genre warning.** The riot scene's conventions — the mistaken accusation, the crowd, the timely officer, the citizenship revealed at the last moment (22:25) — are the conventions of the ancient novel as well as of history `[S]`. That observation is often used to discount the narrative. It cuts no ice either way: conventional shapes are how ancient writers narrate real events too. Note it if the congregation will meet it; stake nothing on it.

### 13. Copycat

**Normative:**

- **21:14's *Τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω*.** A prayer the church may always pray, and Luke gives it to a community that has just lost an argument.
- **21:19–20's pattern:** report what God has done; glorify God for it. The subject of the report is *ὧν ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός*, not what Paul achieved.
- **21:25's principle.** Gentile believers are not placed under the law. James restates it unprompted, in the middle of asking Paul to observe it. **The one thing in this passage that is explicitly legislated is the freedom of the Gentiles.**
- **The willingness of 21:13** — to accept cost for the name — is transferable as a disposition.

**Descriptive — do not transfer:**

- **The vow, the shaving, the expenses.** A first-century Jewish rite in a standing temple. Nothing here legislates ritual accommodation, and the temple is gone.
- **Agabus's method.** Prophetic sign-acts are not a technique to be copied, and 21:11's formula is not a licence for "Thus says the Spirit" over one's own impressions.
- **21:4 and 21:12.** The disciples' inference is reported, not endorsed. **Do not build a doctrine of guidance on either side of this verse.**
- **Paul's decision to go.** Descriptive. Acts does not say he was right; it says he was not persuaded, and it lets the community's *τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω* stand as the last word.
- **James's proposal.** Whether it was wise is genuinely contested and the text does not settle it. See Difficult Verses.

**The specific trap.** Because the passage contains a real prophetic oracle and real spiritual counsel that pull in opposite directions, it is mined for a theory of guidance. **The text will not support one.** What it supports is narrower and more useful: *the Spirit's information was consistent and the humans' inferences from it varied.* That is a finding about this narrative, not a method.

### 14. Bible Timeline

Pentecost is the deadline (20:16), so the arrest falls a few days after Paul's arrival, in the spring. In Acts' internal reckoning this is roughly twenty-five years after the ascension and the last time Paul is a free man in the narrative: the next two years are Caesarean custody (24:27), then the voyage, then two more years in Rome (28:30).

In redemptive-historical terms the pericope sits at a joint the whole book has been approaching: **the moment when the Jerusalem temple and the Gentile mission become, in practice, incompatible** — not because Paul says so (he is in the temple, paying for sacrifices) but because a crowd in the temple will not have him there. Luke's care to show Paul inside the temple, purified and paying, is what makes the incompatibility the crowd's doing and not Paul's.

### 15. Who Am I?

**The Jerusalem elders** are men managing a real pastoral problem with a practical proposal and a limited grasp of what is about to happen. They are not villains and Luke does not present them as such: they glorify God for the Gentile mission (v.20) and restate the Gentiles' freedom unprompted (v.25). Their proposal fails, and it fails through no fault they could have foreseen — the trouble comes from **Asian** Jews (v.27), not Jerusalem ones.

**The crowd** acts on a mistaken inference the narrator corrects in the next sentence (v.29). That is the most sobering thing in the pericope: sincere religious outrage, in the right place, about the wrong fact.

**Paul** is the object of nearly every verb after v.27. He is seen, seized, dragged, beaten, arrested, bound, carried, followed. He says nothing between v.13 and v.37.

**The modern hearer.** Not Paul; usually the friends at Tyre and Caesarea, who love the man, hear something true from God, draw a reasonable conclusion, are wrong, and end by praying rightly. That is where most people in a congregation actually stand.

**God's part.** The Spirit testifies (4, 11) and speaks with the LORD's own formula (11); God is the subject of what has been done among the Gentiles (19); and the will that is finally invoked is the Lord's (14). Paul's arrest is not narrated as a defeat for that will, and it is not narrated as its obvious execution either. Luke leaves it unexplained — and then, in 23:11, has the Lord stand by Paul at night and say *θάρσει*.

### 16. So What?

**First: the church's certainty and the world's certainty are different things, and only one of them is available.** The reader has *ἀσφάλεια* (Luke 1:4) and heard it preached *ἀσφαλῶς* (Acts 2:36). The tribune cannot get at *τὸ ἀσφαλές* (21:34) and neither can Festus (25:26). The gospel is not less certain for being unprovable in a magistrate's court; it is differently certain, and Luke's whole two-volume project is built on the difference.

**Second: a good man's reputation was destroyed by accurate-sounding hearsay, and the leaders who knew it was false could not stop it.** *κατηχήθησαν … περὶ σοῦ* (v.21) — they had been *taught* about Paul. James knows it is *οὐδέν* (v.24). The plan to correct it is reasonable, public and expensive, and it does not work. Congregations know this situation.

**Third: obedience is not always vindicated in the narrative.** Paul goes to Jerusalem, is nearly killed, and is arrested; the collection he came to deliver is mentioned only in a defence speech (24:17); the vow is never completed. Luke does not tidy any of it. **What he gives instead is the sentence the friends said when they had run out of arguments** — *τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω* — the sentence Jesus prayed in the garden, now said by people who could not see how it would turn out. That is the passage's pastoral centre.

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**Τάδε λέγει τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον (21:11).** The Septuagintal messenger formula is *τάδε λέγει Κύριος*, several hundred times across the prophets. Luke substitutes the Holy Spirit. `[T]` on the substitution. The formula is not decorative — it is the standing claim to speak the words of YHWH — and Luke lets a Christian prophet use it of the Spirit without comment. Compare 13:2 (*εἶπεν τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον*) and 28:25 (*καλῶς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον ἐλάλησεν διὰ Ἠσαΐου*).

**διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος ἔλεγον … μὴ ἐπιβαίνειν (21:4).** The construction is an imperfect of repeated telling (*ἔλεγον*) with an infinitive of prohibition. Grammatically the *διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* attaches to *ἔλεγον* — the *telling* was through the Spirit. Whether the *content of the prohibition* was is exactly what the Greek leaves open, and what interpreters supply. **The honest reading, and the one 20:23 supports, is that the Spirit's testimony was consistently about what awaited (bonds), and the prohibition was the disciples' loving inference from it.** `[T]` on the syntax; `[I]`, moderate–high.

**δήσας ἑαυτοῦ τοὺς πόδας καὶ τὰς χεῖρας (21:11).** SBLGNT and NA28 read *ἑαυτοῦ*; feet before hands, which is the order of someone binding himself. A detail the English versions keep and readers skip.

**ξυρήσονται (21:24).** SBLGNT prints the future middle indicative after *ἵνα* (NA28 apparatus records the aorist subjunctive *ξυρήσωνται* in A B C L Ψ and others). Either way the subject is the four men, not Paul: **Paul pays; they shave.** Paul's own act is *ἁγνίσθητι σὺν αὐτοῖς* — be purified along with them. He is not represented as taking a Nazirite vow himself here.

**συνέβη βαστάζεσθαι (21:35).** *συμβαίνω* + infinitive, "it came about that he was being carried". Luke uses *συμβαίνω* for the incidental and unplanned (3:10; 20:19). The present infinitive makes it durative — he was carried the whole way up.

**ἐπεφώνουν … ἄλλοι ἄλλο τι (21:34).** The classic Greek idiom for a crowd at cross purposes. Luke used it verbatim at 19:32 of the Ephesian assembly (*ἄλλοι μὲν οὖν ἄλλο τι ἔκραζον*) `[T]`. **The two riots in Acts are described with the same idiom, and in both the crowd does not know why it is there.**

**τὸ ἀσφαλές (21:34).** Neuter adjective as substantive, "the certain thing", i.e. the facts. Luke's word, and the abstract noun of Luke 1:4.

### Textual Variants

Worked from **the NA28 apparatus for chapters 20–21**, supplied 9 September 2026 and filed at `_texts/logos-exports/NA28 apparatus Acts 20-21.txt`. Everything below is `[T]` from the edition.

**The character of the chapter's tradition.** Acts 21 is one of the most heavily Western-attested chapters in the NT: of roughly forty variation units in vv.1–36, **D (Codex Bezae) is cited in the great majority**, frequently alone or with *gig* and the Syriac. This is the standing feature of Acts' textual history and it matters here more than usual, because several of D's readings are not slips but *interpretation*.

**Four variants with exegetical weight.**

**(a) 21:20 — *ἐν τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις* / *ἐν τῇ Ἰουδαίᾳ*.** Text (*how many myriads there are **among the Jews** who have believed*): A B C E 33. 945. 1175. 1739. 1891. 2818 vg bo; Eusebius. Variants: *τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις* 𝔓⁷⁴ · *Ἰουδαίων* L Ψ 323. 614. 1241. 1505 𝔪 sy · ***ἐν τῇ Ἰουδαίᾳ* D gig p sy sa** · omitted א. **D localises the myriads to Judea**, which materially shrinks the claim: on the text, tens of thousands of *Jews everywhere* have believed; on D's reading, tens of thousands *in Judea*. The text is better attested and is the harder claim. Worth knowing because 21:20 is the single most-cited verse against reading Acts as narrating a church that has left Israel, and the Western tradition felt its force.

**(b) 21:22 — the length of James's question.** Text (*πάντως ἀκούσονται ὅτι ἐλήλυθας*, "they will certainly hear that you have come"): B C\* 453. 614. 1175. 1739. 2818 sy co. Longer: *δεῖ συνελθεῖν πλῆθος· ἀκούσονται γάρ* ("a crowd must gather, for they will hear") 𝔓⁷⁴ א A C E 33. 945. 1739 latt; similarly D L Ψ 𝔪. **The longer reading turns an observation into a requirement** — that an assembly must be convened. The shorter is likelier original (it is hard to see why a scribe would remove a convening-instruction) and it keeps James's proposal informal rather than juridical.

**(c) 21:25 — the Western reshaping of the decree. *Verified 10 September 2026, and the flag is now removed.*** D L 323. 614. 1241 𝔪 gig add *μηδὲν τοιοῦτον τηρεῖν αὐτοὺς εἰ μή* ("that they observe no such thing except…") before the four items; C E Ψ and others have a variant of the same expansion; D and gig also mark an omission at the end of the list. Text: 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B 33. 1175 vg sy co.

As first written this section said the Western text "is being handled the same way it handles the decree at 15:20 and 15:29" and marked the claim `[S]`, because the apparatus in hand covered chapters 20–21 only. **The apparatus for the whole book was then filed, and the claim checks out** `[T]`:

| | 15:20 | 15:29 | 21:25 |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***καὶ πνικτοῦ* omitted** | **D gig; Ir^1739mg** | **D l; Ir^1739mg Tert Hier** | D gig mark an omission |
| **negative Golden Rule added** — *καὶ ὅσα ἂν μὴ θέλωσιν αὐτοῖς γίνεσθαι ἑτέροις μὴ ποιεῖν* | **D 323. 945. 1739. 1891 sa; Ir^1739mg Eus^1739mg** | **D 323. 614. 945. 1739. 1891 l p w sy sa; Ir^1739mg Eus^1739mg Cyp** | — |

**The pattern is consistent and it is a single editorial instinct at work in all three places: drop the one purely ritual item (*πνικτόν*, strangled meat) and add an ethical maxim.** The Western text converts the apostolic decree from a food-law compromise into a moral rule — and it does so at every one of the decree's three appearances in Acts. Irenaeus attests both moves in the second century, so the reshaping is early. `[T]` on all three units; `[I]`, high, that it is one instinct rather than three accidents.

**What follows for preaching v.25.** Not that the text is uncertain — NA28's reading is well attested at each point — but that **the decree was the part of Acts the early church most wanted to adjust**, which is worth one sentence before it is quoted as a fixed formula.

**(d) 21:35–36 — the Western text removes *λαός*.** At 21:35 D latt sy read ***λαοῦ*** for *ὄχλου* ("the violence of **the people**"), and at 21:36 **D gig omit *τοῦ λαοῦ*** altogether. `[T]` from the apparatus. Taken together, the Western tradition **flattens the *ὄχλος → λαός* alternation** that §7 identifies as the point of the two clauses — either by making both *λαός* or by removing it. **That is external evidence that the alternation is marked**, and it is the same phenomenon observed at 20:28, where the Byzantine tradition normalised an unusual word order. *Twice in two consecutive pericopes, the scribes have smoothed exactly the feature the exegesis turns on.* Note the method: **where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied.**

**Two smaller units.** 21:20 ⸀*κύριον* (D Ψ 323. 614. 1241. 1505 𝔪 gig vg sy sa) for *θεόν* — the same *θεός/κύριος* alternation as 20:28, and here of no consequence. 21:31 ⸁*συγκέχυται* (𝔓⁷⁴ א E L Ψ 𝔪) for *συγχύννεται* (א\* A B D 33 vg) — perfect against present; the present is more vivid and better attested among the primary uncials.

### Historical and Cultural Background

Only what the text needs.

**The temple barrier.** The charge at 21:28 presupposes the *soreg* inscription forbidding Gentiles to pass into the inner courts on pain of death `[S]`. Luke does not explain it, which tells us his first readers did not need it explained. Two things follow: the charge was capital, and the crowd's action was not lawless in their own eyes.

**The vow and its cost.** Paying another's offerings was a recognised act of piety `[S]`. Numbers 6:14 requires a lamb, a ewe-lamb and a ram per votary, plus grain and drink offerings — four men is a considerable outlay. **The proposal is expensive, and Luke's word is *δαπάνησον* (v.24), an NT rarity `[T]`.** James is asking Paul to make a costly, public, verifiable gesture.

**The Antonia and the steps.** The *παρεμβολή* (v.34) overlooked the temple court and was reached by stairs `[S]`; the tribune's cohort was garrisoned there precisely for festival crowds, which is why the response is so quick.

**"The Egyptian" (21:38).** Just outside the pericope, but it explains the tribune's behaviour inside it: he takes Paul for a known insurgent, which is why he binds first and asks afterwards.

### Original Audience Reception

**Narrative-world.** The Jerusalem believers hear a report of Gentile conversions and glorify God, then immediately raise a problem about Paul's reputation. Both reactions are sincere.

**For Theophilus and the book's first readers**, three things land.

1. **The charge against Paul was false, and the narrative proves it before the charge is made.** A reader who has heard that Paul taught Jews to abandon Moses now has, in order: the Jerusalem leadership saying the report is baseless, Paul purifying himself in the temple, and Paul paying for sacrifices.
2. **The Gentile mission was endorsed by James and the elders, who glorified God for it** — and the decree that protects Gentile freedom is restated by the Jerusalem side, unprompted.
3. **The church in Judea is enormous and law-observant.** Whatever a Gentile reader had been told about the movement's relation to Israel, *μυριάδες … ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου* is Luke's own figure.

**One thing they would have heard that we do not.** The passion-shape of the arrest (Headlines 4–6) works on a reader who has just finished Luke's Gospel and has the *Αἶρε* cry and the following crowd in recent memory. A modern congregation that has not read Luke 23 lately will not hear it. **This is a case where the preacher should simply read Luke 23:18, 26–27 aloud before Acts 21:30–36.** `[I]`.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

- **The temple, from Stephen to Paul.** 6:13–14 (the charge), 7:47–50 (Stephen's answer), 21:28–30 (the charge again, and the doors shut). Acts' temple thread begins with a false-witness charge and ends with a mistaken-inference charge; in between, the temple is where the apostles pray and preach (2:46; 3:1; 5:20–21).
- **Zeal.** Simon the Zealot (1:13), the myriads zealous for the law (21:20), Paul zealous for God (22:3) — and Paul's zeal, on his own account, once made him a persecutor (22:4; 26:9–11). Acts is not simple about zeal.
- **The Spirit as speaker.** 13:2, 21:11 with the LORD's formula, 28:25. The Spirit says what will happen and does not say what to do about it.
- **Prophecy fulfilled quietly.** Joel's daughters (2:17 → 21:9); the Miletus bonds (20:23 → 21:33); Jesus' Olivet warning (Luke 21:12 → Acts 21:27ff.).
- **Vindication deferred.** Stephen's charge is answered by his vision (7:56) and his death; Paul's is answered by four acquittals and no release.

### Schnittjer Pass

Three observations in the later-uses-earlier key.

1. **Numbers 6 is used as *register*, not as *citation*.** Luke does not quote the law; he narrates in its vocabulary, which is a way of making a claim about the action without arguing for it. The effect is that a reader who knows Numbers 6 sees Paul obeying it, and a reader who does not sees a man in a temple. `[I]`.
2. **Luke's Gospel is used as a *template with an inversion*.** The Luke 23 crowd mourns; the Acts 21 crowd bays. Luke's technique here is not to make Paul a second Jesus but to run the same scene with the *λαός* on the other side. That is a comment on the *λαός*, not on Paul. `[I]`, moderate–high.
3. **The messenger formula is *transferred*.** *Τάδε λέγει Κύριος* → *Τάδε λέγει τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον*. This is the same transfer-operation the overview identifies in the Servant Songs (role moved outward), applied now to the prophetic formula itself. `[I]`, moderate.

### Christological Reading

Christ is named twice — *τοῦ ὀνόματος τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ* (v.13) and *τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα* (v.14) — and both are on the lips of people under pressure.

**Ground.** What Paul is ready to die for is *the name* — Acts' standing shorthand for the whole confession (4:12; 5:41; 9:15–16). And 9:16 said *ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματός μου παθεῖν*; 21:13 is Paul saying he is ready for exactly that, in the same terms. `[T]`.

**Pattern, carefully.** The passion-shape is real and verbally anchored (Headlines 4–6), and it must not be turned into "Paul is a second Christ". Three controls the text supplies: the crowd's role is *inverted*, not repeated; the Gethsemane words are given to **the companions**, not to Paul; and Paul is rescued, not crucified. **Luke is showing that the servant's road runs the same way as the master's, not that the servant does the master's work.** The passage's Christology is that the Lord whose will is done in Gethsemane is the Lord whose will is invoked at Caesarea, and the same road follows.

**Moralism check.** The temptation is "be resolute like Paul" — and it is unusually attractive here because Paul is magnificent at v.13. Three guards. (a) Luke never says Paul was right to go. (b) The prayer that closes the argument is the *companions'* surrender, not Paul's resolve, and it is a prayer about **the Lord's** will. (c) Paul spends the second half of the pericope entirely passive. **The gospel here is not that a brave man went to Jerusalem; it is that the Lord's will was done in Jerusalem while a brave man was being dragged out of a temple.**

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**21:4 — the Spirit tells Paul not to go, and he goes.** The three available readings: (i) the Spirit forbade and Paul disobeyed; (ii) the Spirit revealed the suffering and the disciples added the prohibition; (iii) the prohibition was a genuine word but conditional/testing. **(ii) is best supported by the Greek** (*διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* attaches to *ἔλεγον*) **and by 20:23** (the Spirit's consistent content is *that bonds await*, not *therefore do not go*), and it is consistent with 21:12, where the same inference is drawn by "we and the local residents" with no claim of inspiration at all. But (i) has real defenders `[S]` and the text does not exclude it. **Name the three, argue for (ii), do not pretend it is settled.**

**21:20–26 — was James's proposal right, and was Paul right to accept it?** Genuinely contested, and Luke adjudicates neither. What the text supplies for the discussion: Paul had taken a vow himself, unasked (18:18); James restates Gentile freedom in the same breath (v.25); the narrative shows Paul complying and the plan failing for a reason nobody could have foreseen (v.27, Asian Jews); and Paul later cites the purification as his defence (24:18) without embarrassment. **None of that settles the wisdom of the plan; all of it tells against reading Paul as having compromised the gospel.**

**21:25 — is James informing Paul of the decree he helped agree?** Read flatly, James tells Paul something Paul already knows (15:22–29, and Paul delivered it, 16:4). Two honest options: Luke is reminding the *reader*, not Paul; or the sentence is James marking a boundary — *this proposal does not touch the Gentiles*. The *δέ* favours the second. `[I]`, moderate.

**21:26 — was the offering ever made?** *ἕως οὗ προσηνέχθη* is the announced terminus, and v.27 interrupts before it arrives. Luke does not say. See Open Questions.

**21:11 — did the oracle come true as spoken?** Agabus says *οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι* will bind Paul and hand him over to the Gentiles. In the event the Jews **beat** him and the **Romans** bind him (v.33), and the tribune hands him to Felix. This is regularly raised as an inaccuracy `[S]`. Three responses worth having: the Jews' action is the proximate cause of both; ancient causal idiom readily attributes an act to whoever set it in motion; and Luke, who reports the oracle, evidently saw no problem. **Say the difficulty exists rather than letting a hearer find it alone.**

## Convergent Findings

1. **Luke composed 21:17–36 and 24:10–21 together.** Tool 7 (*ἁγνίζω*, *προσφορά*, *θόρυβος* — three lemmata whose only other Acts occurrences are in 24:17–18), Tool 11 Move 4, and the Original Audience section converge. Paul's defence before Felix recites this scene item by item. **High** — three independent lexical links in two verses.

2. **The pericope is built on Luke's Gospel more than on the Old Testament.** Tool 5, Tool 11 (Move 1 and the allusion table) and Tool 12 converge: six Gospel echoes, four of them verbally unique pairs in the NT or in Luke–Acts, against zero OT citation formulae and one sustained non-citational use of Numbers 6. **The overview's rule of thumb — that the later Pauline material is citation-light and should lean on Move 4 — is right about citations and wrong about sources.** High.

3. **Luke exculpates Paul four times before the arrest, in four different registers.** Tool 1 (purpose), Tool 6 (the v.29 aside), Tool 15 and the Original Audience section: the leadership's verdict (v.24), the narrated compliance in Mosaic vocabulary (v.26), the narrator's flat correction (v.29), and the later defence speech that recites it (24:17–18). **High.**

4. **The passage's certainties belong to God and its uncertainties to everybody else.** Tool 6 (four narratorial silences), Tool 9, Tool 15 and Headline 1 converge. The Spirit's information is consistent; every human inference from it — the Tyrians', the companions', James's, the crowd's, the tribune's — is partial or wrong. **Moderate–high**, and it is the pericope's governing shape.

5. **Where the manuscript tradition tidies, the exegesis should look.** Tool 7 and Textual Variants converge on the *ὄχλος/λαός* alternation: the feature §7 identifies as load-bearing is precisely what D removes. Combined with the Byzantine normalisation at 20:28, this is now a **method finding** worth carrying into later runs. **Moderate–high.**

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Building a doctrine of guidance on 21:4

**How it sounds:** "Even Paul got it wrong — the Spirit said don't go"; or "the disciples' feelings overrode the Spirit's word".
**Why it is wrong:** the Greek attaches *διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* to the telling, 20:23 says the Spirit's consistent content is *that bonds await*, and Luke declines to adjudicate. Both confident readings claim more than the text gives.
**Instead:** preach what is actually there — the Spirit gave information, the people who loved Paul drew a conclusion, and the conclusion was theirs.

### Pitfall 2: Making James and the elders the villains

**How it sounds:** "Legalistic Jerusalem forces Paul into a compromise, and disaster follows."
**Why it is wrong:** they glorify God for the Gentile mission (v.20), state the charge against Paul is baseless (v.24), restate Gentile freedom unprompted (v.25), and the trouble comes from **Asian** Jews (v.27), not from them. And Paul had taken a vow of his own accord at Cenchreae (18:18).
**Instead:** if the proposal is to be criticised, criticise it as a plan that did not work, and say why it did not — a fact nobody in the room could have known.

### Pitfall 3: Preaching the passion parallels as "Paul is like Jesus"

**How it sounds:** a list of correspondences with Paul in the Christ-shaped role.
**Why it is wrong:** the strongest parallel is an **inversion** (the crowd that mourned now bays), the Gethsemane words are given to the **companions**, and Paul is rescued. Luke is describing the road, not duplicating the Saviour.
**Instead:** preach that the servant's road runs where the master's ran — and let the differences do their work.

### Pitfall 4: Mining the travel narrative

**How it sounds:** a devotional on Philip's four daughters, or on Mnason the old disciple.
**Why it is wrong:** the overview's trap-list names this for Acts generally. Luke's own interest in v.9 is that Joel's promise has landed; his interest in v.16 is lodging.
**Instead:** if 21:9 is preached, preach it as 2:17 fulfilled — that is the reason it is there.

### Pitfall 5: Treating 21:28 as though the accusation might be true

**How it sounds:** careful even-handedness about whether Paul really did bring a Greek into the temple.
**Why it is wrong:** v.29 says flatly that they *supposed* it, and Luke names the mistake. This is the one accusation against Paul that Acts explicitly labels false.
**Instead:** say so plainly, and let the congregation feel what it is to be destroyed by an inference.

### Pitfall 6: Reading 21:20's *μυριάδες* past

**How it sounds:** the verse treated as background colour on the way to the riot.
**Why it is wrong:** it is Luke's own figure for law-observant Jewish believers, and it is the strongest datum in the book against reading Acts as narrating a church that has left Israel. Both major versions weaken it to "thousands".
**Instead:** give the number, and let it stand against whatever supersessionist assumption the congregation brought.

## Book-Overview Tensions

**1. The intertextual map has no Numbers entry, and should.**
*Recommendation:* add **Numbers 6 LXX → 21:23–27, high** — six lexical items including *ἡμέραι τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ* (Num 6:5, verbatim; NT hapax) and the seven days of Num 6:9 — with the note that this is **narration in the source's register rather than allusion**, a distinct category the map does not currently have. Numbers is otherwise unused in Acts, so the entry is "not live", which is itself informative.

**2. The map treats Luke's Gospel as background rather than as a tracked source.**
Six echoes here are verbally unique pairs across the NT or Luke–Acts (*πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ* + *ἀκολουθέω*; *θέλημα* + *γινέσθω*; *παραδίδωμι εἰς χεῖρας*; *ἐπεφώνουν*; *Αἶρε*; *θυγατέρες* + *προφητεύω*). *Recommendation:* give the Gospel its own row in the intertextual map, marked live, so that later runs test it as deliberately as they test Isaiah.

**3. The early↔late echo table has one row from Acts 21 and should have several.**
*The overview has* the 6:13/21:28 charge row and the 10:15/21:28 purity row (both via the sweep). *This run supplies*, all `[T]` on distribution: 2:17→21:9 (*θυγατέρες* + *προφητεύω*, the only two); 11:18→21:14+20 (*ἡσυχάζω* + *ἐδόξαζον τὸν θεόν*, both elements); 15:12→21:19 (*ἐξηγέομαι* + *ὧν ἐποίησεν ὁ θεὸς ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*); 16:19→21:30 (*ἐπιλαμβάνομαι* + *ἕλκω*, the only two); 9:15→21:35 (*βαστάζω*); 18:18→21:23 (*εὐχή*, the only two); 21:24/26/34→24:17–18 (three lemmata); 2:6→21:27/31 (*συγχέω*, the book's first and last).

**4. *ἀπόστολος* — the overview's open question, now answerable in one direction.**
The word's last occurrence is 16:4 `[T]`. **21:18 is the decisive datum:** at the last meeting of the Jerusalem church in Acts, the people present are *Ἰάκωβος* and *πάντες οἱ πρεσβύτεροι* — and no apostle is mentioned. Taken with 20:17–28 (elders and overseers, no apostle), the pattern is consistent: **Acts' account of church leadership after ch. 16 is an eldership.** That does not explain *why* Luke drops the word, but it establishes that the omission is systematic rather than incidental, which the overview's question did not yet have. `[T]`.

**5. This unit's weighting should be raised from Standard to ⭐ HIGH.**
*The overview marks* 21:1–36 **Standard**. *This run finds* the densest internal-echo load in the book (26 Move-4 rows), a high-confidence LXX substratum the map lacks, six verbally unique Gospel echoes, four genuinely contested verses (21:4; 21:11; 21:20–26; 21:25), and a chapter of unusually heavy Western attestation. **Sweep depth is not enough to preach this.** Recommendation: promote to ⭐ HIGH, which would make thirteen ⭐ units.

**6. A method note the overview should carry.**
Twice now — the Byzantine normalisation of the word order at 20:28, and the Western removal of *λαός* at 21:35–36 — the manuscript tradition has smoothed exactly the feature the exegesis turned on. *Recommendation:* add to the overview's honesty notes: **where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied; scribal smoothing is external evidence that a feature is marked.**
