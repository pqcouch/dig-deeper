# The Leper, the Law and the Lord — Two-Framework Analysis

*A mentoring analysis of Ben Cadge's sermon on Luke 5:12–16, read against John Sailhamer's text/event distinction (with Schnittjer and Postell) and the Proclamation Trust framework of Cooper, Davies and Styles, with suggestions for talking it through with Ben.*

## 1. Overview

**The sermon.** Luke 5:12–16 (ESV), preached at a church in Herne Bay. It runs to about 4,950 words, roughly 35–38 minutes. The structure is announced and alliterated: *the Leper* (his condition, his plea), *the Law* (Leviticus 13–14 as a ten-point gospel typology), *the Lord* (Jesus' touch and word, then two lessons from vv. 14–16). The refrain is "able and willing".

**Proportions.** Introduction, prayer and reading take about 1,335 words (27%), of which around 1,100 come before the text is opened. The Leper takes 930 words, the Law 1,250 (25%), the Lord 560, the applications 740, and the close 140. The Law is the longest point, and the Lord, the point about Jesus himself, is the shortest.

**The character of the sermon in one sentence.** It is warm, clear, pastorally alert and Christ-centred, and its best moment is a text-level one: hearing that Jesus answers the leper's "if you will" with "I will". Its main weakness is that it reaches for other texts (Leviticus in the middle, Mark at the end) before it has finished hearing what Luke has done. So both frameworks end up saying much the same thing: the sermon's convictions are right, and Luke's own text would have supported them more strongly than the routes the sermon took.

**A note on the frameworks.** Sailhamer's own work is on the Pentateuch and the Tanak, and the PT skill says an NT sermon needs only the PT framework. Both frameworks are applied here all the same, for two reasons. The sermon's longest section is an exposition of Leviticus 13–14, which is Sailhamer's home ground. And Luke tells us plainly that he is a composing author: he says he has written *kathexēs*, "in order" (Luke 1:3). Sailhamer's question ("why has this author written it this way, and placed it here?") applies to Luke about as directly as it applies anywhere in the canon.

**Texts checked.** The Greek of Luke 5:1–32 and the Lukan cross-references was checked against SBLGNT. Leviticus 8, 9, 13–15, Numbers 5 and 12, and 2 Kings 5 were checked against WLC (BHS text) and Swete's LXX, both from the `_texts/` corpus. The English quoted from the sermon is the ESV that Ben preached from.

## 2. The Sailhamer analysis

*with Schnittjer's narrative categories and Postell on compositional seams*

### 2.1 Opening summary

This is a mixed sermon, with the event-centred moves outnumbering the text-centred ones. That is the normal profile for evangelical exposition. Point 1 rests mainly on reconstruction: the man's decaying body, his smell, his exclusion, and his psychology ("had not heard enough about Jesus to know whether he'd even consider someone like him worthy"). Point 2 is a typology built from the Levitical ritual to New Testament doctrine, one element at a time, and not from anything Luke does with Leviticus. Point 3 turns on Mark 1:45, which is an event harmonised into Luke's text.

Running through all of this, though, is real attention to the text, and at one point it is exact. The sermon notices that the man says *ean thelēs* ("if you will") and that Jesus answers *thelō* ("I will"). It builds the whole sermon on that exchange. That is a leading-word observation, even though Ben doesn't use the term, and it is the right one.

### 2.2 What is working at the text level

**The θέλω hinge.** "Lord, if you will, you can make me clean… I will; be clean" (vv. 12–13). The sermon sees that the man's plea contains two claims: he is sure Jesus can, and unsure whether Jesus will. It also sees that Jesus answers exactly the uncertain one, in the man's own word. This is Sailhamer's instinct at micro level: the meaning lies in how the author has worded the exchange, not in a reconstruction of the man's feelings. "Able and willing" as a refrain is therefore not a slogan laid over the text. It comes out of the text's own wording.

**Noticing the posture and the address.** "He bows down low… He calls Jesus Lord." Luke's *pesōn epi prosōpon* and *Kyrie* are both registered. As §2.4 shows, the sermon stands one verse away from seeing what Luke is doing with them.

**Reading 5:14 as obedience to Moses.** The sermon notes that Jesus sends the man to the priest "as Moses commanded". That is the passage's only explicit link to the Torah, and it is the right door into Leviticus.

**"Outside the camp."** The sermon observes that the leper's place is "outside the camp", *miḥûṣ lammaḥăneh* (Lev 13:46), and that the priest must himself "go outside the camp" (14:3). That is attention to narrative space in Schnittjer's sense, and it is correct.

**The canonical conviction.** "Jesus said that all of scripture pointed towards him." That conviction is sound. Luke gives it in Jesus' own words: "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44). The sermon would be stronger for citing Luke here and not a general memory, because it is Luke's own warrant for going to Leviticus.

### 2.3 Where event-centred and structure-forcing moves appear

**(a) The medical reconstruction.** "We remember that Luke was a medical doctor. And what he's describing is actually really quite precise… His skin would have been falling off in places… He may have been missing fingers… he would have looked like the walking dead."

This makes two moves, and both reach behind the text. The first is the old argument that Luke's vocabulary is technically medical (Hobart, 1882), which Cadbury took apart in the 1920s. *Plērēs lepras* is not clinical language.[^1] The second is a description of Hansen's disease (lost fingers, necrosis), which is not what *ṣāraʿat*/*lepra* designates. Leviticus applies *ṣāraʿat* to garments (13:47–59) and houses (14:33–53). And in the notorious 13:12–13, a man whose *ṣāraʿat* covers him "from head to foot" is pronounced *clean*, while uncleanness returns when *bāśār ḥay*, raw or "living" flesh, appears (13:14). So "full of leprosy" cannot simply be read as "the worst possible case" in Levitical terms.

The text offers something better on both counts. First, *plērēs* is one of Luke's characteristic words, and it sits a chapter earlier on Jesus: *Iēsous de plērēs pneumatos hagiou*, "Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit" (4:1). In Luke-Acts the idiom describes what possesses a person: full of the Spirit and faith (Acts 6:5; 11:24), of grace and power (6:8), of good works (9:36), of deceit (13:10). The man full of leprosy meets the man full of the Holy Spirit. That contrast is not stated, so it should be offered lightly. But it is a textual contrast, and "the walking dead" is not. Second, if Ben wants the "walking dead" image, and it is a good one, the Torah supplies it. When Miriam is struck with *ṣāraʿat*, Aaron pleads, "Let her not be as one dead, whose flesh is half eaten away when he comes out of his mother's womb" (Num 12:12). The leper's prescribed dress (torn clothes, loosened hair, covered lip, Lev 13:45) matches the dress of mourning. The man is dressed as a mourner for himself. The image is right; its source should be the Torah, not a pathology textbook.

**Harm:** mild. The picture is vivid and pastorally effective, and it is not false as a picture of misery. But it is partly anachronistic, and it trains a congregation to think Luke's authority lies in his medical accuracy, not in his composition.

**(b) The psychology of "if".** "The man had not heard enough about Jesus to know whether he'd even consider someone like him worthy of his time." Luke says nothing about what the man had heard. The text supplies only the "if", and the "if" does its work in the exchange, not in the man's inner life. It sets up *thelō*. Ben's pastoral application (we hide from Jesus after a bad week) does not need the psychology. It follows directly from the fact that the only thing the text lets the man doubt is the will of Jesus, and the only thing Jesus says in reply is his will.

**(c) The ten-point typology: flat, and built from outside Luke.** This is the most significant methodological issue in the sermon, and it needs care, because much of what it contains is true.

The method is one-to-one: leper = sinner; beyond skin-deep = sin reaching the heart; outside the camp = alienation; priest goes out = incarnation; two birds = death and release; shaving = new birth; oil = Spirit; blood = atonement; eighth day = resurrection. Schnittjer calls this flat typology, a string of single equations, where the text invites *polyacoustic* hearing. Three specific problems follow from it.

First, **Luke does not make these connections.** Luke's single link to Leviticus in the passage is "as Moses commanded" (5:14). The sermon says we "draw these connections in a way that the original reader might have done", but that is asserted and not shown. A reader of Luke would more naturally have drawn the connection Luke placed one chapter earlier, which is Naaman (§2.4).

Second, **the roles are mis-assigned.** The sermon maps the priest who goes outside the camp onto Christ. But in Luke 5 Jesus does not take the priest's part. He sends the man *to* the priest. And the Levitical priest never healed anyone. Leviticus 14:3 is exact: the priest goes out and looks, "and if the leprous disease is healed (*nirpāʾ*) in the leprous person…". The priest diagnoses, excludes, inspects and certifies. The healing itself belongs to God, as the king of Israel says, tearing his clothes: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man sends word to me to cure a man of his leprosy?" (2 Kgs 5:7). In Luke 5 Jesus does the part no priest could do, and the priest is left to certify it. That is a *higher* Christology than the typology offers, and it comes straight from the text.

Third, **the most striking feature of the rite is missed.** On the eighth day the priest puts blood from the guilt offering "on the lobe of the right ear… the thumb of the right hand, and the big toe of the right foot" of the man being cleansed (Lev 14:14). The Hebrew of that verse matches, nearly word for word, what Moses did to Aaron at his ordination (Lev 8:23), the oil is then put on the same three places (14:17) and poured on his head (14:18), as the anointing oil was poured on Aaron's head (8:12). The only other rite in Scripture that marks ear, thumb and toe is the ordination of priests (Exod 29:20; Lev 8:23–24). The eighth day itself is the day Aaron's ministry began (9:1). The cleansed leper is not simply let back into society, as the sermon says ("allowed to reenter society"). He is brought back into the presence of God with the marks of a priest's consecration. The sermon's items 8 and 9 ("oil is placed on them… blood is placed on them") pass straight over this. It is the one place where Leviticus itself makes a bold theological claim through the ritual, and it leads to a gospel reality more precise than "paid for by Christ's blood": the unclean become a kingdom of priests (Exod 19:6; 1 Pet 2:9; Rev 1:6).

Some items do hold up. "Outside the camp" has a New Testament warrant in Hebrews 13:11–13, though that text applies it to the cross, not the incarnation. The oil/Spirit link has Lukan support, since the Nazareth manifesto the sermon quotes says "he has anointed (*echrisen*) me" (4:18). And the newborn image the sermon reaches for with the shaving (a stretch as it stands) has a real text behind it, just not in Leviticus. When Naaman is cleansed, "his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child (*kivśar naʿar qāṭōn*)" (2 Kgs 5:14). Ben's instinct was right. He looked for it in the wrong chapter.

**Harm:** this is the sermon's weightiest issue. The typology is not wrong theology, but it lets Leviticus stand in for Luke. It takes a quarter of the sermon, and it pushes the passage's own centre, Jesus, into the shortest point.

**(d) Why outside the camp? Leviticus's leading idea.** The sermon explains the exclusion socially: "completely ostracized from society", "scratching out a meagre existence at the margins". The Torah gives a theological reason: "that they may not defile their camp, *in the midst of which I dwell*" (Num 5:3), and "lest they die in their uncleanness by defiling my tabernacle that is in their midst" (Lev 15:31). Schnittjer's leading idea for Leviticus is *the danger of holiness*: the unclean are kept away from the holy presence for their own protection. That is what makes Luke 5:13 so startling, and the sermon comes close to saying it ("instead of the uncleanness flowing from the man… it was Christ's cleanness that spread"). Read against Leviticus's own logic, the touch means that the holy presence has come out to the unclean man, and it does not destroy him. It cleanses him. That is the text-centred form of Ben's best theological point, and it would have tied points 2 and 3 together.

**(e) Mark imported into Luke.** "In the Gospel of Mark, you find out that's exactly what happened, because the leper didn't obey Jesus… The result here is that Jesus' ministry is threatened." The whole first application ("obeying Christ is often nuanced") rests on this.

This is the classic Sailhamer case: reconstructing the *event* from both accounts and then preaching the event, not Luke's text. Mark 1:45 says *ho de exelthōn ērxato kēryssein polla*, "he went out and began to proclaim it freely". Luke has deliberately rewritten that sentence: *diērcheto de mallon ho logos peri autou*, "but now even more the report about him went abroad" (5:15). Luke's subject is the report, not the man. Luke has chosen not to record the leper's disobedience. A sermon on Luke that makes that disobedience its main application is preaching the one feature of the event that Luke's authorship left out.

What Luke put in its place is worth more. The crowds come "*to hear* and to be healed", with hearing first (5:15, compare 5:1, "to hear the word of God"). "But he himself (*autos de*) would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (5:16). The periphrastic imperfect *ēn hypochōrōn… kai proseuchomenos* marks a habit. So Luke's answer to the "celebrity healer" danger that Ben rightly identifies is not the leper's fault. It is Jesus' own withdrawal to prayer.

**(f) Smaller event-moves.** "Jesus' heart for this man… was one of absolute love and compassion." Luke has, in fact, *left out* the emotion that Mark records at this point (Mark 1:41). He lets the outstretched hand, the touch and the word carry it. The inference is fair, but the observation that Luke withholds the feeling and keeps the will is more interesting. "The church… shall march up to the very gates of hell" goes beyond Matthew 16:18, and it is not Luke. And the "AI going rogue" aside is harmless, but it is a news item standing where the text could have stood.

### 2.4 The deepest missed opportunity: Naaman, and "Am I God?"

The sermon's stated aim is to help the congregation see that "although this Jesus is a man like us, he's not just a man like us… He's God himself." The text proves that, and the sermon never draws on the proof.

**Luke's composition.** Luke is the only evangelist who puts the Nazareth sermon before this healing. In it, Jesus says: "there were many lepers in Israel in the time of the prophet Elisha, and none of them was cleansed (*ekatharisthē*), but only Naaman the Syrian" (4:27). This is the first leper in Luke, and he arrives one chapter later. The reader Luke has formed comes to 5:12 already holding the question 4:27 raised: *who cleanses lepers?*

**The word.** Jesus' imperative *katharisthēti*, "be clean", occurs in the whole of Swete's Septuagint only once: at 4 Reigns (2 Kings) 5:13, where Naaman's servants urge him, "He said to you, *Wash and be clean*" (*Lousai kai katharisthēti*; Hebrew *rəḥaṣ ûṭəhār*).[^2] The same imperative is in all three Synoptics, so the word itself is shared tradition. But only Luke has placed Naaman immediately before it, so in Luke the echo is heard.

**The question the echo raises.** In 2 Kings 5, cleansing a leper is the test of deity. The king of Israel reads the letter, tears his clothes and cries: *hă'ĕlōhîm ʾānî ləhāmît ûləhaḥăyôt*, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (5:7). Even Elisha does not touch Naaman. He sends a message, and Naaman is cleansed by washing seven times in the Jordan at a distance. In Luke 5, Jesus stretches out his own hand, touches the man, speaks one word, and the leprosy leaves "immediately". The outstretched hand (*ekteinas tēn cheira*) is language Luke himself uses of God's healing: "while you stretch out your hand to heal" (Acts 4:30).

**The compositional seam (Postell).** Luke has also set the healing inside a deliberate frame. Just before it, *anēr hamartōlos*, a "sinful man" (Peter), falls at Jesus' knees saying "Depart from me… Lord" (5:8). Then *anēr plērēs lepras*, a "man full of leprosy", falls on his face saying "Lord… make me clean" (5:12). Just after it, the Pharisees ask, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (5:21), and Jesus says, "I have not come to call the righteous but sinners" and "those who are well have no need of a physician" (5:31–32). So Luke has placed the leper between two scenes that make the move from uncleanness to sin explicitly. The sermon's central claim, that Jesus' dealing with leprosy "is a deliberate picture of how Jesus deals with the disease of sin", is correct, and it is *Luke's* claim, made by arrangement. The sermon proved it the long way round, through ten points of Leviticus, when the short way lay four verses in each direction.

Had the sermon taken this route, it would have gained everything it wanted and lost nothing: freshness (the aim of its introduction), the Old Testament's depth (the aim of point 2), the deity of Christ (the aim of its opening), and the sin/cleansing connection (the aim of point 3). And all of it would come from Luke.

### 2.5 Summary judgement (Sailhamer)

The instincts for attending to the text are there. Ben hears repeated words (*thelō*), notices narrative space ("outside the camp"), and believes the Law speaks of Christ. What is missing is the discipline of *staying in the author's text long enough*. On two occasions (Leviticus, Mark) the sermon goes to another text before asking what Luke has done with this one. Sailhamer's method would open up something simple and repeatable for him: read the neighbours before the parallels, and let the author's own echoes (4:27, 2 Kings 5) lead the canonical move, not a list of equivalences. The payoff would be more Christology, not less, which is the usual result of this method.

## 3. The Proclamation Trust analysis

### 3.1 Opening summary

The sermon is text-*anchored*, and in point 1 it is text-*driven*. It is warm, unified by a strong refrain, and it lands clearly on Christ and the cross. On Davies' terms, it has found the passage's real pastoral Question, but it finds it halfway through point 1 when it should have opened with it. And its final applications come from Mark and from a general list, not from Luke's purpose. On Styles' terms, it arrives at the gospel mainly through the Leviticus typology and a tour of other texts, not through Luke's own canonical placement. On Cooper's terms, it reads normally and well at the micro level, but it hardly reads the macro level (placement, sequence, Luke's frame).

A seven-second sermon for the passage might run:

**Question?** Will the Lord have me as I am, unclean and not yet sorted out?

**Answer!** Jesus is both able and willing. What the law could only diagnose, exclude and certify, and what only God could do, he does with a touch and a word.

**So what…** Come to him unclean, without waiting to be clean enough. And, like the man sent to the priest, let your cleansing become a testimony.

Ben's sermon has the Question and the Answer. Its So What splits in two, and only one half belongs to the passage.

### 3.2 What is working well

**A real refrain, earned from the text.** "Able and willing" is said a dozen times, and it holds the sermon together from the leper's plea to the closing prayer. Davies' first test (can the purpose be said in one sentence, and does the sermon keep returning to it?) is largely met.

**The best application is sharp and personal.** "Just one bad week, and we've slipped up again… And our instinct is so often to hide from Jesus. Hide until… we feel clean enough." This lands on real people, not stereotypes. It is also exactly what the passage addresses, because the man's only doubt is Jesus' willingness. Ben has heard this application for himself ("Perhaps you're like me…"), and it shows. This is the sermon at its best on Davies' terms.

**The cross arrives, and it answers the passage's question.** "By going to the grave and coming back again, he's proven that he's willing to help us." The "willing" of 5:13 is grounded in the cross. That is a good Styles move: the Little (one leper, one word) is completed in the Large (the death and resurrection that prove the will). It might have been anchored more tightly in Luke, since Gethsemane is Luke's own place for the will of Jesus submitting to the Father's (22:42), but the instinct is sound.

**The King's manifesto.** Setting the healing against Luke 4:18–19 ("the Spirit of the Lord is upon me… good news to the poor… liberty to the oppressed") treats the miracle as the King's programme in action, which is exactly Styles' "gospel of the King".

**Pastoral warmth and rapport.** The water-shortage story is local, affectionate, and gets the congregation laughing and listening. The sermon never talks down, and the closing prayer gathers up its main point. These are real strengths in a preacher.

### 3.3 Where the framework is not fully applied

**Cooper: macro-level reading is thin.** Micro-level observation is good (θέλω, posture, "Lord"). But the sermon never asks why Luke put this story *here*. The placement (§2.4: 4:27 before it, 5:8 and 5:17–32 around it) is where Cooper's "careful reading at the macro level" would have produced the sermon's main argument. Cooper's warning sign, "treating the text as a springboard", fits point 2 fairly: for 1,250 words the text is Leviticus, and Luke is waiting offstage.

**Davies: the introduction poses the wrong Question.** The first 1,100 words address *over-familiarity* ("our tendency to glaze over"). That is a real problem, but it is the preacher's problem with the passage, not the passage's pastoral problem. The passage asks a different question: *will he have me?* Ben finds that Question himself in point 1, and it is the sermon's finest passage. Davies would say: put it at the front. The water story can stay if it is aimed at the right thirst. An illustration about something taken for granted fits "we're over-familiar". An illustration about thinking you are too far gone to be welcome fits the text.

**Davies: the "them then" is announced but not inhabited.** "I want this passage to really land for us, land like it would have done for the original readers back when Luke wrote this gospel." After that, the sermon doesn't return to Luke's readers. Luke tells us who they are: Theophilus and those like him, who need "certainty concerning the things you have been taught" (1:4), in a Gospel that has just said God's cleansing went to a *Syrian* (4:27) and will later have a *foreigner* return to give thanks (17:18). For readers outside Israel, the question "will he have someone like me?" was very pressing. Inhabiting that would have made the "us now" much sharper.

**Davies: the closing application is "spotting", not "hearing".** The final section moves to "the right response is sometimes a lot more nuanced", and then to a list: missionary work, witnessing on the bus, honouring Jesus at home, honest bookkeeping, avoiding gossip. Each item is good. None comes from this passage. Worse, the step that leads there comes from Mark (§2.3e), so the application relies on a text the congregation wasn't reading. This is Davies' "application that could have been generated without reading this passage", and it pulls against the sermon's own unified purpose. The sermon has two So Whats: "come to him unclean" (from Luke) and "obedience is nuanced" (from Mark).

What the passage offers in its place is close to what Ben wanted to say. Jesus sends the cleansed man to do something *ordinary and obedient*: go to the priest and offer what Moses commanded, "for a testimony to them" (*eis martyrion autois*, 5:14). The very next verse brings in "Pharisees and teachers of the law… from every village of Galilee and Judea and from Jerusalem" (5:17). The cleansed man's ordinary obedience *is* his witness, first of all to the religious establishment. That grounds Ben's point about honouring Jesus in the home and the workplace in the text itself: cleansed people are sent back into ordinary life as evidence. It doesn't downgrade evangelism, and it doesn't need Mark.

**Styles: the Christ-connection is partly a "bouncing ball".** The sermon reaches Christ in point 2 by leaving Luke for Leviticus, and in point 3 by gathering Luke 4:18, Matthew 16:18 and a general "Adam/curse/cure" arc. It does get there, and warmly. But Styles' test asks whether the Christ-connection comes from *within the passage's canonical place*. Luke 4:27 and 2 Kings 5 are that place. There, the passage itself poses the question that only the King answers: *Am I God, to make alive?*

**Styles: moralism at the edges.** "Even Jesus needed to spend time in communion with his Father. And if he did, so much more do we." This is imitation, and in Luke it is not quite wrong, because Luke does model the church's prayer on Jesus' (compare Acts 1:14; 4:24–31). But 5:16 functions in Luke as the *King's* answer to popularity (*autos de*, "but he himself…"), a contrast with the crowds, before it is an example to copy. Read that way, it becomes gospel before it becomes duty: the one we come to is himself kept in the Father's will, which is why his "I will" can be trusted.

**Styles: every Christian a preacher, half-caught.** Ben raises the question of witness, which is to his credit, but he frames it as a caution against over-valuing evangelism. Styles would reverse the emphasis. Luke 5:15 shows "the word" (*ho logos*) going out, which is the Acts refrain ("the word of God increased", Acts 6:7; 12:24). And 5:14 shows a cleansed man sent as testimony. The passage calls the congregation to be witnesses, not to be wary of witnessing.

### 3.4 The deepest missed opportunity (PT)

**The ending.** The sermon's So What should have come from 5:14–16 as Luke wrote it: the cleansed man sent as testimony, the word spreading, the crowds coming to *hear*, and the King withdrawing to pray. What it actually rests on is a detail from Mark that Luke took out. Fixing this one thing would unify the sermon (one purpose, not two), bring its application back to its own text, and keep what Ben wanted to say about everyday obedience, now properly grounded. It would also cut the sermon's length and sharpen its landing.

### 3.5 Summary judgement (PT)

This is a preacher with good pastoral instincts, a real gift for rapport, a clear structure, and a secure grasp of the gospel. He has already found the passage's Question for himself. The PT framework would help him in three ways: (1) open with the passage's Question, not his own; (2) let the sermon's longest point be about the text he is preaching; and (3) draw the application from what the author has kept and left out, not from a harmony of the Gospels. None of these changes his theology. All of them would make the sermon more obviously *Luke's*.

## 4. Where the two frameworks agree and diverge

In this sermon they agree to an unusual degree. Both would move the weight from Leviticus to Luke's own frame. Both would replace the Markan ending with 5:14–16. Both affirm the θέλω hinge as the heart of the sermon.

They diverge over **point 2**. A PT reader would accept a Leviticus section in principle. The triangle becomes a rectangle for the Old Testament, and typology is part of canonical context. The PT critique is about proportion and method: too long, too flat, detached from Luke. Sailhamer would press further. For him the messianic meaning of Leviticus 14 is not a set of retrospective correspondences, but something the text itself composes: a rite in which an unclean man is *ordained* on the eighth day, set inside a book whose question is how anyone unclean can live near the holy God. On that reading, the ordination parallel (Lev 14:14 // 8:23) is not "one more typological point". It is Leviticus's own argument, and Luke 5 is its fulfilment. The PT approach would add a better eleventh point. Sailhamer would replace the ten points with one.

This matters for how the conversation with Ben is framed. The issue is not that he used typology. It is that the list approach, where each element has a counterpart, kept him from hearing what the rite as a whole is saying.

## 5. Additional scholarly contribution

**On *ṣāraʿat* and "leprosy".** Milgrom (*Leviticus 1–16*, AB 3, 1991) and Wenham (*Leviticus*, NICOT, 1979) both argue that biblical *ṣāraʿat* is not Hansen's disease. Milgrom's wider thesis is that ritual impurity symbolises death. On that view *ṣāraʿat* is a kind of living death, which Numbers 12:12 states outright. Wenham notes the likeness between the cleansed leper's anointing and the consecration of priests. In Greek medical writing, *lepra* describes scaly skin conditions; Hansen's disease is *elephantiasis*.[^3]

**On "Luke the physician".** W. K. Hobart, *The Medical Language of St. Luke* (1882), argued for technical medical vocabulary in Luke-Acts. H. J. Cadbury (*The Style and Literary Method of Luke*, 1920, and later articles) showed that the same vocabulary is widespread among educated non-medical writers. Luke's profession rests on Colossians 4:14, not on his diction.

**On the eighth day.** Resurrection as the "eighth day" is patristic (*Epistle of Barnabas* 15.8–9; Justin, *Dialogue* 41, 138), not New Testament. The Gospels say "the first day of the week". The typology is venerable, but it should be presented as the church's reading, not as Luke's.

**On Luke 4:27 and Naaman.** The link between the Nazareth sermon's Naaman saying and Luke's first leper healing, and on to the Samaritan leper who returns in 17:11–19, is widely discussed in the commentaries on Luke.[^4] The lexical fact that *katharisthēti* occurs in the LXX only at 4 Reigns 5:13 was checked in the corpus for this analysis.

## 6. Accuracy and reference checks

- **"Luke was a medical doctor… really quite precise."** See §5. Col 4:14 supports the profession, but not the precision.
- **Missing fingers, necrosis.** This describes Hansen's disease, not *ṣāraʿat*/*lepra*.
- **"The law demanded… no one was allowed to touch him."** Touching human uncleanness defiles (Lev 5:3), which is fair. Leviticus 13:46 requires him to live "alone, outside the camp". Luke, though, places this man "in one of the cities".
- **"Cover the lower part of their face."** Fair for *ʿal-śāpām yaʿṭeh* (Lev 13:45), "cover the upper lip". The torn clothes and loose hair are mourning signs, which the sermon doesn't mention.
- **"Outside the camp… where they put their rubbish."** Partly right: the dead were carried out (Lev 10:4–5). But Leviticus 4:12 calls the place outside the camp where the sin-offering remains are burned "a clean place".
- **"Leviticus… contains all of the laws."** An overstatement. Law runs from Exodus to Deuteronomy.
- **The rite's sequence** (two birds, washing and shaving, seventh-day shaving, eighth-day offerings, blood and oil) is summarised accurately. What it leaves out is Lev 14:14 // 8:23.
- **"Jesus rose on the eighth day."** Patristic, not New Testament (see §5).
- **"He told the religious authorities" (Luke 4:18).** He spoke in the Nazareth synagogue, to his home town, not to "the religious authorities".
- **"In the Gospel of Mark… the leper didn't obey."** Correct for Mark 1:45. Luke 5:15 rewrites it without the disobedience.
- **"Gates of hell… march up to."** Matthew 16:18 says the gates of Hades "shall not prevail". The marching is interpretation, and the verse is not in Luke.
- **Transcript "I will be clean."** This is almost certainly the transcriber's run-together of "I will; be clean". Worth checking that Ben said it with the pause, since the whole sermon depends on it.

## 7. Talking it through with Ben

The analysis above is far more than one conversation can hold, and most of it should stay in your notes. What follows is a suggested shape for the conversation itself.

**Aim for one shift, not ten corrections.** If Ben leaves with one habit, it should be this: *before leaving the passage for another text, read the neighbours and ask what the author has done*. Almost everything above comes back to that habit, so the conversation can be built around it and nothing else.

**Start with his own account.** Ask what he most hoped people would take home, and what he found hardest in preparing. He will almost certainly say "able and willing", and he will probably say the Leviticus section was the hardest work. Both answers lead naturally into what follows.

**Affirm specifically, and mean it.** Tell him that the "if you will" / "I will" observation is the best thing in the sermon, and why: it came from the words of the text, not from a list. Tell him the "hiding after a bad week" paragraph is the passage's real question, and that he found it himself. Mention the warmth, the rapport and the clear structure. Specific praise also shows him what his best work looks like, so he can repeat it.

**Then lead with questions, not verdicts.** Three questions, one for each major issue, each one he can answer from his Bible at the table:

1. *"Is this the first leper in Luke? Where else has one come up?"* Let him find 4:27. Then ask him to read 2 Kings 5:7 aloud. Once he has "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?", he has the sermon's thesis from the text, and he will see it without being told.
2. *"In Leviticus 14, who actually heals the leper? What does the priest do?"* Let him find 14:3. Then give him the ordination parallel (14:14 beside 8:23) as a gift, not a correction. It is the kind of discovery that makes a young preacher want to go back into Leviticus, and that is what you want.
3. *"Where does the leper's disobedience come from, Luke or Mark? Why might Luke have left it out?"* Let him put 5:15 beside Mark 1:45. Then ask what Luke puts there instead: *hearing*, *testimony*, *prayer*.

**Offer the reframe of his ending as a way to keep what he wanted.** His instinct that ordinary obedience in home and work honours Christ is sound. Show him that 5:14 gives it a better foundation ("go, show yourself to the priest… for a testimony to them") than Mark's story does. That way the correction arrives as support for his own point.

**Keep the smaller items for a later conversation.** The Hansen's disease point, Hobart and Cadbury, the eighth day and Matthew 16:18 are all real, but together they would feel like a marking exercise. Mention one at most, and only if he asks. The "Luke the physician" point is the most useful, because it will recur.

**Leave him something to do.** Ask him to redraft either the introduction (so it opens with *will he have me?*) or the ending (built on 5:14–16), and bring it back. A redraft he writes himself will teach more than a redraft you give him. The reworked point 2 in the appendix is for you to have in reserve, if he asks to see what it might look like.

**Setting.** An unhurried hour over coffee, Bibles open (ideally with a Greek text on the table if he reads it), and his manuscript in front of him, not this document. If he wants the written notes afterwards, send §2.4 and §3.4 only, as the two "deepest missed opportunities". They carry the substance without the full apparatus.

## 8. Critical assessment of this analysis

**The Naaman echo may be weighted too heavily.** *Katharisthēti* is shared by all three Synoptics, so it is not Luke's own word choice. The Lukan element is the *placement* after 4:27, and an audience's ability to hear a single LXX imperative is uncertain. The argument stands on 4:27 and 2 Kings 5:7 even without the lexical point. The lexical point supports it, and should not be presented as the main evidence.

**"Full of leprosy" / "full of the Holy Spirit" is a suggestion, not a demonstration.** The two are a chapter apart, and Luke doesn't draw the contrast. It deserves a sentence at most.

**The typology critique should not become a prohibition.** Reading Leviticus 14 christologically is legitimate, and there is patristic precedent for much of what Ben did. The objection is to the flat, one-for-one method, to the proportion, and to the lack of any tie to Luke. It is not an objection to going to Leviticus at all. A sermon that went there briefly, for the "danger of holiness" logic and the ordination parallel, would be better than one that left Leviticus out.

**The Mark critique could seem pedantic to a young preacher.** Harmonising Gospel accounts is normal in evangelical preaching, and Ben was open about naming Mark. Whether this is a real fault depends on one's view of the Gospels as distinct compositions. On both frameworks it is a fault, but it should be raised as a question ("why did Luke leave it out?"), not a rule.

**The introduction critique should allow for context.** A visiting preacher in a new congregation may need longer to build rapport than a pastor at home. The fifth of the sermon spent before the text may have done relational work that a manuscript analysis can't measure.

## Appendix: Point 2 reworked

*Kept in Ben's voice and at roughly his length, about 800 words, compared with 1,250. For reference only; see §7 on whether to show it.*

> So let's keep turning that tap. We've met the leper. But to feel the weight of what happens next, we need to go back to the law, to Leviticus 13 and 14. And Jesus himself gives us permission to do that: at the end of this Gospel, Luke tells us, Jesus said that everything written about him "in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms" had to be fulfilled. So let's look for him here.
>
> First question: *why* did the leper have to live outside the camp? We might think it was about hygiene — keep the sick away from the healthy. But the Bible gives a different reason. Numbers 5 says the unclean were sent outside the camp "that they may not defile their camp, *in the midst of which I dwell*." The problem wasn't mainly that he was ill. It was that God lived in the middle of the camp, and uncleanness and the holy presence of God cannot be near each other without danger. Leviticus 15 says it plainly: it was "lest they die in their uncleanness." The camp wasn't pushing him out to protect *itself*. The law was keeping him away to protect *him*.
>
> So the leper lived as a man already mourning his own death — torn clothes, hair unbound, face covered, crying "Unclean, unclean." When Moses' sister Miriam was struck with it, her brother Aaron begged, "Let her not be as one dead." That's what this was: a living death, at a distance from God.
>
> Second question: what could the priest do about it? And here's the surprise. The answer is: almost nothing. Listen to Leviticus 14:3: "the priest shall go out of the camp, and the priest shall look, *and if the leprous disease is healed* in the leprous person…" Do you see? The priest could diagnose. He could exclude. He could inspect. He could certify. But he could not heal. Only God could do that. When the king of Israel was asked to cure the Syrian general Naaman of his leprosy, he tore his clothes and said, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" Cleansing a leper was the kind of thing only God does.
>
> Third question: when God *did* heal, what happened next? There was a long ceremony — two birds, one killed and the other dipped in its blood and set free; washing; shaving; waiting seven days. And then, on the eighth day, something extraordinary. The priest took blood and put it on the man's right ear, his right thumb, and the big toe of his right foot, and then did the same with oil.
>
> Now if you know Leviticus — and I know you all do — that should ring a bell. Because there's only one other ceremony in the whole Bible where that happens: in Leviticus 8, when Moses ordained Aaron and his sons as priests. The same blood, the same ear, the same thumb, the same toe. And do you know what day Aaron's ministry began? The eighth day.
>
> So the cleansed leper wasn't just allowed back into society. He was brought back into the presence of God marked like a priest. The man who had been kept furthest from God is now brought as close as anyone could come.
>
> Now carry all that back to Luke 5. A man in that living death falls on his face and says to Jesus — not to a priest — "Lord, if you will, you can make me clean." Nobody ever said that to a priest; priests couldn't. He's saying it to the one who can do what only God can do. And Jesus does what no priest ever did: he stretches out his hand and *touches* him. The holy one comes out to the unclean man — and instead of the man being destroyed by holiness, he's cleansed by it.
>
> And then — notice this — Jesus sends him *to* the priest. Why? "For a proof to them." The priest's job was to look and certify. So the priest is about to look at this man, and certify that God has been at work. The law's own inspector is sent the evidence.
>
> That's the gospel in Leviticus. We're unclean — not on our skin, but in our hearts. We can't make ourselves clean, and no priest, no ritual, no amount of religious effort can do it either. But Jesus can, and he will. And the ones he cleanses, he doesn't just readmit at the edge of the camp. He brings them near. Peter would later write to ordinary Christians and call them "a royal priesthood". That's the leper's story. That's ours.
