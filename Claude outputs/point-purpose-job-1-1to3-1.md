# Point & Purpose: Job 1:1–3:1

**Sermon:** 1 of the nine-sermon Job series, preached after the introductory sermon on the whole book
**Exegesis:** `Job/dig-deeper-job-1-1to3-1` (the unit report, 28 September 2026), with `book-overview-job` (v0.1.1) and the structure claim audit
**Study text:** NASB95 · **Pulpit text:** ESV (declared 28 September 2026), checked against the Logos export for every note below
**Date:** 28 September 2026

Warrant tags: `[T]` derivable from the text · `[I]` a reasonable inference from it · `[S]` supplied by a secondary source.

---

## Scope

**Single sermon on 1:1–3:1.** The unit is set by the text, not chosen. The prose frame runs to 3:1, the first poetry begins at 3:2, and Leningrad puts a petuḥah after 3:1 `[T]`. The unit has 36 verses, and its meaning lies in the doubling, so it must be preached whole:
- two councils, two tests, two verdicts;
- what repeats shows the pattern;
- what changes shows the escalation.

A sermon on chapter 1 alone would end on 1:21 and preach "the patience of Job", which is the unit's own besetting misreading. `[I]`

**Series context.** The introductory sermon will already have set out the book's shape: the question "for nothing?" (1:9) and God's verdict on Job and the friends (42:7–8). Sermon 1 therefore does not introduce the book. It can take for granted that the congregation knows the ending, and spend its time on the thing only the prologue can do: showing the reader heaven, and then showing the man on earth who never sees it.

**Reading.** Read the whole unit aloud. It is narrative, and takes about six minutes. The ESV's own paragraphing shows the scenes, and its three scene openings ("Now there was a day … Now there was a day … Again there was a day", 1:6, 13; 2:1) let the congregation hear the structure as it is read. If time forces a shorter reading, use the series plan's anchors: 1:8–12, 20–22; 2:3, 9–10, and always 2:13–3:1.

## Fallen Condition Focus

**We cannot be sure whether we love God or only what he gives, and when he takes it away we reach for an explanation — blaming ourselves, blaming him, or giving up on him — because we cannot see the scene behind our loss, and we do not know how to sit with others in theirs.**

**Where it comes from.** It is the unit report's candidate FCF, "Anchored".
- **The Accuser's charge.** It is exactly this suspicion: הַחִנָּם יָרֵא אִיּוֹב אֱלֹהִים ("Does Job fear God for nothing?", 1:9). `[T]`
- **Four responses on earth.** The unit shows four responses to loss.
  - Job worships (1:20–21).
  - The wife gives up, with "Curse God and die" (2:9).
  - The messenger explains: the "fire of God" (1:16) is his reading of the disaster, not the narrator's.
  - The friends at first simply sit (2:13).
  - `[T]`
- **What the first readers shared with us.** They knew Deuteronomy's logic, blessing for obedience and curse for disobedience (Deut 28:12, 35, both quoted in the unit). So they were ready to read loss as guilt, as we are. `[I]`

## Teaching Point

**God is worth fearing for nothing: when the Accuser says no one serves God except for pay, the LORD stakes his name on his servant, sets the Accuser's limits and owns the outcome; and Job, stripped of everything but his life and never told why, blesses the name and receives both good and evil from God's hand — the answer the Accuser could not believe, given in full by the Servant who was hated without cause.**

- **Subject:** Is God worth fearing for nothing? (1:9)
- **Complement:** Yes. God stakes his own name on it (1:8; 2:3). Job's worship proves it in deed and word, with no explanation given (1:20–22; 2:10). Christ, the true Servant, proves it perfectly.

**Built from** the unit report's convergent findings:
- **Headline 1:** one question tested twice, and answered with its own word — חִנָּם at 1:9 and 2:3.
- **Headline 2:** the ברך ("bless") thread, and the single קלל ("curse").
- **Headline 3:** the reader alone sees heaven.
- **Headline 5:** the three responses.

**Why the TP makes God the subject.**
- The question at 1:9 concerns Job's integrity, but also God's worth. `[I]`
- It is God who opens both councils (1:8; 2:3).
- It is God who sets both limits: רַק ("only", 1:12), then אַךְ ("only", 2:6). `[T]`
- It is God who says, "you incited me against him to destroy him without reason (חִנָּם)" (2:3, ESV). `[T]`

## Purpose Statement

**To move hearers from a faith that quietly trades on God's gifts to worship of God for himself.** Differentiated by hearer:

- **The grieving:** assure them that loss can come "without reason" in them and still lie under God's hand. They may tear the robe and bless the name in the same act (1:20–21).
- **The explainers** — which is most of us: stop them before they speak. The council is shown to the reader, never to Job, so no one may tell a sufferer which scene lies behind their pain. Commend the friends' seven silent days (2:13) as the church's first work of comfort.
- **The half-hearted:** press the Accuser's question on their own worship. Would it survive the hedge coming down (1:10)?
- **The sceptic,** who shares the Accuser's suspicion that all religion is self-interest: show them a man who worshipped with nothing left. Then show them the Son. When the Accuser offered him "all these things" for worship, he refused (Matt 4:9–10). And he was hated "without a cause" (John 15:25).

The motive throughout is **God's worth**, and the Son's obedience in our place, not Job's example. `[I]`

## Candidate Big Question

**"Do you love God — or only what he gives you?"**

A longer form for the introduction: *"If God took away everything he has given you, and never told you why, would you still worship him — and is he worth it?"*

*Candidate.* The preacher settles the final wording. The Big Question should echo whatever form the introductory sermon gave the series' governing question, if it set one.

## Proposed Structure

Four movements. The first two follow the scenes, with the opening portrait joined to the first council (1:1–12) and the first test standing alone (1:13–22). The last two follow Leningrad's paragraphs, which join the second council to the second test (2:1–10) and set the friends and 3:1 apart (2:11–3:1). `[T]` for the scene markers and the petuḥot, as the WLC transcribes Leningrad.

### 1. The question heaven asks (1:1–12)

*The man on earth, the court in heaven, and the question: "Does Job fear God for no reason?"*
- **The portrait (1:1–5).** Blameless, upright, God-fearing, rich, a father praying for his children "continually".
- **The scene changes to heaven (1:6).** God stakes his name on Job: "Have you considered my servant Job?" (1:8).
- **The Accuser's charge (1:9–11).** Job's piety is bought, and the hedge is the bribe.
- **The permission, with its limit (1:12).**

*Function:* raise the FCF. Place the congregation in heaven's gallery, where Job never stands, and hand them the question. **Say plainly** that the question is about God's worth as much as Job's integrity.

### 2. The first answer: "Blessed be the name" (1:13–22)

*Four messengers, one blow in four parts, and a man who falls to the ground — to worship.*
- **Disaster falls (נפל) three times** (1:15, 16, 19). **Job falls once**, to worship (1:20). `[T]`
- **Five verbs before a word is spoken** (1:20): he arose, tore his robe, shaved his head, fell on the ground — and worshipped.
- **Then the answer,** "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (1:21).
- **The verdict:** "In all this Job did not sin or charge God with wrong" (1:22).

*Hinge:* the word the Accuser swore Job would use *against* God is the word Job uses *for* God. The Hebrew has ברך ("bless") in both places, 1:11 and 1:21. **The preacher must supply this; no English version carries it** (see the pulpit-text note).

*Function:* the first half of the TP. Worship that grieves, and blesses a God who is not a paymaster.

### 3. The second answer: good and evil from God (2:1–10)

*The same court again, with two new sentences, and a test that goes from possessions to the body.*
- **God's admission:** "you incited me against him to destroy him without reason" (2:3).
- **The Accuser's proverb:** "Skin for skin!" (2:4).
- **The limit moves inward:** from "only … not him" (1:12) to "only spare his life" (2:6).
- **The blow,** from sole to crown (2:7), the words of the covenant curse (Deut 28:35) falling on a man in whom there is no cause.
- **The wife's counsel,** in God's own words turned into despair: "Do you still hold fast your integrity? Curse God and die" (2:9 = 2:3).
- **Job's reply:** "Shall we receive good from God, and shall we not receive evil?" (2:10).

*Hinge:* 2:3. Say it without softening. The Greek translator softened it; the preacher must not.

*Function:* the TP's second half. God is worth fearing even when the body goes, and God owns the outcome rather than handing it to the Accuser. Receiving good and evil from God is faith, not fatalism: the narrator rules it no sin (2:10).

### 4. Silence, and a cry (2:11–3:1)

*Three friends, who come to "show him sympathy and comfort him", weep, throw dust, and sit seven days and seven nights without a word — until Job opens his mouth.*
- **The friends' best moment** is their silence. The narrator gives it a reason: "for they saw that his suffering was very great" (2:13). Job will later say it was their wisdom (13:5). `[T]`
- **The unit ends on the one real curse in the prologue,** קלל ("curse"). It falls not on God but on his day (3:1). Lament has begun. The book will not call it sin (42:7).

*Landing — the Christ turn.* The Accuser's question has one complete answer.
- **The Son was tested by the Accuser.** Offered "all these things" for his worship, he refused and worshipped God alone (Matt 4:8–10).
- **He was hated "without a cause"** (John 15:25; the Psalms' חִנָּם).
- **He bore the curse itself, not only its words,** "having become a curse for us" (Gal 3:13).
- **He cried out, and was answered** by the Father who raised him.
- **Because of him the Accuser has been thrown down** (Rev 12:10).

So the church may sit with the suffering in silence, and let them cry, without needing to explain. The answer to "for nothing?" has been given, and it was not given by us. `[I]` — the typology is moderate (unit report, Christological Reading).

*Function:* press the PS. Silence before explanation; lament as faith's language; worship grounded in Christ, not in the hedge.

## Keep Honest

1. **Do not stop at 1:21.**
   - "The patience of Job" from chapter 1 alone is the unit's besetting misreading.
   - The torn robe comes before the blessing, and chapter 3 follows it.
   - End at 3:1, and let the cry stand.
2. **Do not protect God by blaming Satan.**
   - God opens both councils, sets both limits, and says "without reason" (2:3).
   - Job ascribes the loss to the LORD and to God (1:21; 2:10), and the narrator rules both no sin.
   - Hold the tension; do not resolve it.
3. **Do not tell anyone "God is proving you".**
   - The council is disclosed to the reader, not to Job.
   - The friends will make the equivalent move and be condemned (42:7).
   - The pastoral application is restraint, not a key to someone's suffering.
4. **Do not villainise Job's wife, or blame the children.**
   - She has one line, has lost the same ten children, and gets no narrator's verdict. Job rebukes her words and includes her in "we" (2:10).
   - The text never says the children's feasts were sinful. Job's "It may be" (1:5) is never confirmed, and the children are not "replaced" in 42:13.
   - Bereaved hearers will be listening.
5. **Do not preach the synthetic links as proven.**
   - The Gen 22 inversion, Exod 9 at 2:12, Lam 4:8, and the 1:4 ↔ 3:1 "his day" link are verified in their words but open in their design. They are claim-audit targets.
   - Use them as colour if at all, never as the sermon's hinge.
   - The Deut 28 links (1:10; 2:7) are firmer and may bear weight.

## Christ-centred check

1. **"Be like Job", or "trust the Christ whom Job points to"?**
   - The TP's subject is God's worth, and its complement ends in the Servant.
   - Job's worship is the evidence in the story, not the demand on the hearer.
   - The one command-shaped application (sit in silence with the suffering) flows from grace received, not from a standard to meet. **Pass.**
2. **Is Christ the subject or merely an example?**
   - Movement 4 presents Christ as the one who answers the Accuser's charge in our place and throws the Accuser down (Rev 12:10), not as a better Job. **Pass.**
3. **Is the good news grounded in Christ's saving work?**
   - Yes: the curse borne (Gal 3:13), the accuser cast down (Rev 12:10), grace given "as a gift" (δωρεάν, Rom 3:24). **Pass.**
   - *Caution:* Gal 3:13 is a conceptual escalation, not a citation of Job. Present it as the canon's answer, not as Job's meaning.

## Pulpit-text note (ESV)

Checked against the ESV export for Job 1–3 and for the parallels (Gen 22; Exod 9; Deut 28; 2 Sam 1; Ps 113).

**Where the ESV helps the plan.** Six notes; the pulpit text does real work for this sermon.
- **1:9 / 2:3 — "for no reason … without reason".** The ESV keeps חִנָּם audibly the same in the Accuser's charge and God's admission. **The TP's hinge is in the pew Bible.** (NASB95 has "for nothing … without cause", which hides it.)
- **1:6, 13; 2:1 — "Now there was a day … Now there was a day … Again there was a day".** The scene markers are audible, so the reading itself shows the structure.
- **1:22 / 2:10 — "In all this Job did not sin …" twice.** The two verdicts are a visible pair, and "with his lips" (2:10) is kept.
- **2:3 / 2:9 — "holds fast his integrity" / "hold fast your integrity".** The wife's echo of God is word for word. Movement 3 can simply point to it.
- **2:10 — "Shall we receive good from God, and shall we not receive evil?"** The ESV keeps רַע ("evil"), where the NASB95 has "adversity". That is stronger for the TP, and "all this evil" at 2:11 follows.
- **2:7 — "from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head".** This is word for word the ESV's Deut 28:35, so the covenant-curse link is audible. Only the disease word differs ("loathsome sores" against Deuteronomy's "grievous boils").

**Where the congregation will not hear what the plan rests on.** Four notes.
- **Movement 2's hinge — 1:11 "curse you" / 1:21 "blessed be the name".** Both are ברך ("bless") in the Hebrew. The ESV, rightly for sense, has "curse" at 1:5, 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9. **Supply it:** "The word Satan uses in verse 11 — the word our Bibles rightly translate 'curse' — is in Hebrew the word 'bless'. Satan swore Job would 'bless' God to his face. And Job did — in the other sense."
- **3:1 — "cursed the day of his birth".** The Hebrew has only "his day", echoing the sons' feasts "each one on his day" (1:4, which the ESV keeps). The link is optional colour (Keep Honest 5), but the object of the curse matters: it is the day, not God. The ESV makes that plain enough.
- **1:12 against Gen 22:12.** The ESV has "do not stretch out your hand" (Job) against "Do not lay your hand" (Genesis), so the verbal echo is lost. It is not needed for the plan.
- **2:12 — "toward heaven".** The ESV keeps the direction, but its Exod 9:8 has "in the air", so the plague echo is lost. It is not needed for the plan (Keep Honest 5).

**Minor.**
- 1:9 "fear God" is kept, and matches Gen 22:12's "you fear God" — usable if the Abraham parallel is mentioned.
- 1:20 "fell on the ground and worshiped" against 2 Sam 1:2 "fell to the ground and paid homage": close, not identical.
- 2:13 "suffering" for NASB95 "pain" has no bearing.

**Note on the unit report.** The first issue of the unit report said the ESV loses the Deut 28:35 link at 2:7. Checking the export at this stage showed that the sole-to-crown phrase is kept in all three versions. The report has been corrected and re-rendered.
