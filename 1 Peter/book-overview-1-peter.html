CANONICAL POSITION

Section

  • Collection: General Epistles (Hebrews–Jude)
  • This book’s position: Middle of the General Epistles; the first of two Petrine letters
  • Reading implication: Non-Pauline apostolic letter; pastoral instruction to churches the author did not plant but has apostolic responsibility for. Broadens the NT’s voice beyond Paul — Peter and Paul share common tradition (cf. 1 Cor 15:11) but Peter’s emphasis on exile identity and the suffering-to-glory pattern is distinctive
  • Presupposes: Torah (Exodus 12, 19, 24; Leviticus holiness code; Genesis — Abraham/Sarah, Noah); Prophets (Isaiah extensively — chs. 8, 11, 28, 40, 43, 52–53); Psalms (34, 118); Hosea 1–2; Proverbs 3, 11; the Gospel events (Christ’s death, resurrection, ascension); early Christian tradition shared with Paul
  • Building or reflecting? Building — constructing a theological framework for how the eschatological people of God should live as a marginalised minority in a hostile society, grounded in Christ’s suffering-to-glory pattern
  • Preceding book (canonical): James — shares diaspora language (Jas 1:1) and concern for practical conduct, but addresses internal community issues (wealth, speech, favouritism) while 1 Peter addresses the community’s relationship with hostile outsiders
  • This book’s unique contribution: The NT’s most sustained treatment of how gospel identity shapes conduct under social pressure — neither accommodating nor retaliating, but doing good under fire, following Christ’s pattern, trusting the righteous Judge
  • Handoff to next book: 2 Peter shifts from external threats (social hostility) to internal threats (false teachers). The “stand firm” of 1 Peter 5:12 becomes the “grow in grace and knowledge” of 2 Peter 3:18 — having weathered persecution, the church must now guard its doctrine

This Matters Because

1 Peter is the New Testament’s most concentrated treatment of how the gospel of Jesus Christ shapes the believer’s engagement with a hostile culture. It draws more heavily on the OT per verse than almost any other NT book, and its hermeneutical key (1:10–12) explicitly claims that the prophets wrote to serve these communities. Peter constructs the church’s identity from Israel’s Scriptures and then shows what that identity demands in the midst of social suffering. Without 1 Peter, the canon would lack a sustained, apostolic treatment of Christian identity and conduct under social pressure — neither retreating nor retaliating but doing good while trusting God for vindication.

STORYLINE PLACEMENT

  • Timeline: Written c. AD 62–65 from Rome (“Babylon,” 5:13), probably during Nero’s reign but before the Neronian persecution proper
  • Historical situation: Peter, nearing the end of his life, writes from Rome to predominantly Gentile Christian communities scattered across five regions of northern Asia Minor (modern Turkey). These communities have come to faith, received OT instruction, and now find that their commitment to Christ has made them social outsiders. Their situation is one of verbal abuse, slander, marginalisation, and discrimination — not yet state-sponsored persecution or martyrdom
  • Original audience situation: Gentile converts who have turned away from the pagan lifestyle of their neighbours (4:3–4), now experiencing hostility precisely because they no longer participate in the social and religious life of their communities. They are “surprised” by the fiery ordeal (4:12), suggesting this hostility is relatively new or intensifying

What would be missing without this book: Without 1 Peter, the NT would lack a sustained pastoral treatment of how the gospel reshapes identity and conduct for believers living as a marginalised minority. Paul addresses similar themes (e.g., Romans 12–13), but 1 Peter makes the exile identity its central organising principle and develops the suffering-to-glory pattern as the normative shape of Christian existence between the times. It also provides the NT’s most extensive Christological use of Isaiah 53 outside the Gospels, the clearest hermeneutical statement about how the OT prophets serve the church (1:10–12), and the definitive apostolic instruction on Christian cultural engagement — neither conformity nor resistance, but a differentiated doing-good-under-pressure that creates what Sach calls “the contradiction.”

Diagnostic sentence: If you were a Gentile convert in northern Asia Minor in the early 60s — recently turned from paganism, now slandered by former friends, losing social standing, wondering whether this gospel that promised joy has brought nothing but grief — this book gives you a complete reorientation of your identity (you are God’s chosen people with an imperishable inheritance), a pattern for your conduct (do good even when it costs you, following Christ who did the same), and a promise for your future (the God of all grace will himself restore you after a little while) — grounded in Scripture, centred on Christ, sealed by apostolic authority — that you cannot get from Paul’s letters (which address different situations), from James (which addresses internal community issues), or from Hebrews (which addresses the temptation to return to Judaism rather than paganism).

Intertextual Echoes in the Opening

The Exodus 24 echo (“sprinkling with blood”) in 1:2 signals that the letter will read the church’s experience through the lens of Israel’s story. This is confirmed by the cascade of OT allusions that follows: new birth (1:3), inheritance language (1:4), tested-by-fire imagery (1:7), prophets who served the church (1:10–12), girding loins for the journey (1:13), Passover lamb (1:19), holiness code (1:16), and the exodus from futility (1:18).

STORYLINE DEVELOPMENT

Opening Move

Peter opens with an eruption of praise (1:3–12) that establishes everything the letter will build on. In one long Greek sentence (1:3–12), he grounds his readers’ identity in the work of the triune God: the Father’s mercy has given them new birth into a living hope through Christ’s resurrection, securing an imperishable inheritance guarded in heaven for them. Their present suffering is temporary (“a little while,” 1:6) and purposeful (testing faith’s genuineness, 1:7), and what they now experience is what prophets and angels longed to understand (1:10–12). This opening move reframes suffering: it is not evidence that the gospel has failed but confirmation that they stand at the climax of salvation history. Narratologically, Peter removes suspense deliberately (Joseph): he tells the audience how the story ends — living hope, imperishable inheritance, salvation ready to be revealed — before they have heard the full weight of what they must endure. Knowing the ending gives them courage for the middle. This is pastorally shrewd: a congregation that knows God will restore them (5:10) can hear the command to suffer without despair.

Movement Type

Spiral with escalation. Peter cycles through the same themes (identity → conduct → suffering → Christ → hope) with increasing intensity. The spiral tightens as the letter progresses: suffering moves from embedded/supportive material (sections 1–2) to mainline argument (section 3). The letter does not move linearly from doctrine to application (as in Paul’s Romans or Ephesians), but spirals through indicative and imperative together, each revolution pressing deeper into the reality of suffering and the adequacy of Christ.

Summary of the Book’s Logic

Peter grounds the identity of his readers in God’s electing grace and Christ’s resurrection (1:3–2:10), then shows what that identity demands in a hostile world — doing good even when slandered, following Christ’s example of non-retaliatory suffering, trusting the righteous Judge (2:11–4:11) — and finally normalises suffering as the characteristic shape of Christian existence between Christ’s first and second comings, assuring readers that the God of all grace will restore them after they have suffered a little while (4:12–5:11). The whole letter is framed by 5:12: “This is the true grace of God. Stand firm in it.”

In Isaiah’s own context, this cornerstone is God’s answer to the leaders’ “covenant with death” — their trust in Egyptian alliance rather than divine provision (28:14–15). The cornerstone represents everything the false refuge lacks: tested, precious, sure. Those who trust it “will never be stricken with panic” when crisis comes (28:16b). First, Peter shifts from “do not fear what they fear” (subjective — the fear captivating the people) to “do not fear their threat” (objective — the threat persecutors pose), using the plural αὐτῶν to make the reference unmistakable (Van Rensburg). The pastoral logic: when believers fear their persecutors, they de facto grant those persecutors lordship over their lives; “revere Christ as Lord” (3:15a) reclaims that lordship. Second, Peter substitutes τὸν Χριστόν for κύριον τὸν θεόν — a text demanding absolute reverence for YHWH is redirected to Christ without argument or defence. Key Methodological Notes

  1. Explicitness decreases through the letter (Egan). The body opening has full introductory formulae and extended quotations; by the body closing, quotations are shorter and integrated more seamlessly. This is a deliberate authorial pattern, not carelessness.
  2. Isaiah dominates. Of the six most significant OT passages in the letter, four are from Isaiah (8, 28, 40, 53), plus allusions to Isaiah 11, 43, and 52. The Isaianic narrative of God’s restored presence among his suffering people provides the theological spine.
  3. Psalm 34 provides the ethical framework. Its vocabulary permeates the letter and its quotation at 3:10–12 functions as the structural hinge. The psalm’s message — God protects and vindicates the righteous sufferer — is the pastoral heart of 1 Peter.
  4. Contexts travel. When Peter quotes Isaiah 28:16, the broader context of Isaiah 28 (judgment on those who trust in wrong things, salvation for those who trust God’s cornerstone) informs his argument. When he quotes Psalm 34, the psalm’s superscription (David fleeing Abimelech, sojourning among enemies) resonates with the exile theme.
  5. 1 Peter 1:10–12 is the hermeneutical key (Sargent). Peter claims the prophets wrote to serve the church — their words find their primary meaning in Christ’s sufferings and glories. This produces what Sargent calls a “primitive” and “sectarian” use of Scripture: the OT is read as addressing the church’s situation directly, with determinate meaning oriented toward Christ and his people.
  6. Cumulative cultic imagery paints the community as God’s dwelling place (Mbuvi). Individual cultic references — blood-sprinkling (1:2), Passover lamb (1:19), holiness code (1:15–16), spiritual house and holy priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices (2:5), the Spirit of glory resting on them (4:14), judgment beginning at God’s household (4:17) — are individually noted throughout the map above. But their cumulative weight deserves attention: taken together, they construct a picture of the believing community as the place where God dwells among his people. For a community distanced from the Jerusalem temple’s cultus, being told “you are the temple” carries enormous pastoral force. This is not a full-blown temple theology (Peter never uses ναός) but the imagery is pervasively temple-adjacent, and the preacher should feel its cumulative resonance, especially when preaching 2:4–10 and 4:12–19.

Why Isaiah? The Intertextual Programme

The intertextual map above catalogues which Isaiah texts appear where. But the prior question — why does Peter reach for Isaiah at all, and how do the individual allusions work together? — is what transforms the map from a scholarly apparatus into a preaching tool.

Peter isn’t proof-texting Isaiah. He’s inhabiting Isaiah’s narrative. Isaiah tells a story with a specific shape: God’s people are scattered in exile → God’s enduring word goes out announcing return (Isa 40) → a servant suffers unjustly, bearing sins without retaliation (Isa 53) → the servant’s disciples are told not to fear what others fear but to revere the LORD alone (Isa 8:12–13) → God’s Spirit rests on his people (Isa 11:2) → God’s presence is restored among a reconstituted people who are a holy priesthood proclaiming his mighty acts (Isa 43:20–21; 61:6) → the day of visitation comes bringing judgment and vindication (Isa 10:3). That narrative arc is the arc of 1 Peter, applied christologically to the churches in Asia Minor.

The sequence of Isaiah allusions through the letter broadly follows the sequence of the Isaianic narrative itself (Egan). God’s people are scattered as exiles (1:1). God’s imperishable word has been preached to them as gospel — and Peter identifies this explicitly as Isaiah’s word (1:24–25, quoting Isa 40:6–8). Christ the cornerstone is laid in Zion — believe and you will not be put to shame; stumble and you fall (2:6–8, quoting Isa 28:16 and 8:14). The community is constituted as a people called to declare God’s mighty acts (2:9, echoing Isa 43:20–21). Christ the servant suffered without retaliation, bearing sins in his body, healing by his wounds (2:22–25, weaving five clauses from Isa 53). Believers are told not to fear their persecutors but to revere Christ as Lord — a text demanding reverence for YHWH redirected to Christ without argument (3:14–15, quoting Isa 8:12–13). The Spirit of glory and of God rests on those who suffer for Christ’s name — the messianic endowment of Isaiah 11:2 now applied to the church (4:14). The day of visitation approaches (2:12, echoing Isa 10:3).

Why Isaiah rather than Jeremiah or Ezekiel? Because Isaiah’s narrative of exile → suffering servant → restored divine presence → ultimate vindication is the narrative that most precisely fits what the churches in Asia Minor are living through. They are exiles (1:1). They are suffering unjustly (2:19–20). They need to know that God’s presence is with them — not in a temple building but in their community as God’s dwelling place (2:5; 4:14). They need to be assured that vindication follows suffering, because that is the pattern established by the servant himself (1:11; 5:10). Isaiah wrote their story before they lived it. This is exactly what 1:10–12 claims: the prophets wrote to serve you. The sufferings destined for Christ and the subsequent glories (τὰ εἰς Χριστὸν παθήματα καὶ τὰς μετὰ ταῦτα δόξας) — that is the Isaianic pattern.

But the fit goes deeper than narrative arc. Reading the Isaiah passages in their original context reveals that every one of them addresses the same underlying choice: trust in human schemes (false refuge) or trust in the LORD alone. In Isaiah 8:12–13, the faithful remnant must choose between conspiracy theories and political fear on the one hand, and reverent awe of the LORD on the other — for those who trust, God becomes a sanctuary; for those who reject, a stumbling stone (8:14). In Isaiah 28:14–22, the leaders have made “a covenant with death” — trusting Egyptian alliance rather than God’s tested cornerstone — and those who rely on the cornerstone “will never be stricken with panic” when crisis comes. In Isaiah 40:6–8, all human strength is temporary (“grass withers, flowers fall”) but God’s word endures forever — the herald announces regime change: “Here is your God!” In Isaiah 53, the servant who is “high and lifted up” (using the same throne-room language as 6:1) becomes the altar itself, bearing the sin that has blinded nations — and those who trust the servant’s work receive the sight that no previous intervention could provide. In Isaiah 11:2, the Spirit rests on the coming king who is the antithesis of faithless Ahaz — where Ahaz trusted human schemes, this king trusts the word of God alone. Peter’s churches face exactly the same decision Isaiah’s audience faced: trust in social conformity (the false refuge of blending back into pagan life to avoid suffering) or trust in Christ as Lord (the tested cornerstone who will never put them to shame). Every Isaiah passage Peter quotes is a moment in this choice. The congregation is not just living inside Isaiah’s narrative — they are facing Isaiah’s question.

Peter deploys Isaiah 40–48 as a coherent block, not a quarry of isolated verses. Beyond the individual allusions traced above, Peter applies the entire courtroom-witness commission of Isaiah 40–48 as an integrated programme. In Isaiah 40–48, the sevenfold “I am He” (אֲנִי הוּא) establishes God’s identity, and the sevenfold “fear not” (אַל־תִּירָא) equips his people emotionally, so that by 48:20 they are ready to be sent. The logic is integrated: suffering refines (48:10) → fear is displaced (41:10ff) → witness is assigned (43:10, 21) → God’s praises are proclaimed. Crucially, Isaiah 48 reframes exile as mission: the place to tell the whole world who the king of the world is, it turns out, is out there in the world. “You are my witnesses” is not an emergency plan; it is the original plan, now executed through exiles.

Peter applies this entire sequence: the furnace of refinement (1:7; 4:12 ← Isa 48:10), the fear-not programme (3:14 ← Isa 8:12–13, tracking Isaiah 41–44’s witness-equipping logic), and the witness vocation itself (2:9 ← Isa 43:21, fused with Exod 19:5–6). The ἀπολογία of 3:15 extends the commission into the specific context of suffering under pressure, and the ἀναστροφή vocabulary throughout the letter (1:15, 18; 2:12; 3:1–2, 16) describes conduct that itself functions as testimony. The preacher who understands this will preach suffering in 1 Peter not merely as something to endure but as the furnace that equips God’s witnesses — the Isaianic logic applied to the church’s mission.

The preaching implication: When preaching any passage that contains an Isaiah allusion, the preacher should not treat it as an isolated proof text Peter happened to find useful. It is a moment in a story the congregation is being invited to inhabit. “You are living inside this story. Isaiah wrote the chapter about exile — that’s your chapter. He wrote the chapter about the servant who suffered without retaliating — that’s Christ’s chapter, and it’s become yours through union with him. He wrote the chapter about God’s Spirit resting on his restored people — that’s happening to you right now, even as you suffer. And he wrote the chapter about vindication — that chapter is coming.” Each Isaiah allusion is a window onto this larger narrative, and the congregation should feel that they are inside it.

Key Vocabulary Threads

The “imperishable” thread: The word ἄφθαρτος and its cognates migrate through the letter, applying the same quality to different realities:

This thread teaches the congregation to see “imperishability” as a category — everything that matters in the Christian life shares this quality, in contrast to everything that perishes (gold, flesh, glory of grass). The entire thread is generated by Isaiah 40:6–8 (quoted at 1:24–25): “all flesh is like grass… but the word of our God endures forever.” Peter’s “imperishable” vocabulary is his way of applying Isaiah’s grass/word contrast to every dimension of the Christian life — inheritance, seed, beauty, crown. Everything his readers previously trusted (silver, gold, social standing, the approval of pagan neighbours) is grass. It withers. What God has given them through the gospel endures. The thread also includes the “perishable” counterparts: not with perishable things like silver or gold (1:18), the unfading crown of glory (5:4, ἀμαράντινον — possibly echoing Isa 28:5).

This transformation tracks Isaiah’s own “fear not” programme. Isaiah 41–44 contains a sevenfold “do not fear” sequence (41:10, 13, 14; 43:1, 5; 44:2, 8) whose explicit purpose is to equip Israel for witness: “Do not tremble, do not be afraid… You are my witnesses” (44:8). Comfort is not an anaesthetic — it is deployment briefing. Peter follows the identical pattern: the “fear not” of 3:14 (from Isa 8:12–13) is immediately followed by the witness command: “always be prepared to give an answer to everyone who asks you to give the reason for the hope that you have” (3:15b). Fear is displaced not so they can relax but so they can testify. The preacher who sees this connection will preach 3:14–15 as mission deployment, not merely pastoral reassurance.

The “precious” (τίμιος/ἔντιμος) thread: Isaiah 28:16 describes the cornerstone as ἐκλεκτὸν ἔντιμον — “chosen and precious.” Peter picks up this vocabulary and weaves it through the letter as a linking term:

The “precious” vocabulary migrates from the cornerstone to Christ’s blood to Christ himself to believers’ valuation of Christ. The logic is Isaianic: what you consider precious reveals where you’ve placed your trust. Those who find the cornerstone precious build their lives on him; those who don’t stumble over the very stone that could have saved them. This reinforces the trust-vs-false-refuge theme running through all Peter’s Isaiah quotations.

The “herald/good news” (εὐαγγελίζω) thread: Isaiah 40:9 commissions a herald to announce regime change (“Here is your God!”), and Isaiah 52:7 celebrates the herald announcing the servant’s accomplished work (“Your God reigns!”). Peter uses the same vocabulary:

10 4:6 “the gospel was preached even to the dead” The herald’s announcement reaches even beyond death

Peter is not using εὐαγγελίζω generically. He is identifying what happened to his readers as Isaiah’s herald announcement — the regime-change proclamation that the king is returning, the exile is ending, and the old order is over. The gospel is not advice or even theology; it is the announcement of accomplished fact. The herald model from Isaiah 40:9 and 52:7 defines what “good news” means throughout 1 Peter.

The “witness vocation” thread: Isaiah 43:21’s τὰς ἀρετάς μου (“my praises/excellencies”) becomes Peter’s τὰς ἀρετὰς ἐξαγγείλητε at 2:9. But the witness theme does not appear only there:

The preacher should help the congregation see that their suffering is vocational: they suffer as witnesses, not despite being witnesses.

WHAT PREACHERS TEND TO MISS ABOUT 1 PETER

1. Treating suffering as the theme rather than grace

Preachers often frame the series as “how to handle suffering” and build every sermon around the suffering motif. But Peter’s own summary is not “this is the suffering you must endure” but “this is the true grace of God — stand firm in it” (5:12). Grace is the theme; suffering is the context. The series should feel like a letter about grace that takes suffering seriously, not a letter about suffering with some grace thrown in. If your congregation leaves the series thinking mainly about suffering rather than the overwhelming grace they stand in, the balance has shifted from Peter’s.

2. Flattening “exile” into pure metaphor and losing the social reality

Peter’s readers are not just spiritually homeless — they are experiencing real social displacement, ostracism, and loss of standing. The exile language has theological depth (citizenship in heaven), but it also names a concrete sociological reality. Preaching “we’re all just passing through” without feeling the sting of what these believers have lost (friendships, family harmony, professional opportunities, social belonging) spiritualises the letter beyond Peter’s intent. The challenge is to hold the theological and social dimensions together.

3. Separating Christ as example from Christ as substitute

In 2:21–25, Peter holds together Christ’s exemplary suffering (leaving you a pattern to trace) and his substitutionary death (he bore our sins in his body on the tree). Some traditions emphasise the example and downplay the atonement; others rush to the atonement and neglect the example. Peter cannot say one without saying the other. The example has power precisely because the one who models non-retaliation is also the one who bore sins. Similarly in 3:18: Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous — both exemplary and unique. Preach both every time Peter holds them together.

4. Missing the Psalm 34 architecture

Many preachers treat the Psalm 34 quotation at 3:10–12 as an incidental proof text. Woan’s research shows it functions as the structural hinge of the letter — its vocabulary has been running through chapters 1–3 in sequential order, and its quotation is the pivot point between the conduct section and the suffering section. If you miss this, you miss the fact that Peter has built his entire ethical framework on a single psalm about the righteous sufferer whom God protects. This also means the “fear God / don’t fear people” dynamic is not a minor point but the psalm’s central logic applied to the readers’ situation.

5. Moralising the household codes

The temptation is to preach 2:18–3:7 as a freestanding ethics lesson: “wives, submit; slaves, obey; husbands, be kind.” But Peter’s household codes are embedded within his larger argument about how doing good under unjust treatment serves as witness. The slave passage is not primarily about workplace ethics — it’s about how Christ’s pattern of innocent suffering applies to the most powerless members of the community. The wives passage is not primarily about gender roles — it’s about how to live faithfully when your most intimate relationship is a context of opposition. Rip these from their literary context and they become moralistic; within Peter’s argument, they are Christological.

6. Resolving 3:18–22 too quickly (or avoiding it)

The “spirits in prison” passage tempts preachers to either skip it (too hard) or spend the whole sermon on it (fascinating puzzle). Both miss the pastoral function. Whether Christ proclaimed victory to fallen angels or the Spirit of Christ preached through Noah, the point for Peter’s argument is the same: Christ’s suffering led to total triumph over all hostile powers (3:22). That’s what the suffering church needs to hear. Spend proportionate time on the exegetical options but land on the pastoral point: the one who suffered for you now reigns over everything that threatens you.

7. Treating 4:12 as a new topic rather than the climax

Because the doxology at 4:11 feels like a conclusion, preachers sometimes treat 4:12–5:11 as an appendix or a separate letter. Starwalt’s discourse analysis shows that 4:12–19 is the denouement — the point toward which the entire letter has been building. Suffering has been present since 1:6 but gradually rising in salience: embedded in supportive material (2:18–20), acknowledged as possibility with the optative mood (3:14, 17), and finally declared as normative reality with the imperative mood (4:12). The letter builds to the moment when Peter can finally say “do not be surprised” — a statement he has been preparing his readers to receive across the entire preceding argument.

8. Ignoring the corporate dimension

Modern Western readers naturally apply 1 Peter to individual suffering. But Peter writes to communities, not isolated individuals. The love commands (1:22; 4:8), the brotherhood language (2:17; 5:9), the elder instructions (5:1–5), and the “one another” emphasis all show that standing firm is a communal activity. No one stands firm alone. The preacher should resist the pull to make every application individual and ensure the congregation hears Peter’s vision of a community that holds together under pressure.

9. Preaching the “do good” motif as passive niceness

Peter’s ἀγαθοποιέω (“do good”) is not bland niceness — it’s a deliberate, counter-cultural strategy. In a world that thinks Christians are evildoers (2:12), doing visible good creates what Sach calls “the contradiction”: they slander you as evil, but your conduct won’t let the accusation stick. This requires active, costly, conspicuous goodness — not just avoiding sin but pursuing good so visibly that even enemies are eventually shamed into acknowledging it (2:15; 3:16). It’s offensive, not defensive.

10. Missing the “already/not yet” tension in the eschatology

Peter holds together a present salvation (“you have been born again,” 1:3, 23; “you have tasted that the Lord is good,” 2:3; “by his wounds you have been healed,” 2:24) with a future consummation (“the salvation ready to be revealed in the last time,” 1:5; “the grace to be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ,” 1:13; “when his glory is revealed,” 4:13; “the unfading crown of glory,” 5:4). Preachers who collapse this into either “it’s all future” (creating despair) or “it’s all present” (creating false triumphalism) distort Peter’s pastoral strategy. The “little while” of present suffering (1:6; 5:10) is real, but it’s set within “eternal glory in Christ” (5:10).

11. Underplaying the new-exodus motif

Peter draws extensively on exodus imagery in the first section: girding loins (1:13), Passover lamb (1:19), holiness code (1:16), ransomed from slavery (1:18), Sinai covenant language (2:9). These aren’t decorative — they locate the readers’ experience within Israel’s defining story. God rescued Israel from Egypt to make them his holy people; God has rescued these Gentile believers from pagan futility to make them his holy people. The exodus is not just background — it’s the typological framework for understanding their conversion and calling.

NOTES FOR PREACHING

Key Textual Observations

1. The “himself” of 5:10 is the series’ emotional punchline. After ten weeks of imperatives directed at the readers (be holy, do good, don’t fear, stand firm, arm yourselves, entrust yourselves), the final word is what God will do: “The God of all grace… will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you.” The shift from human responsibility to divine promise is the structural payoff of the entire series. If the congregation doesn’t feel relief at this moment, the series hasn’t built the tension properly. Note also that 5:10 functions as the letter’s entire narrative in miniature (Joseph): calling (ὁ καλέσας = election), suffering (παθόντας), and vindication (καταρτίσει, στηρίξει, σθενώσει, θεμελιώσει = God restoring, confirming, strengthening, establishing) are all compressed into a single verse. The preacher can use this verse as the point where every thread the series has been tracing — election, suffering, faithful response, divine vindication — converges.

2. Psalm 34’s vocabulary runs sequentially through 1:1–3:9. Woan demonstrates that the key terms from Psalm 34:12–16 appear progressively in the same order throughout the first half of the letter before surfacing explicitly in the quotation at 3:10–12. This means Peter didn’t just drop in a proof text — he built his entire first-half rhetoric on the psalm’s structure. The quotation at 3:10–12 functions as a Janus, looking backward (summarising what preceded) and forward (grounding what follows). Preach it as the hinge it is.

3. The explicitness of OT quotations decreases through the letter. Egan observes that Peter moves from full introductory formulae and extended quotations in the body opening (1:13–2:10) to shorter, more seamlessly integrated allusions in the body closing (4:12–5:11). This isn’t carelessness — it’s a deliberate pattern. The early sections establish the scriptural framework; the later sections assume it. The preacher should be alert to echoes (e.g., Isaiah 53 vocabulary in 4:1, 13, 19; Isaiah 11:2 in 4:14) even when Peter doesn’t flag them with quotation marks.

4. The optative mood in 3:14 and 3:17 is pastorally deliberate. Starwalt and others note that Peter uses the optative (“if you should suffer”) rather than the indicative (“since you are suffering”) in chapter 3. Achtemeier suggests Peter is softening the discussion of a sensitive topic — slowly revealing the extent and normalcy of suffering without hitting his readers with it all at once. The optative of chapter 3 gives way to the imperative of 4:12 (“do not be surprised”). The preacher should respect this graduated revelation: don’t normalise suffering before Peter does.

5. “Do good” (ἀγαθοποιέω) is not passive niceness but counter-cultural strategy. In a context where Christians are accused of being evildoers (2:12), conspicuous goodness is an offensive weapon. Sach’s “live the contradiction” captures this: they call you evil, but your conduct won’t let the accusation stick. The “doing good” motif runs from 2:15 through to the very last instruction: “entrust yourselves to a faithful Creator while doing good” (4:19). It’s the thread that holds the whole conduct section together.

6. Christ as both ὑπογραμμός and ὑποφέρω. In 2:21–25, Peter holds together Christ as pattern (ὑπογραμμός — a template for tracing, like a child learning to write by tracing letters) and Christ as substitute (he bore our sins, 2:24). Wilkins notes that the pattern has power precisely because the one who models non-retaliation is also the one who bore sins. These are not competing theories of the atonement (contra Morna Hooker) but two inseparable aspects of one work. Every time Peter mentions Christ’s suffering as example, he immediately adds Christ’s suffering as salvation. Preach both; don’t choose.

7. The Lord’s Supper connection. 1 Peter 1:2 (“sprinkling with his blood”) echoes the covenant inauguration of Exodus 24. Peter’s readers are a people constituted by blood — not the blood of bulls but the precious blood of Christ, like a lamb without blemish (1:19). The series offers natural opportunities for communion services, particularly at weeks 2 (ransom), 5 (bore our sins), and 11 (stand firm in grace).

8. Week 10 is the series’ emotional crux. The Big Question — “Why does God let his own people suffer?” — is the question the whole letter has been building toward. The answer is not philosophical theodicy but pastoral reframing: sharing Christ’s sufferings now is the path to sharing his glory later. The landing needs weight, not glibness. Consider the tone of a hospital visit, not a victory rally.

9. Peter’s commands are invitations to inhabit a different world, not a checklist to tick off. Boring observes that the paraenetic material in 1 Peter functions not primarily as a list of instructions but as an alternative vision of reality. The reader is not simply weighing up whether to obey a command; they are being addressed as though they already live within a narrative world — a world where God created, Christ suffered and triumphed, the Spirit rests on the suffering church, and vindication is certain. The commands assume this world is real. This means the preacher’s job across the series is not merely to exhort (“do this!”) but to make the narrative world so vivid and compelling that the exhortation becomes the natural, almost obvious, response. People don’t need more willpower; they need to see the world Peter sees.
