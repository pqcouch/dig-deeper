# Claim audit 3 — Colossians — Auditor B

**Scope.** Six field and design claims (B1–B6). Every count was re-run from the corpus: the SBLGNT with its lemma index for the New Testament, Swete for the Greek Old Testament (with Rahlfs-Hanhart from the Logos exports checked wherever an export exists), the WLC for the Hebrew, and the NA28 text and apparatus of Colossians and Ephesians. Anything from my own memory of scholarship is tagged [S] and carries no verdict.

**How the six-run scan works, in words.** Each content word of the New Testament passage is cut back to an accent-free stem and matched against the beginnings of words in Swete. Every Swete chapter is then scored six ways: as a whole chapter, by its best eight-verse stretch and by its best sixteen-verse stretch, each once with all the shared words and once with the rarer words only. Rarer words count for more. A chapter's "median rank" is its fourth-best rank of the six. Three things follow. First, the scan cannot see compound or augmented forms: for Col 2:16–23 it reports no match anywhere in Swete for ζάω ("live"), although ζῶντες ("living") stands at Wis 14:22. It also misses θιγγάνω ("touch"), which is at Exod 19:12 as θιγεῖν ("to touch"). Second, a stem can catch a different word. Third, as the null tests below show, a high rank for *some* chapter is the normal result for *any* Pauline passage.

## B1 — Wisdom 14 as the polemical partner of Colossians 2:16–23

### What I tested
I re-ran the six-run scan of Col 2:16–23 against Swete. I sense-checked every Swete word the scan credited to Wisdom 14. I re-ran the scan leaving out each of the shared stems in turn, and two at a time. I scanned four other boundaries of the passage. I built a null of 80 Pauline eight-verse passages: 60 drawn at random from Romans to Titus (excluding Col 2:16–23), and 20 chosen as comparable polemical, food, calendar or idolatry passages (for example Gal 4:3–10, Rom 14:1–8, 1 Cor 8:1–8, 1 Cor 10:14–21, 1 Tim 4:1–8, Titus 1:10–16, Phil 3:2–9, Rom 1:18–25 and Col 2:8–15). Finally I searched for the θρησκ- stem ("worship, religion") anywhere in the word, across Swete and the NT.

### Results
**Reproduction.** The six ranks hold exactly. Wisdom 14 ranks 1, 1, 2, 2, 2 and 2, so its median is 2. 2 Maccabees 7 ranks 2, 3, 1, 3, 1 and 1 (median 2). Isaiah 1 ranks 6, 4, 3, 1, 3 and 6 (median 4). 4 Maccabees 5 ranks 3, 2, 57, 32, 4 and 5 (median 5).

**The θρησκ- stem.** Searched anywhere in the word, it occurs in exactly six Swete verses: Wis 11:15; 14:16, 18, 27; 4 Macc 5:6, 13. That count holds, and the control works: ἐθρήσκευον ("they worshipped"), an augmented form, is found at Wis 11:15. In the NT, θρησκεία ("religion, worship") occurs at Acts 26:5, Col 2:18 and Jas 1:26–27, with θρησκός ("religious") at Jas 1:26. ἐθελοθρησκία ("self-made religion") is a hapax at Col 2:23.

**Sense check of what the scan matched in Wisdom 14.** Eleven stems were credited.
- θρησκεία / θρησκεύω ("worship"), at 14:16, 18 and 27: a genuine contact, and pejorative there.
- φθορά ("corruption"), at 14:12 and 25: here it is moral ruin, "corruption of life". In Col 2:22, εἰς φθορὰν τῇ ἀποχρήσει ("for perishing through use"), it is the physical perishing of food. Same word, different sense.
- τιμή / τιμάω ("honour; to honour"), at 14:15, 17 and 20: honouring images. In Col 2:23, ἐν τιμῇ τινι ("of any value"), it means worth. Partly a false friend.
- σοφία ("wisdom"), at 14:2 and 5: the shipwright's craft and God's wisdom. In Col 2:23, λόγον … σοφίας ("a reputation for wisdom"). Generic.
- κόσμος ("world"), ἄνθρωπος ("human being"), θεός ("God"), θέλω ("wish"), μέλλω ("be about to") and κρατέω ("hold, rule"): generic. κρατυνθέν ("strengthened", 14:16) and κρατοῦντι ("to the ruler", 14:19) do not match the sense of οὐ κρατῶν τὴν κεφαλήν ("not holding fast to the head", Col 2:19).
- ποσίν ("feet", 14:11, "a snare for the feet of the foolish") was credited to πόσις ("drink", Col 2:16). **That is a false friend.**

**Leave-one-out (Wisdom 14's median rank).**

| Left out | Wisdom 14 | Best chapter then |
|---|---|---|
| nothing | 2 | Wisdom 14 and 2 Macc 7 tie at 2 |
| θρησκ- ("worship") | **9** | 2 Macc 7 (1) |
| φθορ- ("corruption") | 6 | 2 Macc 7 (1) |
| the false friend πόσις / ποσίν ("drink" / "feet") | **5** | 2 Macc 7 (1) |
| θρησκ- and φθορ- | 32 | 2 Macc 7 (1) |
| τιμ- ("honour") | 2 | — |
| σοφ- ("wisdom") | 2 | — |

The claim names μυστήρια ("mysteries"), but no μυστήριον ("mystery") occurs in Col 2:16–23. Its contribution to the scan is therefore nil, and that part of the claim reaches outside the passage to 1:26–27 and 2:2.

**Boundary stability (Wisdom 14's median rank).**

| Passage | Wisdom 14 | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Col 2:16–23 | 2 | |
| Col 2:8–23 | 2 | |
| Col 2:18–23 | 3 | |
| Col 2:20–23 | 7 | |
| Col 2:16–19 | 16 | Isaiah 1 is first here |

Wisdom 14 is not stable once θρησκεία ("worship") at 2:18 or ἐθελοθρησκία ("self-made religion") at 2:23 falls outside the window.

**Null distribution.** The claim says median 2 is "rare in a null". It is not.
- Random Pauline eight-verse passages (60): the best chapter reaches median 1 in 23 cases, 2 in 15, 3 in 7, 4 in 10, 5 in 2 and 6 in 3. So 38 of 60 (63 %) have some Swete chapter at median 2 or better.
- Comparable passages (20): 14 of 20 (70 %).
- Two chapters both at median 2 or better, as here: 8 of 60 random and 3 of 20 comparable passages (about 13–15 %).

The meaningless top partners in the null show what the scan measures. Est 8 (with its long Greek additions) tops 1 Thess 3:6–13 and 1 Tim 1:3–10. 3 Macc 5 tops 1 Cor 1:1–9. 1 Esd 4 tops 1 Cor 9:1–12. 2 Macc 7 itself tops Col 2:8–15 and ties for top here, and nobody proposes a martyrdom narrative as the partner of Col 2:16–23.

**Is Wisdom 14 a "hub" chapter?** No, which counts in its favour. Across the 80 null passages, Wisdom 14 reached median 2 or better only twice: Rom 1:18–25, a recognised Wisdom 13–15 passage [S], and 1 Tim 1:6–13, a vice list akin to Wis 14:24–26. So its appearance at the top is specific. But that specificity is carried almost entirely by one stem, θρησκ- ("worship").

**Positive control on the scan itself.** Col 2:22 quotes Isa 29:13 almost word for word: κατὰ τὰ ἐντάλματα καὶ διδασκαλίας τῶν ἀνθρώπων ("according to the commands and teachings of men"). ἔνταλμα ("commandment") occurs in only four Swete verses: Job 23:11, 12; Isa 29:13; 55:11. Yet in the scan of the whole of 2:16–23, Isaiah 29 has a median rank of only 22. It rises to 2 only when the window is cut to 2:20–23. So the scan can miss a certain allusion in a longer window. A median rank is weak evidence in either direction.

**Rival: 4 Maccabees 5.** The only other θρησκ- ("worship") chapter in Swete is 4 Maccabees 5, and it is closer to the *subject* of Col 2:16–21 than Wisdom 14 is. There the tyrant presses Eleazar to taste pork: ἀπογεύεσθαι ("to taste", 5:2) and ἀπογευσάμενος ("having tasted", 5:6), compounds the scan could not see beside γεύσῃ ("taste", Col 2:21). The issue is food law, and θρησκεία ("religion") names *Jewish* observance (5:6, 13). Acts 26:5 likewise uses θρησκεία for Paul's Pharisaic religion. So the word is the natural Greek name for a Jewish regime of observance. It does not need Wisdom's anti-idol polemic to explain it. The pejorative colour in Col 2:18 comes from τῶν ἀγγέλων ("of the angels") and from the compound in 2:23. 4 Maccabees is probably first-century CE and may postdate Colossians [S], so its availability is doubtful. It is cited here as evidence of usage, not as a source.

**The other stated contacts.**
- ἐπιταγαῖς ("by commands", Wis 14:16) is not a Colossians word. Colossians has δόγμα ("decree", 2:14), δογματίζομαι ("submit to decrees", 2:20), ἔνταλμα ("commandment", 2:22) and διδασκαλία ("teaching", 2:22). This contact is conceptual only.
- κρύφια μυστήρια ("secret mysteries", Wis 14:23) are pagan initiation rites. In Col 1:26–27 and 2:2, τὸ μυστήριον ("the mystery") is God's plan, hidden and now revealed. That is the sense of Daniel 2: μυστήριον ("mystery") is in seven verses of the Old Greek chapter (2:18, 19, 27, 28, 29, 30, 47). Dan 2:47 has μυστήρια κρυπτά ("hidden mysteries"). The sense of Wisdom 14 is the opposite of Colossians' sense.
- The human origin of false worship (Wis 14:14–16) is a real thematic contact. But Col 2:22 takes it explicitly from Isa 29:13, not from Wisdom.

### Verdict
**Needs reframing. Recommended rating: low.**

The rank reproduces, but "rare in a null" is false: about two-thirds of Pauline passages produce a chapter at median 2 or better. Wisdom 14's place depends on one stem, and correcting a single false friend drops it to median 5. What survives is a word-level observation: θρησκεία ("worship") is rare in the Greek Old Testament, and in Wisdom 14 and 4 Maccabees 5 it is applied to worship judged false. It can be noted as such. It is not a "polemical partner" chapter.

## B2 — Isaiah 1:13–14 at Colossians 2:16 and 2:23

### What I tested
- The calendar triad in a single Swete verse, with both spellings of the new moon: νουμηνία / νεομηνία ("new moon").
- πλησμονή ("satiety, surfeit") in Swete: every verse, its sense, and whether it meets a calendar word.
- The wording of Isa 1:13–14 in Rahlfs and the WLC.
- Ranks and leave-one-out.
- Word order against Hos 2:11 and Ezek 45:17.

### Results
**The calendar triad.** ἑορτή ("feast") with νουμηνία / νεομηνία ("new moon") and σάββατα ("sabbaths") in one Swete verse:
- 1 Chr 23:31 (spelt νεομηνίαις, "new moons");
- 2 Chr 2:4; 31:3;
- 1 Esd 5:51;
- Neh 10:33;
- Hos 2:11;
- Ezek 45:17;
- 1 Macc 10:34;
- and, if compounds are admitted, Jdt 8:6, which has προσαββάτων ("eves of sabbaths"), σαββάτων ("sabbaths"), προνουμηνιῶν ("eves of new moons") and ἑορτῶν ("feasts").

**Correction.** The stated list of seven omits 1 Chr 23:31, so the count is **eight** (nine with Judith).

**Isa 1:13–14 is not itself a one-verse triad.** Isa 1:13 has νουμηνίας ("new moons") and σάββατα ("sabbaths"). Isa 1:14 has νουμηνίας ("new moons") and ἑορτάς ("feasts"). Rahlfs agrees with Swete word for word in both verses.

**πλησμονή ("satiety").** It occurs in 27 Swete verses, plus Pss 5:20 in the Psalms of Solomon. The search is on the stem anywhere in the word, and its control works: Isa 1:14 is returned. **Isa 1:14 is the only verse in which it meets any calendar word. That part of the claim holds.** Rahlfs agrees (εἰς πλησμονήν, "into a surfeit"). The WLC has לָטֹרַח ("a burden") at Isa 1:14, so the contact is Greek-only. That also holds.

**The sense of πλησμονή.** Elsewhere it is almost always being filled with food:
- bread and meat "to the full" in the wilderness: Exod 16:3, 8, where κρέα ("meat") stands in the same verse;
- harvests: Lev 25:19; 26:5;
- the manna, "bread of angels … sent to them to the full" (Ps 77:25 in LXX numbering), with σάρκας ("flesh, meat") rained down two verses later at 77:27;
- Sodom's "surfeit of bread": Ezek 16:49;
- also Hag 1:6; Isa 55:2; Hos 13:6.

Isa 1:14 is the one place where it is used of God, metaphorically: "you have become a surfeit to me", that is, nauseating. In Col 2:23, πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός ("for the gratification of the flesh") belongs to the food sense. It sits in a passage about βρῶσις ("eating", 2:16) and γεῦσις ("tasting", 2:21). The ordinary food passages are therefore closer in sense than Isa 1:14. The only Swete verse in which πλησμονή ("satiety") falls within two verses of σάρξ ("flesh") is Ps 77:25. It is the same word with a **different sense: a false friend at the level of meaning**, though not of lexeme.

**Ranks and leave-one-out (Isaiah 1's median rank).**

| Scan | Isaiah 1 |
|---|---|
| Col 2:16–23, as scanned | 4 (ranks 1–6) |
| without πλησμονή ("satiety") | 16 |
| without the three calendar words | 30 |
| without both | 77 |
| Col 2:16–19 only (no πλησμονή) | 1 |

Isaiah 1 is not a hub: across the 80 null passages it reached median 5 or better only four times. Both strands carry weight, and the calendar strand is the stronger. But a calendar strand cannot favour Isaiah 1 over the other calendar texts on vocabulary alone. In the same scan Hos 2 and Ezek 45 sit at medians 136 and 124. This is only because their chapters share nothing else with the passage; their verse-level match to 2:16 is closer.

**Word order.** Col 2:16 lists ἑορτῆς ("feast"), νουμηνίας ("new moon"), σαββάτων ("sabbaths"). That order is matched exactly by Hos 2:11 and Ezek 45:17 in Swete, and by their Hebrew: חַג ("feast"), חֹדֶשׁ ("new moon"), שַׁבָּת ("sabbath"). Chronicles, Nehemiah and 1 Esdras run in reverse (sabbaths, new moons, feasts). Isa 1:13–14 has a different order spread over two verses. Hos 2:11 is a text of cessation ("I will put an end to … her feasts, her new moons and her sabbaths"). Ezek 45:17 concerns the feasts of the future temple, which bears on σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων ("a shadow of the things to come", Col 2:17) [S, as a reading]. On the verse level, both are at least as close to Col 2:16 as Isaiah 1 is.

**A point in Isaiah's favour that the claim did not name.** Isa 1:13 in Swete and Rahlfs ends νηστείαν καὶ ἀργίαν ("fasting and idleness"). The WLC has אָוֶן וַעֲצָרָה ("iniquity and solemn assembly"). So the Greek Isaiah alone sets *fasting* beside new moons and sabbaths. That would suit the ascetic regime of Col 2:18, 23: ταπεινοφροσύνη ("humility", often understood as fasting [S]) and ἀφειδία σώματος ("severity to the body"). Like πλησμονή ("satiety"), it is a Greek-only plus.

### Verdict
**Needs reframing. Recommended rating: low–moderate.**

The claim set its own test: "the πλησμονή contact is the test". The contact is unique at the level of words, but it fails on sense. Isaiah's "surfeit" is God's disgust; Colossians' is the flesh's gratification, the ordinary food sense found in Exodus 16 and Psalm 77. On the triad, Hos 2:11 and Ezek 45:17 match Col 2:16's order exactly. Isaiah 1 does not, and its triad is not even in one verse. Isa 1:13–14 can stand as one of several calendar texts. Its Greek-only pluses, πλησμονή ("satiety") and νηστεία ("fasting"), are worth noting as a possible faint layer, but not as "the" source.

## B3 — The ἀρεσκ- family ("pleasing") at 1:10 → 3:20 → 3:22 as designed

### What I tested
- Lemma counts in the SBLGNT for ἀρεσκεία ("pleasing"), ἀρέσκω ("please"), εὐάρεστος ("well-pleasing"), εὐαρεστέω ("be pleasing"), εὐαρέστως ("acceptably"), ἀρεστός ("pleasing") and ἀνθρωπάρεσκος ("people-pleaser"), across the NT and in Paul.
- A baseline: for ten opening prayers in eight Pauline letters, how many of the prayer's content-word families recur in the letter's ethical section, and how many of those are rare in the letter.
- A sense check of the Pauline uses.
- The NA28 apparatus at 3:20 and 3:22.

### Results
**Counts.** All hold.
- In Colossians, each member occurs exactly once: ἀρεσκεία ("pleasing") at 1:10, εὐάρεστος ("well-pleasing") at 3:20, ἀνθρωπάρεσκος ("people-pleaser") at 3:22. There is no ἀρέσκω ("please") or εὐαρεστέω ("be pleasing").
- ἀρεσκεία ("pleasing") is an NT hapax.
- ἀνθρωπάρεσκος ("people-pleaser") occurs only at Col 3:22 and Eph 6:6.
- Across the Pauline corpus (Romans to Titus), the root occurs 25 times. Romans has 7, 1 Corinthians 4, 1 Thessalonians 3, Colossians 3, Galatians 2 (both at 1:10), Ephesians 2, and 2 Corinthians, Philippians, 2 Timothy and Titus 1 each.

**"In everything."** κατὰ πάντα ("in everything") does occur at 3:20 and 3:22. But NA28 brackets it at 3:22 as omitted by 𝔓⁴⁶ 075 0278 81 1241ˢ and part of the Coptic. And πᾶς ("all, every") is everywhere in Colossians: 39 times, in 30 of its 95 verses. That makes it a weak link to πᾶσαν ἀρεσκείαν ("all pleasing", 1:10).

**Baseline.** I compared content-word families (nouns, verbs and adjectives, cognates merged, divine names excluded) in each opening prayer with those in the ethical section.
- Colossians 1:9–14 → 3:1–4:6: 13 of 39 families recur (33 %), four of them rare in the letter (three times or fewer).
- Ephesians 1:15–23 → 4:1–6:20: 32 of 49 (65 %), fifteen of them rare.
- Philemon 4–7 → 8–22: 7 of 16.
- 1 Thessalonians 1:2–3 → 4:1–5:22: 6 of 10.
- Romans 1:8–15 → 12:1–15:13: 10 of 39.
- 2 Thessalonians 1:11–12 → 3:1–15: 3 of 12.

For nine of the ten opening prayers, at least one family that is rare in the letter carries from the prayer into an ethical section. The only exception is 1 Corinthians, tested against 5–6 and 10:23–11:1. So a prayer word reappearing in the paraenesis is the norm, not a signal. Narrowing to the household code: Col 1:9–14 shares about six root families with 3:18–4:1. These are κύριος ("Lord"), πᾶς ("all"), πατήρ ("father"), ἀγάπη / ἀγαπάω ("love; to love"), ἔργον / ἐργάζομαι ("work; to work"), and the ἀρεσκ- ("pleasing") and κληρ- ("lot, inheritance") families (with ἀκούω / ὑπακούω, "hear / obey", as a compound). Two of these are rare: ἀρεσκ- and κληρ-.

**The Pauline idiom.** Each part of the 1:10 → 3:20–22 chain has a stock parallel elsewhere in Paul:
- 1 Thess 4:1: περιπατεῖν καὶ ἀρέσκειν θεῷ ("to walk and to please God"). This is the same pairing as Col 1:10, περιπατῆσαι … εἰς πᾶσαν ἀρεσκείαν ("to walk … for all pleasing"), and it stands at the head of 1 Thessalonians' ethical section.
- Gal 1:10: ἀνθρώποις ἀρέσκειν … Χριστοῦ δοῦλος ("to please people … a slave of Christ"). 1 Thess 2:4 is similar: οὐχ ὡς ἀνθρώποις ἀρέσκοντες ἀλλὰ θεῷ ("not as pleasing people but God"). Both have the same antithesis as Col 3:22–24: ἀνθρωπάρεσκοι ("people-pleasers") against τῷ κυρίῳ Χριστῷ δουλεύετε ("you serve the Lord Christ").
- Rom 14:18: δουλεύων τῷ Χριστῷ εὐάρεστος τῷ θεῷ ("serving Christ is well-pleasing to God").
- Titus 2:9, in a slave code: δούλους … ὑποτάσσεσθαι ἐν πᾶσιν, εὐαρέστους εἶναι ("slaves are to submit in everything, to be well-pleasing"). This has εὐάρεστος ("well-pleasing") plus "in everything", addressed to slaves, with no opening prayer behind it.
- 2 Cor 5:9–11: εὐάρεστοι ("well-pleasing"), κομίσηται ("may receive back") and τὸν φόβον τοῦ κυρίου ("the fear of the Lord"). Col 3:20–25 has the same cluster: εὐάρεστον ("well-pleasing"), φοβούμενοι τὸν κύριον ("fearing the Lord") and κομίσεται ("will receive back").

**Ephesians.** Ephesians has no ἀρεσκ- ("pleasing") word in either opening prayer (1:15–23; 3:14–21). Its εὐάρεστον τῷ κυρίῳ ("pleasing to the Lord", 5:10) belongs to the light-and-darkness section, not the code. Its code keeps ὡς ἀνθρωπάρεσκοι ("as people-pleasers", 6:6). So Ephesians shows the code word travelling *without* the prayer link. That favours shared idiom over a designed echo of 1:10.

### Design table

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Counts verified | Yes, exactly; "in everything" weakened by πᾶς ("all") density and by the 3:22 omission in 𝔓⁴⁶ |
| Baseline | Prayer-to-ethics recurrence of rare families is normal (9 of 10 opening prayers) |
| Sense | Real: "pleasing the Lord" (1:10) and "pleasing in the Lord" (3:20) cohere; 3:22 is the negative (pleasing people) |
| Alternative explanation | Stock Pauline idiom: walk and please (1 Thess 4:1); pleasing people versus slave of Christ (Gal 1:10); a slave code with εὐάρεστος, "well-pleasing" (Titus 2:9) |

### Verdict
**Needs reframing. Recommended rating: low–moderate.**

The counts are exact, and a reader can hear the code answering the prayer. But "by design" is not shown. Recurrence of this kind is the baseline in Paul, and every link in the chain has a stock parallel elsewhere in Paul (1 Thess 4:1; Gal 1:10; Titus 2:9; 2 Cor 5:9). It should be presented as a thematic resonance within Paul's idiom of pleasing, not as a structural device.

## B4 — Colossians 1:12 κλῆρος ("lot") → 3:24 κληρονομία ("inheritance"): the slaves among the heirs

### What I tested
- κληρ- family lemmas ("lot, inheritance"), searched without accents, in every Pauline letter. A first search that kept the accents missed κλῆρος ("lot") at Col 1:12 and was discarded; the corrected search returns it.
- ἀνταπόδοσις ("repayment, reward") in the NT and Swete.
- The Septuagintal phrase μερὶς … κλῆρος ("share … lot").
- A baseline of opening-to-paraenesis recurrence of the κληρ- family.

### Results
**Counts.** These hold.
- Colossians has exactly two κληρ- ("lot, inheritance") words: κλῆρος ("lot", 1:12) and κληρονομία ("inheritance", 3:24).
- ἀνταπόδοσις ("repayment, reward") is an NT hapax (Col 3:24). The NT's other words of this family are ἀνταπόδομα ("repayment", Luke 14:12; Rom 11:9) and ἀνταποδίδωμι ("repay", seven verses).
- Ephesians has five κληρ- words: κληρόω ("be allotted", 1:11), κληρονομία ("inheritance", 1:14, 18; 5:5) and συγκληρονόμος ("fellow-heir", 3:6). Eph 6:8 has κομίσεται παρὰ κυρίου ("he will receive back from the Lord") and no inheritance word. That holds.

**Baseline.** In the Pauline letters the κληρ- family clusters in argument and ethics:
- Romans: 4:13, 14; 8:17 (×3).
- 1 Corinthians: 6:9, 10; 15:50, the "will not inherit the kingdom" formula.
- Galatians: 3:18, 29; 4:1, 7, 30; 5:21.
- Titus: 3:7.

Only Ephesians and Colossians have it in the opening thanksgiving or blessing. Both carry it into the paraenesis: Eph 5:5 and Col 3:24. The sample is two letters, so it gives no statistical baseline. But it shows that opening-to-ethics recurrence of inheritance language is not peculiar to Colossians. Ephesians, however, does not put it in its code.

**Specificity.** Colossians has only two κληρ- ("lot, inheritance") words, so *any* recurrence of the family would link 1:12 to 3:24. The link is therefore exactly as specific as "1:12 → any inheritance language", and no more. What makes it pointed is the addressee. The thanksgiving says the Father ἱκανώσαντι ὑμᾶς εἰς τὴν μερίδα τοῦ κλήρου τῶν ἁγίων ("qualified you for the share of the lot of the saints", 1:12; ὑμᾶς, "you", in NA28, with ἡμᾶς, "us", as a variant). The code tells δοῦλοι ("slaves") that they will receive τὴν ἀνταπόδοσιν τῆς κληρονομίας ("the reward that is the inheritance", 3:24, where the genitive is taken as appositional [S]). Paul's own slave–heir antithesis appears at Gal 4:1 and 4:7: οὐκέτι εἶ δοῦλος ἀλλὰ υἱός· εἰ δὲ υἱός, καὶ κληρονόμος ("you are no longer a slave but a son; and if a son, also an heir"). That shows the thought is Pauline. Slaves could not inherit at law [S].

**The Septuagintal background of 1:12.** μερίς ("share") and κλῆρος ("lot") are paired in eleven Swete verses:
- Deut 10:9; 12:12; 14:26, 28 (Swete's numbering for English 14:27, 29); 18:1, the Levites who have "no share or lot";
- Josh 18:6; 19:9;
- Wis 2:9;
- Isa 57:6; Jer 13:25;
- the Greek additions to Esther at 4:17.

Wis 5:5 has ἐν ἁγίοις ὁ κλῆρος αὐτοῦ ("his lot is among the saints"). This is the inheritance idiom of the land and of the Levites, who had no land. It gives "share of the lot" its weight. It also suits the landless, slaves included, though that application is mine [S].

### Verdict
**Confirmed with nuance. Recommended rating: moderate (not moderate–high).**

The lexical facts are exact, and the sense is strong. 3:24 extends to slaves, explicitly, the inheritance that 1:12 credited to all believers, and Paul elsewhere makes the slave–heir paradox explicit. But with only two κληρ- ("lot, inheritance") words in the letter, the link cannot be told apart from Paul's ordinary inheritance vocabulary. "Design" is therefore a reading, not a demonstrated structure.

## B5 — The household code as 3:17 applied

### What I tested
- The NT search for a relative pronoun with ἐάν / ἄν ("ever") and a subjunctive of ποιέω ("do") within five words, by lemma and parsing in the SBLGNT, with Col 3:17 and 3:23 as the positive control.
- The NA28 text and apparatus at 3:17 and 3:23.
- The proposed parallels.
- The κύριος ("Lord") counts in Colossians and in Ephesians' code.
- The syntax at Col 3:18 and Eph 5:18–22.

### Results
**The phrase count.** It holds. The search returned ten verses. Matt 5:19; 12:50; Mark 3:35; 10:35; John 5:19; 1 Cor 6:18; Gal 5:17; Eph 6:8 are all third-person "whoever does", or a different construction. Gal 5:17, ἃ ἐὰν θέλητε ταῦτα ποιῆτε ("you do the things you want"), has the relative clause on θέλω ("wish"), not on ποιέω ("do"). Only Col 3:17 and 3:23 have the second-person plural "whatever you do". The control works.

**NA28.** The NA28 text at 3:17 reads καὶ πᾶν ὅ τι ἐὰν ποιῆτε ("and everything, whatever you do"), and its apparatus records no variant on ὅ τι ἐάν ("whatever") there. At 3:23, the NA28 text ὃ ἐάν ("whatever") is supported by 𝔓⁴⁶ א* A B C D* F G and others. The majority text, with D¹ K L 630, reads καὶ πᾶν ὅ τι ("and everything, whatever"); Ψ 1505 read πᾶν ὅ τι; 104, 326 and Clement read καὶ πᾶν ὅ ("and everything which"). So the later tradition assimilated 3:23 to 3:17. Early readers heard the link, and some copyists strengthened it.

**Parallels.**
- 1 Cor 10:31: εἴτε τι ποιεῖτε, πάντα εἰς δόξαν θεοῦ ποιεῖτε ("whatever you do, do all to the glory of God"). This is the closest functional twin of 3:17, and it too follows a food discussion.
- Matt 7:12 has πάντα … ὅσα ἐὰν θέλητε ("all … whatever you wish").
- Luke 10:35 has ὅ τι ἂν προσδαπανήσῃς ("whatever more you spend").
- John 2:5 has ὅ τι ἂν λέγῃ ὑμῖν ποιήσατε ("whatever he tells you, do"). These three are structurally different.
- In Ephesians' slave section: NA28 Eph 6:8, ἕκαστος ἐάν τι ποιήσῃ ἀγαθόν ("each one, whatever good he does"); SBLGNT ὃ ἂν ποιήσῃ ("whatever he does"). This stands at the same point as Col 3:23–25, in the reward clause.

**The κύριος ("Lord") counts.** They hold. κύριος occurs 16 times in Colossians (SBLGNT), 9 of them in 3:18–4:1, spread over 6 of the code's 9 verses: 3:18, 20, 22 (×2), 23, 24 (×2) and 4:1 (×2). Two of the nine are *human* masters: τοῖς κατὰ σάρκα κυρίοις ("your earthly masters", 3:22) and οἱ κύριοι ("masters", 4:1). So the Lord is meant seven times, in six verses. Ephesians' code is similarly dense: κύριος occurs 8 times in 7 of the 20 verses of 5:21–6:9. A density of "Lord" language is therefore a feature of the Christianised code as a form, not only of Colossians' tie to 3:17.

**Asyndeton.** Every address in Col 3:18–4:1 begins without a connective: 3:18, 19, 20, 21, 22 and 4:1. The asyndeton at 3:18 is the list form of the code. It marks a new unit, and of itself neither binds the code to 3:17 nor cuts it off. The binding is lexical:
- ἐν κυρίῳ ("in the Lord", 3:18, 20) picks up ἐν ὀνόματι κυρίου Ἰησοῦ ("in the name of the Lord Jesus", 3:17);
- ὃ ἐὰν ποιῆτε ("whatever you do", 3:23) picks up ὅ τι ἐὰν ποιῆτε ("whatever you do", 3:17);
- ἐργάζεσθε ("work", 3:23) picks up ἔργῳ ("deed", 3:17).

The verbal echo, however, falls in the *slave* section only.

**Ephesians.** Eph 5:18–21 runs πληροῦσθε ἐν πνεύματι ("be filled by the Spirit") and then a chain of participles: λαλοῦντες ("speaking"), ᾄδοντες ("singing"), εὐχαριστοῦντες … ἐν ὀνόματι τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ("giving thanks … in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ"), and ὑποτασσόμενοι ("submitting", 5:21). Eph 5:22 then has no verb of its own and depends on that participle. So Ephesians makes *syntactically* explicit the link Colossians makes by juxtaposition, between the "name of the Lord / thanksgiving" verse (Col 3:17 // Eph 5:20) and the code. That favours the claim. Ephesians is very probably the earliest reader of Colossians we have [S].

**A baseline for local repetition.** Lemma trigrams (sequences of three lemmas) that occur exactly twice in the NT, both in one letter and within ten verses, come at about 4 per 100 verses in Colossians, against 8–18 per 100 in Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians and Ephesians. Self-repetition at close range is ordinary Pauline style. Colossians does it less than most, which slightly raises the weight of the 3:17 / 3:23 repeat.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Counts verified | Phrase: yes, exactly (SBLGNT; NA28 text agrees). κύριος ("Lord"): 9 in 6 of 9 verses, yes, with 2 of the 9 human masters |

### Verdict
**Confirmed with nuance. Recommended rating: moderate–high for "3:23 deliberately picks up 3:17"; moderate for "the whole code is 3:17 applied".**

The phrase is unique in the NT to these two verses. Ephesians binds its code syntactically to the parallel of 3:17, and the later manuscripts assimilated 3:23 to 3:17. But the κύριος ("Lord") density is a feature of the code genre, the asyndeton marks the code's list form, and the verbal echo lies in the slave section only. The words-and-function split proposed (high / moderate–high) should be lowered by one step on function.

## B6 — "Fearing the Lord" (3:22) as the LXX formula directed to Christ

### What I tested
- Swete counts under explicit definitions:
  - a verb form of φοβέομαι ("fear") with κύριον ("the Lord", accusative) within four words, with or without the article;
  - the same anywhere in the verse;
  - the noun φόβος ("fear") with κυρίου ("of the Lord") within four words.
  Every verb hit was read for sense.
- The NT by lemma and case.
- The NA28 apparatus at 3:22.
- The referent of κύριος ("Lord") throughout Colossians.

### Results
**Swete counts.**

| Pattern | All Swete verses | Without the Psalms of Solomon, Odes and 1 Enoch |
|---|---|---|
| Verb φοβέομαι ("fear") + κύριον ("the Lord") within four words | 81 | 75 |
| The same with the article, τὸν κύριον | 44 | 40 |
| Verb + κύριον anywhere in the verse | 91 | 85 |
| Noun φόβος ("fear") + κυρίου ("of the Lord") within four words | 40 | 39 |
| Verb or noun, within four words | 121 | 114 |

Reading the 81 verb hits removes three:
- Dan 1:10 in Theodotion, φοβοῦμαι ἐγὼ τὸν κύριόν μου τὸν βασιλέα ("I fear my lord the king"). This is a human master, a usage worth noting beside τοῖς κατὰ σάρκα κυρίοις ("your earthly masters").
- Ps 39:4, where κύριον ("the Lord") is the object of ἐλπίζω ("hope"), not of fear.
- The doubled Susanna 1:2.

Leaving out the six Psalms of Solomon verses as well gives about 72 genuine verses. **"About 70 Swete verses" holds for the verb-only formula**, and it is a floor, because objects such as αὐτόν ("him") are not counted.

**NT, by lemma.** φοβέομαι ("fear") with κύριον ("the Lord") as accusative object: **Col 3:22 only. That holds**, and the control works, since the search returns it. φοβέομαι with θεόν ("God") as object: Luke 18:2, 4; 23:40; Acts 10:2, 22; 13:16, 26; 1 Pet 2:17; Rev 14:7. Rev 15:4, τίς οὐ μὴ φοβηθῇ, κύριε ("who will not fear, O Lord?"), has κύριε ("O Lord") in the vocative, not as object. φόβος ("fear") with a genitive:
- κυρίου ("of the Lord"): Acts 9:31; 2 Cor 5:11;
- θεοῦ ("of God"): Rom 3:18; 2 Cor 7:1;
- Χριστοῦ ("of Christ"): Eph 5:21.

So Col 3:22 is unique only in its *verbal* construction. The NT generally prefers "fear God". The noun phrase "the fear of the Lord", with Christ as Lord, is already at 2 Cor 5:11, where the judgement seat is τοῦ Χριστοῦ ("of Christ", 5:10).

**The variant.** In NA28 at 3:22, θεόν ("God") is read by 𝔓⁴⁶ א² D² K 104 630, the majority text, d and the Clementine Vulgate. The text κύριον ("Lord") is read by א* A B C D* F G L Ψ 048 075 0278 33 81 365 1175 1241ˢ 1505 1739 1881 2464, the Old Latin, the Stuttgart and Wordsworth–White Vulgate, the Syriac, the Coptic and Clement. The claim's sigla are correct. The external evidence for κύριον is broad and early, though 𝔓⁴⁶ is early on the other side [S]. On internal grounds θεόν is the easier reading. It assimilates to the commoner NT phrase and avoids the clash with κυρίοις ("masters") a few words earlier. On transcriptional grounds, κύριον is the reading that best explains the other. The claim stands on the NA28 text.

**The referent.** κύριος ("Lord") occurs sixteen times in Colossians. Every non-human use is Christ, or is naturally read as Christ:
- 1:3 τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ ("our Lord Jesus Christ");
- 2:6 Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν τὸν κύριον ("Christ Jesus the Lord");
- 3:17 κυρίου Ἰησοῦ ("the Lord Jesus");
- 3:24 τῷ κυρίῳ Χριστῷ ("the Lord Christ").

The remaining uses (1:10; 3:13; 3:18, 20, 23; 4:1, 7, 17) are most naturally Christ in a letter that uses κύριος in no other way [S]. Within 3:22–24, the Lord who is feared, served and repays is named τῷ κυρίῳ Χριστῷ ("the Lord Christ"), and Ephesians renders the idea as ἐν φόβῳ Χριστοῦ ("in the fear of Christ", 5:21). The referent is Christ, with as little ambiguity as the letter allows.

**Septuagintal pairings the claim did not name.**
- 1 Reigns (1 Samuel) 12:24: φοβεῖσθε τὸν κύριον καὶ δουλεύσατε αὐτῷ … ἐν ὅλῃ καρδίᾳ ὑμῶν ("fear the Lord and serve him … with all your heart"). The WLC has יְראוּ אֶת־יְהוָה וַעֲבַדְתֶּם אֹתוֹ … בְּכָל־לְבַבְכֶם ("fear YHWH and serve him … with all your heart"). 1 Reigns 12:14 has the same pair with ἀκούσητε τῆς φωνῆς αὐτοῦ ("listen to his voice"). Col 3:22–24 has the same elements: ὑπακούετε ("obey"), ἐν ἁπλότητι καρδίας ("in singleness of heart"), φοβούμενοι τὸν κύριον ("fearing the Lord"), ἐκ ψυχῆς ("from the soul") and δουλεύετε ("you serve"). Among the fear-the-Lord verses, δουλ- ("serve, slave") also occurs at 3 Reigns 18:12; 4 Reigns 4:1 ("your servant … feared the Lord"); and 4 Reigns 17:41.
- Isa 50:10: ὁ φοβούμενος τὸν κύριον ὑπακουσάτω ("let the one who fears the Lord obey").
- Lev 25:43, on masters and Hebrew slaves: οὐ κατατενεῖς αὐτὸν ἐν τῷ μόχθῳ, καὶ φοβηθήσῃ κύριον τὸν θεόν σου ("you shall not rule him harshly in his toil, and you shall fear the Lord your God"). Rahlfs agrees. The WLC has only וְיָרֵאתָ מֵאֱלֹהֶיךָ ("and you shall fear your God"), so κύριον ("the Lord") here is Greek-only. Compare Col 4:1, which tells masters εἰδότες ὅτι καὶ ὑμεῖς ἔχετε κύριον ("knowing that you too have a Lord").

### Verdict

The counts hold: about 70 verb-only verses in Swete, and Col 3:22 is the only NT verse with the verb and κύριον ("the Lord") as its object. The better-attested text is κύριον, and the referent is Christ by the letter's own usage and by 3:24. The nuance: the transfer of "the fear of the Lord" to Christ is not new at Col 3:22. It is already at 2 Cor 5:10–11, and Eph 5:21 makes it explicit. It is a shared Pauline move, not a Colossian innovation. The fear-and-serve pairing of 1 Reigns 12:24 is the closest formulaic background.

## New candidates

1. **4 Maccabees 5 (for Col 2:16–21).** This is the only Swete chapter outside Wisdom with θρησκεία ("religion"), here used of Jewish observance. It concerns being forced to taste unclean food, with ἀπογεύεσθαι ("to taste"). It is closer in subject than Wisdom 14, but probably too late to be a source [S]. Use it as evidence that θρησκεία was the ordinary word for a Jewish regime of observance (compare Acts 26:5). It should not be proposed as a partner.
2. **Exodus 19:12–13 (for Col 2:21).** μὴ ἅψῃ μηδὲ γεύσῃ μηδὲ θίγῃς ("do not handle, do not taste, do not touch") has a Swete verse with both θιγεῖν ("to touch") and ὁ ἁψάμενος ("the one who touches") at Exod 19:12, in the Sinai prohibition, with οὐχ ἅψεται ("shall not touch") at 19:13. θιγγάνω ("touch") is otherwise absent from Swete, and the scan cannot see it. This is worth a separate test, given Col 2's angel and Sinai language [S]. In the present evidence it is low.
3. **1 Reigns (1 Samuel) 12:24 and 12:14 (for Col 3:22–24).** "Fear the Lord and serve him … with all your heart" (Swete; the WLC agrees). Fear, service and heart come together as in 3:22–24. This is a formulaic background, rated moderate, and stronger than any single source the B6 claim named.
4. **Leviticus 25:43 (for Col 4:1).** A master-and-slave law closing with "and you shall fear the Lord your God". κύριον ("the Lord") is in Swete and Rahlfs; the WLC has only "your God". This is low–moderate as background for the masters' clause.
5. **Psalm 18:8–14 in LXX numbering (Ps 19 in the Hebrew) (for Col 3:22–24).** ὁ φόβος κυρίου ("the fear of the Lord", 18:10), ὁ δοῦλός σου ("your servant", 18:12, 14), ἀνταπόδοσις πολλή ("great reward", 18:12) and καρδία ("heart", 18:9, 15). Rahlfs agrees. It is the only Swete passage where ἀνταποδο- ("reward"), δουλ- ("servant") and φοβ- ("fear") fall within four verses of one another. The WLC has עֵקֶב רָב ("great reward") and יִרְאַת יְהוָה ("the fear of YHWH"). This is a possible, low–moderate echo behind ἀνταπόδοσις ("reward"), Colossians' NT hapax.
6. **Hos 2:11 and Ezek 45:17 (for Col 2:16).** These are better verse-level matches for the calendar triad than Isa 1:13–14, by exact order. Ezek 45:17's future-temple setting deserves testing against σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων ("a shadow of the things to come", 2:17).
7. **Psalm 77:24–27 in LXX numbering (for Col 2:18, 23).** ἄρτον ἀγγέλων ("bread of angels") and εἰς πλησμονήν ("to the full") at 77:25, with σάρκας ("flesh, meat") at 77:27 and βρώματα / βρῶσις ("food, eating") at 77:18, 30. This is a closer sense-match for πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός ("gratification of the flesh") than Isa 1:14, though probably coincidental. Low.
8. **2 Corinthians 5:9–11 (for B3 and B6 together).** εὐάρεστοι ("well-pleasing"), κομίσηται ("may receive back") and τὸν φόβον τοῦ κυρίου ("the fear of the Lord"), before the judgement seat of Christ. It is the Pauline cluster that Col 3:20–25 redeploys. That makes it the best single argument that the code's vocabulary is Pauline idiom rather than an internal echo designed within Colossians.
