## Settled from the Corpus First

**Every one of these was checked before the questions were written, so that the Assistant is asked only what the corpus cannot answer.** They also supply the rarity counts and named rivals the questions need.

**On 2:17 — the phrase Luke substitutes into Joel.** *ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις* ("in the last days") stands in **one line of the whole Swete corpus**: **Isaiah 2:2**. And in the New Testament the **articular plural** stands in **one verse only — Acts 2:17** `[T]`. The other New Testament "last days" expressions are different forms: 2 Timothy 3:1 and James 5:3 have *ἐν ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις* without the article; Hebrews 1:2 and 2 Peter 3:3 have *ἐπ' ἐσχάτου / ἐσχάτων τῶν ἡμερῶν*; John's seven are all the **singular** *τῇ ἐσχάτῃ ἡμέρᾳ*. **So the concept was common Christian idiom and this exact form was not.** That distinction is what question 1.1 turns on, and it is why the question must name the articular plural rather than "the last days".

**On 22:22 — the crowd's cry.** *αἴρω* with *ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* stands in **three lines of Swete** — Isaiah 53:8, and Daniel 7:4 in both the Old Greek and Theodotion (the winged lion "lifted from the earth") — and in **two verses of the New Testament**, Acts 8:33 and 22:22 `[T]`. **Four occurrences in the whole Greek Bible, in three texts.** Daniel 7:4 is the named rival and is implausible in sense, but it must be named. **The phrase is not idiom**, which is the single most important thing question 1.2 needs to carry, because it is the objection a commentator will raise first.

**On 20:28 — the overseers.** *ἐπίσκοπος* stands in **sixteen verses of Swete** (**corrected 16 October 2026 from "fourteen", which was understated: the first search's pattern missed the nominative and dative plurals**): overseers of temple works (4 Reigns 12:11; 2 Chronicles 34:12, 17), temple and palace guard officers (4 Reigns 11:15, 18), civil and military officers (Numbers 31:14; Judges 9:28; Nehemiah 11:9, 14, 22; Esther 2:11; 1 Maccabees 1:51), Eleazar (Numbers 4:16), **God himself** (Job 20:29; Wisdom 1:6), and **Isaiah 60:17**, which reads *καὶ δώσω τοὺς **ἄρχοντάς** σου ἐν εἰρήνῃ, καὶ τοὺς **ἐπισκόπους** σου ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ* `[T]`. **Isaiah 60:17 is the only verse in the Greek Old Testament where God is the subject of "give" and overseers are the object** — the correction to the count does not touch it, because the six added verses are all human appointments or God as overseer himself `[T]`. **And the one structural parallel is 1 Maccabees 1:51, where Antiochus "made overseers over all the people" to enforce apostasy**: in the Greek Old Testament, overseers are appointed over a people twice — as God's promise and as a tyrant's instrument `[I]`. The claim in the overview is therefore verified as a distribution; what is unverified is whether anyone has noticed it.

**On 9:7 against 22:9 — and this one changes the question.** The standard solution is that *ἀκούω* with the **genitive** means "hear the sound of" and with the **accusative** "hear and understand". **Luke's own usage does not support it.** Taking every verse in Luke–Acts where *ἀκούω* governs *φωνή* `[T]`, parsed from the index:

| Case | Verses | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| **Genitive** | **9:7**; **11:7**; **22:7** | 9:7 the companions, who do not see; **11:7 Peter, who understands and answers**; **22:7 Paul himself, who understands and answers** |
| **Accusative** | **9:4**; 22:9; 22:14; 26:14 | 9:4 Paul, who understands; 22:9 the companions, who do not; 22:14 the commission; 26:14 Paul again |

**Corrected 16 October 2026: Acts 2:6 has been struck from the genitive row.** Its *τῆς φωνῆς* is a genitive absolute — *γενομένης δὲ τῆς φωνῆς ταύτης* — and is not governed by *ἀκούω* at all; what *ἤκουον* governs there is *λαλούντων αὐτῶν*. **The governed set is seven verses, not eight**, and the two decisive pairs are untouched `[T]`.

**Two facts kill the distinction as a solution here.** First, **9:4 and 22:7 report the same moment — Paul's own comprehending hearing — one with the accusative and one with the genitive.** Second, **11:7 and 22:7 both use the genitive of hearing that is plainly understood and answered.** So in Luke the genitive does not mean "heard a sound without words", and the two cases are not kept apart. **Question 2.1 therefore does not ask whether the distinction holds; it asks how the grammars and commentators handle Luke's actual usage.** That is a question they may well not have been asked.

**On 23:3 — the whitewashed wall.** Four Greek Old Testament candidates, and the **Hebrew settles something the Greek alone leaves open** `[T]`:

| Candidate | Greek | Hebrew |
|---|---|---|
| **Deuteronomy 27:2, 4** — the stones at the Jordan, plastered, to carry the words of the law | *κονιάσεις αὐτοὺς **κονίᾳ*** — **the only occurrence of *κονιάω* in the LXX Pentateuch** | *וְשַׂדְתָּ אֹתָם **בַּשִּׂיד*** — the root **שׂוד / שִׂיד**, "to coat with lime" |
| **Ezekiel 13:10–15** — the wall the false prophets daub, which God tears down | ***ἀλείφω***, not *κονιάω*; *κατασκάψω τὸν τοῖχον ὃν ἠλείψατε* | ***טוּח*** with ***תָּפֵל***, "to daub with whitewash"; and 13:14 *וְהָרַסְתִּי אֶת־**הַקִּיר*** — God as subject |
| **Daniel 5:5** (Old Greek and Theodotion) | *ἐπὶ τὸ **κονίαμα** τοῦ **τοίχου*** — **the only place in the Greek OT where plaster and wall stand together** | Aramaic |
| **Proverbs 21:9** | *ἐν **κεκονιαμένοις** μετὰ ἀδικίας* — the only other perfect passive participle | — |

**The Hebrew shows the Greek distinction is not a translator's variation: Deuteronomy 27 and Ezekiel 13 use different Hebrew words for different operations**, and the LXX renders them with different Greek words accordingly. **So Acts 23:3's *κεκονιαμένε* belongs to the Deuteronomy 27 word-family and not to Ezekiel 13's** — while Ezekiel 13 supplies the *image* and the agent (God breaking down the wall), which is what 23:3's next clause says. That is the shape question 2.2 must put, and it is a better question than "which one is it?"

**And one thing the corpus has already closed, so do not ask it.** *κονιάω* stands in **two verses of the New Testament** — Matthew 23:27 and Acts 23:3 — and **Luke's Gospel does not contain the word** `[T]`. So the "whitewashed tombs" saying is *not* available to Luke's reader from Luke's Gospel, and no question should be framed as though Acts 23:3 echoed it.

## 1. Allusion History

**These three are the round's reason for existing.** Two of them are claims the overview now carries at moderate–high with **no secondary corroboration whatever**, which is the weakest position any claim in the Acts work occupies.

**1.1 Isaiah 2:2 behind Acts 2:17**

- **Ask:** Which commentators on Acts 2:17 discuss Peter's substitution of "in the last days" (ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις) for the Septuagint's "after these things" (μετὰ ταῦτα) at Joel 3:1 / 2:28, what explanation do they give for the change, and do any of them identify Isaiah 2:2 as the source of the substituted phrase? Please give page references.
- **Why:** This is one of two new Isaianic entries in the overview's intertextual map, held at moderate–high. The corpus has established that the phrase stands once in the whole Greek Old Testament, at Isaiah 2:2, whose oracle is the nations coming to Jerusalem and the word going out from it — which would make the substitution a deliberate signal at the head of the Gentile mission. **What is missing is whether anyone has ever said so.**
- **Watch for:** (a) an answer that explains the *change* without naming a *source*, which is the commonest treatment and is not an answer to the question — if that happens, ask the follow-up below; (b) a claim that the phrase is "standard eschatological language", which the corpus contradicts for this exact articular form and supports for the concept, so insist on the form; (c) **the inflating words** — "widely recognised" standing on one footnote.
- **Follow-up if the source goes unnamed:** You mentioned [author]. Does he say anything about where the phrase "in the last days" comes from, given that in the Septuagint it occurs only at Isaiah 2:2?
- **Then ask the same question of:** Litwak (*Echoes of Scripture in Luke-Acts*) and Parsons (Paideia) by name — Litwak especially, since a monograph on Luke's use of Scripture is where this would be treated if anywhere.

**1.2 Isaiah 53:8 behind Acts 22:22**

- **Ask:** Which commentators discuss a link between the crowd's cry in Acts 22:22, "Away with such a fellow from the earth" (Αἶρε ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς τὸν τοιοῦτον), and the quotation of Isaiah 53:8 at Acts 8:33, "his life is taken away from the earth" (αἴρεται ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς ἡ ζωὴ αὐτοῦ)? What wording do they point to, and does anyone argue it is shared idiom instead?
- **Why:** The other new Isaianic entry, and the strongest single finding of the 21:37–23:11 run. The phrase stands in two verses of the New Testament, both in Acts, and in two texts of the Greek Old Testament; and the 8:33 occurrence is a quotation Luke has expounded, so the words are already loaded in his own book. **If this is noticed in the literature it rises to high; if it is argued against as idiom, the overview row should be reframed.**
- **Watch for:** (a) the idiom objection, which is the right objection and should be welcomed — ask for the evidence given for it, not just the assertion; (b) **an answer that slides to the general "Paul is portrayed like Jesus" theme** instead of this phrase, which is a different and much weaker claim; (c) whether Daniel 7:4 is raised as the rival, in which case the answer is reading the Greek carefully and can be trusted further.
- **Then ask:** Does any commentator on Acts 22:22 observe that the same Greek phrase appears in the quotation of Isaiah 53 at Acts 8:33?

**1.3 Isaiah 60:17 and 1 Clement 42:5 behind Acts 20:28**

- **Ask:** Which commentators on Acts 20:28 discuss the background of the term "overseers" (ἐπισκόπους), and do any of them cite Isaiah 60:17 — where the Septuagint has God say "I will give your rulers in peace and your overseers in righteousness" — or 1 Clement 42:5, which quotes that verse in connection with the appointment of overseers?
- **Why:** The overview holds this row at moderate and has done since v2.2, with "pending Logos" written against it. The corpus has now verified the distribution: of **sixteen** occurrences of *ἐπίσκοπος* in the Greek Old Testament (corrected from fourteen — see above), **Isaiah 60:17 is the only one where God gives them**. With Isaiah 43:21 and 63:9–11 already behind the same verse, a fourth Isaianic text behind 20:28 would make the verse the densest Isaianic site in Acts.
- **Watch for:** (a) **whether 1 Clement is actually being quoted or supplied from general knowledge** — if the citation has no footnote, treat it as the Assistant's own recall and settle it by opening 1 Clement directly, which the library now allows in two editions (see 3.1 for the caveat); (b) an answer about the *office* of overseer in the pastoral epistles, which is a different question; (c) a date assumption — "1 Clement is later, so it depends on Acts" — doing the work in place of evidence.

## 2. Lexicon, Grammar and Text

**2.1 *ἀκούω* with the genitive and the accusative at Acts 9:7 and 22:9**

- **Ask:** How do the standard grammars (Wallace, BDF, Robertson) classify the difference between "hearing the voice" with the genitive (φωνῆς, Acts 9:7) and with the accusative (φωνήν, Acts 22:9), and how do they handle the fact that Luke uses the genitive at Acts 22:7 and 11:7 of hearing that is plainly understood and answered, and the accusative at 9:4 of the same moment he reports with the genitive at 22:7?
- **Why:** The genitive/accusative distinction is the standard harmonisation of the apparent contradiction between 9:7 and 22:9, and **the corpus shows Luke does not observe it** (see Settled from the Corpus First). Both the 21:37–23:11 dig and any sermon on Acts 22 need to know whether the grammars have faced this or whether the solution is repeated without being tested against Luke's usage. **The question is deliberately framed so that it cannot be answered by restating the rule.**
- **Watch for:** (a) a bare restatement of the rule with no engagement — that is itself the answer, and worth recording as such; (b) an answer that treats the question as being about the *contradiction* rather than about the *grammar*, which is the easier question; (c) reasoning from the English, since both the NASB95 and the ESV render 22:9's *οὐκ ἤκουσαν* as "did not understand" and have therefore already decided the point in translation.
- **Do this one in the Exegetical Guide as well**, at Acts 22:9 — it is free, and its Word By Word section will give the morphology and the lexicon entries (BDAG on *ἀκούω* with the two cases) without spending credits.

**2.2 The whitewashed wall at Acts 23:3**

- **Ask:** Which commentators on Acts 23:3 discuss the background of "whitewashed wall" (τοῖχε κεκονιαμένε), and do any of them cite Ezekiel 13:10–15 (the daubed wall God tears down), Deuteronomy 27:2–4 (the stones plastered to carry the law), or Daniel 5:5 (the writing on the plaster of the wall)? Does any of them treat it simply as an insult rather than a scriptural image?
- **Why:** The 21:37–23:11 run assembled four candidates and declined to choose, and the overview declined to carry a row until this is checked. The corpus has narrowed the question usefully: **the Greek word-family at Acts 23:3 is Deuteronomy 27's, not Ezekiel 13's — and the Hebrew confirms the two were always different words** — while **Ezekiel 13 supplies the image and the agent**, God breaking the wall down, which is what Paul's next clause says. So the live question is whether the verse carries a *scriptural* image at all, and if so whether word or image governs.
- **Watch for:** (a) an answer built on Matthew 23:27's "whitewashed tombs" — **the corpus has established that Luke's Gospel does not contain the word**, so a Matthean echo is not available to Luke's reader and an answer resting on it is reasoning from the English Bible as a whole rather than from Luke–Acts; (b) whether anyone notices that *κονιάω* in the Pentateuch occurs only at Deuteronomy 27.

**2.3 *ἀκρίβεια* at Acts 22:3 and the preface**

- **Ask:** Which commentators on Acts 22:3 discuss Paul's phrase "according to the strict manner of the law of our fathers" (κατὰ ἀκρίβειαν τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου), and does any of them connect the noun ἀκρίβεια with Luke's own ἀκριβῶς at Luke 1:3?
- **Why:** A smaller question, and a cheap one to add to this group. The 21:37–23:11 run found that *ἀκρίβεια* is a New Testament hapax at 22:3; that *ἀκριβῶς* stands in Luke–Acts only at Luke 1:3 and Acts 18:25; and that the comparative *ἀκριβέστερον* is four times in the New Testament, **all in Acts**, twice of the enquiry the conspirators are pretending to want (23:15, 20). **That makes the preface's own method-word part of a pattern.** The dig holds the irony at moderate and would like to know whether it is noticed.
- **Watch for:** an answer about Pharisaic education generally, which is well covered and is not the question.

## 3. Textual Points in Prose *(the lookups are free; one Assistant question each)*

**3.1 Does the library hold Metzger, 1 Clement, and Alexander?** *(**Answered, 15 October 2026.** Recorded rather than removed, because what it found reshaped three questions.)*

- **Not a question — a search**, and it cost nothing, which was the argument for putting it first.
- **The answer.** Metzger's *Textual Commentary*: **not owned**. 1 Clement: **owned twice** — Glimm's translation in *The Apostolic Fathers* (Fathers of the Church, 1947) and the complete text in Menzies' *Ante-Nicene Fathers* volume (1897). Alexander's *Preface to Luke's Gospel*: **not owned**; the library has only her chapter on ancient book production in Bauckham's *The Gospels for All Christians* (1998).
- **What follows.** **3.2 and 3.3 are reframed below**: the apparatus and *The Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible* do the lookup, and the Assistant is asked a different question — not what Metzger concluded, but who argues the case and on what grounds. **1.3 can now be settled by reading 1 Clement 42:5** instead of asking about it. **4.1 loses its Alexander clause**, and the Luke 1:4 question that was optional there becomes that group's one Assistant question.
- **The caveat on 1 Clement, and it matters.** Both editions are **English translations**, and the Isaiah 60:17 argument turns on **Clement's Greek** — on whether his quotation keeps the Septuagint's *ἐπισκόπους*, and on what he does with Isaiah's "rulers in peace". A translation can establish that Clement quotes the verse *in connection with appointing overseers*, which is the load-bearing point and is what 1.3 needs. It cannot establish that his wording is Isaiah's wording. **Read 42:5 for the first; do not claim the second from it.** The `_texts/` corpus holds no Apostolic Fathers text, so the Greek needs a separate Logos resource or the point stays open.

**3.2 Acts 20:28 — who argues the variant, and what the Lexham notes say**

- **First, free:** open **The Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible** at Acts 20:28 and read both entries — *τοῦ θεοῦ* against *τοῦ κυρίου*, and *τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου*. Expect brief notes, not argument: Brannan and Loken record a decision and its basis compactly, which is a different thing from the UBS committee's reasoning.
- **Then ask:** Which commentators on Acts 20:28 argue the text-critical question at length — both the choice between "the church of God" (τοῦ θεοῦ) and "the church of the Lord" (τοῦ κυρίου), and the word order "with the blood of his own" (τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου) — and what reasons do they give for preferring one reading over the other?
- **Why:** The one part of that crux the apparatus could not settle, open since 9 September. The apparatus itself is already in hand and has established two things the run needed: **𝔓⁷⁴ A C\* Ψ 33 D E all read *τοῦ κυρίου***, so NA28's decision for *θεοῦ* must be internal rather than external — which corrects the sweep's claim of "strong Alexandrian support"; and the marked second-attributive word order is read by every major uncial while **the Byzantine tradition normalises it** toward the Hebrews idiom. **What is wanted is reasoning, not a verdict** — and with Metzger absent, the reasoning has to come from whoever rehearses it in the commentaries. Bruce, Barrett, Witherington, Keener and Peterson all treat this crux; which of them the library holds, and how far each goes, is part of what the question returns.
- **Watch for:** a bare verdict — a letter-grade, or "the harder reading" with nothing behind it. Ask for the argument. And watch for a slide into **the theology of "God's own blood"**, which is a separate question the run has already worked; what is open here is the text.

**3.3 Acts 11:20 — the one place NA28 runs against the narrative**

- **First, free:** **The Lexham Textual Notes** at Acts 11:20, and the Exegetical Guide's Textual Variants section at the same verse.
- **Then ask:** Which commentators on Acts 11:20 argue for "Greeks" (Ἕλληνας) against the printed "Hellenists" (Ἑλληνιστάς), and what weight do they give to the contrast with verse 19, where the scattered spoke to Jews only?
- **Why:** The place where NA28's printed text is hardest to square with the narrative logic. NA28 prints *Ἑλληνιστάς*; *Ἕλληνας* is read by **𝔓⁷⁴, א², A and D\*** — and 11:19 says the scattered spoke to Jews *only*, with v.20 drawn as a contrast, which reads naturally only if the new hearers are not Jews.

  **Corrected 16 October 2026, and the correction matters.** This question was first written as "the one place in the whole Acts work where NA28's printed text runs against the narrative logic", and said that the internal and the early external evidence agree against the edition. **Re-reading the apparatus shows that is not so.** *Ἑλληνιστάς* is headed by **B**, with D's corrector and the whole majority tradition; א reads *Ἕλληνας* only **by correction** (א\* has the slip *εὐαγγελιστάς*), and D reads it in its **first hand** only. So the early external evidence is **split between B and 𝔓⁷⁴ A**, not united against the printed text. Worse for the preferred reading: **A reads *Ἕλληνας* at 9:29 as well** (⸀ Ελληνας A 424), where almost nothing follows it — so A's reading at 11:20 looks like **A's own habit of normalising *Ἑλληνιστάς***, not an independent witness. The internal argument stands on its own; the external claim was overstated and is withdrawn. The NASB95 has "Greeks", the ESV "Hellenists". **This matters beyond the verse**, because 11:20 is where Acts' Gentile mission either does or does not begin at Antioch. The question is now framed as *who argues it*, because the attestation is already in hand: what is missing is not an edition's verdict but a case made in prose.
- **Watch for:** a date or theology assumption standing in for manuscript reasoning. And note that this is a case where **the internal and the early external evidence agree against the edition**, which is unusual enough that a good discussion will say so — **if nobody in the library says so, that silence is itself the finding** and should be recorded.

## 4. The State of a Debate

**4.1 The *κατηχέω* and *ἀσφάλεια* thread — a cross-reference search, not an Assistant question**

- **Not a question — a search**, and this is the correction the 11 October round produced. In Logos, run a **cross-reference or citation search** for commentaries on **Acts 21:34, 22:30 and 25:26** that cite **Luke 1:4**; and separately for treatments of Luke 1:4's *ἀσφάλεια* that mention any of those three verses. **Alexander's *Preface to Luke's Gospel* is not owned** (3.1), so the one monograph on the preface is out of reach; the Assistant question below takes its place.
- **Why:** The Assistant was asked this on 11 October and returned **lexical grouping only** — reference-work entries, plus one commentary that groups 21:34 and 22:30 with 2:36 while assigning them *different senses*, which is a commentator seeing the words and not reading them as one designed irony. **The status is therefore "no treatment located", not "no treatment exists", and a second Study Assistant question would return the same thing.** A citation search answers a different question: who, writing on the later verses, reaches back to the preface.
- **Watch for:** nothing — a search returns what it returns. **A nil result is a real finding here** and should be recorded as one, because the claim is held at moderate–high on its distribution alone and that is where it will stay.
- **Then ask — and this is now the group's one Assistant question, not an optional extra:** Which commentators on Luke 1:4 discuss what ἀσφάλεια means for the purpose of Luke–Acts, and does any of them trace the word or its cognates into Acts? **Widen the scope to Luke commentaries for this one.** It is doing the work Alexander's *Preface* would have done, through the commentary tradition rather than a monograph, so expect the answer to be thinner and more scattered — ask for the fullest single treatment rather than a survey, and note which commentators do not raise the question at all.

**4.2 Acts 23:6 — substance or tactics?**

- **Ask:** What are the main explanations of Paul's declaration "I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees; I am on trial for the hope and resurrection of the dead" at Acts 23:6, who holds each, and what evidence do they give? In particular, do any commentators treat it as a tactical move to divide the council rather than as a statement of the real issue?
- **Why:** The most contested act in the 21:37–23:11 unit, and the sweep flagged it as a preaching trap. **The run added a datum the debate may not have:** *ἀνάστασις* is the grievance on which the Sanhedrin first moved against the church at 4:2, so Paul is naming the original charge, not inventing a wedge — and he says the same thing four more times with no Sadducee present (24:15, 21; 26:6–8; 28:20). **The question is what weight the literature gives to each side.**
- **Watch for:** an answer that resolves it by Paul's character rather than by the text; and whether anyone cites 4:2.

**4.3 Acts 21:4 — the Spirit's prohibition**

- **Ask:** What are the main explanations of Acts 21:4, where the disciples at Tyre tell Paul "through the Spirit" not to go to Jerusalem, who holds each, and what evidence do they give for whether Paul was disobeying a divine prohibition?
- **Why:** The first of the 21:1–36 run's four contested verses, and the one most likely to reach a pulpit as a doctrine of guidance. The run argued that the Greek attaches *διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* to the telling, that 20:23 shows the Spirit's consistent content is *that bonds await* rather than *therefore do not go*, and that Luke declines to adjudicate — but recorded that **the disobedience reading has real defenders** and named none of them. **This question is to find out who they are and what they argue.**
- **Watch for:** the inflating words. This is a question where "most scholars agree" is likely to stand on very little.

## 5. Background

**5.1 Claudius Lysias's purchased citizenship**

- **Ask:** What is the evidence for the purchase of Roman citizenship under Claudius, as the tribune claims at Acts 22:28, "I acquired this citizenship with a large sum of money", and how do commentators on Acts assess the historical plausibility of Paul's counter-claim to citizenship by birth in Tarsus?
- **Why:** The 21:37–23:11 run flagged the Claudian detail as *moderate, consider verifying*, resting on Dio as a commonplace. It bears on the unit's second scene and on how much weight the three-citizenships finding can take.
- **Watch for:** a study-Bible-tier answer on a question that wants a historian; name a tier if the first answer is thin.
