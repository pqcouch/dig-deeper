## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged at whole-section scope:

1. **Three silences nest inside one another at the centre of Solomon II, and chapters 26–27 sit inside all three.** `[T]` *High — every figure countable.* Reading the section straight through, three vocabularies drop out and return, and their spans are **concentric**:

   | What falls silent | From → to | Verses |
   |---|---|---|
   | **the king / ruler** | 25:7 → 28:14 | **91** |
   | **the divine name** | 25:23 → 28:4 | **65** |
   | **righteous / wicked** | 25:27 → 27:27 | **57** |

   Chapters 26–27 — **fifty-five consecutive verses** — contain **no YHWH, no *ʾĕlōhîm*, no *ṣaddîq*, no *rāšāʿ*, no *melek*, no *mōšēl*, no *šōpēṭ* and no *mišpāṭ*.** Every authority that had been settling cases for the reader withdraws together, and returns in reverse order. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Vocabulary, Positional Necessity, Author's Purpose.
   **This is the 2A run's fourth headline — "the section withholds the divine verdict and the two-column sort" — made exact, and extended.** 2A could see two of the three silences and could not see where any of them ended, because all three end after 27:27. **The third silence, the king's, is new: it is the longest, and it is the outermost.**
   And **26:4–5 sits at the dead centre of the nest.** The section that removes God, the moral categories and the magistrate from the page is the section that says: answer a fool according to his folly, and do not answer a fool according to his folly — *you* judge which.

2. **⚠ Correction. The longest divine-name-free stretch in the sentence literature is Proverbs 25:23–28:4 — sixty-five verses — not chapter 13.** `[T]` *High.* Three project documents state that "chapter 13 has no divine name in twenty-five verses — the longest God-free stretch in the sentence literature": `dig-deeper-proverbs-10-1to15-33` (Headline 2), `proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis`, and `point-purpose-proverbs-series-introduction`. **The claim about chapter 13 itself is true; the superlative is false twice over.**
   - The true longest in the sentence literature is **25:23 → 28:4, sixty-five verses** — two and a half times chapter 13.
   - **Chapter 13 is not even the longest stretch in Solomon I.** The last divine name before it is 12:22 and the next is 14:2, so the actual span is **12:23 → 14:1, thirty-two verses.**

   The error was possible because no run had ever counted across a section boundary. It is corrected in all three documents in this issue.
   **And the correction improves the underlying observation rather than damaging it.** The Solomon I trough is wider than one chapter and the *petuchah* at 13:25 falls *inside* it; and the Solomon II silence **ends exactly at a transmitted paragraph marker** — see Headline 3.

3. **The sixty-five-verse silence ends on the paragraph break, and the two things that break it arrive one verse apart.** `[T]` *High.* The last verse of the God-free paragraph is **28:4** — which contains *tôrâ* **twice** ("those who forsake the law … those who keep the law"), the first occurrence of *tôrâ* anywhere in Solomon II. A *petuchah* follows it. **The very next verse, 28:5, names the LORD**: "those who seek the LORD understand all." Law returns in the last verse before the break; God returns in the first verse after it. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Vocabulary, Positional Necessity.
   *tôrâ* occurs in only four verses in the whole section — **28:4 (×2), 28:7, 28:9, 29:18** — all of them after the marker, none of them in 25–27. **The section that is silent about God is silent about Torah, and the two end their silence together at the same seam.**
   This is the whole-section form of the 2B run's second headline (the *tôrāh* envelope 28:4 ∥ 29:18). 2B could see the envelope; it could not see that its opening verse is also the terminus of a sixty-five-verse silence that began three chapters earlier.

4. **Solomon II is paragraphed five times more densely than Solomon I — and three of its eight paragraphs are exactly twenty-nine verses long.** `[T]` *High on the counts; the twenty-nines are recorded as observation, not design.* The received Hebrew carries **eight markers in 138 verses, all *petuchot*** — one per 17.3 verses. Solomon I carries **four in 375** — one per 93.8. **The transmission divided this collection 5.4 times more finely than the one it stands beside.**

   | # | Paragraph | Verses |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | 25:1–13 | 13 |
   | 2 | 25:14–20 | 7 |
   | 3 | 25:21–26:21 | **29** |
   | 4 | 26:22–27:22 | **29** |
   | 5 | **27:23–28:4** | 9 |
   | 6 | 28:5–16 | 12 |
   | 7 | 28:17–29:17 | **29** |
   | 8 | 29:18–27 | 10 |

   Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Genre. **This reconciles the two half-runs exactly** — 2A found 25:13, 25:20, 26:21, 27:22; 2B found 28:4, 28:16, 29:17, 29:27; there are eight and no others, and BHS carries no *setumot* here.
   **Five of the eight paragraphs cross a chapter division** — the chapter numbering and the transmitted paragraphing agree only at 25:13, 25:20 and 29:27.

5. **The *kābēd* thread does not end at 27:27. It lands at 29:23 — and 29:23 answers 25:27.** `[T]` on the occurrences; `[I]` *moderate–high* on the reading. The 2A run identified *kābēd* as the Leitwort of 25–27 and said it "runs from the first saying to the last." At whole-section scope it runs **past** the last:

   > 25:2 the glory of God, and the glory of kings · 25:27 **"to seek one's own glory is not glory"** · 26:1, 26:8 honour is unfitting for a fool · 27:3 the fool's provocation is heavier than stone · 27:18 the servant who guards his master **will be honoured** · **29:23 "one's pride will bring him low, but he who is lowly in spirit will obtain honour."**

   Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Structure, Move 4, Author's Purpose. **The Leitwort poses its problem in chapter 25 and receives its answer in the penultimate paragraph of the collection.** 25:27 says what honour is not to be sought by; 29:23 says what it is obtained by. Neither half-run could see the pair, because the two verses fall on opposite sides of the cut.

6. **Two opposite gradients cross at the 27:27/28:1 seam — and that seam is the one place the transmission refuses to break.** `[T]` on the counts; the proxies are stated below. Counted by verses:

   | | 25 | 26 | 27 | 28 | 29 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | **comparison** (*like/as*) | 39% | 46% | 4% | 7% | 0% |
   | **contrast** (*but*) | 4% | 0% | 15% | **68%** | 52% |
   | **second person** (*you/your*) | 32% | 7% | 22% | **0%** | 7% |

   Comparison is front-loaded, contrast back-loaded, and **the two curves cross inside chapter 27, which is the trough of both** — the least comparative and the second-least antithetical chapter. What chapter 27 has instead is address: it speaks to *you*. And **chapter 28 contains no second-person verse at all**, while carrying the highest contrast rate in the section.
   **Yet the Masoretic paragraph runs 27:23 → 28:4, straight through the join.** Surfaced by: Genre, Parallels, Structure, Translations. **Where the vocabulary changes most sharply, the transmission insists there is no break** — which is the strongest available argument that 25–29 is one collection and not two.
   *Method note:* the ESV "but" and "like/as" counts are translation proxies, deliberately coarse, and they run in the same direction as the Hebrew work done cluster-by-cluster in 2A and 2B (which counted six antithetical sayings in 25–27 against thirty-three in 28–29). They are offered as gradient, not as absolute rate — 2A's third headline established that most Hebrew comparisons here carry **no** particle and that the ESV's "like" is supplied.

7. **The Septuagint of Solomon II carries six verse-units with no Hebrew counterpart — and one of them is a verse of Ben Sira.** `[T]` on the Greek; `[S]` on the Sirach identification. **25:10a, 25:20a, 26:11a, 27:20a, 27:21a, 28:17a**, and **no Hebrew verse in the section lacks a Greek counterpart** — the Greek is a superset here. Rate: one plus per 23 verses, against Solomon I's nineteen in 375 (one per 19.7) — **comparable, not distinctive**. See Textual Variants for the full text and the Sirach parallel.

## The Passage

Proverbs 25:1–29:27 — 138 verses, headed **"These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied"** (25:1). It is the only collection in the book with a datable editorial act attached to it: a named king, a named century, and a verb of transmission.

**The census, by chapter:**

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Two figures deserve to be set beside Solomon I at once.** Solomon I names God in **55 of 375 verses** — one verse in 6.8. Solomon II names him in **7 of 138** — one verse in 19.7. **The Hezekiah collection names God less than a third as often as the collection it follows.** And *kəsîl*, the fool, is not spread across the section: **eleven of its fourteen occurrences fall in chapter 26**, which is why that chapter reads as it does.

## The Sixteen Tools

Worked here **only where 138-verse scope changes the answer.** For everything else the two half-runs stand.

**1 · Context.** The collection's frame is royal at both ends and empty of kings in the middle. Kings occupy 25:1–6 — the superscription, the glory of kings, the unsearchable heart of kings, the wicked removed from the king's presence, the warning not to push yourself forward before him — and then vanish for **ninety-one verses**, returning at 28:15 with a wicked ruler like a roaring lion and running through 29:2, 4, 12, 14, 26. `[T]` **The collection made by royal scribes opens at court, walks away from court for three chapters, and comes back to it to say that justice comes from the LORD and not from the ruler's face** (29:26).

**2 · Genre.** Solomon II is not one genre but a sequence of them, and the sequence is the argument. Chapter 25 is **courtly and second-person** — how to behave near power. Chapter 26 is **taxonomic and third-person** — the fool, the sluggard, the whisperer, catalogued. Chapter 27 is **relational and second-person again** — friend, neighbour, flock. Chapters 28–29 are **antithetical and forensic**, the two-column sort resumed. `[I]` *High confidence on the description.* The 2A run named 25–27 "instruction by comparison" and 2B named 28–29 "the antithetical resumption"; the whole shows that **27:23–27, the shepherd poem, is the pivot** — the last extended second-person passage before the section turns forensic, and the passage the transmission binds to 28:1–4.

**3 · Structure.** Eight paragraphs (Headline 4). The three twenty-nine-verse paragraphs are 25:21–26:21, 26:22–27:22 and 28:17–29:17. `[T]` The regularity is recorded because it is countable and unexpected; **no compositional claim is made from it** — with eight paragraphs averaging 17 verses, three coincident lengths is striking but not beyond chance, and no source known to this project notices it. Flagged to Open Questions.

**4 · Repetition.** Four threads run the whole length and none of them was visible to a half-run:

| Thread | Course across 138 verses |
|---|---|
| *kābēd* | 25:2 → 25:27 → 26:1, 8 → 27:3, 18 → **29:23** (Headline 5) |
| *bāṭaḥ* | **25:19** trust in a treacherous man → 28:1 the righteous are confident → 28:25 in the LORD → 28:26 in your own heart → 29:25 in the LORD |
| *tôrâ* | silent through 25–27 → **28:4** ×2 → 28:7 → 28:9 → 29:18 |
| "in his own eyes" | 26:5, 26:12, 26:16 → **28:11** |

**The *bāṭaḥ* chain is the correction the whole-section view supplies to 2B.** 2B's fifth headline began the chain at 28:1 ("bold as a lion" is *yibṭāḥ*) and traced it forward. But the root's **first** occurrence in Solomon II is **25:19** — *"trusting in a treacherous man in time of trouble is like a bad tooth or a foot that slips."* `[T]` **The section opens with an image of misplaced trust and only afterwards tells you where trust belongs.** The negative case is stated a hundred verses before the positive one.

**5 · Vocabulary.** See the census. The one word worth adding at this scope is *tôʿēbâ*: three occurrences, **26:25, 28:9, 29:27**. The middle one is also a *tôrâ* verse — *"if one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination"* — the only place in Solomon II where the two vocabularies meet. And the last of them is the final verse of the collection — where, uniquely in Proverbs, the abomination runs **both ways**: "an unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, but one whose way is straight is an abomination to the wicked." `[T]` Solomon I's *tôʿēbâ* sayings are almost all "abomination **to the LORD**". **Solomon II ends by handing the word to human parties on both sides** — which is of a piece with a collection that has spent three chapters withdrawing the divine verdict.

**6 · Parallels and 7 · Linking words.** See the gradient table in Headline 6, and 2A/2B for the saying-level classifications.

**8 · Author's purpose.** At this scope the collection has a discernible shape of intent: **teach a man to read situations when no authority is present, then tell him who the authority is.** Chapters 25–27 remove God, the categories and the king, and hand the reader judgement (26:4–5), discernment of friends (27:6, 14), and the management of what he can actually see (27:23, "know well the condition of your flocks"). Chapters 28–29 restore all three and say where they finally reside: Torah (28:4, 7, 9; 29:18), the LORD (28:5, 25; 29:25, 26), and justice in the king (29:4, 14). `[I]` *Moderate–high.*

**9–16 · Translations, Historical setting, Quotation/Allusion, Christ, Application, So What, Positional Necessity, Difficult verses.** Worked in the two half-runs; extended below only where scope changes them.

**Positional Necessity, at section scope.** Would the section work if 28–29 preceded 25–27? `[I]` **No** — and the reason is Headline 3. The sixty-five-verse silence is only intelligible as a silence if the reader has been given God and the categories first (in Solomon I, and in 25:2, 5, 22, 26) and then has them taken away. Reversed, 26:4–5 would be a puzzle handed to a reader who had never been told the answer exists. **The order is: verdict given · verdict withheld · verdict restored** — and the restoration is not a repetition, because it now names Torah, which the first movement never did.

## Extensions

**Original languages.** The three concentric silences (Headline 1) are a fact about Hebrew lexemes and survive translation intact — any English version shows them. Two things do not survive: the comparative particle question (2A, Headline 3 — the ESV supplies "like" where the Hebrew juxtaposes), and *yibṭāḥ* at 28:1, which the ESV renders "are bold as a lion" and which is the same root as the "trusts" of 28:25, 28:26 and 29:25. **A reader in English cannot see that 28:1 begins a chain.** Category-1 translation loss under the Three-Way Triage.

**Textual variants.** Six Greek pluses, none with a Hebrew counterpart, and no Hebrew verse without a Greek one.

| Ref | Greek | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **25:10a** | χάρις καὶ φιλία ἐλευθεροῖ … | "Grace and friendship set free; keep them for yourself, that you may not be reproached." |
| **25:20a** | ὥσπερ σὴς ἱματίῳ καὶ σκώληξ ξύλῳ … | "As a moth in a garment and a worm in wood, so a man's grief harms the heart." |
| **26:11a** | ἔστιν αἰσχύνη ἐπάγουσα ἁμαρτίαν … | "There is a shame that brings sin, and there is a shame that is glory and grace." |
| **27:20a** | βδέλυγμα κυρίῳ στηρίζων ὀφθαλμόν … | "An abomination to the Lord is he who fixes the eye; the undisciplined are unrestrained in tongue." |
| **27:21a** | καρδία ἀνόμου ἐκζητεῖ κακά … | "The heart of the lawless seeks evil; the upright heart seeks knowledge." |
| **28:17a** | παίδευε υἱόν, καὶ ἀγαπήσει σε … | "Discipline your son and he will love you, and give order to your soul." |

Three of these matter.
- **26:11a is Sirach 4:21**, essentially word for word (ἔστιν γὰρ αἰσχύνη ἐπάγουσα ἁμαρτίαν, καὶ ἔστιν αἰσχύνη δόξα καὶ χάρις). `[T]` on the Greek text of both; `[S]` on the direction of dependence, which this project does not attempt to settle. **A verse stands in the Greek Proverbs and in Ben Sira, and in the Hebrew of neither.** Its placement is apt: it follows the dog returning to its vomit, and distinguishes the shame that leads to sin from the shame that leads to honour.
- **27:20a supplies an "abomination to the Lord" saying that the Hebrew section does not have** — and it lands in chapter 27, inside the sixty-five-verse silence. `[I]` The Greek tradition, whatever its *Vorlage*, does not preserve the silence; it names the Lord where the Hebrew does not. Worth knowing before appealing to the silence in a sermon: it is a feature of the Hebrew text, not of every ancient text of Proverbs.
- **28:17a is a doublet of 29:17** ("discipline your son, and he will give you rest"), relocated. This is the same phenomenon the Solomon I run found nineteen times: the Greek tradition redistributes material rather than only adding it.

**Historical background.** 25:1 is the book's one dated editorial note: Hezekiah's men, therefore late eighth century, therefore roughly **two hundred and fifty years after Solomon**. `[T]` on the claim of the verse; `[S]` on the chronology. The verb is *heʿtîqû*, "removed / transcribed / transferred" — an act of copying out, not of composing. **The collection presents itself as an anthology of older material made by a later court**, which is exactly the situation in which one would expect a section on how to behave near a king (25:1–6) and a section on how to judge for yourself when the king is not looking (26–27).

**Biblical theology.** The nested-silence structure has a canonical analogue worth naming carefully. `[I]` *Moderate.* The pattern — authority given, authority withdrawn so that the people must act on what they have been taught, authority restored with the law named explicitly — is the shape of Deuteronomy's provision for life in the land after Moses (Deut 17:18–20, the king who copies the law; Deut 31, the law deposited against Moses' absence). The link is structural, not verbal, and is offered as an analogy, not an allusion. **The verbal Torah links are the ones 2B established** — Exod 22:25 / Lev 25:36 at 28:8; Gen 9:6 at 28:17; Lev 5:1 at 29:24; Exod 32:25 at 29:18.

**Difficult verses.** 26:4–5 remains the section's crux and is now placed: it stands at the centre of the nest, in the paragraph 25:21–26:21, at the point of maximum authorial withdrawal. `[I]` **The contradiction is the pedagogy.** The text does not resolve it because resolving it would supply the judgement the section is training the reader to make.

**Christological reading.** Two lines, both disciplined. `[I]`/`[S]`
- **25:21–22**, established in 2A as the Covenant Code's enemy-duty, is where the New Testament goes for love of enemies (Rom 12:20; behind Matt 5:44). It sits four verses inside the God-free stretch.
- **29:23 — "one's pride will bring him low, but he who is lowly in spirit will obtain honour" — is the *kābēd* thread's answer**, and it is the sentence Jesus repeats almost verbatim (Luke 14:11; 18:14) and Paul narrates (Phil 2:8–9: he humbled himself, **therefore God highly exalted him**). `[S]` on the NT links, `[T]` on the Hebrew. **The section's Leitwort asks in 25:27 how honour may be had without being sought, and the New Testament answers by pointing at a man who did not seek it and received it.** This is the strongest Christological line in Solomon II and it is only visible at whole-section scope, because 25:27 and 29:23 sit on opposite sides of the old cut.

**Original audience reception.** A Judahite hearer in Hezekiah's court would have recognised 25:1–6 as his own working world and 27:23–27 as the estate economy underneath it. `[I]` The three-chapter withdrawal of the king would not have read as irreligion but as realism: **most decisions are made where no magistrate is watching.**

## Preaching Pitfalls

1. **Do not preach the silence as the section's message without saying it ends.** The pull of Headline 1 is towards a sermon on "God is not mentioned, therefore live wisely by your own lights." The text does not say that. **It restores God, Torah and the king in chapters 28–29, and the restoration is the point of the withdrawal.** Preach the nest whole or not at all.
2. **Do not make 26:4–5 a puzzle to be solved.** Every attempt to harmonise it removes the thing it is doing. If the sermon ends with the congregation knowing which verse applies when, the sermon has replaced the text's training with the preacher's judgement.
3. **Do not rest weight on the Hebrew silence without noting 27:20a.** The Greek names the Lord inside the stretch. The silence is a feature of the Masoretic text, and should be described as such.
4. **Do not let chapter 26 become a sermon on difficult people.** Eleven *kəsîl* verses in twenty-eight is a catalogue, and a catalogue preached as portraiture invites the congregation to identify others. The chapter's own sting is 26:12 — *"do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him"* — which turns the catalogue on the reader.
5. **Do not claim 25:1 dates the sayings.** It dates the copying. `[T]` The verb is *heʿtîqû*.
6. **Do not preach 28:1 as courage without the trust-chain.** "Bold as a lion" is *yibṭāḥ*, and English hides it. The chapter goes on to say what such a man may and may not trust in.

## So What

**Solomon II teaches a man to be good when no one with authority is in the room, and then tells him why that is not the same as being on his own.**

For three chapters the collection withdraws every figure that had been deciding cases — God, the moral categories, the king — and hands the reader situations instead: a fool who must be answered and must not be, a friend whose wounds are faithful, a neighbour who blesses too loudly in the morning, a flock whose condition must be known. **This is not a lapse into secular pragmatism. It is apprenticeship.** And when the authorities return at 28:4–5, they return differently: not as a verdict handed down, but as *tôrâ* to be kept and a LORD to be sought — the one thing the first movement never named.

The last word is 29:27, where the abomination runs both ways, and 29:23, where honour comes to the man who did not go looking for it.

## Open Questions

1. **Are the three twenty-nine-verse paragraphs (25:21–26:21, 26:22–27:22, 28:17–29:17) coincidence?** No source known to this project comments. *Low priority; recorded as observation only.*
2. **Which way does the 26:11a / Sirach 4:21 dependence run?** Requires a library query on Greek Proverbs and Ben Sira. *Moderate priority — it bears on the date of Greek Proverbs.*
3. **Does 27:20a's "abomination to the Lord" reflect a Hebrew *Vorlage* or a Greek expansion?** *Moderate priority.*
4. **Is the concentric-silence structure noticed anywhere in the literature?** The individual observation about 25–27's reticence is common; the nesting with the king's absence as outermost has not been seen in any source consulted. *Worth one query before preaching it.*
