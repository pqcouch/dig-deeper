What exists is the *Masoretic tradition*, whose standard complete witness is Codex Leningradensis (B19a, dated 1008/9), which the Westminster Leningrad Codex transcribes and which *BHS* prints diplomatically. ### 25:1–28

**1** גַּם־אֵ֭לֶּה מִשְׁלֵ֣י שְׁלֹמֹ֑ה אֲשֶׁ֥ר הֶ֝עְתִּ֗יקוּ אַנְשֵׁ֤י ׀ חִזְקִיָּ֬ה מֶֽלֶךְ־יְהוּדָֽה׃  
These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah, king of Judah, transcribed.

**2** כְּבֹ֣ד אֱ֭לֹהִים הַסְתֵּ֣ר דָּבָ֑ר וּכְבֹ֥ד מְ֝לָכִ֗ים חֲקֹ֣ר דָּבָֽר׃  
It is the glory of God to conceal a matter, But the glory of kings is to search out a matter.

**3** שָׁמַ֣יִם לָ֭רוּם וָאָ֣רֶץ לָעֹ֑מֶק וְלֵ֥ב מְ֝לָכִ֗ים אֵ֣ין חֵֽקֶר׃  
As the heavens for height and the earth for depth, So the heart of kings is unsearchable.

**4** הָג֣וֹ סִיגִ֣ים מִכָּ֑סֶף וַיֵּצֵ֖א לַצֹּרֵ֣ף כֶּֽלִי׃  
Take away the dross from the silver, And there comes out a vessel for the smith;

**5** הָג֣וֹ רָ֭שָׁע לִפְנֵי־מֶ֑לֶךְ וְיִכּ֖וֹן בַּצֶּ֣דֶק כִּסְאֽוֹ׃  
Take away the wicked before the king, And his throne will be established in righteousness.

**6** אַל־תִּתְהַדַּ֥ר לִפְנֵי־מֶ֑לֶךְ וּבִמְק֥וֹם גְּ֝דֹלִ֗ים אַֽל־תַּעֲמֹֽד׃  
Do not claim honor in the presence of the king, And do not stand in the place of great men;

**7** כִּ֤י ט֥וֹב אֲמָר־לְךָ֗ עֲֽלֵ֫ה הֵ֥נָּה מֵֽ֭הַשְׁפִּ֣ילְךָ לִפְנֵ֣י נָדִ֑יב אֲשֶׁ֖ר רָא֣וּ עֵינֶֽיךָ׃  
For it is better that it be said to you, “Come up here,” Than for you to be placed lower in the presence of the prince, Whom your eyes have seen.

**8** אַל־תֵּצֵ֥א לָרִ֗ב מַ֫הֵ֥ר פֶּ֣ן מַה־תַּ֭עֲשֶׂה בְּאַחֲרִיתָ֑הּ בְּהַכְלִ֖ים אֹתְךָ֣ רֵעֶֽךָ׃  
Do not go out hastily to argue your case; Otherwise, what will you do in the end, When your neighbor humiliates you?

**9** רִֽ֭יבְךָ רִ֣יב אֶת־רֵעֶ֑ךָ וְס֖וֹד אַחֵ֣ר אַל־תְּגָֽל׃  
Argue your case with your neighbor, And do not reveal the secret of another,

**10** פֶּֽן־יְחַסֶּדְךָ֥ שֹׁמֵ֑עַ וְ֝דִבָּתְךָ֗ לֹ֣א תָשֽׁוּב׃  
Or he who hears it will reproach you, And the evil report about you will not pass away.

**11** תַּפּוּחֵ֣י זָ֭הָב בְּמַשְׂכִּיּ֥וֹת כָּ֑סֶף דָּ֝בָ֗ר דָּבֻ֥ר עַל־אָפְנָֽיו׃  
Like apples of gold in settings of silver Is a word spoken in right circumstances.

**12** נֶ֣זֶם זָ֭הָב וַחֲלִי־כָ֑תֶם מוֹכִ֥יחַ חָ֝כָ֗ם עַל־אֹ֥זֶן שֹׁמָֽעַת׃  
Like an earring of gold and an ornament of fine gold Is a wise reprover to a listening ear.

**13** כְּצִנַּת־שֶׁ֨לֶג ׀ בְּי֬וֹם קָצִ֗יר צִ֣יר נֶ֭אֱמָן לְשֹׁלְחָ֑יו וְנֶ֖פֶשׁ אֲדֹנָ֣יו יָשִֽׁיב׃ **׃פ**  
Like the cold of snow in the time of harvest Is a faithful messenger to those who send him, For he refreshes the soul of his masters.

**14** נְשִׂיאִ֣ים וְ֭רוּחַ וְגֶ֣שֶׁם אָ֑יִן אִ֥ישׁ מִ֝תְהַלֵּ֗ל בְּמַתַּת־שָֽׁקֶר׃  
Like clouds and wind without rain Is a man who boasts of his gifts falsely.

**15** בְּאֹ֣רֶךְ אַ֭פַּיִם יְפֻתֶּ֣ה קָצִ֑ין וְלָשׁ֥וֹן רַ֝כָּ֗ה תִּשְׁבָּר־גָּֽרֶם׃  
By forbearance a ruler may be persuaded, And a soft tongue breaks the bone.

**16** דְּבַ֣שׁ מָ֭צָאתָ אֱכֹ֣ל דַּיֶּ֑ךָּ פֶּן־תִּ֝שְׂבָּעֶ֗נּוּ וַהֲקֵֽאתֽוֹ׃  
Have you found honey? Eat only what you need, That you not have it in excess and vomit it.

**17** הֹקַ֣ר רַ֭גְלְךָ מִבֵּ֣ית רֵעֶ֑ךָ פֶּן־יִ֝שְׂבָּעֲךָ֗ וּשְׂנֵאֶֽךָ׃  
Let your foot rarely be in your neighbor’s house, Or he will become weary of you and hate you.

**18** מֵפִ֣יץ וְ֭חֶרֶב וְחֵ֣ץ שָׁנ֑וּן אִ֥ישׁ עֹנֶ֥ה בְ֝רֵעֵ֗הוּ עֵ֣ד שָֽׁקֶר׃  
Like a club and a sword and a sharp arrow Is a man who bears false witness against his neighbor.

**19** שֵׁ֣ן רֹ֭עָה וְרֶ֣גֶל מוּעָ֑דֶת מִבְטָ֥ח בּ֝וֹגֵ֗ד בְּי֣וֹם צָרָֽה׃  
Like a bad tooth and an unsteady foot Is confidence in a faithless man in time of trouble.

**20** מַ֥עֲדֶה בֶּ֨גֶד ׀ בְּי֣וֹם קָ֭רָה חֹ֣מֶץ עַל־נָ֑תֶר וְשָׁ֥ר בַּ֝שִּׁרִ֗ים עַ֣ל לֶב־רָֽע׃ **׃פ**  
Like one who takes off a garment on a cold day, or like vinegar on soda, Is he who sings songs to a troubled heart.

**21** אִם־רָעֵ֣ב שֹׂ֭נַאֲךָ הַאֲכִלֵ֣הוּ לָ֑חֶם וְאִם־צָ֝מֵ֗א הַשְׁקֵ֥הוּ מָֽיִם׃  
If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat; And if he is thirsty, give him water to drink;

**22** כִּ֤י גֶֽחָלִ֗ים אַ֭תָּה חֹתֶ֣ה עַל־רֹאשׁ֑וֹ וַֽ֝יהוָ֗ה יְשַׁלֶּם־לָֽךְ׃  
For you will heap burning coals on his head, And the LORD will reward you.

**23** ר֣וּחַ צָ֭פוֹן תְּח֣וֹלֵֽל גָּ֑שֶׁם וּפָנִ֥ים נִ֝זְעָמִ֗ים לְשׁ֣וֹן סָֽתֶר׃  
The north wind brings forth rain, And a backbiting tongue, an angry countenance.

**24** ט֗וֹב שֶׁ֥בֶת עַל־פִּנַּת־גָּ֑ג מֵאֵ֥שֶׁת מדונים וּבֵ֥ית חָֽבֶר׃  
It is better to live in a corner of the roof Than in a house shared with a contentious woman.

**25** מַ֣יִם קָ֭רִים עַל־נֶ֣פֶשׁ עֲיֵפָ֑ה וּשְׁמוּעָ֥ה ט֝וֹבָ֗ה מֵאֶ֥רֶץ מֶרְחָֽק׃  
Like cold water to a weary soul, So is good news from a distant land.

**26** מַעְיָ֣ן נִ֭רְפָּשׂ וּמָק֣וֹר מָשְׁחָ֑ת צַ֝דִּ֗יק מָ֣ט לִפְנֵֽי־רָשָֽׁע׃  
Like a trampled spring and a polluted well Is a righteous man who gives way before the wicked.

**27** אָ֘כֹ֤ל דְּבַ֣שׁ הַרְבּ֣וֹת לֹא־ט֑וֹב וְחֵ֖קֶר כְּבֹדָ֣ם כָּבֽוֹד׃  
It is not good to eat much honey, Nor is it glory to search out one’s own glory.

**28** עִ֣יר פְּ֭רוּצָה אֵ֣ין חוֹמָ֑ה אִ֝֗ישׁ אֲשֶׁ֤ר אֵ֖ין מַעְצָ֣ר לְרוּחֽוֹ׃  
Like a city that is broken into and without walls Is a man who has no control over his spirit.

### 26:1–28

**1** כַּשֶּׁ֤לֶג ׀ בַּקַּ֗יִץ וְכַמָּטָ֥ר בַּקָּצִ֑יר כֵּ֤ן לֹא־נָאוֶ֖ה לִכְסִ֣יל כָּבֽוֹד׃  
Like snow in summer and like rain in harvest, So honor is not fitting for a fool.

**2** כַּצִּפּ֣וֹר לָ֭נוּד כַּדְּר֣וֹר לָע֑וּף כֵּ֥ן קִֽלְלַ֥ת חִ֝נָּ֗ם לא תָבֹֽא׃  
Like a sparrow in its flitting, like a swallow in its flying, So a curse without cause does not alight.

**3** שׁ֣וֹט לַ֭סּוּס מֶ֣תֶג לַחֲמ֑וֹר וְ֝שֵׁ֗בֶט לְגֵ֣ו כְּסִילִֽים׃  
A whip is for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, And a rod for the back of fools.

**4** אַל־תַּ֣עַן כְּ֭סִיל כְּאִוַּלְתּ֑וֹ פֶּֽן־תִּשְׁוֶה־לּ֥וֹ גַם־אָֽתָּה׃  
Do not answer a fool according to his folly, Or you will also be like him.

**5** עֲנֵ֣ה כְ֭סִיל כְּאִוַּלְתּ֑וֹ פֶּן־יִהְיֶ֖ה חָכָ֣ם בְּעֵינָֽיו׃  
Answer a fool as his folly deserves, That he not be wise in his own eyes.

**6** מְקַצֶּ֣ה רַ֭גְלַיִם חָמָ֣ס שֹׁתֶ֑ה שֹׁלֵ֖חַ דְּבָרִ֣ים בְּיַד־כְּסִֽיל׃  
He cuts off his own feet and drinks violence Who sends a message by the hand of a fool.

**7** דַּלְי֣וּ שֹׁ֭קַיִם מִפִּסֵּ֑חַ וּ֝מָשָׁ֗ל בְּפִ֣י כְסִילִֽים׃  
Like the legs which are useless to the lame, So is a proverb in the mouth of fools.

**8** כִּצְר֣וֹר אֶ֭בֶן בְּמַרְגֵּמָ֑ה כֵּן־נוֹתֵ֖ן לִכְסִ֣יל כָּבֽוֹד׃  
Like one who binds a stone in a sling, So is he who gives honor to a fool.

**9** ח֭וֹחַ עָלָ֣ה בְיַד־שִׁכּ֑וֹר וּ֝מָשָׁ֗ל בְּפִ֣י כְסִילִֽים׃  
Like a thorn which falls into the hand of a drunkard, So is a proverb in the mouth of fools.

**10** רַ֥ב מְחֽוֹלֵֽל־כֹּ֑ל וְשֹׂכֵ֥ר כְּ֝סִ֗יל וְשֹׂכֵ֥ר עֹבְרִֽים׃  
Like an archer who wounds everyone, So is he who hires a fool or who hires those who pass by.

**11** כְּ֭כֶלֶב שָׁ֣ב עַל־קֵא֑וֹ כְּ֝סִ֗יל שׁוֹנֶ֥ה בְאִוַּלְתּֽוֹ׃  
Like a dog that returns to its vomit Is a fool who repeats his folly.

**12** רָאִ֗יתָ אִ֭ישׁ חָכָ֣ם בְּעֵינָ֑יו תִּקְוָ֖ה לִכְסִ֣יל מִמֶּֽנּוּ׃  
Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him.

**13** אָמַ֣ר עָ֭צֵל שַׁ֣חַל בַּדָּ֑רֶךְ אֲ֝רִ֗י בֵּ֣ין הָרְחֹבֽוֹת׃  
The sluggard says, “There is a lion in the road! A lion is in the open square!”

**14** הַ֭דֶּלֶת תִּסּ֣וֹב עַל־צִירָ֑הּ וְ֝עָצֵ֗ל עַל־מִטָּתֽוֹ׃  
As the door turns on its hinges, So does the sluggard on his bed.

**15** טָ֘מַ֤ן עָצֵ֣ל יָ֭דוֹ בַּצַּלָּ֑חַת נִ֝לְאָ֗ה לַֽהֲשִׁיבָ֥הּ אֶל־פִּֽיו׃  
The sluggard buries his hand in the dish; He is weary of bringing it to his mouth again.

**16** חָכָ֣ם עָצֵ֣ל בְּעֵינָ֑יו מִ֝שִּׁבְעָ֗ה מְשִׁ֣יבֵי טָֽעַם׃  
The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes Than seven men who can give a discreet answer.

**17** מַחֲזִ֥יק בְּאָזְנֵי־כָ֑לֶב עֹבֵ֥ר מִ֝תְעַבֵּ֗ר עַל־רִ֥יב לֹּֽא־לֽוֹ׃  
Like one who takes a dog by the ears Is he who passes by and meddles with strife not belonging to him.

**18** כְּֽ֭מִתְלַהְלֵהַּ הַיֹּרֶ֥ה זִקִּ֗ים חִצִּ֥ים וָמָֽוֶת׃  
Like a madman who throws Firebrands, arrows and death,

**19** כֵּֽן־אִ֭ישׁ רִמָּ֣ה אֶת־רֵעֵ֑הוּ וְ֝אָמַ֗ר הֲֽלֹא־מְשַׂחֵ֥ק אָֽנִי׃  
So is the man who deceives his neighbor, And says, “Was I not joking?”

**20** בְּאֶ֣פֶס עֵ֭צִים תִּכְבֶּה־אֵ֑שׁ וּבְאֵ֥ין נִ֝רְגָּ֗ן יִשְׁתֹּ֥ק מָדֽוֹן׃  
For lack of wood the fire goes out, And where there is no whisperer, contention quiets down.

**21** פֶּחָ֣ם לְ֭גֶחָלִים וְעֵצִ֣ים לְאֵ֑שׁ וְאִ֥ישׁ מדונים לְחַרְחַר־רִֽיב׃ **׃פ**  
Like charcoal to hot embers and wood to fire, So is a contentious man to kindle strife.

**22** דִּבְרֵ֣י נִ֭רְגָּן כְּמִֽתְלַהֲמִ֑ים וְ֝הֵ֗ם יָרְד֥וּ חַדְרֵי־בָֽטֶן׃  
The words of a whisperer are like dainty morsels, And they go down into the innermost parts of the body.

**23** כֶּ֣סֶף סִ֭יגִים מְצֻפֶּ֣ה עַל־חָ֑רֶשׂ שְׂפָתַ֖יִם דֹּלְקִ֣ים וְלֶב־רָֽע׃  
Like an earthen vessel overlaid with silver dross Are burning lips and a wicked heart.

**24** בשפתו יִנָּכֵ֣ר שׂוֹנֵ֑א וּ֝בְקִרְבּ֗וֹ יָשִׁ֥ית מִרְמָֽה׃  
He who hates disguises it with his lips, But he lays up deceit in his heart.

**25** כִּֽי־יְחַנֵּ֣ן ק֭וֹלוֹ אַל־תַּֽאֲמֶן־בּ֑וֹ כִּ֤י שֶׁ֖בַע תּוֹעֵב֣וֹת בְּלִבּֽוֹ׃  
When he speaks graciously, do not believe him, For there are seven abominations in his heart.

**26** תִּכַּסֶּ֣ה שִׂ֭נְאָה בְּמַשָּׁא֑וֹן תִּגָּלֶ֖ה רָעָת֣וֹ בְקָהָֽל׃  
Though his hatred covers itself with guile, His wickedness will be revealed before the assembly.

**27** כֹּֽרֶה־שַּׁ֭חַת בָּ֣הּ יִפֹּ֑ל וְגֹ֥לֵ֥ל אֶ֝בֶן אֵלָ֥יו תָּשֽׁוּב׃  
He who digs a pit will fall into it, And he who rolls a stone, it will come back on him.

**28** לְֽשׁוֹן־שֶׁ֭קֶר יִשְׂנָ֣א דַכָּ֑יו וּפֶ֥ה חָ֝לָ֗ק יַעֲשֶׂ֥ה מִדְחֶֽה׃  
A lying tongue hates those it crushes, And a flattering mouth works ruin.

### 27:1–27

**1** אַֽל־תִּ֭תְהַלֵּל בְּי֣וֹם מָחָ֑ר כִּ֤י לֹא־תֵ֝דַ֗ע מַה־יֵּ֥לֶד יֽוֹם׃  
Do not boast about tomorrow, For you do not know what a day may bring forth.

**2** יְהַלֶּלְךָ֣ זָ֣ר וְלֹא־פִ֑יךָ נָ֝כְרִ֗י וְאַל־שְׂפָתֶֽיךָ׃  
Let another praise you, and not your own mouth; A stranger, and not your own lips.

**3** כֹּֽבֶד־אֶ֭בֶן וְנֵ֣טֶל הַח֑וֹל וְכַ֥עַס אֱ֝וִ֗יל כָּבֵ֥ד מִשְּׁנֵיהֶֽם׃  
A stone is heavy and the sand weighty, But the provocation of a fool is heavier than both of them.

**4** אַכְזְרִיּ֣וּת חֵ֭מָה וְשֶׁ֣טֶף אָ֑ף וּמִ֥י יַ֝עֲמֹד לִפְנֵ֥י קִנְאָֽה׃  
Wrath is fierce and anger is a flood, But who can stand before jealousy?

**5** ט֭וֹבָה תּוֹכַ֣חַת מְגֻלָּ֑ה מֵֽאַהֲבָ֥ה מְסֻתָּֽרֶת׃  
Better is open rebuke Than love that is concealed.

**6** נֶ֭אֱמָנִים פִּצְעֵ֣י אוֹהֵ֑ב וְ֝נַעְתָּר֗וֹת נְשִׁיק֥וֹת שׂוֹנֵֽא׃  
Faithful are the wounds of a friend, But deceitful are the kisses of an enemy.

**7** נֶ֣פֶשׁ שְׂ֭בֵעָה תָּב֣וּס נֹ֑פֶת וְנֶ֥פֶשׁ רְ֝עֵבָ֗ה כָּל־מַ֥ר מָתֽוֹק׃  
A sated man loathes honey, But to a famished man any bitter thing is sweet.

**8** כְּ֭צִפּוֹר נוֹדֶ֣דֶת מִן־קִנָּ֑הּ כֵּֽן־אִ֝֗ישׁ נוֹדֵ֥ד מִמְּקוֹמֽוֹ׃  
Like a bird that wanders from her nest, So is a man who wanders from his home.

**9** שֶׁ֣מֶן וּ֭קְטֹרֶת יְשַׂמַּֽח־לֵ֑ב וּמֶ֥תֶק רֵ֝עֵ֗הוּ מֵֽעֲצַת־נָֽפֶשׁ׃  
Oil and perfume make the heart glad, So a man’s counsel is sweet to his friend.

**10** רֵֽעֲךָ֨ ורעה אָבִ֡יךָ אַֽל־תַּעֲזֹ֗ב וּבֵ֥ית אָחִ֗יךָ אַל־תָּ֭בוֹא בְּי֣וֹם אֵידֶ֑ךָ ט֥וֹב שָׁכֵ֥ן קָ֝ר֗וֹב מֵאָ֥ח רָחֽוֹק׃  
Do not forsake your own friend or your father’s friend, And do not go to your brother’s house in the day of your calamity; Better is a neighbor who is near than a brother far away.

**11** חֲכַ֣ם בְּ֭נִי וְשַׂמַּ֣ח לִבִּ֑י וְאָשִׁ֖יבָה חֹרְפִ֣י דָבָֽר׃  
Be wise, my son, and make my heart glad, That I may reply to him who reproaches me.

**12** עָר֤וּם רָאָ֣ה רָעָ֣ה נִסְתָּ֑ר פְּ֝תָאיִ֗ם עָבְר֥וּ נֶעֱנָֽשׁוּ׃  
A prudent man sees evil and hides himself, The naive proceed and pay the penalty.

**13** קַח־בִּ֭גְדוֹ כִּי־עָ֣רַב זָ֑ר וּבְעַ֖ד נָכְרִיָּ֣ה חַבְלֵֽהוּ׃  
Take his garment when he becomes surety for a stranger; And for an adulterous woman hold him in pledge.

**14** מְבָ֘רֵ֤ךְ רֵעֵ֨הוּ ׀ בְּק֣וֹל גָּ֭דוֹל בַּבֹּ֣קֶר הַשְׁכֵּ֑ים קְ֝לָלָ֗ה תֵּחָ֥שֶׁב לֽוֹ׃  
He who blesses his friend with a loud voice early in the morning, It will be reckoned a curse to him.

**15** דֶּ֣לֶף ט֭וֹרֵד בְּי֣וֹם סַגְרִ֑יר וְאֵ֥שֶׁת מדונים נִשְׁתָּוָֽה׃  
A constant dripping on a day of steady rain And a contentious woman are alike;

**16** צֹפְנֶ֥יהָ צָֽפַן־ר֑וּחַ וְשֶׁ֖מֶן יְמִינ֣וֹ יִקְרָֽא׃  
He who would restrain her restrains the wind, And grasps oil with his right hand.

**17** בַּרְזֶ֣ל בְּבַרְזֶ֣ל יָ֑חַד וְ֝אִ֗ישׁ יַ֣חַד פְּנֵֽי־רֵעֵֽהוּ׃  
Iron sharpens iron, So one man sharpens another.

**18** נֹצֵ֣ר תְּ֭אֵנָה יֹאכַ֣ל פִּרְיָ֑הּ וְשֹׁמֵ֖ר אֲדֹנָ֣יו יְכֻבָּֽד׃  
He who tends the fig tree will eat its fruit, And he who cares for his master will be honored.

**19** כַּ֭מַּיִם הַפָּנִ֣ים לַפָּנִ֑ים כֵּ֤ן לֵֽב־הָ֝אָדָ֗ם לָאָדָֽם׃  
As in water face reflects face, So the heart of man reflects man.

**20** שְׁא֣וֹל ואבדה לֹ֣א תִשְׂבַּ֑עְנָה וְעֵינֵ֥י הָ֝אָדָ֗ם לֹ֣א תִשְׂבַּֽעְנָה׃  
Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied, Nor are the eyes of man ever satisfied.

**21** מַצְרֵ֣ף לַ֭כֶּסֶף וְכ֣וּר לַזָּהָ֑ב וְ֝אִ֗ישׁ לְפִ֣י מַהֲלָלֽוֹ׃  
The crucible is for silver and the furnace for gold, And each is tested by the praise accorded him.

**22** אִ֥ם תִּכְתּֽוֹשׁ־אֶת־הָאֱוִ֨יל ׀ בַּֽמַּכְתֵּ֡שׁ בְּת֣וֹךְ הָ֭רִיפוֹת בַּֽעֱלִ֑י לֹא־תָס֥וּר מֵ֝עָלָ֗יו אִוַּלְתּֽוֹ׃ **׃פ**  
Though you pound a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with crushed grain, Yet his foolishness will not depart from him.

**23** יָדֹ֣עַ תֵּ֭דַע פְּנֵ֣י צֹאנֶ֑ךָ שִׁ֥ית לִ֝בְּךָ֗ לַעֲדָרִֽים׃  
Know well the condition of your flocks, And pay attention to your herds;

**24** כִּ֤י לֹ֣א לְעוֹלָ֣ם חֹ֑סֶן וְאִם־נֵ֝֗זֶר לְד֣וֹר דור׃  
For riches are not forever, Nor does a crown endure to all generations.

**25** גָּלָ֣ה חָ֭צִיר וְנִרְאָה־דֶ֑שֶׁא וְ֝נֶאֶסְפ֗וּ עִשְּׂב֥וֹת הָרִֽים׃  
When the grass disappears, the new growth is seen, And the herbs of the mountains are gathered in,

**26** כְּבָשִׂ֥ים לִלְבוּשֶׁ֑ךָ וּמְחִ֥יר שָׂ֝דֶ֗ה עַתּוּדִֽים׃  
The lambs will be for your clothing, And the goats will bring the price of a field,

**27** וְדֵ֤י ׀ חֲלֵ֬ב עִזִּ֗ים לְֽ֭לַחְמְךָ לְלֶ֣חֶם בֵּיתֶ֑ךָ וְ֝חַיִּ֗ים לְנַעֲרוֹתֶֽיךָ׃  
And there will be goats’ milk enough for your food, For the food of your household, And sustenance for your maidens.

### 28:1–28

**1** נָ֣סוּ וְאֵין־רֹדֵ֣ף רָשָׁ֑ע וְ֝צַדִּיקִ֗ים כִּכְפִ֥יר יִבְטָֽח׃  
The wicked flee when no one is pursuing, But the righteous are bold as a lion.

**2** בְּפֶ֣שַֽׁע אֶ֭רֶץ רַבִּ֣ים שָׂרֶ֑יהָ וּבְאָדָ֥ם מֵבִ֥ין יֹ֝דֵ֗עַ כֵּ֣ן יַאֲרִֽיךְ׃  
By the transgression of a land many are its princes, But by a man of understanding and knowledge, so it endures.

**3** גֶּ֣בֶר רָ֭שׁ וְעֹשֵׁ֣ק דַּלִּ֑ים מָטָ֥ר סֹ֝חֵ֗ף וְאֵ֣ין לָֽחֶם׃  
A poor man who oppresses the lowly Is like a driving rain which leaves no food.

**4** עֹזְבֵ֣י ת֭וֹרָה יְהַֽלְל֣וּ רָשָׁ֑ע וְשֹׁמְרֵ֥י ת֝וֹרָ֗ה יִתְגָּ֥רוּ בָֽם׃ **׃פ**  
Those who forsake the law praise the wicked, But those who keep the law strive with them.

**5** אַנְשֵׁי־רָ֭ע לֹא־יָבִ֣ינוּ מִשְׁפָּ֑ט וּמְבַקְשֵׁ֥י יְ֝הוָ֗ה יָבִ֥ינוּ כֹֽל׃  
Evil men do not understand justice, But those who seek the LORD understand all things.

**6** טֽוֹב־רָ֭שׁ הוֹלֵ֣ךְ בְּתֻמּ֑וֹ מֵעִקֵּ֥שׁ דְּ֝רָכַ֗יִם וְה֣וּא עָשִֽׁיר׃  
Better is the poor who walks in his integrity Than he who is crooked though he be rich.

**7** נוֹצֵ֣ר תּ֭וֹרָה בֵּ֣ן מֵבִ֑ין וְרֹעֶה זֽ֝וֹלְלִ֗ים יַכְלִ֥ים אָבִֽיו׃  
He who keeps the law is a discerning son, But he who is a companion of gluttons humiliates his father.

**8** מַרְבֶּ֣ה ה֭וֹנוֹ בְּנֶ֣שֶׁךְ ובתרבית לְחוֹנֵ֖ן דַּלִּ֣ים יִקְבְּצֶֽנּוּ׃  
He who increases his wealth by interest and usury Gathers it for him who is gracious to the poor.

**9** מֵסִ֣יר אָ֭זְנוֹ מִשְּׁמֹ֣עַ תּוֹרָ֑ה גַּֽם־תְּ֝פִלָּת֗וֹ תּוֹעֵבָֽה׃  
He who turns away his ear from listening to the law, Even his prayer is an abomination.

**10** מַשְׁגֶּ֤ה יְשָׁרִ֨ים ׀ בְּדֶ֥רֶךְ רָ֗ע בִּשְׁחוּת֥וֹ הֽוּא־יִפּ֑וֹל וּ֝תְמִימִ֗ים יִנְחֲלוּ־טֽוֹב׃  
He who leads the upright astray in an evil way Will himself fall into his own pit, But the blameless will inherit good.

**11** חָכָ֣ם בְּ֭עֵינָיו אִ֣ישׁ עָשִׁ֑יר וְדַ֖ל מֵבִ֣ין יַחְקְרֶֽנּוּ׃  
The rich man is wise in his own eyes, But the poor who has understanding sees through him.

**12** בַּעֲלֹ֣ץ צַ֭דִּיקִים רַבָּ֣ה תִפְאָ֑רֶת וּבְק֥וּם רְ֝שָׁעִ֗ים יְחֻפַּ֥שׂ אָדָֽם׃  
When the righteous triumph, there is great glory, But when the wicked rise, men hide themselves.

**13** מְכַסֶּ֣ה פְ֭שָׁעָיו לֹ֣א יַצְלִ֑יחַ וּמוֹדֶ֖ה וְעֹזֵ֣ב יְרֻחָֽם׃  
He who conceals his transgressions will not prosper, But he who confesses and forsakes them will find compassion.

**14** אַשְׁרֵ֣י אָ֭דָם מְפַחֵ֣ד תָּמִ֑יד וּמַקְשֶׁ֥ה לִ֝בּ֗וֹ יִפּ֥וֹל בְּרָעָֽה׃  
How blessed is the man who fears always, But he who hardens his heart will fall into calamity.

**15** אֲרִי־נֹ֭הֵם וְדֹ֣ב שׁוֹקֵ֑ק מֹשֵׁ֥ל רָ֝שָׁ֗ע עַ֣ל עַם־דָּֽל׃  
Like a roaring lion and a rushing bear Is a wicked ruler over a poor people.

**16** נָגִ֗יד חֲסַ֣ר תְּ֭בוּנוֹת וְרַ֥ב מַעֲשַׁקּ֑וֹת שנאי בֶ֝֗צַע יַאֲרִ֥יךְ יָמִֽים׃ **׃פ**  
A leader who is a great oppressor lacks understanding, But he who hates unjust gain will prolong his days.

**17** אָ֭דָם עָשֻׁ֣ק בְּדַם־נָפֶשׁ עַד־בּ֥וֹר יָ֝נ֗וּס אַל־יִתְמְכוּ־בֽוֹ׃  
A man who is laden with the guilt of human blood Will be a fugitive until death; let no one support him.

**18** הוֹלֵ֣ךְ תָּ֭מִים יִוָּשֵׁ֑עַ וְנֶעְקַ֥שׁ דְּ֝רָכַ֗יִם יִפּ֥וֹל בְּאֶחָֽת׃  
He who walks blamelessly will be delivered, But he who is crooked will fall all at once.

**19** עֹבֵ֣ד אַ֭דְמָתוֹ יִֽשְׂבַּֽע־לָ֑חֶם וּמְרַדֵּ֥ף רֵ֝קִ֗ים יִֽשְׂבַּֽע־רִֽישׁ׃  
He who tills his land will have plenty of food, But he who follows empty pursuits will have poverty in plenty.

**20** אִ֣ישׁ אֱ֭מוּנוֹת רַב־בְּרָכ֑וֹת וְאָ֥ץ לְ֝הַעֲשִׁ֗יר לֹ֣א יִנָּקֶֽה׃  
A faithful man will abound with blessings, But he who makes haste to be rich will not go unpunished.

**21** הַֽכֵּר־פָּנִ֥ים לֹא־ט֑וֹב וְעַל־פַּת־לֶ֝֗חֶם יִפְשַׁע־גָּֽבֶר׃  
To show partiality is not good, Because for a piece of bread a man will transgress.

**22** נִֽבֳהָ֥ל לַה֗וֹן אִ֭ישׁ רַ֣ע עָ֑יִן וְלֹֽא־יֵ֝דַע כִּי־חֶ֥סֶר יְבֹאֶֽנּוּ׃  
A man with an evil eye hastens after wealth And does not know that want will come upon him.

**23** מ֘וֹכִ֤יחַ אָדָ֣ם אַ֭חֲרַי חֵ֣ן יִמְצָ֑א מִֽמַּחֲלִ֥יק לָשֽׁוֹן׃  
He who rebukes a man will afterward find more favor Than he who flatters with the tongue.

**24** גּוֹזֵ֤ל ׀ אָ֘בִ֤יו וְאִמּ֗וֹ וְאֹמֵ֥ר אֵֽין־פָּ֑שַׁע חָבֵ֥ר ה֝֗וּא לְאִ֣ישׁ מַשְׁחִֽית׃  
He who robs his father or his mother And says, “It is not a transgression,” Is the companion of a man who destroys.

**25** רְחַב־נֶ֭פֶשׁ יְגָרֶ֣ה מָד֑וֹן וּבוֹטֵ֖חַ עַל־יְהוָ֣ה יְדֻשָּֽׁן׃  
An arrogant man stirs up strife, But he who trusts in the LORD will prosper.

**26** בּוֹטֵ֣חַ בְּ֭לִבּוֹ ה֣וּא כְסִ֑יל וְהוֹלֵ֥ךְ בְּ֝חָכְמָ֗ה ה֣וּא יִמָּלֵֽט׃  
He who trusts in his own heart is a fool, But he who walks wisely will be delivered.

**27** נוֹתֵ֣ן לָ֭רָשׁ אֵ֣ין מַחְס֑וֹר וּמַעְלִ֥ים עֵ֝ינָ֗יו רַב־מְאֵרֽוֹת׃  
He who gives to the poor will never want, But he who shuts his eyes will have many curses.

**28** בְּק֣וּם רְ֭שָׁעִים יִסָּתֵ֣ר אָדָ֑ם וּ֝בְאָבְדָ֗ם יִרְבּ֥וּ צַדִּיקִֽים׃  
When the wicked rise, men hide themselves; But when they perish, the righteous increase.

### 29:1–27

**1** אִ֣ישׁ תּ֭וֹכָחוֹת מַקְשֶׁה־עֹ֑רֶף פֶּ֥תַע יִ֝שָּׁבֵ֗ר וְאֵ֣ין מַרְפֵּֽא׃  
A man who hardens his neck after much reproof Will suddenly be broken beyond remedy.

**2** בִּרְב֣וֹת צַ֭דִּיקִים יִשְׂמַ֣ח הָעָ֑ם וּבִמְשֹׁ֥ל רָ֝שָׁ֗ע יֵאָ֥נַֽח עָֽם׃  
When the righteous increase, the people rejoice, But when a wicked man rules, people groan.

**3** אִֽישׁ־אֹהֵ֣ב חָ֭כְמָה יְשַׂמַּ֣ח אָבִ֑יו וְרֹעֶ֥ה ז֝וֹנ֗וֹת יְאַבֶּד־הֽוֹן׃  
A man who loves wisdom makes his father glad, But he who keeps company with harlots wastes his wealth.

**4** מֶ֗לֶךְ בְּ֭מִשְׁפָּט יַעֲמִ֣יד אָ֑רֶץ וְאִ֖ישׁ תְּרוּמ֣וֹת יֶֽהֶרְסֶֽנָּה׃  
The king gives stability to the land by justice, But a man who takes bribes overthrows it.

**5** גֶּ֭בֶר מַחֲלִ֣יק עַל־רֵעֵ֑הוּ רֶ֝֗שֶׁת פּוֹרֵ֥שׂ עַל־פְּעָמָֽיו׃  
A man who flatters his neighbor Is spreading a net for his steps.

**6** בְּפֶ֤שַֽׁע אִ֣ישׁ רָ֣ע מוֹקֵ֑שׁ וְ֝צַדִּ֗יק יָר֥וּן וְשָׂמֵֽחַ׃  
By transgression an evil man is ensnared, But the righteous sings and rejoices.

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

1. **In 138 verses the divine name stands in six, and the Masoretic text carries a sixty-five-verse stretch — 25:23 to 28:4 — in which God is not named at all; but the Greek tradition does not keep that silence.** Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Translations, Structure. יְהוָה ("the LORD") occurs in 25:22; 28:5, 25; 29:13, 25, 26 — 4.3% of the collection's verses against 9.5% across the book (87 of 915 verses, WLC) `[T]`. Swete's Greek has ten divine-name verses in the same window, of which one (27:18) is a human master and two are not in the Hebrew: **27:20 carries a plus, βδέλυγμα Κυρίῳ ("an abomination to the Lord"), which falls inside the Hebrew's silence and shortens it by roughly a fifth**, and 29:23 supplies Κύριος as the subject of a Hebrew clause that has none `[T]`, Swete. *BHS* notes the 27:20 plus and reconstructs a Hebrew line behind it (apparatus 27:20ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`. **The silence is a real and preachable feature of the text the church reads — and it may be a feature of the Masoretic text rather than of the collection.** Say both.

2. **Every independent measure splits this collection in two at the end of chapter 27, and the two halves are different kinds of writing.** Surfaced by: Structure, Parallels, Repetition, Vocabulary, Linking Words. Lexical opposition runs at 4.8% in 25–27 (4 of 83 verses) and 18.2% in 28–29 (10 of 55); imperative-bearing verses at 12.0% against 1.8%; of the fifteen verses in the collection whose first word is a comparative כְּ ("like"), fourteen fall in 25–27 and **chapter 28 has none**; צַדִּיק ("righteous") stands in one verse of 25–27 and eight of 28–29; תּוֹרָה ("law, instruction") in none and four; בָּטַח ("to trust") in none and four; the divine name in one and five `[T]`, WLC. Six measures, all pointing the same way, none of them dependent on the others.

3. **The one occurrence of דַּעַת ("knowledge") in the whole collection is about the poor — and neither the study text nor the pulpit text lets the congregation hear the word.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Translations. דַּעַת stands in 39 verses of Proverbs and in exactly one of these 138: 29:7, רָשָׁע לֹא־יָבִין דָּעַת ("the wicked does not understand knowledge"), whose first colon is יֹדֵעַ צַדִּיק דִּין דַּלִּים ("the righteous knows the cause of the poor") `[T]`, WLC. NASB95 renders the noun "such concern"; NIV84 "no such concern". In a collection that has withdrawn the book's whole wisdom vocabulary — מוּסָר ("discipline") absent from all 138 verses though present in 30 elsewhere, the noun יִרְאָה ("fear") absent though present in 14, בִּינָה ("understanding") absent though present in 14, לֵץ ("scoffer") absent though present in 17 — the single surviving occurrence of "knowledge" is not decoration. It is where the collection puts the word it has otherwise refused.

4. **The collection takes 3:7's prohibition, turns it into four case studies, and welds them to its own opening sentence at 28:11.** Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion (Move 4), Structure. The formula חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו ("wise in his own eyes") stands in five verses of Proverbs with חָכָם itself as the adjective — 3:7; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11 — and four of the five are here `[T]`, WLC. Independently, the root חקר ("to search out"), which carries the collection's programmatic first sentence (25:2, כְּבֹד אֱלֹהִים הַסְתֵּר דָּבָר וּכְבֹד מְלָכִים חֲקֹר דָּבָר, "the glory of God is to conceal a matter, the glory of kings to search out a matter"), occurs in six verses of Proverbs, four of them here: 25:2, 3, 27; 28:11 `[T]`. **Both chains land on the same verse.** 28:11 — חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו אִישׁ עָשִׁיר וְדַל מֵבִין יַחְקְרֶנּוּ ("the rich man is wise in his own eyes, but the poor who has understanding searches him out") — takes the searching that 25:2 made the glory of kings and hands it to a poor man, who uses it on the rich man's self-assessment.

5. **Romans 12:20 quotes the Greek of 25:21–22 verbatim and stops one clause short, because Paul has just given that clause's verb to God.** Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion (full Triad), Translations, Linking Words. Rom 12:19 quotes Deut 32:35, ἐμοὶ ἐκδίκησις, ἐγὼ **ἀνταποδώσω** ("vengeance is mine, I will repay"). Rom 12:20 then reproduces Swete's Prov 25:21–22 word for word as far as ἄνθρακας πυρὸς σωρεύσεις ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοῦ ("you will heap coals of fire on his head") — and omits what stands next in both the Greek and the Hebrew: ὁ δὲ Κύριος **ἀνταποδώσει** σοι ἀγαθά, וַיהוָה יְשַׁלֶּם־לָךְ ("and the LORD will repay you") `[T]`, Swete + SBLGNT + WLC. *BHS* cites Rom 12:20 among the witnesses at 25:21 (apparatus 25:21ᵃ, ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`. The verb Paul drops is the verb he has just assigned to God three sentences earlier; and his own next sentence ends νίκα ἐν τῷ **ἀγαθῷ** τὸ κακόν ("overcome evil with good") — the ἀγαθ- of the clause he cut, returning as the weapon rather than as the wage.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview's microscript stands and this collection tests it: wisdom in Proverbs is something a man acquires and the one thing he cannot do is certify that he has acquired it `[S: overview]`. That is a book-level claim, and the fourth headline above is this collection's independent contribution to it — reached here from the חקר and חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו chains before the overview's echo table was re-opened.

**Collection level, and the text states it.** 25:1 is the only verse in Proverbs that reports how any of its material came to be where it is: גַּם־אֵלֶּה מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה אֲשֶׁר הֶעְתִּיקוּ אַנְשֵׁי חִזְקִיָּה מֶלֶךְ־יְהוּדָה ("these also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah, king of Judah, transcribed") `[T]`. Three things follow, and only the first is `[T]`:

- The material is presented as **gathered**, not composed. גַּם ("also") ties it to 10:1's heading; the relative clause credits a named group with the act of transmission. The collection's own account of itself is that somebody found this and copied it out `[T]`.
- The verb is עתק in the hiphil, הֶעְתִּיקוּ. Its ordinary sense is *to move, remove, transfer*; this is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where it means something like *transcribe* `[I]`, high confidence — the sense is inferred from the object (מִשְׁלֵי, "proverbs of") and the agents (אַנְשֵׁי, "men of"), not from a lexicon entry the verse supplies. The Greek reads ἐξεγράψαντο ("they copied out"), which settles the sense for the Greek tradition at least `[T]`, Swete.
- Everything about **date, occasion, and the relation of this collection to Hezekiah's reform** is reconstruction `[S]`. The verse names a king and explains nothing, exactly as 1:1 does. An overview or a sermon that supplies the eighth-century court as the collection's setting is supplying it.

**What the collection is doing.** Two purposes run together, and the two halves carry them unevenly.

25–27 works almost entirely by **comparison**: fourteen of the collection's fifteen comparative-כְּ openings, six of its seven completing כֵּן ("so") clauses, and the concentration of similes at 26:1–27:19 `[T]`. It hands the reader pictures and leaves him to make the identification — a snowfall in harvest, a dog returning, a door on its hinge, iron on iron. The verdict is almost never stated; the image *is* the verdict.

28–29 works by **antithesis and consequence**: the righteous and the wicked, what happens to a land when either is in power, and — four times, in four different formulations — what the poor receive at the hands of the powerful `[T]`. Where 25–27 shows, 28–29 sorts.

**The purpose of the whole, stated as an inference:** to set a reader loose on several hundred cases with the categories withdrawn and the name of God nearly withheld, so that the judgement he forms is his own and is therefore evidence about him `[I]`, high confidence — every element of the inference is a counted feature of the text (the absent vocabulary, the divine-name rate, the simile density, the collapse of direct address after 27:11).

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 22:17–24:34 re-opened the book's direct address after twelve chapters of third-person sentences. It called its material דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים ("words of the wise", 22:17; 24:23), named nobody, and worked at high second-person density — 15.9% of its verses carry an imperative and 20.7% a second-person verb, against 1.1% and 0.5% in 10:1–15:33 `[T]`, WLC. It closed with the sage's own eyewitness report of the sluggard's field (24:30–34) and the paragraph marker at 24:34.

**Necessity answer.** Two things, and the second is the harder one.

First, **the address has to be withdrawn.** 22:17–24:34 put a teacher in the room: *hear my words, apply your heart, do not envy, I passed by the field of a sluggard and I looked and I received instruction.* A book whose stated purpose is to make self-certified wisdom impossible cannot end with the student still being told the answers. 25:1 removes the teacher: the imperative rate falls from 15.9% to 12.0% in 25–27 and then to 1.8% in 28–29, and the vocative בְּנִי ("my son") — twenty-two occurrences across the book — appears for the **last time in the book** at 27:11 and is never used again `[T]`, WLC. The reader is progressively left alone with the cases.

Second, **the source has to change.** 22:17–24:34 credits its material to חֲכָמִים ("the wise"), an unnamed guild. 25:1 credits its material to Solomon and its *transmission* to a named court three centuries later. The book has now shown the reader three different things a heading can do — attribute (1:1; 10:1), categorise (22:17; 24:23), and report transmission (25:1) — and it has done them in that order `[T]`. A reader who has passed 25:1 knows that what he is reading has a history, and has been told so by the book.

**Implication.** This is why the sermon on this unit cannot be "here are some proverbs from chapters 25 to 29". Its position is its argument: it is the point at which Proverbs stops teaching and starts examining.

**Revisited after Tool 11 (Move 2/Move 4).** The internal-echo work adds a third answer the positional question could not reach on its own. 3:7's אַל־תְּהִי חָכָם בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("do not be wise in your own eyes") is a prohibition addressed to a son who is present to hear it. It needs a place in the book where the son is *not* present — where the formula can be turned round into the third person and applied to four men who were never warned. That place is here, and it is the only place in the book where it happens four times `[T]`.

#### Immediate context

**Before.** 24:23–34 — the appendix to the words of the wise: partiality in judgement, the order of work (build the house last), the lying witness, the refusal of retaliation (24:29, לֹא־אֶעֱשֶׂה־לּוֹ כַּאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה לִי, "I will do to him as he has done to me" — rejected), and the sluggard's field. **24:29's rejection of retaliation sits eighteen verses before 25:21–22's command to feed the enemy, and the two are the book's only sustained treatment of the subject** `[T]`. The seam is not a change of topic.

**After.** 30:1 — דִּבְרֵי אָגוּר בִּן־יָקֶה הַמַּשָּׂא ("the words of Agur son of Jakeh, the oracle"), and with it a sage who confesses he has no wisdom (30:2–3) and asks who has ascended to heaven and come down (30:4). **The collection that withheld the name is followed immediately by the two verses in which the book most nearly asks for one** `[T]`. In the Greek tradition it is not: see Tool 14.

**Section and book.** 25:1–29:27 is one of the book's seven headed sections and the second of its two large sentence collections, 138 verses against 375. Its heading is the last in the book to name Solomon `[T]`.

### 3. Structure

**What the transmitted layout gives.** The Leningrad layout marks eight paragraph breaks inside the collection — after 25:13, 25:20, 26:21, 27:22, 28:4, 28:16, 29:17 and 29:27 `[T]`, WLC. That is one break every 17 verses, against one every 94 in 10:1–22:16 (four markers in 375 verses) `[T]`. **This collection is the most heavily divided stretch of sentence-material in the book as Leningrad prints it.**

Two of the eight repay attention and one does not:

- The break after **28:4** falls at the last verse of the sixty-five-verse divine silence; 28:5 names יְהוָה. **The layout marks the exact point at which God is named again** `[T]`.
- The break after **29:27** is the collection's close, and 29:27 is a symmetrical two-clause verse — תּוֹעֲבַת צַדִּיקִים אִישׁ עָוֶל וְתוֹעֲבַת רָשָׁע יְשַׁר־דָּרֶךְ ("an unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, and he who is upright in the way is an abomination to the wicked") — in which the second clause reverses the first term for term `[T]`. The collection ends by handing the reader both verdicts and refusing to adjudicate between them.
- The break after **25:13** does not fall at any boundary the vocabulary marks, and I can find no argument it carries. It is reported and left.

**`[unchecked — apparatus spread]`** on all eight. *Parashoth* are a feature of the manuscript's layout, not of the composition, and the corpus holds one manuscript tradition and prints them without variants: it cannot show how widely any of these is attested. None of the three observations above may enter the book overview, or a sermon's load-bearing structure, until a library pass on the manuscript spread has been run. Confidence capped at moderate.

**What the vocabulary gives — the two-collection division.** This is the structural finding, and it does not depend on the apparatus at all. Six measures, each computed independently against the WLC:

**The proposed internal structure.** Derived from the vocabulary before the layout was consulted:

- **25:2–7 — the court.** מֶלֶךְ ("king") in 25:2, 3, 5, 6 — four of the collection's seven king-verses in six verses, and the only cluster `[T]`. Opens with God and kings side by side (25:2) and ends with a man being moved down the table (25:7).
- **25:8–20 — speech, neighbour and the wrong word.** רֵעַ ("neighbour") at 25:8, 9, 17, 18; the quarrel, the secret, the word fitly spoken, the faithless messenger, the song sung to a heavy heart.
- **25:21–28 — the enemy, the wind, the city without a wall.** Contains the collection's one divine name before ch. 28 (25:22) and its one צַדִּיק (25:26).
- **26:1–12 — the fool.** כְּסִיל ("fool") in eleven of twelve verses (26:1, 3–12) `[T]`. **Bracketed by an inclusio:** 26:1 לֹא־נָאוֶה לִכְסִיל כָּבוֹד ("honour is not fitting for a fool") and 26:8 כֵּן־נוֹתֵן לִכְסִיל כָּבוֹד ("so is he who gives honour to a fool"), with 26:12 closing the block by putting a man *below* the fool `[T]`.
- **26:13–16 — the sluggard.** עָצֵל ("sluggard") in all four verses, and in no other verse of the collection `[T]`. Four of the book's fourteen.
- **26:17–28 — the quarreller and the liar.** Strife, the pretended joke, the whisperer, the smooth lips, the pit dug.
- **27:1–22 — the friend, and the self.** Boasting, another's praise, open rebuke, the friend nearer than a brother, iron sharpening iron, the crucible for a man's praise. **27:11 stands here: the last בְּנִי in the book.**
- **27:23–27 — the flocks.** The collection's only sustained multi-verse poem: five verses on a single subject with a continuous argument (כִּי, "for", at 27:24) `[T]`. In a book of one-verse sentences this is an anomaly worth preaching.
- **28:1–29:27 — the righteous, the wicked, the ruler and the poor.** Held together by a four-verse frame: 28:12, 28:28, 29:2, 29:16, each on what happens to a people when the righteous or the wicked come up `[T]`.

**Three nested silences, and they all end inside chapter 28.** Measured as the longest run of consecutive verses in which a vocabulary is wholly absent `[T]`, WLC:

| Vocabulary | Longest silence | Verses |
|---|---|---|
| מֶלֶךְ + מָשַׁל + נָגִיד (ruler of any kind) | 25:7 – 28:14 | **91** |
| יְהוָה (the divine name) | 25:23 – 28:4 | **65** |
| צַדִּיק + רָשָׁע (the book's governing antithesis) | 25:27 – 27:27 | **57** |

**Chapters 26 and 27 fall entirely inside all three.** A reader who opens the collection meets a king, God and the righteous/wicked antithesis within twenty-six verses, then hears none of the three for two whole chapters, and gets all three back in chapter 28. This is the two-collection division arriving from a fourth direction, and it is the single most economical way to state this collection's shape.

`[S: superseded run]` **on the framing, `[T]` on every figure.** The observation that the three silences nest is not mine: it is the one-line description the archived August file carries in `descriptions.tsv`, which I met at the filing stage after the text-work was complete. I have verified all three figures independently against the corpus — including the 91, which is the gap on the *combined* ruler vocabulary and not on מֶלֶךְ alone (that gap is 108) — and the framing is credited rather than absorbed.

**The 28:12 / 28:28 bracket.** 28:12 and 28:28 share 71% of their consonantal skeleton and both turn on בְּקוּם רְשָׁעִים ("when the wicked rise") `[T]`, WLC. 28:28's closing verb יִרְבּוּ צַדִּיקִים ("the righteous increase") is picked up at once by 29:2's בִּרְבוֹת צַדִּיקִים ("when the righteous increase") — a catchword across the chapter division `[T]`. *BHS* independently links the two verses, proposing that 28:12's יְחֻפַּשׂ be read as a hithpael by analogy with 28:28 (apparatus 28:12ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`.

### 4. Linking Words

Sentence-collections run on juxtaposition, not on connectives, and most of this one has none. Four exceptions matter.

- **כִּי ("for") at 25:7, 25:22, 26:25, 27:1, 27:24** — the collection's only grounding conjunctions. Two of them do real work. **25:22's כִּי** grounds the command to feed the enemy in a consequence (coals, and the LORD's repayment) — and it is the clause Paul keeps while dropping what follows it (see Tool 11). **27:24's כִּי** grounds the whole flock-poem: *know the condition of your flocks* **because** *riches are not forever* `[T]`.
- **פֶּן ("lest") at 25:8, 25:10, 25:16, 25:17, 26:4, 26:5** — six occurrences, all in 25–26, and two of them are 26:4 and 26:5. The particle is the collection's characteristic warning connective and it disappears entirely after 26:5 `[T]`.
- **26:4–5, the one place the collection argues with itself.** אַל־תַּעַן כְּסִיל כְּאִוַּלְתּוֹ **פֶּן**־תִּשְׁוֶה־לּוֹ גַם־אָתָּה ("do not answer a fool according to his folly, lest you also be like him") / עֲנֵה כְסִיל כְּאִוַּלְתּוֹ **פֶּן**־יִהְיֶה חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו ("answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes"). **The two verses are built on the same skeleton and differ only at the verb's polarity and in the content of the פֶּן clause.** The logic is not a contradiction and not a paradox: each verse states a *risk*, and the two risks are incompatible, so the reader is required to judge which is live. This is the collection's method made explicit in two verses `[T]`.
- **Comparative מִן at 25:7, 24, 25; 26:12, 16; 27:3, 5, 10; 28:6; 29:20** — the "better … than" and "more than" constructions. Ten verses; the collection's second-commonest connective after כְּ. Three of them open with טוֹב ("better"): 25:24, 27:5, 28:6 `[T]`.

### 5. Parallels

**What is *not* here is the finding.** Proverbs 10:1–15:33 is the book's antithesis engine: 29.3% of its verses set a fixed opposition pair against itself across the וְ ("and, but") — righteous/wicked, wise/fool, good/evil, truth/falsehood. In 25–27 the figure is 4.8% `[T]`, WLC. *(The measure: a verse counts if it contains both members of one of ten fixed lemma pairs — צַדִּיק/רָשָׁע, חָכָם/כְּסִיל, חָכָם/אֱוִיל, טוֹב/רַע, צְדָקָה/רִשְׁעָה, יָשָׁר/עִקֵּשׁ, תָּמִים/רָשָׁע, חָכָם/עָצֵל, אֱמֶת/שֶׁקֶר, דַּעַת/אִוֶּלֶת. It is a proxy for antithetic parallelism, not a substitute for reading; it under-counts oppositions carried by a phrase rather than a lexeme, and I report it as a gradient rather than as a census. It reproduces the book overview's independently derived figure for 16:1–22:16 to within a tenth of a point.)*

**What replaces it — emblematic parallelism.** The collection's dominant figure is the *emblem*: an image in the first colon, its application in the second, with or without an explicit כֵּן ("so"). Seven verses carry the explicit form — 26:1, 2, 8, 19; 27:8, 19; 28:2 — against eighteen in the whole book `[T]`, WLC. A further fourteen carry the image without the marker, opening with a comparative כְּ and leaving the reader to supply the "so": 25:13; 26:11, 18, 23, 27; 27:3, 26; 29:11 among them `[T]`.

Three worth the preacher's attention:

- **26:1** — כַּשֶּׁלֶג בַּקַּיִץ וְכַמָּטָר בַּקָּצִיר כֵּן לֹא־נָאוֶה לִכְסִיל כָּבוֹד ("like snow in summer and like rain in harvest, so honour is not fitting for a fool"). The emblem is not *unpleasant* but *out of season* — snow and rain are good things arriving when they ruin the crop. The verse is about timing, not about weather `[T]`.
- **27:19** — כַּמַּיִם הַפָּנִים לַפָּנִים כֵּן לֵב־הָאָדָם לָאָדָם ("as in water face reflects face, so the heart of man reflects man"). The Hebrew has no verb in either colon; the parallel *is* the argument, and both its halves are ambiguous in the same way — a man sees himself in water, and sees himself in another man, or a man's heart answers to another's as reflections answer. The ambiguity is in the Hebrew and both English versions close it in the same direction `[T]`.
- **26:7 and 26:9** — דַּלְיוּ שֹׁקַיִם מִפִּסֵּחַ **וּמָשָׁל בְּפִי כְסִילִים** / חוֹחַ עָלָה בְיַד־שִׁכּוֹר **וּמָשָׁל בְּפִי כְסִילִים** ("like the legs which hang down from the lame, so is a proverb in the mouth of fools" / "like a thorn which falls into the hand of a drunkard, so is a proverb in the mouth of fools"). **The same second colon, twice, two verses apart, with two different emblems** `[T]`, WLC — the collection's tightest internal repetition, and the only place in Proverbs where one half-line is given two pictures. The subject is a מָשָׁל ("proverb") in the wrong mouth: the book, in the middle of its longest run of proverbs, twice warns about what happens when the wrong person quotes one.

**A deliberate break in the pattern.** 26:1–12 runs the fool through twelve consecutive verses, eleven of them naming כְּסִיל. 26:12 breaks the frame: רָאִיתָ אִישׁ חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ ("do you see a man wise in his own eyes? there is more hope for a fool than for him"). The block's subject is dropped as the *comparator*. Whatever has been said about the fool for eleven verses is now the good news `[T]`.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**N/A** — this is a sentence-collection with no narrative frame and no authorial voice standing outside events. 25:1's superscription is the nearest thing to an editorial intrusion in the collection, and it is treated under Tools 1 and 2 where it belongs. The one first-person voice, 27:11's אָשִׁיבָה חֹרְפִי דָבָר ("that I may reply to him who reproaches me"), is a speaking father inside the material, not a narrator above it.

### 7. Vocabulary

#### 7a. The absences — and each one has passed a positive control

This collection is defined as much by the words it does not use as by the words it does. Every nil return below was tested by running the identical lemma search against the whole book: each returned a substantial number of verses elsewhere, which proves the search was working and the absence is evidence rather than a broken query `[T]`, WLC.

| Word | Verses in Proverbs | Verses in 25:1–29:27 |
|---|---|---|
| מוּסָר ("discipline, instruction") | 30 | **0** (the verb יסר twice, 29:17, 19) |
| יִרְאָה ("fear", noun) | 14 | **0** |
| יָרֵא ("to fear", verb/adj.) | 4 | **0** |
| בִּינָה ("understanding") | 14 | **0** |
| לֵץ ("scoffer") | 17 | **0** |
| עָנִי ("afflicted") | 8 | **0** |
| אֶבְיוֹן ("needy") | 4 | **0** |
| דַּעַת ("knowledge") | 39 | **1** (29:7) |
| חָכְמָה ("wisdom") | 38 | 3 |
| תְּבוּנָה ("understanding") | 19 | 1 |

**The book's motto is not here.** יִרְאַת יְהוָה ("the fear of the LORD") — the phrase of 1:7, 9:10, and fourteen further verses — does not occur once in these 138 verses, in either of its elements `[T]`. Nor does מוּסָר, the second noun of the book's purpose statement (1:2). The vocabulary the prologue spent nine chapters building is, in the collection the book's own heading attributes to Solomon and credits to Hezekiah's men, almost entirely withdrawn. *High confidence — ten lemma searches, each positively controlled.*

**The scoffer's absence is the one the overview did not note.** לֵץ ("scoffer") is a Proverbs signature: seventeen verses, from 1:22 to 24:9, and the third member of the book's fool-triad alongside כְּסִיל and אֱוִיל. He is absent from the whole collection `[T]`. The fool and the sluggard are here in force; the man who *mocks* correction is not. What the collection has kept is the figure who can be shown a case; what it has dropped is the figure who refuses to look.

#### 7b. The words it does keep, and where they cluster

- **חָכָם ("wise")** — 8 verses (25:12; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11; 29:8, 9, 11). **In four of the eight it is a false claim**, the חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו formula. This is the only stretch of Proverbs where the word for "wise" is, more often than not, ironic `[T]`.
- **כְּסִיל ("fool")** — 14 verses, eleven of them in 26:1–12 `[T]`.
- **עָצֵל ("sluggard")** — 4 verses, all in 26:13–16 `[T]`.
- **תּוֹרָה ("law, instruction")** — 4 verses (28:4 twice, 28:7, 9; 29:18), a third of the book's twelve. Between 7:2 and 28:4 the word occurs once in the whole book (13:14) `[T]`. **The vocabulary of תּוֹרָה all but vanishes from the Solomonic collections and returns in a cluster at the end of this one.** And it returns *absolute* — not תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ ("your mother's teaching", 1:8) or תּוֹרָתִי ("my teaching", 3:1), but תּוֹרָה with no possessor. The overview flags that the book never says this is Israel's law `[S: overview]`; what the corpus adds is that the unpossessed form is concentrated here, at the point where the personal teacher has just been withdrawn. *Moderate-to-high confidence on the significance; the count itself is high.*
- **בָּטַח ("to trust")** — 4 verses, all in 28–29 (28:1, 25, 26; 29:25), and **all four in the half where the divine name returns** `[T]`. Two name יְהוָה as the object (28:25; 29:25); 28:26 names the wrong object — בּוֹטֵחַ בְּלִבּוֹ הוּא כְסִיל ("he who trusts in his own heart is a fool"), which is 3:5's בְּטַח אֶל־יְהוָה בְּכָל־לִבֶּךָ ("trust in the LORD with all your heart") turned inside out `[T]`; see Tool 11, Move 4.
- **סתר ("to conceal")** — the verb stands in five verses of Proverbs and **four of them are here**: 25:2 (God conceals); 27:5 (love concealed); 27:12 (the prudent hides himself); 28:28 (men hide themselves) `[T]`. The cognate noun סֵתֶר adds 25:23, לְשׁוֹן סָתֶר ("a secret tongue") `[T]`. Set beside the four חקר ("search out") verses (25:2, 3, 27; 28:11) and the גלה ("uncover") verses (25:9; 26:26; 27:5), **concealment and disclosure is the collection's most evenly distributed theme** — present in every chapter, stated programmatically in its first sentence, and carried to the end.
- **שָׁפֵל / שׁפל ("low, to bring low")** — three verses in the whole of Proverbs, **two of them here, and they are the collection's first and last** `[T]`: 25:7 מֵהַשְׁפִּילְךָ לִפְנֵי נָדִיב ("than for you to be placed lower before the prince") and 29:23 גַּאֲוַת אָדָם תַּשְׁפִּילֶנּוּ וּשְׁפַל־רוּחַ יִתְמֹךְ כָּבוֹד ("a man's pride will bring him low, but a humble spirit will obtain honour"). The collection opens with a man moved down a table and closes with pride moved down by itself. *High confidence — three occurrences in the book, two of them framing this unit.* Both are read together by Luke 14:7–11; see Tool 11.
- **הלל ("to praise, to boast")** — 4 verses (25:14; 27:1, 2; 28:4) `[T]`. Three of them prohibit self-praise or expose a false one; 27:2 supplies the rule — יְהַלֶּלְךָ זָר וְלֹא־פִיךָ ("let another praise you, and not your own mouth"). Read with 27:21, מַצְרֵף לַכֶּסֶף וְכוּר לַזָּהָב וְאִישׁ לְפִי מַהֲלָלוֹ ("the crucible for silver and the furnace for gold, and a man by his praise"), the collection has a small self-assessment theory: the verdict must come from outside, and being praised is itself a test.

#### 7c. Proper nouns — the inventory, and it is almost empty

Run against the WLC morphology: the entire collection contains **three proper names, all in 25:1** — שְׁלֹמֹה ("Solomon"), חִזְקִיָּה ("Hezekiah"), יְהוּדָה ("Judah") — plus the divine name in six verses and שְׁאוֹל ("Sheol") at 27:20 `[T]`. **No person, place, people or nation is named anywhere in the 137 verses after the superscription.** By contrast the prologue names David and Israel (1:1) and Egypt (7:16), and the chapters that follow name Agur, Jakeh, Ithiel and Lemuel (30:1; 31:1) `[T]`.

*What this does:* the canonical-history check (name-hyperlinks, outstanding words, name-wordplay) has almost nothing to work on and returns effectively nil, **and that nil is the finding**. The collection has removed every hook by which a case could be tied to a particular history. The cases are anonymous on purpose.

**One name-hyperlink does operate, and only one.** חִזְקִיָּה at 25:1 is the sole canonical hyperlink in the collection, and it is load-bearing for the sermon rather than for the exegesis. What a reader carries into 25:1 depends entirely on whether he has read 2 Kings 18–20 / Isaiah 36–39, and Proverbs supplies none of it. The load-the-story rule therefore applies with a warning attached: **whatever the preacher brings from Hezekiah's reign is brought, not found.** `[I]` at best, and the sermon must say so. *(This project has a completed synoptic dig on that material, `dig-deeper-hezekiah-isaiah-36-2kings-18`, which is the place to go for the story rather than to this report.)*

#### 7d. Two words that carry more than they look

- **הֶעְתִּיקוּ (25:1)** — treated under Tool 1. The hiphil of עתק elsewhere means to move or remove (Gen 12:8; Job 9:5; 32:15). Here it is the act of copying a text out. `[I]`, high confidence.
- **גֶּחָלִים (25:22)** — "coals". The image is disputed and the dispute is old: a punishment (burning shame or judgement heaped on the enemy), or an Egyptian penitential rite in which a man carried a brazier on his head as a public sign of repentance `[S]`, moderate confidence — the rite is attested in secondary literature, not in this text, and the verse itself supplies no gloss. **What the verse does supply is the second colon, and it is decisive for the direction**: וַיהוָה יְשַׁלֶּם־לָךְ ("and the LORD will repay *you*"). Whatever happens to the enemy's head, the promised outcome attaches to the doer, not to the target. See Tool 11.

### 8. Translations

The NASB95 (study text) and the NIV84 (declared pulpit text for this engagement) agree across most of the collection, and where they agree nothing is reported. Five divergences bear on findings.

**Pulpit divergence note (NIV84).**

1. **29:7 — the collection's only דַּעַת, rendered away by both.** Hebrew: יֹדֵעַ צַדִּיק דִּין דַּלִּים רָשָׁע לֹא־יָבִין דָּעַת. NASB95: "The righteous is concerned for the rights of the poor, The wicked does not understand such concern." NIV84: "The righteous care about justice for the poor, but the wicked have no such concern." **Both versions resolve דָּעַת into "such concern", and NIV84 additionally drops "understand".** The congregation will not hear the word "knowledge" anywhere in this collection. The rendering is defensible — דָּעַת here is very likely the *knowing* of the first colon, resumed — but the preacher who wants headline 3 must read the verse himself, because neither text in his hand will give it to him. **This is the single most important translation note in the report.**
2. **28:12 and 28:28 — two different Hebrew verbs levelled into one English phrase.** 28:12 has יְחֻפַּשׂ אָדָם (from חפשׂ, "to be searched out"); 28:28 has יִסָּתֵר אָדָם (from סתר, "to hide oneself") `[T]`, WLC. NASB95 gives "men hide themselves" in both; NIV84 gives "men go into hiding" and "people go into hiding". The levelling makes the bracket *more* audible, which helps the sermon — but it costs the link between 28:12's verb and the collection's חקר/searching theme, and it costs 28:28's link to 25:2's הַסְתֵּר. Say the bracket from the English; say the root-play only from the Hebrew.
3. **28:19 — the ironic doubling survives in NASB95 and is halved in NIV84.** Hebrew: עֹבֵד אַדְמָתוֹ **יִשְׂבַּע**־לָחֶם וּמְרַדֵּף רֵקִים **יִשְׂבַּע**־רִישׁ ("he who works his land will be *satisfied* with bread; he who chases empty things will be *satisfied* with poverty") — the same verb twice `[T]`. NASB95: "plenty of food … poverty in plenty", which keeps it. NIV84: "abundant food … his fill of poverty", which keeps one half. The joke is that the idler gets exactly what the worker gets: a full measure.
4. **26:12 / 29:20 — the identical second colon, and the different first verbs.** The Hebrew B-line is word-for-word identical in both verses: תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ ("there is more hope for a fool than for him") `[T]`, WLC. The A-lines open with different verbs of seeing — רָאִיתָ at 26:12, חָזִיתָ at 29:20. Both English versions render both openings identically ("Do you see a man…"), which makes the pair audible and hides the variation. That is the right trade for the pulpit; note only that the Hebrew varies the verb and repeats the verdict, not the reverse.
5. **25:27b — do not build anything on it.** Hebrew: וְחֵקֶר כְּבֹדָם כָּבוֹד, which as it stands says something like "and the searching out of their glory is glory". NASB95 supplies a negative from the first colon — "Nor is it glory to search out one's own glory"; NIV84 does the same — "nor is it honourable to seek one's own honour". **Neither negative is in the Hebrew.** *BHS* marks the colon **corrupt** (`crrp`, apparatus 25:27^{b–b}) and records the Greek's quite different τιμᾶν δὲ χρὴ λόγους ἐνδόξους ("but one ought to honour glorious words"), with two conjectural restorations `[S: BHS]`. The verse is a landmine; see Preaching Pitfalls.

**Ancient versions check — and the Three-Way Triage.** Every Hebrew/Greek divergence in this collection is classified below. None is resolved by a preference for either text.

*Category 1 — translation loss (a fact about the Hebrew that no translation carries).*
- The חקר chain (25:2, 3, 27; 28:11) breaks in Greek: 25:2's חֲקֹר becomes τιμᾷ ("honours"), 25:3's אֵין חֵקֶר becomes ἀνεξέλεγκτος ("unsearchable"), 28:11's יַחְקְרֶנּוּ becomes καταγνώσεται ("will condemn him"). Four Hebrew occurrences of one root, four unrelated Greek words `[T]`, Swete. The English versions break it too. **A finding that rests on this chain can only be stated from the Hebrew, and the count is "in the Hebrew".**
- The סתר chain (25:2; 27:5, 12; 28:28) likewise: κρύπτει, μεμιγμένης, κρύπτονται, στένουσιν — and 28:28's Greek has no hiding verb at all `[T]`, Swete.
- The יִשְׂבַּע doubling at 28:19 does not survive into Greek.

*Category 2 — substantive variants (weighed on the evidence; no default for the Masoretic text).*
- **25:21 — the objects.** The Hebrew reads הַאֲכִלֵהוּ **לָחֶם** … הַשְׁקֵהוּ **מָיִם** ("feed him *bread* … give him *water* to drink"). The Greek, Syriac, Vulgate **and Rom 12:20** read simply ψώμιζε αὐτόν … πότιζε αὐτόν, with no objects; *BHS* lists Rom 12:20 among the witnesses and proposes deleting both nouns (apparatus 25:21ᵃ, ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* the shorter reading is supported by four independent witnesses including the NT, and the longer is easily explained as a natural filling-out. But the nouns are also exactly what a Greek translator would find redundant. **I leave it open and note only that Paul's text lacked them.** *Moderate confidence; the variant does not affect the sense.*
- **26:2 — ketiv/qere, and the senses are opposite.** The WLC prints the unpointed ketiv לא, with the qere לוֹ in the Masorah. Read as לֹא: קִלְלַת חִנָּם **לֹא** תָבֹא ("an undeserved curse does **not** come"). Read as לוֹ: "comes **to him**." *BHS* directs that the ketiv be read, with the Greek and the Targum (apparatus 26:2ᵃ) `[S: BHS]`. NASB95 ("does not alight") and NIV84 ("does not come to rest") both follow the ketiv. *Assessment:* the ketiv has the versions and the sense of the emblem — a bird that never lands — behind it. **High confidence in the ketiv; but note in the sermon that the pointed tradition preserved the alternative, because the pastoral difference is large.**
- **26:12 — first person or second.** The Cairo Codex, a few manuscripts and the Greek read רָאִיתִי / εἶδον ("*I* have seen") for the MT's רָאִיתָ ("*you* have seen"); *BHS* records it (apparatus 26:12ᵃ) `[S: BHS]`. Swete's 29:20, by contrast, has ἐὰν ἴδῃς ("if you see") — second person `[T]`. **So in the Greek the pair 26:12 / 29:20 is split between a sage's testimony and a reader's question, while in the Hebrew both are addressed to the reader.** *Assessment:* the MT's uniformity is what makes the pair a pair, and the Greek's variation is the easier reading to have arisen. Moderate-to-high confidence for the MT.
- **27:20 — a Greek plus inside the Hebrew's silence.** Swete adds, after 27:20a: βδέλυγμα Κυρίῳ στηρίζων ὀφθαλμόν, καὶ οἱ ἀπαίδευτοι ἀκρατεῖς γλώσσῃ ("an abomination to the Lord is he who fixes the eye, and the untaught are unrestrained in tongue"). *BHS* reports it and reconstructs תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה עֹצֶה עֵינָיו וּכְסִילִים עַזֵּי לָשׁוֹן (apparatus 27:20ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* **this is the one variant in the collection with a preaching consequence, because it falls inside the sixty-five-verse divine silence and shortens it.** Its first colon is close to 16:30 and could be a harmonising expansion; equally a line lost by homoeoteleuton is possible. Genuinely undecided. **The sermon should state the silence as a feature of the text the congregation holds, and should not claim it as the collection's original design.** *Moderate confidence that the MT is shorter here for a reason; no confidence at all about which reason.*
- **29:23 — the Greek supplies a subject.** Hebrew: וּשְׁפַל־רוּחַ יִתְמֹךְ כָּבוֹד ("and a humble spirit will obtain honour") — agentless. Swete: τοὺς δὲ ταπεινόφρονας ἐρείδει δόξῃ **Κύριος** ("but the Lord upholds the lowly in mind with glory") `[T]`. *Assessment:* the Greek may reflect a Hebrew text with the divine name, or a translator making explicit what he thought obvious. **Either way it is the second place where the Greek names God in this collection and the Hebrew does not.** Moderate confidence that the Hebrew is original, since agentless passives are the collection's habit.

*Category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text (stands whichever text is prior).*
- **25:21–22 → Rom 12:20**, verbatim from the Greek including ἄνθρακας πυρὸς σωρεύσεις ("you will heap coals of fire"). Reported under Tool 11.
- **29:23 → Luke 14:11 / Matt 23:12** by way of the ταπειν- vocabulary the Greek carries. Reported under Tool 11.
- **26:11 → 2 Pet 2:22** — and this one is the exception. 2 Peter's κύων ἐπιστρέψας ἐπὶ τὸ ἴδιον ἐξέραμα does **not** reproduce Swete's ὥσπερ κύων ὅταν ἐπέλθῃ ἐπὶ τὸν ἑαυτοῦ ἔμετον: different verb, different noun for the vomit `[T]`, Swete + SBLGNT. He calls it τὸ τῆς ἀληθοῦς **παροιμίας** ("the true proverb") — the Greek book's own title — and pairs it with a washed sow that is in no biblical book. **This is an independent Greek rendering of Prov 26:11, not a quotation of the Septuagint.** See Book-Overview Tensions.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The dominant register is cool.** Not cold — the collection is vivid, often funny, and occasionally brutal — but it declines to raise its voice. There is no direct plea, no "my son, listen" after 27:11, no lament, no doxology, and no verse that tells the reader how to feel about what he has just read. The observations are laid down and left.

**What carries it.**

- **The withdrawal of the second person.** 12.0% of verses in 25–27 carry an imperative; 1.8% in 28–29 `[T]`. By ch. 29 the reader is being shown, not addressed.
- **Domestic and agricultural detail, unglossed.** Snow in harvest (25:13), the door on its hinge (26:14), the hand in the dish and too tired to lift it back (26:15), the neighbour who blesses you loudly early in the morning (27:14), goats' milk enough for the household (27:27), iron on iron (27:17). The specificity is the tone.
- **Comedy, and it is deadpan.** 26:13–16 is a four-verse sketch: the sluggard says there is a lion in the street; he turns on his bed as a door on its hinge; he has buried his hand in the dish and cannot face the return journey; and he is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who can answer sensibly. The joke lands entirely without commentary, and the fourth verse turns it from comedy into the collection's theme `[T]`.
- **A sudden warmth, once.** 27:5–10 is the collection's one stretch of affection: open rebuke better than hidden love, faithful the wounds of a friend, ointment and perfume rejoice the heart, do not forsake your own friend or your father's friend, better a near neighbour than a far brother. **27:11's בְּנִי sits in the middle of it, and it is the last time the book says it** `[T]`. If there is a place where this collection's voice softens, it is here, and it is immediately before the vocative is retired.
- **A sudden chill, once.** 29:1 — אִישׁ תּוֹכָחוֹת מַקְשֶׁה־עֹרֶף פֶּתַע יִשָּׁבֵר וְאֵין מַרְפֵּא ("a man who hardens his neck after much reproof will suddenly be broken, and there is no remedy"). פֶּתַע ("suddenly") and אֵין מַרְפֵּא ("no remedy") are the collection's hardest words, and they open its last chapter `[T]`.

**Soundtrack.** For 25–27, a chamber piece — a small number of instruments, each phrase complete in itself, nothing developed. For 28–29, something with a pulse underneath: the same four-beat figure returning (28:12, 28:28, 29:2, 29:16), a little louder each time.

- **Concealment and disclosure.** 25:2 (God conceals, kings search); 25:9 (do not reveal another's secret); 25:23 (the secret tongue); 26:24–26 (the hater disguises with his lips, and his wickedness is revealed in the assembly); 27:5 (open rebuke against concealed love); 27:12 (the prudent hides himself); 28:13 (he who covers his transgressions will not prosper); 28:28 (men hide themselves) `[T]`. Nine verses across all five chapters.
- **When the wicked rise.** 28:12, 28:28, 29:2, 29:16 — four verses on the same subject, each with the verb of rising or increasing, two of them nearly identical `[T]`. Add 28:15 (a roaring lion and a rushing bear is a wicked ruler over a poor people) and 29:12 (a ruler who listens to falsehood — all his servants become wicked).
- **Eating, and stopping.** 25:16 (found honey? eat your fill and no more, lest you vomit); 25:17 (make your foot rare in your neighbour's house, lest he be full of you and hate you — the *same verb* שׂבע in consecutive verses, applied to honey and then to a person `[T]`); 25:27 (it is not good to eat much honey); 26:11 (the dog returns to its vomit); 27:7 (a sated soul tramples the honeycomb; to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet). **The honey/satiety thread runs five verses and turns on satiety as the enemy of appetite.**

**Near-repetition worth the preacher's eye.** 26:7 and 26:9 close with the identical colon וּמָשָׁל בְּפִי כְסִילִים and open with two different emblems (Tool 5). 26:12 and 29:20 close with the identical colon תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ and open with two different verbs of seeing (Tool 8). **In both pairs the verdict is fixed and the picture varies** — which is, in miniature, what the collection does with 138 cases.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources.** The book overview's headline finding governs here: Proverbs contains no citation formula, no marked quotation, and no appeal to an earlier book by name `[S: overview]`, and the present collection contains no proper name after 25:1, which removes the other principal carrier of allusion (Tool 7c). **There is no external OT citation to run the Triad on in the outward direction.** The Triad's work in this report is therefore (a) on the collection *as a source* for the New Testament, where it runs in full, and (b) Move 4, which is where the material actually lives.

#### The collection as a source — three New Testament uses

**Prov 25:21–22 → Romans 12:20** *(high confidence — verbatim quotation)*

*Source context.* 25:21–22 stands in the third block of the collection's first chapter, after the quarrel material (25:8–10) and the emblems of good and bad speech (25:11–20), and immediately before the north wind and the secret tongue (25:23). It is an imperative pair with a כִּי ("for") clause attached — the only commanded action in the collection that concerns an enemy, and one of only two treatments of retaliation in the book. The other is eighteen verses earlier, at 24:29: אַל־תֹּאמַר כַּאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה־לִי כֵּן אֶעֱשֶׂה־לּוֹ ("do not say, 'as he has done to me, so I will do to him'") `[T]`. **The source context is not an isolated maxim; it is the positive half of a pair whose negative half sits just over the collection seam.**

*Book usage.* Romans has been quoting Proverbs for four verses before it reaches this one, and this is the finding. Rom 12:16 ends μὴ γίνεσθε φρόνιμοι παρ' ἑαυτοῖς ("do not be wise in your own estimation") — Swete's Prov 3:7 reads μὴ ἴσθι φρόνιμος παρὰ σεαυτῷ `[T]`, Swete + SBLGNT. Rom 12:17 ends προνοούμενοι καλὰ ἐνώπιον πάντων ἀνθρώπων ("taking thought for what is good before all men") — Swete's Prov 3:4 reads προνοοῦ καλὰ ἐνώπιον Κυρίου καὶ ἀνθρώπων `[T]`. **So Rom 12:16–20 opens with Prov 3:7, continues with Prov 3:4, and closes with Prov 25:21–22** — three Proverbs texts in five verses, with Deut 32:35 between the last two. *High confidence on all three verbal matches; moderate-to-high that Paul is working from Proverbs deliberately rather than from shared moral idiom.*

And the two ends of Paul's Proverbs bracket are the two ends of *this collection's own* theme. Prov 3:7's prohibition is the verse this collection turns into four case studies (headline 4). Paul quotes the prohibition and then, four verses later, the collection that applies it.

*OT-to-OT.* Deut 32:35 and Prov 25:22 were already in conversation before Paul reached them, and they share a verb. Deut 32:35 LXX: ἐγὼ **ἀνταποδώσω** ("I will repay"). Swete's Prov 25:22: ὁ δὲ Κύριος **ἀνταποδώσει** σοι ἀγαθά ("and the Lord will repay you good things"). The Hebrew uses two different verbs (שׁלם at Prov 25:22, נקם/שׁלם at Deut 32:35) but the Greek uses one `[T]`, Swete. **Paul has two texts in front of him in which God repays, and they disagree about who receives the repayment.** He resolves it by amputation: he quotes Deuteronomy's repayment in full and cuts Proverbs' repayment off. What remains of Prov 25:22 is the coals; what is removed is the wage.

*What it adds.* Three things the collection cannot say on its own.
- Paul reads 25:21–22 as an **anti-retaliation text**, which is how its own context reads it (24:29) — so the New Testament and the collection agree, and the agreement is earned rather than imposed.
- Paul's cut settles the **direction of the coals** for a Christian reading, and settles it against the punitive sense: the clause he deletes is the one that promised the doer a return, and the sentence he substitutes is νίκα ἐν τῷ ἀγαθῷ τὸ κακόν ("overcome evil with good"). The good is no longer what you are paid; it is what you fight with. *High confidence on the omission and the substitution; moderate on the intention, since an omission can always be economy rather than argument.*
- The bracket Rom 12:16–20 makes between Prov 3:7 and Prov 25:21–22 is **exactly the bracket this collection makes internally** between the prohibition and its four case studies. That convergence was reached here from the Hebrew chains before Romans was opened.

**Prov 25:6–7 (with 29:23) → Luke 14:7–11** *(25:6–7 high confidence; 29:23 moderate — a family, not a single source)*

*Source context.* 25:6–7 closes the collection's opening court block. אַל־תִּתְהַדַּר לִפְנֵי־מֶלֶךְ וּבִמְקוֹם גְּדֹלִים אַל־תַּעֲמֹד כִּי טוֹב אֲמָר־לְךָ עֲלֵה הֵנָּה מֵהַשְׁפִּילְךָ לִפְנֵי נָדִיב ("do not claim honour before the king, and do not stand in the place of great men; for it is better that it be said to you, 'come up here', than for you to be placed lower before the prince"). It follows 25:5, where the wicked is removed from before the king so the throne may be established, and 25:2–3, where the king's heart is unsearchable. **The advice is not modesty as a virtue; it is prudence before a man whose mind you cannot read** `[T]`.

*Book usage.* Luke has not cited Proverbs earlier in the Gospel; the source is cold for Luke `[T]`, SBLGNT. But the verbal contact is specific. Swete's 25:7 reads κρεῖσσον γάρ σοι τὸ ῥηθῆναι **Ἀνάβαινε** πρὸς μέ, ἢ **ταπεινῶσαί** σε ἐν προσώπῳ δυνάστου. Luke 14:10: Φίλε, **προσανάβηθι** ἀνώτερον — and 14:11: ὁ ὑψῶν ἑαυτὸν **ταπεινωθήσεται** `[T]`. The invitation to come up, the humiliation before the great, the banquet setting, and the "better for you" logic are all in the Greek of Proverbs, and Luke's parable is a narrative expansion of them.

*OT-to-OT.* Luke 14:11's aphorism has a family behind it — Job 22:29; Ezek 21:31; and, in this collection, 29:23, גַּאֲוַת אָדָם תַּשְׁפִּילֶנּוּ וּשְׁפַל־רוּחַ יִתְמֹךְ כָּבוֹד, whose Greek supplies both the ταπειν- root and δόξα ("glory") `[T]`, Swete. **The point worth making is structural rather than lexical: 25:7 and 29:23 are two of only three occurrences of שׁפל in the whole of Proverbs, and they are this collection's first and last** `[T]`, WLC. Luke 14:7–11 attaches a parable built on the first to an aphorism in the family of the last. *Moderate confidence that Luke has this collection specifically in view for 14:11; high for 14:8–10 on 25:6–7.*

*What it adds.* It shows what the collection's cool observational method looks like when a teacher takes it up and presses it. Proverbs says: here is how a banquet works, and the lower place is the safer one. Luke's Jesus says the same and then reverses the frame — ὅταν κληθῇς ("when you are invited") becomes, four verses later, an invitation list of the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind, who cannot repay (14:13–14). **The collection's own prudence is honoured and then outgrown.**

**Prov 26:11 → 2 Peter 2:22** *(high confidence on the reference; the wording is not the Septuagint's)*

*Source context.* 26:11 stands tenth in the twelve-verse fool block, two verses before 26:12 turns the block's comparator on the self-wise man. כְּכֶלֶב שָׁב עַל־קֵאוֹ כְּסִיל שׁוֹנֶה בְאִוַּלְתּוֹ ("like a dog that returns to its vomit, a fool repeats his folly"). The verb שׁנה ("to repeat, to do again") is the point: the fool's defining act is *recurrence*, and the block's argument is that he cannot be reached by honour (26:1, 8), by a rod (26:3), by an answer or by silence (26:4–5), or by a message (26:6) `[T]`.

*Book usage.* 2 Peter has no other Proverbs citation `[T]`, SBLGNT.

*OT-to-OT.* None — the sow proverb 2 Peter pairs with it is not biblical `[T]`.

*What it adds.* Two things, and the second is a correction to this project's own generalisation. First, 2 Peter reads the dog-verse as describing **apostasy**, not mere foolishness — the ones who have escaped the defilements of the world and are entangled again (2:20). That is a legitimate extension of שׁנה, and it takes the emblem's force seriously. Second, **2 Peter does not quote the Greek text of Proverbs.** Swete has ὥσπερ κύων ὅταν ἐπέλθῃ ἐπὶ τὸν ἑαυτοῦ ἔμετον; 2 Peter has κύων ἐπιστρέψας ἐπὶ τὸ ἴδιον ἐξέραμα — a different verb and a different noun `[T]`. He introduces it as τὸ τῆς ἀληθοῦς παροιμίας ("the matter of the true proverb"), which is what one says of a saying in circulation, not of a text being cited. See Book-Overview Tensions.

**A fourth candidate, derived independently and reported at moderate confidence.** **Prov 27:1 → James 4:13–16.** Swete's 27:1: μὴ **καυχῶ** τὰ εἰς **αὔριον**, οὐ γὰρ **γινώσκεις** τί τέξεται ἡ ἐπιοῦσα. Jas 4:13: Σήμερον ἢ **αὔριον** πορευσόμεθα …; 4:14: οὐκ **ἐπίστασθε** τὸ τῆς **αὔριον** …; 4:16: νῦν δὲ **καυχᾶσθε** ἐν ταῖς ἀλαζονείαις ὑμῶν `[T]`, Swete + SBLGNT. Three lexical contacts — tomorrow, not knowing, boasting — in the same order, and James's passage is an expansion of exactly the situation Proverbs states in one line. *Moderate-to-high confidence.* A fourth contact is available and I rate it lower: Swete's 25:6 opens μὴ **ἀλαζονεύου** ἐνώπιον βασιλέως, and ἀλαζονεία occurs in the NT only at Jas 4:16 and 1 Jn 2:16 — but ἀλαζον- is common Hellenistic moral vocabulary and the link is *uncertain*. Move 1 only, per the significance rule.

#### Move 4 — internal echo *(required; this is where the collection's allusive work is)*

The opening unit (1:1–9:18) was held open throughout, per the standing rule.

**`Internal:` 3:7 → 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11.** *Answers §earlier. High confidence.* אַל־תְּהִי חָכָם בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("do not be wise in your own eyes") is the prohibition; the four here are the case studies, and 26:12 supplies the verdict (more hope for a fool). **Four of the book's five occurrences of the formula with חָכָם as the adjective fall in this collection** `[T]`, WLC, phrase-verified with maqqef, paseq and sof pasuq normalised. *What it adds:* the collection is not a miscellany with a theme running through it; it is the place the book chose to work out its governing concern, and it does so at exactly the point where the teacher has stopped speaking.

**`Internal:` 3:5 → 28:26.** *Answers §earlier; inverted. High confidence.* בְּטַח אֶל־יְהוָה בְּכָל־לִבֶּךָ ("trust in the LORD with all your heart", 3:5) against בּוֹטֵחַ בְּלִבּוֹ הוּא כְסִיל ("he who trusts in his own heart is a fool", 28:26) `[T]`, WLC — the same verb בטח, the same noun לֵב, and the preposition moved from God to the self. 28:25 and 29:25 restore the right object in the same two chapters. *What it adds:* the collection's one explicit naming of the wrong object of trust sits eight verses from its two namings of the right one.

**`Internal:` 25:2 → 28:11.** *Planted and resolved within the collection. High confidence.* The חקר chain (Tool 7b). *What it adds:* headline 4. The searching that the collection's first sentence gave to kings is exercised, at the collection's other end, by a poor man on a rich one.

**`Internal:` 1:7, 9:10 → the whole collection, by absence.** *Pattern-break absence — and the pattern is citable, so the observation is admissible.* The established pattern: יִרְאַת יְהוָה ("the fear of the LORD") opens the book (1:7), opens the second half of the prologue's argument (9:10), and stands in **fourteen verses in all** — 1:7, 29; 2:5; 8:13; 9:10; 10:27; 14:26, 27; 15:16, 33; 16:6; 19:23; 22:4; 23:17 `[T]`, WLC (the noun יִרְאָה, lemma 3374, with יְהוָה; 31:30 uses the adjective יְרֵאַת, lemma 3373, and is a different construction). **The last of the fourteen is 23:17 — thirty-four verses before this collection begins.** The reader hears the book's motto for the last time in the words of the wise, and then never again in Solomonic material. In 25:1–29:27 neither element occurs `[T]`. **Weight: moderate**, per the standing cap on absence arguments, and flagged for a commentary check. *What it adds:* the book's motto is not restated where a reader most needs it, which is precisely when he is being asked to judge cases alone.

**`Internal:` 6:10–11 // 24:33–34 and the cross-seam repetitions.** *Planted for later / already resolved.* Seven whole or partial bicola in this collection recur elsewhere in the book (Tool 10 and the doublet table under Convergent Findings). Six of the seven pair with 16:1–22:16 or 10:1–15:33; the collection has no doublet with 22:17–24:34 or with 1–9 `[T]`, WLC. *What it adds:* whatever the collections are, this one draws on the same circulating stock as the two Solomonic collections and not on the words of the wise.

**`Internal:` 20:9 → not answered here.** *Explicit nil, recorded.* The book's unanswered question — מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי ("who can say, 'I have cleansed my heart'?") — receives no answer in these 138 verses. The collection's four portraits of the self-wise man are its nearest approach, and they sharpen the question rather than resolve it. 30:12 is where it is picked up `[S: overview]`.

**Addressee differentiation.** Not applicable: the book opens by addressing one party (בְּנִי, "my son"), not several, so there is no differentiated opening to resolve. The single addressee's **disappearance** after 27:11 is reported under Tool 2 instead.

### 12. Genre

**Hebrew wisdom — the sentence collection (מָשָׁל), in its most concentrated form, with one embedded poem.**

The reading rules are the standard ones and they matter more here than anywhere else in the book. A מָשָׁל states how the world usually runs; it is not a promise, and the collection supplies its own counter-cases (25:26, a righteous man giving way before the wicked; 28:15, a wicked ruler over a poor people; 29:2, a people groaning). **Book-level trap 4 — "preaching the collections as an argument" — bites here harder than anywhere** `[S: overview]`, and the vocabulary evidence in Tool 3 is the honest basis for the boundaries this report proposes: they are lexical concentrations, not an argument the text is making.

Three genre notes specific to this collection.

- **The emblem is the dominant sub-form** (Tool 5), and an emblem is read by identification, not by deduction. The preacher's task with 26:14 is not to explain the hinge but to let the congregation see the man.
- **27:23–27 is not a sentence collection.** Five verses, one subject, a continuous argument with a grounding כִּי, and a second-person address that the surrounding material has abandoned `[T]`. It is a short didactic poem embedded in a sentence collection, and it should be preached as a unit or left alone — not mined for a verse.
- **26:4–5 is genre-teaching.** The pair does not resolve; it tells the reader what kind of book he is holding. Two true generalisations with incompatible applications, set side by side, with the judgement left to him. *If the sermon needs one verse to carry the collection's method, it is these two.*

### 13. Copycat

**Largely N/A** — this is wisdom, not narrative: there are no characters with histories to imitate or avoid, and the figures that appear (the fool, the sluggard, the whisperer, the rich man, the poor man with understanding) are types, not persons. Three notes are nevertheless worth making, because the descriptive/prescriptive distinction bites in an unusual way here.

- **Almost nothing in this collection is commanded.** Eleven verses in 138 carry a morphological imperative — 25:2, 7, 9, 16, 17, 21; 26:5; 27:11, 13, 23; 29:17 — and nine carry an אַל ("do not") prohibition — 25:6, 8, 9; 26:4, 25; 27:1, 2, 10; 28:17 `[T]`, WLC. **The two lists together make nineteen verses of direct address in 138, and seventeen of the nineteen are in 25–27.** The overwhelming majority of the material is *description*: this is what a fool is like; this is what happens when the wicked rise. **The temptation is therefore the reverse of the usual one** — not to prescribe what is merely described, but to convert an observation into a rule. 26:14 does not tell anyone to get out of bed; it shows a man on a hinge.
- **The nineteen addressed verses are the exception and should be preached as commands:** 25:6–9 (do not claim honour; do not go out hastily to argue; argue your case with your neighbour; do not reveal another's secret), 25:16–17 (eat your fill and no more; make your foot rare), 25:21 (feed your enemy), 26:4–5 (the pair), 26:25 (do not believe him), 27:1–2 (do not boast; let another praise you), 27:10 (do not forsake your friend), 27:11 (be wise, my son), 27:13 (take his garment), 27:23 (know the condition of your flocks), 28:17 (do not support him), 29:17 (discipline your son) `[T]`. **Seven of the nineteen concern speech or the mouth; three concern an enemy or a neighbour in trouble.**
- **26:4–5 cannot be obeyed as a rule at all**, and that is the point (Tool 12). It is descriptive of two risks, prescriptive of neither, and the reader is required to supply the judgement. Preaching it as a command in either direction destroys it.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** Attributed to Solomon (united monarchy, tenth century), transmitted by a named group under Hezekiah (Judah, late eighth century) — the second of those two is what the text reports `[T]`; both dates are reconstruction `[S]`. The collection contains no event, no historical reference, and no name after its heading (Tool 7c), so the timeline places the *collection* and can place nothing inside it.

**Canonical position (Phase 0.55).** Extracted from the book overview v2.0 rather than re-derived `[S: overview]`, with one addition this run contributes.

- **Section.** Ketuvim, third in the BHS order — Psalms, Job, **Proverbs**, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
- **Reading implication.** Proverbs follows the two books that press hardest on whether the world is morally legible, and answers them by handing the reader cases. **This collection is the sharpest form of that answer**, because it is the place where the book withdraws its categories and leaves him with the cases alone.
- **Presupposes.** Nothing this collection cites; see Tool 11. The book-level table stands `[S: overview]`.
- **Handoff.** Immediately to Agur's confession (30:2–4) and to the acrostic (31:10–31).
- **Neighbours.** Job before, Ruth after, at book level.

**The addition, and it bears on the series' shape: the Greek does not put this collection here.** Swete's Proverbs has no chapters 30 and 31 as separate units at all. The Hebrew's 30:1–14 stands after 24:22, and 30:15–33 with 31:1–9 after 24:34; **chapters 25–29 follow them, and 31:10–31 follows as Swete's 29:27–49** `[T]`, Swete — verified by reading the file's own chapter boundaries, and confirmed by *BHS* at 30:1ᵃ, which states the arrangement in as many words `[S: BHS]`.

The consequence: **in the Greek tradition the Hezekiah collection is the last of the book's collections, and the acrostic follows it immediately.** In the Hebrew, Agur and Lemuel stand between them. So the question "what does this collection hand on to?" has two answers depending on which Bible is in view — a sage who confesses he has no wisdom (Hebrew), or a woman whose works praise her in the gates (Greek). **Report both; resolve neither.** *High confidence on the fact of the divergence; the direction of dependence is not recoverable from either text and is left open.*

**Where the reader is, and what has happened since.** The cross. Three things that changes about reading this collection:

1. **The withheld name has been given.** The collection's method is to present cases with God almost unnamed and let the reader judge. This side of the resurrection, the reader knows the judge and has met him; the collection's reticence is no longer an absence but a deferral.
2. **The searching has been reversed.** 25:2 gives the searching-out of matters to kings, and 28:11 gives it to a poor man with understanding. 1 Cor 2:10 gives it to the Spirit, who πάντα ἐραυνᾷ ("searches all things"), including τὰ βάθη τοῦ θεοῦ ("the depths of God") — the very thing 25:2 said God conceals `[T]`, SBLGNT. *Moderate confidence that this is a deliberate canonical development; high that the two texts stand in the same field.*
3. **25:21–22 has been taken up and altered** (Tool 11). A Christian reads it through Rom 12:20 and cannot read it as a technique for winning.

### 15. Who Am I?

The collection has no characters, so the question becomes: which of these types is being held up as a mirror?

| Figure | Function | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **The man wise in his own eyes** (26:12; 28:11) | **Representative of the reader** | This is the identification the collection is engineering. 26:12's verdict — *there is more hope for a fool than for him* — is aimed at whoever is confident he is not the fool of the preceding eleven verses. The reader who gets to 26:12 feeling secure has failed the block. |
| **The fool** (26:1–11) | Warning, and a foil | He exists in this block chiefly to set up 26:12. Preaching 26:1–11 without 26:12 inverts the passage. |
| **The sluggard** (26:13–16) | Warning, ending in the mirror | Same move in four verses: comedy, comedy, comedy, then 26:16 — *wiser in his own eyes than seven men*. |
| **The king / the ruler** (25:2–7; 28:15, 16; 29:4, 12, 14) | Neither type of Christ nor example | The king here is a figure whose heart is unsearchable (25:3) and before whom one is prudent — and, in 28–29, one who may be a roaring lion (28:15) or may establish the land by justice (29:4, 14). **He is an environment, not a model.** |
| **The poor man with understanding** (28:11) and **the righteous who knows the cause of the poor** (29:7) | The nearest thing to a positive model, and it is not a heroic one | Neither is presented as achieving anything; each is presented as *seeing correctly* — the one sees through the rich man's self-estimate, the other sees the poor man's case. |
| **The friend who wounds** (27:6) | Example, and a rare one | נֶאֱמָנִים פִּצְעֵי אוֹהֵב ("faithful are the wounds of one who loves"). One of very few places in the collection where a person is commended without qualification. |

**The Moses-is-me check has an unusual shape here.** There is no hero to insert oneself into. The danger is the opposite: reading the collection as a gallery of other people. **The collection's own structure forbids it** — 26:12 and 28:11 exist to close that exit.

**Where Christ stands.** Not as a figure in the text; the collection contains none. See the Christological Reading extension.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the collection seeks.** To be judged by the cases rather than to judge them. The material is constructed so that the reader's verdicts are evidence about the reader: the emblem invites an identification, the two halves of 26:4–5 force a choice, and four times the collection describes a man who assessed himself and was wrong. **The intended response is not "now I know how to be wise" but "I cannot mark my own paper."**

**Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.**

*How it tells us to think.*
- That the world is legible enough to be read in cases, and that reading cases is a real competence, not a substitute for revelation.
- That self-assessment is the specific point at which competence fails. **The collection never says the four self-wise men are stupid; 26:16's sluggard and 28:11's rich man are not fools by the book's definitions.** They are people whose faculty for judging others is intact and whose faculty for judging themselves is not.
- That God's absence from the argument is not God's absence from the world. 25:2 says as much in the collection's first sentence: the concealing is God's *glory*.

*What to stop.*
- Grading yourself (26:5, 12, 16; 28:11).
- Boasting about tomorrow (27:1) and praising yourself (27:2).
- Taking revenge — including the refined form, where the coals are the revenge (25:21–22 with Rom 12:20).
- Covering your transgressions (28:13).

*What to start.*
- Letting somebody else do the assessing (27:2, 21) and welcoming the rebuke when it comes (27:5, 6; 29:1).
- Knowing the cause of the poor (29:7) — which the collection treats as the one form of דַּעַת ("knowledge") worth naming.
- Knowing well the condition of your flocks (27:23) — the collection's one sustained positive instruction, and it is about diligent attention to what is actually in front of you.

*The motive, and it must not be skipped.* The collection supplies almost no motive clauses — that is its method. The motives have to come from where the book itself puts them (1:7; 9:10; 3:5–7) and, for a Christian congregation, from Rom 12:19–21, which is where the New Testament tells us why we feed an enemy: because vengeance belongs to God and has been dealt with elsewhere. **A sermon that takes this collection's imperatives without importing their grounds will produce moralism by construction, because the collection deliberately withholds the grounds.**

**Four audiences.**
- *For me:* which of the four self-wise portraits is the one I would be least willing to be told I resemble? That reluctance is the diagnosis.
- *For a Christian friend:* 27:5–6 — open rebuke against concealed love, and the wounds of a friend. This collection is unusually direct about what real friendship costs.
- *For the church together:* 28:12, 28:28, 29:2, 29:16 — what happens to a people when the righteous or the wicked come up. The collection is more interested in public life than any other part of Proverbs, and 29:7 makes the treatment of the poor the test.
- *For an unbeliever:* 26:12's verdict is the sharpest evangelistic sentence in the book. It does not argue that you are a fool; it says there is more hope for one than for a man who is sure he is not.

**Prayer in response.** For honest self-knowledge rather than a good self-estimate; for the grace to receive an open rebuke; for eyes that see the cause of the poor; and thanks that the name withheld here has been given, and the question of 20:9 answered, by someone outside the book.

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**The hinge word: חקר ("to search out").** Six verses in Proverbs, four here (25:2, 3, 27; 28:11) `[T]`, WLC, lemma-verified. 25:2 puts the root in the collection's programmatic sentence and pairs it against its opposite, סתר ("to conceal"): כְּבֹד אֱלֹהִים הַסְתֵּר דָּבָר וּכְבֹד מְלָכִים חֲקֹר דָּבָר. Note the symmetry the English cannot keep — two identical objects (דָּבָר, "a matter") and two construct chains, כְּבֹד אֱלֹהִים / וּכְבֹד מְלָכִים, differing only in the second noun. The symmetry is visual and syntactic rather than morphological: the WLC parses הַסְתֵּר as a hiphil imperative and חֲקֹר as a qal infinitive construct `[T]`, so the balance the eye sees is not a balance of forms. **The verse is a balance, not a contrast:** concealing and searching are given the same word for what they are (כָּבוֹד, "glory") and the same object. Then 25:3 undercuts it — וְלֵב מְלָכִים אֵין חֵקֶר ("and the heart of kings is unsearchable") — so the searcher is himself unsearchable. And 28:11 completes the movement: יַחְקְרֶנּוּ, "he searches him out", with a poor man as subject.

**The one name of God that is not the name: אֱלֹהִים (25:2).** The generic noun occurs in five verses of Proverbs — 2:5, 2:17, 3:4, **25:2**, 30:9 `[T]`, WLC. Three are in the prologue and one is Agur's. **25:2 is the sole occurrence of אֱלֹהִים anywhere between 4:1 and 29:27** — that is, in the whole of the book's Solomonic material. *High confidence — one lemma search, positively controlled.* Where the collection's first sentence names God, it uses the word that does not invoke the covenant, and it pairs him with kings.

**The unpointed forms: ten ketiv readings in 138 verses.** Run mechanically — a word carrying Hebrew consonants and no combining marks at all — the collection yields 25:24 מדונים; 26:2 לא; 26:21 מדונים; 26:24 בשפתו; 27:10 ורעה; 27:15 מדונים; 27:20 ואבדה; 27:24 דור; 28:8 ובתרבית; 28:16 שנאי `[T]`, WLC. *BHS* documents each `[S: BHS]`. **This is an apparatus feature and is reported as one** — the WLC prints the ketiv consonants without vowels and leaves the qere to the Masorah, so any search spelling the qere would have skipped these verses silently. Only 26:2's affects sense (Tool 8). The other nine are orthographic or morphological, and **מדונים / מִדְיָנִים ("contentions") three times is the collection's contribution to a ketiv that recurs across the book (cf. 21:9, 19)** `[S: BHS]`. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`; confidence capped at moderate for anything resting on the distribution.

**Two verbs of seeing, one verdict.** 26:12 רָאִיתָ (ראה, the ordinary verb) and 29:20 חָזִיתָ (חזה, the verb of prophetic vision) `[T]`, WLC — two lemmas of two different roots, followed in both verses by the identical colon תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ. And **29:18's חָזוֹן ("vision"), two verses earlier, is the noun of חזה** `[T]` — בְּאֵין חָזוֹן יִפָּרַע עָם ("where there is no vision the people are unrestrained"), then חָזִיתָ אִישׁ אָץ בִּדְבָרָיו ("do you see a man hasty in his words?"). The root appears twice in three verses and nowhere else in the collection. *Moderate confidence that the juxtaposition is deliberate; high that the root is shared.*

**Sound, once.** 27:17 בַּרְזֶל בְּבַרְזֶל יָחַד וְאִישׁ יַחַד פְּנֵי־רֵעֵהוּ (*barzel bĕbarzel yaḥad, wĕʾîš yaḥad pĕnê rēʿēhû*) — the only verse in the collection where the sound carries the argument. The repeated *b*-*r*-*z*-*l* and the twice-struck יָחַד are the grinding. *BHS* records that the versions read a sharpening verb here (𝔊 ὀξύνει, 𝔗 lṭjš) and proposes pointing it as such (apparatus 27:17ᵃ, ᵇ) `[S: BHS]` — which would make the wordplay explicit rather than merely phonetic. Both English versions translate as if the versions were right ("sharpens"), which is defensible and should be known.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Held deliberately thin, because the text holds it thin.** The collection names one historical figure and no place, event, institution or custom that it explains (Tool 7c). Four background items are genuinely load-bearing and each is marked for what it is.

- **The royal court.** 25:2–7 assumes a reader who may find himself at a king's table, moved up or down it, and who knows that standing in the place of great men is a risk. That much is `[T]` from the verses. **That this implies a wisdom school for courtiers, or an eighth-century Judahite scribal class, is reconstruction** `[S]`, and the sermon should not lean on it.
- **The refiner's crucible.** 25:4 (dross from silver, a vessel for the smith), 27:21 (crucible for silver, furnace for gold, a man by his praise), 26:23 (silver dross overlaid on a potsherd). Three verses drawn from one workshop. The technology is straightforward and the image needs no explaining, but 26:23's force depends on knowing that the glaze is worth nothing and the pot underneath is common. `[I]`, high confidence.
- **Snow in harvest (25:13; 26:1).** The two verses use the same image in opposite directions — snow's cold is refreshing to the one who sends a faithful messenger; snow in summer is disastrous out of season `[T]`. What the reader needs is the agricultural calendar: the grain harvest is late spring, and rain then ruins it. `[I]`, high confidence.
- **The coals (25:22).** See Tool 7d. The Egyptian penitential rite is a secondary proposal, not a datum of the text `[S]`, moderate confidence, and the verse's own second colon points away from a punitive reading.

**What the collection has removed, and why it matters here.** The absence of every proper noun after 25:1 means there is almost no cultural specificity to recover. **That is a feature of the material, not a gap in our knowledge**, and a sermon that fills it with eighth-century colour is decorating rather than expounding.

### Original Audience Reception

**Who they were.** The book constructs one addressee — בְּנִי ("my son"), a young man at the point of leaving supervision `[S: overview]`, `[T]` on the vocative's twenty-two occurrences. This collection is where that addressee is withdrawn: the vocative appears once, at 27:11, and **that occurrence is the last in the book** `[T]`, WLC.

**What they brought.** Nine chapters of argument for wanting wisdom; a motto (1:7); a prohibition against self-assessment (3:7); and — from 22:17–24:34 immediately preceding — a fresh dose of direct address and the last יִרְאַת יְהוָה in the book at 23:17 `[T]`.

**What would have surprised them.**
- **That nobody is talking to them any more.** Seventeen of the collection's nineteen directly addressed verses fall in 25–27; after 27:23 the second person effectively stops. For a reader trained on "hear, my son", the silence of chapters 28–29 is the collection's loudest feature `[I]`, high confidence.
- **That the vocabulary they had been taught is not being used.** No מוּסָר, no יִרְאַת יְהוָה, no בִּינָה, no לֵץ (Tool 7a).
- **That the fool block ends by finding somebody worse than the fool** (26:12).
- **That a poor man is the one doing the searching** (28:11) in a collection that opened by assigning searching to kings (25:2).

**What would have comforted them.** 27:5–10 — the friend, the rebuke, the neighbour near at hand. And 28:13, the collection's one verse of mercy on stated terms: מְכַסֶּה פְשָׁעָיו לֹא יַצְלִיחַ וּמוֹדֶה וְעֹזֵב יְרֻחָם ("he who covers his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will find compassion"). **רחם ("to have compassion") occurs exactly once in the whole of Proverbs, and this is it** `[T]`, WLC — eight verses after the divine name has returned, in a collection that has said nothing about mercy for 129 verses. *High confidence; one lemma search, positively controlled against the whole book.*

**What we bring that they did not.** The answer to 20:9 and 30:4; a cross that has dealt with vengeance (Rom 12:19); and the New Testament's own readings of three of these verses.

**Candidate Fallen Condition Focus** *(feeds `/point-purpose`)*. The shared concern, anchored to the text and shared between the first hearers and us: **we are competent to assess everybody except ourselves, and no amount of accumulated wisdom repairs that — the collection describes four men who were nobody's fool and who got themselves wrong.** Anchors: 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11; 27:2; 28:26; 29:23; and the book's 3:7 and 20:9. A secondary candidate, for a sermon that takes the other half: **we want the rules, and what we are given is a room full of cases and no invigilator.**

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. The hiddenness of God as glory, not deficit** (25:2). The collection opens by naming concealment as God's כָּבוֹד ("glory") and then demonstrates it across 138 verses by barely naming him. The canonical field: Deut 29:28 (the secret things belong to the LORD our God); Isa 45:15 (אָכֵן אַתָּה אֵל מִסְתַּתֵּר, "truly you are a God who hides himself"); Job 28's search that finds no way to wisdom; and, at the other end, 1 Cor 2:10's Spirit who searches τὰ βάθη τοῦ θεοῦ ("the depths of God") and Col 2:3's Christ ἐν ᾧ εἰσιν πάντες οἱ θησαυροὶ τῆς σοφίας καὶ γνώσεως ἀπόκρυφοι ("in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden"). **The trajectory runs from a concealing whose glory is the concealing, to a concealing whose glory is that it has been opened.** *Moderate-to-high confidence; the verbal links are real (סתר / ἀπόκρυφος; חקר / ἐραυνάω) but the trajectory is a reading, not a citation chain.*

**2. Rule, and what a people receives from it.** 28–29 is the most sustained treatment of government in Proverbs: מָשַׁל ("to rule") four times, נָגִיד ("ruler") once, מֶלֶךְ twice, and the four-verse frame on what a land gets when the righteous or the wicked come up (28:12, 28; 29:2, 16) `[T]`. Set against 2 Sam 23:3–4 (he who rules over men righteously, ruling in the fear of God, is as the light of morning) and Isa 11:1–5 (judging the poor with righteousness), and forward to the kingdom in which the criterion is the same. **29:14 is the collection's own statement of it:** מֶלֶךְ שׁוֹפֵט בֶּאֱמֶת דַּלִּים כִּסְאוֹ לָעַד יִכּוֹן ("a king who judges the poor with truth — his throne will be established forever") `[T]`. *High confidence on the theme's presence; moderate on the specific links.*

**3. The poor as the test.** דַּל ("poor") six verses and רָשׁ ("poor") four, all in 28–29 `[T]`; the collection's only דַּעַת is about their cause (29:7); the one who sees through the rich man is a poor man with understanding (28:11); the throne that stands is the one that judges them truly (29:14); and giving to the poor is the collection's only stated route to sufficiency (28:27). Set against Deut 15:7–11, Isa 58, Jas 2:1–7. **The collection has stripped out the book's wisdom vocabulary and kept this.** *High confidence — the distribution is counted.*

### Christological Reading

**Category: trajectory and contrast, not prophecy or typology.** The collection contains no messianic prediction, no figure, and no institution; there is nothing here to typologise, and the honest Christological work is of two kinds.

**1. Contrast — and this collection is where the book's contrast is sharpest.** Four times it describes a man who assessed himself and was wrong (26:5, 12, 16; 28:11), and it supplies no example of a man who assessed himself and was right. 26:12's verdict — תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ ("there is more hope for a fool than for him") — is the book's bleakest sentence about the reader, and it is aimed precisely at competence. Set beside it the book's two unanswered questions: 20:9 (who can say, "I have cleansed my heart"?) and 30:4 (what is his name, and what is his son's name?) `[T]`.

**Only one person in Scripture answers the collection's question without failing its test.** Not because Jesus is wise in his own eyes and gets away with it, but because the assessment he lives by is his Father's — οὐ ζητῶ τὸ θέλημα τὸ ἐμὸν ἀλλὰ τὸ θέλημα τοῦ πέμψαντός με ("I do not seek my own will, but the will of him who sent me", Jn 5:30), and the verdict that matters is spoken over him from outside: οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός ("this is my beloved Son", Matt 3:17) `[T]`, SBLGNT. **That is 27:2 — יְהַלֶּלְךָ זָר וְלֹא־פִיךָ, "let another praise you and not your own mouth" — performed.** *Moderate confidence as a deliberate connection; high as a theological correspondence.*

**2. Trajectory — the searching.** 25:2 says God conceals and kings search; 25:3 says the king's own heart cannot be searched; 28:11 gives the searching to a poor man. **The collection's movement is from the throne to the bottom of the social scale, and that is the direction the gospel takes it further:** ἐπτώχευσεν πλούσιος ὤν ("being rich, he became poor", 2 Cor 8:9), and the one in whom the hidden treasures of wisdom are found (Col 2:3) is the one who had nowhere to lay his head. *Moderate confidence — the movement is in the text; the extension is a reading.*

**3. The withheld name.** 25:22 and 29:26 are the collection's two verses in which יְהוָה does something for a person: he repays the one who feeds his enemy, and משפט for a man comes from him rather than from a ruler `[T]`. In 138 verses of cases, those are the two promises. A Christian sermon may say what the collection does not: that the judgement 29:26 says comes from the LORD rather than from the ruler has now been handed to a man (Jn 5:22, 27), and that the repayment of 25:22 has been made in a currency the verse does not anticipate.

**Moralism check — and this collection sets the trap with unusual care.** Every one of the 138 verses can be preached as advice, and most of them are good advice. *Don't boast. Don't gossip. Don't answer a fool. Work hard. Look after your flocks.* **A sermon assembled from those is exactly what the collection is designed to expose**, because it would be a sermon that assessed the congregation and never itself — which is 26:12 in the pulpit. The gospel grounding is not that Christ helps us keep these proverbs but that the self-assessment the collection destroys is the one thing the gospel does not ask us to repair: the verdict comes from outside, and it has been spoken.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**26:4–5 — the alleged contradiction.** The oldest objection to Proverbs' coherence and the easiest to answer from the text. The two verses are built on the same skeleton and each attaches a different פֶּן ("lest") clause: answer, and you may become like him; do not answer, and he may become confirmed in his own eyes `[T]`. Neither is a rule; both are risks, and the reader must judge which is live. *Rabbinic tradition records the pair as one of the reasons Proverbs' canonicity was discussed* `[S]`, moderate confidence — a well-known report, not verified here.

**25:21–22 — "coals of fire".** See Tools 7d, 8 and 11. The punitive reading survives in popular use ("kill them with kindness — it'll make them squirm"), and it is the reading Rom 12:20's own context forecloses.

**26:2 — "an undeserved curse does not come".** Pastorally live wherever a congregation has any traffic with generational curses or spiritual warfare teaching. The ketiv is almost certainly right and says the curse *does not land* — but note honestly that the qere preserves the opposite and that *BHS* had to adjudicate (Tool 8).

**27:22 — pounding the fool in a mortar.** Hyperbole, and the point is the futility of external correction; the block that precedes it (27:5–6, 17, 21) is about correction that works, which is correction from a friend. Not an argument for severity.

**29:15 and 29:17 — the rod and discipline.** Book-level trap 6 applies `[S: overview]`. שֵׁבֶט וְתוֹכַחַת יִתֵּן חָכְמָה ("a rod and reproof give wisdom", 29:15) and יַסֵּר בִּנְךָ וִינִיחֶךָ ("discipline your son and he will give you rest", 29:17) sit two verses apart, and the second uses יסר, the verb behind מוּסָר. **The root appears exactly twice in the collection — 29:17 and 29:19 — and both times as a verb; the noun מוּסָר, which stands in thirty verses elsewhere in the book, is absent from all 138** `[T]`, WLC. Both occurrences are in the collection's last chapter, eleven and nine verses from the end. The congregation will include people for whom this was not proverbial. Preach the necessity of correction from 29:1 and 27:5–6 before coming near the rod.

**29:18 — "where there is no vision, the people perish".** The most misquoted verse in the collection, usually from the KJV and usually in support of leadership vision-casting. חָזוֹן is prophetic revelation, not organisational foresight; the verb יִפָּרַע means *to run wild, to be unrestrained*, not *to perish*; and the second colon settles the sense — וְשֹׁמֵר תּוֹרָה אַשְׁרֵהוּ ("but he who keeps the law, blessed is he") `[T]`. NASB95 ("vision … unrestrained") and NIV84 ("revelation … cast off restraint") both get it right; the KJV is the source of the trouble. See Preaching Pitfalls.

**25:27b and 26:10** — textually unresolved; see the Textual Variants table. Do not preach either.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: preaching 29:18 as leadership vision

- **What it looks like:** "Where there is no vision, the people perish" — therefore the church needs a five-year plan, and the leader who casts one saves the people.
- **Why it's wrong:** three errors in one sentence, and all three come from the KJV rather than from the Hebrew. חָזוֹן is prophetic revelation — what a prophet *sees*, as at 1 Sam 3:1, Isa 1:1, Ezek 12:22. יִפָּרַע means *runs wild, throws off restraint* (the verb of Exod 32:25, where Aaron let the people loose), not *perishes*. And the second colon settles both: וְשֹׁמֵר תּוֹרָה אַשְׁרֵהוּ ("but he who keeps the law, blessed is he") — the parallel to חָזוֹן is תּוֹרָה, God's word, not a strategic plan `[T]`, WLC.
- **The corrective:** preach it from either modern version in hand — NASB95's "vision … unrestrained" and NIV84's "revelation … cast off restraint" are both right — and preach it inside its cluster: 28:4, 7, 9 and 29:18 are the collection's four תּוֹרָה verses, and 29:18 is the last of them. The verse is about a people with no word from God, in the one collection where God is nearly unnamed.

### Pitfall: "heap burning coals on his head" as sanctified revenge

- **What it looks like:** "Kill them with kindness — nothing gets under their skin like being loved. That's what the coals are."
- **Why it's wrong:** it makes the enemy's discomfort the point, which is the one reading the verse's own second colon and Paul's use both exclude. The Hebrew's motive clause attaches the outcome to the *doer* (וַיהוָה יְשַׁלֶּם־לָךְ, "and the LORD will repay you"), and Paul **removes even that** before concluding νίκα ἐν τῷ ἀγαθῷ τὸ κακόν ("overcome evil with good", Rom 12:21) `[T]`. A sermon that gets satisfaction out of the coals has landed exactly where the New Testament's handling of the verse refuses to land.
- **The corrective:** preach 25:21–22 with 24:29 on one side and Rom 12:19–21 on the other. The collection's own context is the refusal of retaliation; the New Testament's context is that vengeance has an owner and it is not you.

### Pitfall: resolving 26:4–5

- **What it looks like:** "There's no contradiction — verse 4 is about *this* kind of fool and verse 5 about *that* kind," or "verse 4 is when he's sincere, verse 5 when he's mocking." Or, at the other extreme, using the pair to show that Scripture contradicts itself.
- **Why it's wrong:** both moves remove the reader's work, and the reader's work is what the pair exists to require. The two verses are built on the same skeleton and differ at the verb's polarity and in the content of the פֶּן ("lest") clause; each states a real risk, and the risks are incompatible `[T]`. The text supplies no criterion for choosing, and supplying one from outside is the move it forbids.
- **The corrective:** preach the pair as the collection's own instruction manual. This is what it is like to read a מָשָׁל: two true generalisations, incompatible applications, and a judgement you have to make and will be judged by. **If the sermon on this unit needs a single text, this is it.**

### Pitfall: turning the collection into a gallery of other people

- **What it looks like:** a character-study sermon — here is the fool, here is the sluggard, here is the gossip, here is the rich man — with the congregation invited to recognise each one.
- **Why it's wrong:** the collection has built two trapdoors under exactly that sermon, and they are 26:12 and 28:11. Eleven verses on the fool, then *there is more hope for a fool than for a man wise in his own eyes*; a rich man who is wise in his own eyes, then a poor man who sees through him `[T]`. The block's structure makes the confident diagnostician the last subject.
- **The corrective:** preach 26:1–11 *toward* 26:12 and let the turn happen in the pulpit. The right question is not "which of these am I?" but "which of these would I most resent being told I am?"

### Pitfall: filling the silence

- **What it looks like:** "Of course the fear of the LORD is implied throughout" — or a sermon that quotes 1:7 six times to supply what 25–29 does not say.
- **Why it's wrong and why it's also half-right:** the withdrawal is measurable and deliberate-looking (Tool 7a), and preaching over it removes the collection's whole method. But the claim needs its qualification, and this report supplies it: **the Greek tradition does not keep the silence** — 27:20 carries a plus naming the Lord, inside the sixty-five-verse stretch, and *BHS* reconstructs a Hebrew line behind it `[S: BHS]`.
- **The corrective:** say both, and say them in this order. *This is what the Bible in your hands does: 138 verses, six mentions, and sixty-five verses in the middle with none. And we should be honest that the Greek Old Testament does not read it quite that way — which tells us that ancient readers noticed the silence too, and that one tradition found it hard to leave alone.* The second sentence strengthens the first rather than undercutting it.

### Pitfall: preaching 25:27b, 26:10, or building on 27:1's "tomorrow"

- **What it looks like:** a point on "seeking your own glory is not glory" (25:27b), or an exposition of 26:10 ("like an archer who wounds everyone…"), or a New Year sermon on 27:1 that never gets past *carpe diem*.
- **Why it's wrong:** *BHS* marks 25:27b corrupt and 26:10 doubtful, and the English versions supply a negative at 25:27b that is not in the Hebrew `[S: BHS]`. 27:1 is sound, but its Christian sense comes from James 4:13–16, which expands it and supplies the missing clause — ἐὰν ὁ κύριος θελήσῃ ("if the Lord wills", Jas 4:15) `[T]`, SBLGNT.
- **The corrective:** leave 25:27b and 26:10 alone; there are 136 other verses. Preach 27:1 with James.
