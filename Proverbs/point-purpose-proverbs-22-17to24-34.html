Phase 0 — the scope decision

A single sermon on 82 verses, and for once the length is unremarkable — the shortest of the post-frame units, comfortably one sermon.

What matters here is not scope but register, and the change is abrupt. [T], WLC — every figure re-derived for this refresh.

Solomon I (10:1–22:16) The words of the wise (22:17–24:34) Chapters 1–9
Vocatives (בְּנִי) one, at 19:27 five — 23:15, 19, 26; 24:13, 21 fifteen
Verses carrying a second-person form 0.5% / 4.2% by half 48.8% — 40 of 82 36.3%
אַל (“do not”) 0.5% / 2.1% 26.8% — 22 verses 12.5%
כִּי (“for”) grounds rare 22 verses — one for every prohibition —
Paragraph markers 4 in 375 verses 7 in 82 — one every twelve 24 in 256

This is the most directly spoken and most prohibitive material in the book — more so than the long address that opens it. [T] The preacher who carries last week’s method across will flatten it. Solomon I described a world and addressed nobody; this unit addresses a person twenty-two times and says why every time.

A gift for the series. Coming straight after Solomon I, the change of register is itself an argument for preaching the book whole: the same book changes how it speaks to you, and you only notice because you heard the other manner last week.

Fallen Condition Focus

We can profit quietly from people who have no advocate — and we can always say we did not realise.

Kept verbatim from version 1, and the new dig strengthens every anchor. [T]

  • 22:22–23 — “Do not rob the poor because he is poor, / Or crush the afflicted at the gate” — and בַשָּׁעַר is a court, not a doorway [I], high confidence. The verse forbids using a legal process to finish someone off.
  • 22:28 and 23:10 — אַל־תַּסֵּג גְּבוּל עוֹלָם (“do not move the ancient boundary”), the same prohibition twice in twenty-four verses [T]. The first time the reason is the fathers; the second time it is the fatherless.
  • 23:11 — כִּי־גֹאֲלָם חָזָק הוּא־יָרִיב אֶת־רִיבָם אִתָּךְ (“for their Redeemer is strong; He will plead their case against you”). גֹּאֵל stands once in the whole of Proverbs [T], and it stands here.
  • 24:11–12 — “Deliver those who are being taken away to death…” and then the excuse, put in the hearer’s own mouth: כִּי־תֹאמַר הֵן לֹא־יָדַעְנוּ זֶה (“if you say, ‘See, we did not know this’”) [T].

And the new dig adds the sharpest anchor of all, which is a fact about who the reader is in these passages. In all three, the reader is the one warned off — never the one rescued [T]. The unit does not invite the congregation to identify with the poor. It assumes they are the ones the poor may need protecting from.

Teaching Point

Wisdom that comes from no one in particular is worth having only because it has been re-founded on trust in the LORD — and it is tested where nobody is watching, on behalf of people who cannot make you answer for it.

Subject: What makes wisdom from an unnamed source trustworthy? Complement: Not that it works, but that 22:19 has re-grounded it on trust in the LORD — and it proves itself at the gate, the boundary stone and the excuse.

Why this is one idea, and why the wording changed. The collection states its own purpose twice in its opening five verses, before it issues a single prohibition [T]:

לִהְיוֹת בַּיהוָה מִבְטַחֶךָ (“so that your trust may be in the LORD”, 22:19) לְהָשִׁיב אֲמָרִים אֱמֶת לְשֹׁלְחֶיךָ (“that you may give a true answer to him who sent you”, 22:21)

One theological, one vocational — and no other collection in Proverbs says what it is for at all [T]. That is the sermon.

⚠ Why “borrowed” has come out of the Teaching Point. Version 1 made the Egyptian parallel load-bearing. The new dig holds it at [S: BHS] and rests nothing on it — the corpus can establish the Hebrew, the ketiv and the Greek, and can establish nothing about an Egyptian text. The text’s own claim does the same work and is safer: this material is credited to חֲכָמִים (“the wise”) at 22:17 and again at 24:23, and there is no name anywhere in 82 verses [T]. Wisdom from an unnamed collective, re-founded on the LORD — that is on the page. Say the Amenemope story if you wish (see Keep Honest 2); do not build the sermon on it.

Purpose Statement

To move hearers from holding wisdom as accumulated competence to holding it as trust — and to take away the sentence “I didn’t realise”, so that a congregation able to profit quietly from the unprotected is confronted by the Redeemer who takes their case.

Differentiated:

  • For the capable — you have acquired a great deal of practical wisdom, much of it from outside the church, and it works. 22:19 does not tell you to discard it. It tells you what it has to rest on before it is safe.
  • For anyone with power over someone — an employee, a tenant, a debtor, an elderly relative’s paperwork. 23:11 says the other party has counsel, and the counsel is strong.
  • For the church — 22:22’s gate is a court and 24:11 is corporate before it is individual. Do not let either become a line about church giving.
  • For the enquirer — this is the part of the Bible that most obviously overlaps with the world’s own wisdom. Say so, and then say what was done to it at 22:19.
  • For the preacher — 22:21 assumes somebody sent you and expects a straight answer back. You are not the top of the hierarchy in this unit either.

Candidate Big Question

The series question, asked in these words in every sermon:

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s form, and its answer:

“Where is your trust — and would ‘I didn’t realise’ hold?”

Answer: You would know by where your trust rests, not by what you have mastered. The collection re-founds its wisdom at 22:19 on trust in the LORD — and tests it at 24:12, where the plea of ignorance is refused by the one who weighs hearts.

Flagged as candidate: final wording belongs to the manuscript stage.

Proposed structure — four movements

Following the transmitted paragraphs, which for once are generous: seven markers in 82 verses [T], one every twelve, against one every ninety-four across Solomon I.

One deliberate departure, named. The layout runs 23:6–24:14 as a single paragraph of 44 verses — too long to preach as one movement. Movements 2 and 3 cut it at 23:35/24:1, which is where the drunkard’s poem ends and the unit’s last movement begins. Every other boundary below is the manuscript’s own.

Movement Text Function
1. Why you are being told this 22:17–21 (a transmitted paragraph, exactly) Establishes the aim before any content. The preface states its purpose twice and neither is mastery: that your trust may be in the LORD (22:19), that you may give a true answer to him who sent you (22:21) [T]. Note what is missing: no name. The material is credited to חֲכָמִים (“the wise”) and to nobody else. Say the Amenemope story here if it is going to be said at all — and do not count thirty sayings (Keep Honest 2). Surfaces the FCF by naming the stakes: this is wisdom you did not get from God directly, and here is what has been done to it.
2. Twenty-two things not to do, and twenty-two reasons 22:22–23:35 (three transmitted paragraphs) Presses the FCF, and this is where the sermon’s weight falls. The poor at the gate (22:22–23), the boundary stone moved twice (22:28; 23:10), and at 23:11 the word the book uses once — גֹּאֵל (“Redeemer”), of God, standing for the fatherless [T]. Then the ruler’s table (23:1–3), the wealth that flies away (23:4–5), and the drunkard (23:29–35) — six questions in a single line at 23:29, and a man who wakes up and reaches for another [T]. Note the register throughout: every prohibition has a reason attached. The collection never simply commands.
3. “If you say, we did not know” 24:1–22 (the rest of the long paragraph, plus the paragraph that closes at the setumah) Establishes the TP and lands Christ. 24:11–12 is the unit’s climax — a command to rescue those being led to death, and then the excuse taken out of the hearer’s mouth before he can use it, by the one who תֹּכֵן לִבּוֹת (“weighs hearts”) [T]. Three rhetorical questions in one verse, each naming God by a participle. Then close the movement at 24:21 — יְרָא־אֶת־יְהוָה בְּנִי וָמֶלֶךְ (“fear the LORD, my son, and the king”) — the collection’s last word before the seam, and the verse the New Testament divides (Keep Honest 5). And the paragraph before it ends on the promise: יֵשׁ אַחֲרִית וְתִקְוָתְךָ לֹא תִכָּרֵת (“there is a future, and your hope will not be cut off”, 24:14), which is 23:18 repeated [T].
4. And these also 24:23–34 (the two closing paragraphs) A short sting, not a fourth point. The transmitted text marks this off with the only setumah in Proverbs (see below), and it reads as an appendix: partiality in judgement (24:23), the field before the house (24:27), and the sluggard’s broken wall. It ends by quoting chapter 6: 24:33 is 6:10 word for word, and 24:34 varies 6:11 in three places [T]. The wise man tells his own story and finishes it with someone else’s line. Keep it brief; the sermon has already landed.

The seam at 24:22 — three witnesses, and all three are now verified

[T] Worth ninety seconds from the pulpit if the congregation has been following the series’ interest in how the book is put together.

  1. The Hebrew marks it. 24:22 carries a setumah (ס) — the only one in the whole of Proverbs, where every other marker is a petuchah [T], WLC printing the Leningrad layout.
  2. The Greek breaks there — and fills the gap. Fourteen verses of Agur (MT 30:1–14) stand between MT 24:22 and MT 24:23: Swete 24:24–37, with MT 24:23 resuming at Swete 24:38 [T], Swete.
  3. The Greek also expands there. Swete 24:22 carries five pluses with no Hebrew counterpart, on the king’s tongue — μάχαιρα γλῶσσα βασιλέως (“the king’s tongue is a sword”) [T], Swete.

Two traditions that arrange this book differently both treat 24:22 as a hinge. Version 1 flagged the Greek half as not yet verified; it now is, in this project’s own corpus.

Where Christ comes in, and how

Trajectory and contrast, with one New Testament-authorised link. No typology is claimed — and there is one candidate that must be declined.

1 · The Redeemer (23:11) — the primary route, with a correction to make. The גֹּאֵל is the kinsman with both the right and the obligation to take a defenceless relative’s case; Proverbs uses the word once, and puts it exactly where the fatherless are being defrauded [T]. But the גֹּאֵל at 23:11 is God himself, not a human redeemer-figure [T] — so this is trajectory, not typology, and the sermon should not offer it as a type. The trajectory runs through Job 19:25 and Isaiah’s Redeemer-title to Gal 3:13 [S]. And it develops rather than repeats: the unit’s advocate defends the wronged; the New Testament’s παράκλητος (“advocate”, 1 Jn 2:1) defends the wrongdoer [T], SBLGNT. Say that difference aloud — it is the whole distance the gospel travels. Preach it in movement 2.

2 · The excuse, and its afterlife (24:12) — the strongest link, and it is quoted. 24:12 ends וְהֵשִׁיב לְאָדָם כְּפָעֳלוֹ (“and He will render to a man according to his work”). In Greek that clause is ὃς ἀποδίδωσιν ἑκάστῳ κατὰ τὰ ἔργα αὐτοῦ — and Rom 2:6 reads ὃς ἀποδώσει ἑκάστῳ κατὰ τὰ ἔργα αὐτοῦ [T], Swete + SBLGNT. The unit’s argument against an excuse becomes the New Testament’s statement about the last judgement, in the same words — and it recurs at 2 Tim 4:14 and Rev 22:12. Preach it in movement 3, and keep the shape the proverb gives it: what you knew is not the issue; what he knows is. (For the study: Ps 62:13 says the same thing in entirely different Hebrew — no shared lexeme — and the two converge only in Greek. Interesting, and not pulpit material.)

3 · Matthew 25:44 — resonance, offered as resonance. “Lord, when did we see you…?” The same excuse, in the same setting, refused the same way [I], moderate-to-high. The New Testament does not cite Proverbs here. Say “listen to how Jesus answers the same excuse” — never “Jesus is quoting Proverbs.”

4 · “Give me your heart” (23:26). The teacher asks for the whole person, not compliance. See Keep Honest 4 before using it.

Keep honest

1 · Do not preach this as a sentence collection. Verse by verse, one proverb per point, each weighed as a generally-true observation — that is the method Solomon I invites and this unit refuses. It is paragraphed seven times in 82 verses, it argues, and its commands are not generalisations: do not rob the poor is not a claim about what usually happens [T]. Preach the paragraphs.

2 · Do not count thirty sayings — and be careful how you tell the Egyptian story. The two errors come as a pair. At 22:20 the Hebrew we have is unresolved: the ketiv is שלשום (printed unpointed), the qere is שָׁלִישִׁים, Swete has τρισσῶς (“threefold”), and “thirty” is a conjecture proposed on the strength of Amenemope’s thirty chapters [T] + [S: BHS]. NIV84 prints the conjecture in its main text and puts the manuscript readings in a footnote. One sentence handles it: “your Bible says thirty; the Hebrew says something the translators could not settle, and the footnote tells you so.” And the borrowing itself: the resemblance is discussed in every commentary and a series that never mentions it is keeping something back [S] — but say it as a question that is open, not as a fact the sermon rests on, and never let it supply the sermon’s structure.

3 · Do not domesticate 24:11 — or aim it at somebody else. Two failures, opposite directions. “Rescue those being taken away to death” softened into general kindness removes the verse. And 24:12’s excuse used to indict a government, an age or an institution misses its aim: the Hebrew is second-person singular — כִּי־תֹאמַר (“if you say”) [T]. It is a mirror, not a placard.

4 · Do not preach 23:26 in your own voice. “Give me your heart, my son” is the teacher speaking. A preacher who says it as himself is asking the congregation for something no preacher may ask. Attribute it, and let it point past you.

5 · Do not preach 24:21 as it stands, after 1 Peter. The Hebrew gives one verb two objects — יְרָא־אֶת־יְהוָה בְּנִי וָמֶלֶךְ — and Swete keeps it [T]. 1 Pet 2:17 splits it: τὸν θεὸν φοβεῖσθε, τὸν βασιλέα τιμᾶτε (“fear God, honour the king”) — and the king gets the same verb the list has just given to everyone [T], SBLGNT. The distinction is the apostle’s, not the translator’s. Preaching the proverb unqualified gives the state a word the New Testament withholds from it. Two minutes, and it is one of the clearest examples in the series of the canon developing its own material.

6 · Handle 23:13–14 deliberately or leave it. “You shall strike him with the rod and rescue his soul from Sheol.” Both evasions — the skip and the brisk defence — are worse than the difficulty [S: overview, trap 6]. Name the setting (a society of corporal discipline), name the translation decision (NIV84 renders שְׁאוֹל as “death” in the text, with the Hebrew footnoted [T]), name the pastoral fact that some hearers did not meet this proverbially — and move on without a verdict.

Anti-moralism gate

This unit carries the highest moralism risk in the series — twenty-two prohibitions, five vocatives, forty verses of second-person address, and no narrative [T]. A sermon here can become a list of instructions almost by accident.

Test Result
“Be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes — and the text supplies the guard itself. The stated purpose at 22:19 is trust, not conduct, and it stands before the first prohibition; the twenty-two commands are given twenty-two motive clauses precisely so they are not bare [T]. A sermon that reaches the commands without 22:19 has removed the collection’s own safeguard.
Is Christ the subject, or an example? Passes. In movement 2 the actor is the גֹּאֵל who pleads the case — Christ takes the role; he is not offered as a model of taking it. In movement 3 the actor is the one who weighs hearts. In all three vulnerable-person passages the hearer’s part is to stop doing something.
Grounded in Christ’s saving work? Passes. Gal 3:13 behind the Redeemer; Rom 2:6 quoting the unit’s own climax; and 1 Jn 2:1’s advocate, which is where the trajectory arrives.

Passes — but this gate needs watching at the manuscript stage more here than anywhere else in the series. If the sermon’s imperatives outnumber its indicatives, it has drifted, however good the individual points.

Pulpit-text check

Pulpit text: NIV84. Study text NASB95. Most of the plan survives; one loss touches the Christ-landing and must be handled.

What it carries NIV84
The TP’s hinge (22:19) Carried: “So that your trust may be in the LORD, I teach you today, even you.” The sermon’s governing sentence is safe in the pulpit text
23:11 — the Christ-landing’s word Lost. NIV84 has “for their Defender is strong; he will take up their case against you”; NASB95 has “their Redeemer”. גֹּאֵל is the redemption word, and movement 2’s whole trajectory runs through it. The congregation will hear a defender, which is true and is not the point. Say the Hebrew word once and say what it meant — the kinsman who buys a relative back
The boundary pair (22:28 ‖ 23:10) Carried, and identically: “Do not move an ancient boundary stone” at both. The repetition is audible without help
The promise (23:18 ‖ 24:14) Carried, and identically: “there is a future hope for you, and your hope will not be cut off” at both
The climax (24:11–12) Carried: “Will he not repay each person according to what he has done?” — the clause Rom 2:6 uses. NIV84 also fills the Hebrew’s ellipsis at 24:11 with a second imperative where NASB95 marks the supply with “Oh”; nothing in the plan turns on it
The close (24:21) Carried, with the single verb intact — “Fear the LORD and the king, my son”. Keep Honest 5 survives in the pulpit
22:20 Settled as “thirty sayings”. See Keep Honest 2. This is the one place the preacher must say something
23:14 “save his soul from death”, with “Hebrew: Sheol” footnoted

Provenance and hand-off

Every element is drawn from dig-deeper-proverbs-22-17to24-34 (23 September 2026): the FCF from its candidate and the three vulnerable-person passages; the TP from Headline 1 and the Author’s Purpose section, re-founded off the Amenemope claim; the PS from its So What; the structure from its Tool 3 paragraph table; the keep-honest items from its six Preaching Pitfalls, with item 4 the preacher’s own from version 1 [S]. The Rom 2:6 and 1 Pet 2:17 links are the dig’s [T]; Gal 3:13 and the Job/Isaiah trajectory are carried over from version 1 [S].

Received from sermon 5 (Solomon I): a reader who has been told he cannot grade himself, and a collection that ended at 22:16 on עֹשֵׁק דָּל (“oppressing the poor”). This unit’s first prohibition is אַל־תִּגְזָל־דָּל (“do not rob the poor”) [T]. Open with that join — last week’s last verse and this week’s first command are about the same person, and the change of register happens across a subject that does not change.

Hands forward to sermon 7 (25:1–29:27): the Hezekiah transcription, where the divine name almost disappears and the reader is left to assess cases with the invigilator out of the room [S: overview]. After a unit that tells him twenty-two times what not to do and why, that silence will be felt — and it is worth one sentence at the close to set it up.

Not decided here: how much of the Amenemope story to tell aloud (one sentence, or the fuller account), and whether 23:29–35 — the drunkard — earns its own space in movement 2 or is summarised in transit. It is the longest sustained portrait in the book after chapter 7, and it is the one place the unit shows rather than tells.
