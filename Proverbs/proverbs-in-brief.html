Proverbs in Brief

A one-sheet spine and nine cards — the forest, for use while working in the wood. Rebuilt from the nine fresh solo digs of 18–27 September 2026, the book overview v3.0, the macro-synthesis v2.0, the claim audit and the whole-book Greek survey · Issue 2 · 27 September 2026

How to use this. Keep it open beside whichever report you are working in. Every line here is the compressed form of something argued at length elsewhere; where you want the evidence, the source document is named. Nothing in this document is new — if it disagrees with a report, the report wins.

This issue replaces the one of 26 August 2026 entirely. Every unit has been dug again since then, and the August spine rested on a macro-synthesis that has now been superseded. Where the two issues disagree, this one is current; the August file is in _superseded/. The substantive changes are listed at the foot.

The book in one line

Proverbs will make you competent and then refuse to let you certify it — because the verdict on a life is not one you can pass on yourself, and the verdict God passes was earned by his Son and has already been spoken.

The Big Question, asked in every sermon: “How would you know if you were actually wise?”

The nine answers below are not nine restatements. The question stays fixed; the answer develops. That is what makes this a series rather than nine sermons on one book.

The spine — nine things the whole book is doing

From proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis v2.0, where each is argued with anchor pericopes, verse evidence and a confidence rating. Every one cleared a hard gate of three or more pericopes. NT-authorised = high; converging text-first = strong.

  1. The New Testament reaches Proverbs through the Greek. (high) Eight quotations verified; four rest on a Greek reading the Hebrew does not support — Heb 12:5–6, Jas 4:6 ‖ 1 Pet 5:5, 1 Pet 4:18, 2 Cor 9:7. Three uses break the pattern, each differently.
  2. Nine verses deny self-certification, 20:9 asks the question none of them answers, and 31:28–31 supplies the only verdict — from outside. (strong) 3:7 · 12:15 · 16:2 · 21:2 · 26:5, 12, 16 · 28:11 · 30:12 — one per collection.
  3. The motto frames the book and changes grammatical form at the end. (strong) יִרְאַת יְהוָה as a noun in fourteen verses, last at 23:17; absent from all 138 verses of 25–29; revoked in its own words at 30:3; and at 31:30 an adjective, predicated of a person.
  4. Frame and field speak different dialects — and the closing poem returns to the frame’s. (strong) Five lemma-verified chains tie 31:10–31 to personified Wisdom.
  5. The boundaries you plan a series by are Masoretic. (high) The Greek differs at five points, including no superscription at all at 10:1.
  6. Direct address is progressively withdrawn and the reader is handed the cases. (strong) בְּנִי last at 7:1 in the frame; 0.5% second-person in Solomon I; the imperative rate falling 15.9% → 12.0% → 1.8%; the vocative retiring at 27:11; one imperative in the closing poem, and it is addressed to you.
  7. The poor are the test case, and the vocabulary turns covenantal at the close. (strong) עָנִי and אֶבְיוֹן stand together in exactly three verses — 30:14, 31:9, 31:20 — and in none of the 138 verses before them.
  8. The book binds itself by internal repetition, not citation. (strong) One act of quotation in thirty-one chapters (30:5), and it changes two things, both the right two.
  9. Christ is reached by contrast and by an unanswered question, never by typology. (strong; the John 3:13 shape-argument high)

No internal tension to flag this issue. The August spine carried a warning that arguments 5 and 6 pulled against each other on whether the book develops. That warning is withdrawn — those two arguments are gone, and the nine above do not pull against one another. What they do share is a single claim: the book teaches judgement by progressively refusing to exercise it for you.

⚠ One thing to hold carefully. Spine 5 says the divisions are Masoretic; spine 8 says the book is bound by repetition across those divisions. Both are true and they are about different things — the headings are a feature of one tradition, the material crosses them in every tradition. If a sermon needs one sentence: the joints are Masoretic, the stitching is not.

The markers are as the WLC prints the Leningrad layout — a manuscript reading tradition, not an authorial signal, and its spread beyond Leningrad has not been checked. Three gradients worth carrying in your head. [T] Paragraphing gets denser as the book goes on — one marker per 94 verses in Solomon I against one per 4.7 in Agur and Lemuel, a twenty-fold change — and then stops entirely: the closing acrostic is the longest undivided run in the book, twenty-two verses without a marker. The Greek expands the frame most and 30–31 not at all, which is the reverse of where it does its rearranging. And direct address falls away across the whole book (spine 6).

1 · Introduction — the whole book

Doing: planting the question, and showing that the book is designed rather than collected. The one finding: the LORD gives wisdom to people who cannot certify they have it — and the wisdom he finally gave was a person. The design shows in registers that do not depend on each other: a motto frame, an attribution frame and a figure frame, all three countable. Big Question: You wouldn’t — and that is the point. This book ends in a confession, not a diploma. Christ: 1 Cor 1:30 — ὃς ἐγενήθη σοφία ἡμῖν ἀπὸ θεοῦ (“who became for us wisdom from God”). Trap: the survey becomes a lecture. One statistic at most, and only if it lands emotionally — the marker densities and the antithesis percentages are the evidence for the claim, not the claim.

2 · Why this book exists — 1:1–7

Doing: naming the addressee, before anything is asked of him. The one finding: the preamble is bracketed by חָכְמָה וּמוּסָר (“wisdom and instruction”) at v.2 and again at v.7 — a phrase that stands in three verses of the whole Hebrew Bible (the third is 23:23) [T] — and the bracket is the argument: the book says what it is for, then names who will not have it. Big Question: You would start by admitting you are the one it is addressed to. It is written for the simple, not the sage — and its motto is the fear of the LORD. Christ: “something greater than Solomon” (Matt 12:42); σοφία θεοῦ (1 Cor 1:24, 30); Col 2:3. Trap: preaching 1:7a and never quoting 1:7b. There is no imperative anywhere in vv.1–7 [T]; the first arrives at v.8, which is next week. Worth one sentence: three of the four competences the prologue offers are later used of wicked men — עָרְמָה, מְזִמָּה, תַּחְבֻּלוֹת — and v.7 is the only thing that orients them.

3 · The agenda — chapter 2

Doing: setting the posture that everything after it assumes. The one finding: the chapter is one sentence — three אִם (“if”, vv.1, 3, 4) → two אָז (“then”, vv.5, 9), each grounded by a כִּי (“for”, vv.6, 10) → two identical לְהַצִּילְךָ (“to deliver you”, vv.12, 16). And it contains no imperative at all — forty-two verb forms and not one a command, which among the paternal addresses of chapters 1–9 makes it unique [T], WLC. Big Question: You would not know by checking. You would know by receiving — “for the LORD gives wisdom” (2:6) grounds the search rather than rewarding it. Christ: 1 Cor 1:30 — wisdom received, not achieved. Trap: turning vv.1–4 into a five-step programme with v.6 as an encouraging aside. That inverts the chapter. Worth one sentence: what the son does in v.1, God does in v.7 — צפן (“to store up”), its only two occurrences in the chapter, and the divine act comes before the son has finished searching [T].

4 · Two voices in the street — 1:8–9:18

Doing: extracting allegiance rather than behaviour. The one finding: the father stops speaking and Wisdom finishes his sentence for him. בְּנִי stands fifteen times in chapters 1–9 and its last is 7:1; the formula וְעַתָּה בָנִים שִׁמְעוּ־לִי (“now then, sons, listen to me”) stands three times and only three times in the book — 5:7 and 7:24 in the father’s mouth, 8:32 in Wisdom’s [T]. After 7:27 the father never addresses the son again. Big Question: Wisdom is not a quality you audit; it is a voice you obey — and two of them are calling in the same street, both public, both with a house and a table. Christ: Wisdom’s banquet reissued to those who were not looking (Luke 14:21–23). Trap: making Folly obviously bad. Both women have a house, both stand at the high point of the city, both set a table, and both say the same four words — 9:4 and 9:16 are identical in what the two women say; only the narrator’s frame differs [T]. Read them one after the other, aloud, and let the room sit with it. Greek note (Rahlfs): the frame is the most expanded part of the book — eighteen pluses, eight of them in chapter 9 alone.

5 · The Proverbs of Solomon (1) — 10:1–22:16

Doing: showing the change across the collection, not averaging it. The one finding: this is one unit with two methods, and the text makes the join itself at 16:25, which reprints 14:12 [T]. The first half is the contrast engine — 60.3% of verses carrying a character word, 86.9% joining their cola with a waw; the second relocates the seat of judgement — מִן + יְהוָה in five of its eight book-wide occurrences, the man plans / YHWH disposes couplet in seven sentences, three chapters at zero on the opposition measure. Big Question: You would want the two-column sort to place you — and the collection breaks it from inside: “there is a way that seems right” (14:12), “who can say, I have made my heart clean?” (20:9), “all a man’s ways are clean in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirits” (16:2). Christ: 1 Cor 1:30 — he became to us wisdom from God, and righteousness. Trap: quoting a whole-section antithesis average. Opposition runs 33.2% in the first half and 5.2% in the second, with chapters 16, 19 and 20 at zero [T]. An averaged figure is arithmetically true and homiletically false. ⚠ Say “in the Hebrew”. The chapter-16 divine-name cluster is Masoretic — the Greek lacks 16:1, 3, 4 and 6. And Swete has no superscription at 10:1 at all, which is the sharpest single evidence that this unit’s boundaries are Masoretic.

6 · The Words of the Wise — 22:17–24:34

Doing: relocating trust, and admitting where the material came from. The one finding: this is the most directly addressed and most prohibitive material in the book — more so than the long address of chapters 1–9. אַל (“do not”) stands in 22 of the 82 verses, 26.8%, against 12.5% in the frame and 0.5% in Solomon I; 48.8% of verses carry a second-person form; and five בְּנִי vocatives return [T]. The wise do not speak more gently than the father; they speak more bluntly, and almost entirely in the negative. Big Question: You would know by where your trust is placed, not by what you have mastered — wisdom borrowed from Egypt is re-founded at 22:19 on trust in the LORD. Christ: trajectory rather than type. Rom 2:6 blends the Greek of 24:12 with the Greek of Ps 61:13 — in Hebrew the two verses share no lexeme at all — and 1 Pet 2:17 splits 24:21’s single imperative in two. Trap: verse-by-verse treatment, one proverb per point. The unit is paragraphed seven times in 82 verses, it argues, and its commands are not generalisations — do not rob the poor is not a claim about what usually happens. Worth knowing before you print an outline: 22:20’s “thirty sayings” is a conjecture. The ketiv is unpointed שלשום, the qere שָׁלִישִׁים, Swete τρισσῶς, and BHS proposes שְׁלֹשִׁים comparing Amenemope. NASB95 renders the qere; NIV84 prints the emendation in its main text.

7 · The Proverbs of Solomon (2) — 25:1–29:27

Doing: training judgement for when no authority is in the room, then naming the authority. The one finding: three silences nest inside one another, measured as the longest run of consecutive verses in which a vocabulary is wholly absent [T], WLC: the ruler vocabulary (מֶלֶךְ + מָשַׁל + נָגִיד) for 91 verses, 25:7–28:14; the divine name for 65, 25:23–28:4; the righteous/wicked antithesis for 57, 25:27–27:27. Chapters 26 and 27 fall entirely inside all three, and all three end inside chapter 28. Big Question: You would have to judge for yourself — and 25–27 withholds the verdict to make you. Then 28–29 hands the standard back, and it is תּוֹרָה. Christ: 25:21–22 is where the New Testament goes for love of enemies — Rom 12:20 quotes the Greek verbatim and stops one clause short, because Paul has just given that clause’s verb to God (12:19, Deut 32:35). And 29:23 is answered in Luke 14:11 and Phil 2:8–9. Trap: preaching the silence as the message without saying that it ends. The restoration is the point of the withdrawal. And do not overclaim the silence either: the Greek does not keep it — Swete has a plus at 27:20 naming the Lord, inside the sixty-five-verse stretch. Preach it as two movements, not one. Every independent measure splits the collection at the end of chapter 27: lexical opposition 4.8% against 18.2%, imperative-bearing verses 12.0% against 1.8%. A single figure for 25–29 describes no part of it.

8 · The two “kings” — 30:1–31:9

Doing: unseating the sage at the end of a book about becoming one. The one finding: the unit revokes the prologue’s thesis in the prologue’s own words. דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים (“the knowledge of the Holy One”) stands in two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible — 9:10, where it is understanding, and 30:3, where Agur says he has not got it — and all three of the book’s great wisdom nouns occur here once each and every one is negated [T]. Big Question: The wisest man in the book says he does not know — and then receives a word he did not write, and prays. Christ: 30:4 asks for one person who both went up and came down — and that is the discriminating detail: Deut 30:12 has an ascent only, Bar 3:29 brings wisdom down. John 3:13 matches the shape, and answers 30:4’s last question in the same clause. Carry the caution: the singular “his son” is the Hebrew’s; the Greek has the plural, and BHS records it (30:4ᶜ). Trap: answering 30:4 in the first five minutes. A congregation that gets the answer before it has felt the question has been robbed of the only thing that makes the answer worth having. Second trap: preaching Agur as a model of humility — he is not being admirable, he is being accurate. Worth one sentence: 1:8 promised a mother’s teaching and 31:1 is the only place the book supplies it — אֲשֶׁר־יִסְּרַתּוּ אִמּוֹ, and the verb is the root of מוּסָר in its last appearance in the book.

9 · The closing poem — 31:10–31

Doing: letting somebody else say it, and closing the frame. The one finding: the poem is written in personified Wisdom’s vocabulary, and the overlap is countable — five chains, each lemma-verified against the whole book: פְּנִינִים (3:15, 8:11 → 31:10), שַׁעַר (1:21, 8:3 → 31:23, 31), שׂחק (1:26, 8:30–31 → 31:25), אשׁר piel and תמך, both in 3:18 of Wisdom (→ 31:28, 31:19), and מצא (3:13, 8:17, 8:35 → 31:10’s מִי יִמְצָא) [T]. The book does not end by describing a housewife. It ends by describing, in the words it used of Wisdom herself, what wisdom looks like when it is a life rather than a lecture. Big Question: You would never say it of yourself. Somebody else would say it of you — and the poem’s one imperative is “give her praise.” Christ: 1 Cor 1:30 and Col 2:3 — the trajectory runs abstraction → a life → a person, and this poem is the middle term. The scene of 31:28–31 — a life assessed, in public, by somebody with the standing to do it — is the shape of Matt 25:21, and the ground is that the praise here is earned and the verdict a Christian waits for is not. Trap: the poem as a standard for Christian womanhood, preached on Mothering Sunday as a checklist. Three textual defences, all from the poem itself. Genre: an acrostic is a completeness-claim, not a list of requirements. Grammar: the poem gives her no command at all and gives the audience one — תְּנוּ־לָהּ at 31:31, plural. Vocabulary: the words are Wisdom’s, so the subject is wisdom-embodied. And one more: Swete ends the book “give her of the fruit of her lips, and let her husband be praised in the gates.” The impulse to redirect the praise is two thousand years old and the Hebrew resists it — if a sermon lands on the husband, it has quietly taken the Greek’s ending.

Threads that cross sermons

Four things the cards above cannot hold, because each belongs to more than one week.

The mouth, sermons 6 → 8 → 9. פתח (“to open”) stands in four verses of Proverbs — 24:7, where a fool cannot open his mouth in the gate; 31:8 and 31:9, where a king is twice commanded to; and 31:26, where the woman opens hers in wisdom, five verses before she is praised in the gates [T]. If you are preaching 8 and 9 in consecutive weeks, that is the join — and 6 planted it.

The poor, sermons 5 → 6 → 7 → 8 → 9. The poor-and-Maker chain runs 14:31 → 17:5 → 22:2 → 22:16, crossing the 15:33/16:1 seam and landing on Solomon I’s closing verse; 22:22 is the next collection’s first prohibition, on the same subject; 29:7’s one דַּעַת and 29:14’s throne are about the poor; and the covenant words arrive only at the end — עָנִי and אֶבְיוֹן together at 30:14, 31:9 and 31:20, and nowhere earlier.

Self-assessment, every sermon from 2 onwards. 3:7 prohibits it; 12:15, 16:2, 21:2, 26:5, 26:12, 26:16, 28:11 and 30:12 show eight men and one generation who ignored it; 20:9 asks whether anyone could answer it and the book never does; 27:2 forbids the self-praise; and 31:28–31 is where the verdict finally comes, from somebody else’s mouth.

Where the New Testament reads Proverbs, sermons 4 → 9. Thirteen uses verified. Assume the Greek unless you have checked — and know the three that break the pattern: 1 Pet 4:8 follows the Hebrew of 10:12; 2 Pet 2:22 renders 26:11 independently and calls it a παροιμία; John 3:13 answers 30:4 in the Greek’s shape while the clause that makes the answer a Son’s is the Hebrew’s.

Pulpit-text notes worth having at book level

The study text throughout is the NASB95. The pulpit text is declared per engagement — the ESV at your own church, the NIV84 elsewhere — and each point-purpose plan carries its own check. Three divergences are sharp enough to know in advance.

  • NIV84 dismantles 31:19–20. The Hebrew builds the two verses on one skeleton and turns the same hands from the spindle to the poor; NIV84 uses four different verbs. NASB95 keeps it. Read those two verses from the NASB95.
  • NIV84 closes the mouth at all four of the book’s פתח verses — “he has nothing to say” at 24:7, “Speak up” twice at 31:8–9, “She speaks with wisdom” at 31:26. The thread above is invisible in that pulpit Bible at every one of its four points.
  • And NIV84 is the better witness at three places in the closing poem — 31:30 “beauty is fleeting” is what הֶבֶל means where NASB95’s “vain” invites a moralising misreading; 31:25 “laugh” keeps the שׂחק chain NASB95’s “smiles” breaks; and 31:28 “call her blessed” keeps the אשׁר chain NASB95 breaks. The study text is not automatically the better text at the lectern.
