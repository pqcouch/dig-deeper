The Perplexity Pass: Audit and Verdicts

Date: 17 August 2026 · Questions answered: 20 · Adopted as given: 11 · Adopted with qualification: 6 · Rejected or withdrawn: 3

Summary

This was a productive pass, and two of its results change conclusions I had already committed to paper. It also contains one claim that the Hebrew text refutes outright, on the single item flagged do not preach until settled — so the pass is a good illustration of why answers get audited rather than absorbed.

The two findings that change things:

  1. My collections assessment overstated the consensus. I wrote that three sources supported the 15:33/16:1 division and none opposed it, and I flagged the possibility that this was “an artefact of asking a question that presupposed the answer.” It was. There is a substantial strand — McKane, Heim, the form critics — that rejects or bypasses the division, and their grounds are good ones. §2 below.

  2. Fox does not endorse the sub-collections. He notes the shifts and declines to treat them as seams. This is the most authoritative single voice on the question and it cuts against Ansberry. But the answer is sourced from a bookseller listing and a wiki, so it is not yet evidence. §3.

The claim that failed: the widely repeated statement that every verse of Proverbs 2:1–11 begins with aleph and every verse of 2:12–22 with lamed is false. I checked all twenty-two verses against the Westminster Leningrad Codex. What is actually there is better, and is set out in §4.

1. How the answers were graded

Two axes, kept separate throughout, because they came apart repeatedly.

Substance — is the account of the scholarly landscape accurate and useful?

Sourcing — do the citations support it? This pass was often weak here. Among the footnotes are the Watchtower library (on Proverbs 8:22, the most Christologically contested verse in the book), answering-islam, a Oneness Pentecostal apologetics site, a blog titled jesusnotyhwh, a Google Site called “errors in the bible”, and — for the question about what Fox says — an Amazon product page.

That is not a reason to discard the pass. Several answers cite genuinely first-rate work: Emanuel Tov’s own paper on the LXX of Proverbs, Johann Cook in TC, the NETS edition, NET Bible translator notes, a Liberty University doctoral dissertation, Konkel’s lecture notes, and Roland Murphy via Theopolis. Where those carry an answer, it is worth having.

It is a reason to grade every answer twice, and to notice that the weakest sourcing clusters exactly where the doctrinal stakes are highest.

2. Where the pass changes a conclusion — the 15:33/16:1 division

This is the most important result in the pass, and it corrects me.

What I wrote in proverbs-collections-assessment.md §8: “the 15:33/16:1 division is not idiosyncratic to Ansberry; it is a settled commonplace with at least three independent witnesses… three sources have now been found for the division and none against. That is either a settled consensus or an artefact of asking a question that presupposed the answer.”

It was the artefact. Asked directly whether anyone dissents, the answer is that a significant body of scholarship does:

  • W. McKane and the form-critical tradition treat 10:1–22:16 as loosely gathered sentence literature with no sustained macro-structure; attempts to find large-scale divisions overread the evidence.
  • Knut Heim, Like Grapes of Gold Set in Silver (BZAW 273, 2001), argues the collection is deliberately arranged — but in roughly fifty-nine small clusters, not two halves. The organising unit is the cluster; 15:33/16:1 is not a structural hinge in his scheme.
  • Jamieson–Fausset–Brown and older commentaries treat the block as a single undifferentiated series.

The four grounds given against the division are serious, and one of them is decisive in kind rather than degree:

  1. Antithetic and synthetic proverbs are intermingled on both sides — a gradient, not a seam.
  2. There is no superscription at 16:1. Unlike 10:1, 22:17, 24:23 and 25:1, nothing in the text marks it.
  3. Core themes run continuously through 10–22:16.
  4. If the real artistry is at cluster level, a two-half macro-outline may be a modern imposition.

What I am changing. Point 2 is exactly the distinction my own assessment was built on — the difference between book-marked and reconstructed boundaries — and I stated it clearly in §2 of that document. But I then let §8 and §9 drift into treating the reconstructed seam as though the weight of scholarship settled it. The formal data I gathered remain what they were: the antithesis rate really does fall from 74% to 20% at exactly that point, and the righteous/wicked density really does drop threefold. What I cannot claim is that the field agrees on what those facts mean.

The sharpest form of the disagreement is worth keeping in front of us, because it is not about the data at all. Nobody disputes that chapters 10–15 look different from 16–22:16. The dispute is whether a stylistic gradient is evidence of an editorial seam. Heim’s answer — that the material is carefully arranged, but at a scale two orders of magnitude smaller than the proposed halves — is not a denial of design. It is a different account of where the design is.

And there is a tension inside Ansberry’s own footnotes that I missed. Ansberry cites Heim (BZAW 273) at his footnote 7 in chapter 4. He is drawing on a scholar whose central thesis locates the organising structure somewhere other than where Ansberry’s own division puts it. That is not a contradiction — one can accept cluster-level artistry and still propose larger units — but it is a strain, and an honest assessment should name it.

Revised verdict on the 15:33/16:1 seam: the formal shift is [T] and beyond dispute. The collection boundary is [I], contested, and should be presented as one reading among others rather than as a finding. Confidence lowered from high to moderate.

The 27:27/28:1 seam is less affected, because my own four converging measures there are stronger and because the tôrāh datum is independent of the delimiting criteria. But the same caution applies: Fox, on this report, declines that division too.

3. Fox — the answer that is not yet an answer

Reported: Fox recognises the stylistic shifts at 15:33/16:1 and 27:27/28:1 but treats them as gradients and tendencies within single collections, declining to posit separate sub-collections in a source-critical sense.

This is entirely plausible and matches Fox’s general methodological caution. It is also not sourced. The two citations offered are an Amazon listing for the commentary and the SOTS wiki. Neither is Fox. An account of a major commentator’s position, drawn from a bookseller’s page, is exactly the kind of thing that should never enter a report as [S].

Status: unresolved, and now the highest-priority remaining question. Fox, Proverbs 10–31 (AB 18B) is in the Logos library, it covers this ground directly, and the lookup needs no Study Assistant credits. Until it is read, this stays flagged.

Why it matters more than it did: if Fox genuinely declines both divisions, then the two most authoritative voices available — Fox and Heim — are both unpersuaded, and Ansberry’s scheme is a minority structural proposal rather than a commonplace. That would change how the five sweeps should be framed: as one workable segmentation rather than as the structure of the sentence literature.

4. The Proverbs 2 acrostic — checked against the text, and the claim fails

The question was flagged do not preach until settled. The answer came back confidently affirmative, citing Waltke, the USCCB notes, NET notes, and BiblicalTraining, in these terms:

vv. 1–11: lines begin with aleph (first letter); vv. 12–22: lines begin with lamed (12th letter).

I read all twenty-two verses in the Westminster Leningrad Codex. The claim as stated is false, and not marginally.

v. first word letter v. first word letter
1 bĕnî bet 12 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā lamed
2 lĕhaqšîb lamed 13 haʿōzĕbîm he
3 kî kaf 14 haśśĕmēḥîm he
4 ʾim aleph 15 ʾăšer aleph
5 ʾāz aleph 16 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā lamed
6 kî kaf 17 haʿōzebet he
7 wĕṣāpan / yiṣpōn waw / yod 18 kî kaf
8 linṣōr lamed 19 kol kaf
9 ʾāz aleph 20 lĕmaʿan lamed
10 kî kaf 21 kî kaf
11 mĕzimmâ mem 22 ûrĕšāʿîm waw

In vv.1–11, aleph stands at three verses (4, 5, 9), not eleven. Verse 1 begins with bet. In vv.12–22, lamed stands at three verses (12, 16, 20), not eleven — and lamed also opens vv. 2 and 8, inside the supposed aleph half.

But the text yields something better than the claim it refutes, and this is the finding worth keeping:

  • The three aleph verses are the chapter’s logical skeleton. ʾim at v.4 closes the protasis; ʾāz at v.5 and ʾāz at v.9 open the two apodoses. (And ʾim is the first word of the sentence proper at v.1, once the vocative bĕnî is set aside — which is what Waltke’s more careful formulation actually depends on.)
  • The three lamed verses are the second half’s structural heads. lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā at v.12 (“to deliver you” — from the man of perverted speech), lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā again at v.16 (from the strange woman), and lĕmaʿan at v.20 (“so that you may walk”). Two identical purpose-infinitives and a purpose conjunction: rescue, rescue, result.

So the chapter is not an acrostic and not an alphabetic poem in the sense reported. What it has is twenty-two verses, and two small families of clause-initial markers — aleph words carrying the conditional logic, lamed words carrying the purpose clauses. Whether the author intended aleph and lamed to evoke letters 1 and 12 is unprovable, and the case is much weaker than the sources claim.

Verdict: the “do not preach” flag stays on, but the reason has changed. It was flagged as unverified. It is now flagged as substantially overstated in the secondary literature. The 22-verse count is real; the alphabetic architecture is not.

A note on how this failure happened. Several independent-looking sources repeat the same overstatement, which is what made it look well-attested. They are almost certainly all downstream of one compressed summary of Waltke, whose actual claim is narrower. Multiple citations agreeing is not the same as multiple witnesses.

5. Other corrections from the same text check

Having the Hebrew of Proverbs 2 open settled three of my own counts.

√byn in vv.1–11 — my figure of 7 is confirmed. tĕbûnâ v.2; bînâ and tĕbûnâ v.3; tābîn v.5; tĕbûnâ v.6; tābîn v.9; tĕbûnâ v.11. The earlier returned figure of four was an undercount, as suspected. [T] High.

Road-words — 12, not 13, and there is no nātîb. ʾōraḥ and derek v.8; maʿgāl v.9; derek v.12; ʾōraḥ and derek v.13; ʾōraḥ and maʿgāl v.15; maʿgāl v.18; ʾōraḥ v.19; derek and ʾōraḥ v.20. Both my count and the returned count were wrong — I had claimed thirteen including a nātîb at v.18, but v.18 has maʿgĕlōteyhā. The chapter uses three road-words, not four. [T] High.

Nāṣar/šāmar — 4 at vv.8 and 11, confirmed, and the chiasm holds: linṣōr … yišmōr (v.8), tišmōr … tinṣĕrekkâ (v.11). And there is a fifth I had missed: tišmōr at v.20 — “you will keep the paths of the righteous.” That is a genuine addition to the reading: God guards (v.8), understanding guards (v.11), and at the end the son himself keeps (v.20). The verb travels from God to wisdom to the disciple. [T] High.

A second Ketiv/Qere, previously unnoticed: v.8 has Ketiv ḥăsîdô (“his faithful one”, singular) against Qere ḥăsîdāyw (“his faithful ones”, plural). Immaterial to the finding that this is Proverbs’ only ḥāsîd, but it should be recorded.

And 2:22 confirms the verdict in §6: the verb is yikkārētû, from kārat.

7. Reconciling the 27:23–27 contradiction

Two of my documents disagreed, and the pass has now adjudicated.

  • The sweep examined the metaphorical reading of 27:23–27 as a royal hinge, declined it, and flagged it.
  • The collections assessment assumed Ansberry’s janus and used it in the argument for the 27:27/28:1 seam.

The sweep was closer to the field. The literal-husbandry reading is the majority; the royal-hinge reading is a recognised minority. The “crown” of v.24 is the single lexical hook, and most commentators do not think one word overrides sustained agricultural imagery.

What this does to the 27:27/28:1 seam: less than it might appear. My case for that seam never depended on the janus — it rested on four converging measures (antithesis, righteous/wicked density, simile, absolute tôrāh), all of which are text-derived and all of which still stand. The janus was Ansberry’s supporting flourish, not my evidence. But the assessment should stop presenting it as agreed, and §5 of that document needs adjusting accordingly.

A6 in detail — two corrections to my own work

The full Greek of LXX Proverbs 1:7, from Brenton:

Ἀρχὴ σοφίας φόβος Κυρίου, / σύνεσις δὲ ἀγαθὴ πᾶσι τοῖς ποιοῦσιν αὐτήν· / εὐσέβεια δὲ εἰς Θεὸν ἀρχὴ αἰσθήσεως· / σοφίαν δὲ καὶ παιδείαν ἀσεβεῖς ἐξουθενήσουσιν.

First, my theos observation is withdrawn. I had written that the Greek reads phobos theou rather than kyriou, and drew from it a point about the motto sounding like general piety rather than covenant loyalty. Brenton reads Κυρίου; the θεόν I had seized on belongs to the plus. The error came from taking a Greek form quoted in a secondary summary without opening an edition — which is the same failure mode as the acrostic claim in §4, committed by me rather than by a source.

Second, and more important: the Greek does not level the rēʾšît / tĕḥillat distinction. It doubles it. I had argued that the LXX “assimilates 1:7 to 9:10” by substituting sophia for daʿat, and offered that as mild evidence against making much of the Hebrew’s variation. The plus shows the reverse: the Greek makes two “beginning” statements where the Hebrew makes one — ἀρχὴ σοφίας and ἀρχὴ αἰσθήσεως, the latter being the natural counterpart of Hebrew daʿat. The translator kept both terms and gave each its own clause. The earliest interpreter we have found the distinction worth expanding, not worth dropping. My argument against B3 is withdrawn, and the evidence now leans mildly the other way.

(A third detail worth keeping: the Greek renders ʾĕwîlîm, “fools”, with ἀσεβεῖς, “the ungodly” — a clean instance of the LXX moralising Proverbs’ intellectual vocabulary of folly.)

And one small correction running the other way

The returned road-word list gives derek at 2:6. Proverbs 2:6 reads daʿat — “from his mouth come knowledge and understanding”. The derek is at 2:8. The total of four is unaffected, and the slip is worth recording only because it is the mirror image of my own nātîb error: both of us mislocated one word while getting the count right.

A detail from the same search worth preserving: the three occurrences of maʿgāl in Proverbs 2 (vv. 9, 15, 18) are each rendered differently in the NASB. The English reader meets three unrelated words where the Hebrew repeats one — the same translation-loss as ṣāpan at vv.1/7. Proverbs 2 is unusually punishing to the English-only reader, and that is now demonstrable rather than impressionistic.

9. A note on method

The pass answered twenty questions and got roughly fifteen of them substantially right. That is a good return, and it opened two lines — Heim, and the dissent from the 15:33/16:1 division — that the library-only approach had entirely missed.

It also produced one confident, multiply-cited claim that the biblical text refutes in five minutes of checking, and it sourced a major commentator’s position to a bookseller.

The two failure modes are opposite and both matter. A closed library gives you fewer sources, well attributed, and a false sense that what it lacks does not exist. An open search gives you far more, unevenly, and a false sense of consensus when several pages repeat one compressed summary. Neither substitutes for the text.

The most useful thing in this entire pass was not any of its answers. It was that one of them was wrong in a checkable way, and checking it produced a better structural observation about Proverbs 2 than the literature had offered.
