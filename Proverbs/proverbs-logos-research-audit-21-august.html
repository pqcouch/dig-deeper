1 · ⬆ The ʿormâ / serpent link — commentators do make it, and one of them makes it better than I did

This was flagged as “the single most important verification item in the report,” with the instruction: if no commentator makes the connection, downgrade to uncertain. The instruction does not fire — but the gain is narrower than the first issue of this audit claimed, and part of it was already banked.

What was already done, on 15 August. The report had checked this and found Lange (deriving ʿormâ from ʿāram, glossing it via ʿārûm, “used of the serpent, Gen. 3:1”) and Stabnow. Its verdict was careful and it still stands: “It moves the lexical observation from my own proposal to [S]-supported… It does not support the stronger claim that Proverbs 1:4 alludes to Genesis 3.” Keep the root-link as fact and the allusion as suggestion.

What this pass adds is the theological move, and it is sourced twice.

  • Barnes (1879) is decisive: the teacher offers the “subtilty” which “may turn to evil (Exod. 21:14) and become as the wisdom of the serpent (Gen. 3:1), but which also takes its place, as that wisdom does, among the highest moral gifts (Matt. 10:16).” [S]
  • NET: “The related adjective described the serpent as ‘shrewd, crafty, cunning’ (Gen 3:1)… There is no virtue for simpletons to be unaware in this world; they need to be wise as serpents.” [S]
  • Barry (Faithlife) and Stabnow (CSB) both note the Gen 3:1 comparison and the Proverbs/elsewhere split.

Verdict, stated precisely. Three layers, and they do not all move together:

Layer Status
ʿormâ and ʿārûm share a root, and ʿārûm is used of the serpent [S] since 15 August (Lange, Stabnow). Fact.
Proverbs offers the naïve a quality which in the serpent was evil, and offers it as a good [S] as of this pass (Barnes, NET), moderate–high. Preach it.
Proverbs 1:4 deliberately alludes to Genesis 3:1 Still a suggestion. Barnes says the quality “may… become as the wisdom of the serpent” — a comparison, not an allusion claim. No source asserts the allusion. Moderate, and no higher.

And Barnes supplies something the report did not have: Matthew 10:16. “Be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” The New Testament authorises the very move Proverbs 1:4 makes — Christ commands the serpent’s quality, paired with a dove’s innocence. That is a far stronger landing than an unsourced observation about roots, and it should go into the sermon.

⚠ But keep the two claims apart — this upgrade does not cover both halves of Headline Finding 2.

Claim Status
ʿormâ shares its root with the serpent’s ʿārûm, and Proverbs offers it to the naïve as a good Sourced. Preach it.
mĕzimmâ is positive throughout the frame (1:4; 2:11; 3:21; 5:2; 8:12) and negative throughout the sentence collections (12:2; 14:17; 24:8), by design Distribution [T]; design claim still unsourced — no commentator makes it, not even Fox. The standing ruling holds: keep as a suggestion, do not preach as design.

Stabnow adds a useful precision for the first claim: ʿormâ and its adjective are always positive in Proverbs (12:16, 23; 13:16; 14:8, 15, 18; 15:5; 22:3; 27:12) and usually negative elsewhere. So the book has domesticated the word, and Genesis 3 is where it was wild.

3 · ✅ rēʾšît (1:7) and tĕḥillat (9:10) genuinely differ

Confirmed from BHS: 1:7 יִרְאַ֣ת יְ֭הוָה רֵאשִׁ֣ית דָּ֑עַת · 9:10 תְּחִלַּ֣ת חָ֭כְמָה יִרְאַ֣ת יְהוָ֑ה. [T]

This matters because a real finding was resting on it. Rēʾšît carries both “beginning” and “chief part / best” (Num 18:12; Amos 6:6); tĕḥillâ is unambiguously temporal. So 1:7 says something 9:10 does not, and the “start here then move on” misreading of 1:7 is blocked by the book’s own choice of a different noun fourteen verses from the end of the frame. Preach 1:7 as foundation and summit; keep 9:10 as the temporal statement it is.

4 · ✅ The 1:5 question — rephrased here, and RESOLVED at §29 in Part 3

Read §29 first. Waltke answers this on 22 August, and the answer is that the fork below is a false choice — both options are right. What follows is retained as the record of how the question was framed, because the framing is what made the answer legible.

“I don’t understand the question here!” — that is my fault, and it is the second time in this project that a badly-worded question has cost a return. Here it is properly.

The grammar. Proverbs 1:2–6 is one sentence hanging off v.1, and its spine is a chain of five infinitives construct with lāmed — lādaʿat… lĕhābîn… lāqaḥat… lātēt… lĕhābîn. Verse 5 breaks the chain. It switches to two finite verbs: yišmaʿ… wĕyôsep, “let the wise hear and increase in learning.”

The question the break raises. Is v.5 —

  • (a) a second addressee — so the book has two target audiences, the naïve (v.4) and the already-wise (v.5), as several Egyptian instructions do? Or
  • (b) an aside or invitation — the book is aimed at the naïve, and v.5 concedes in passing that the wise will also profit?

Why it cannot be settled from English. The ESV’s “Let the wise hear and increase in learning” reads as a jussive aside; renderings that keep the infinitive rhythm read more like a second purpose. The fork is in the Hebrew syntax, not in the translation.

What is at stake homiletically. On (a), the preacher may address the mature directly from this passage. On (b), the passage names the naïve as its audience and the wise are eavesdropping — which is the reading the report leans to at moderate, on the internal ground that the interludes of chapters 1–9 consistently address the simple.

What would settle it: a technical commentary on the syntax of vv.2–6 — Waltke (NICOT) or Fox (AB 18a) on the function of the finite verbs at v.5. ✅ Waltke did, on 22 August — see §29. This paragraph is the record of the question, not a live request.

5 · ⚠ CORRECTION — the missing lāmed at 1:3b is not emphasis

The datum is confirmed [T]: BHS reads לָ֭קַחַת מוּסַ֣ר הַשְׂכֵּ֑ל צֶ֥דֶק וּ֝מִשְׁפָּ֗ט וּמֵישָׁרִֽים — no preposition before the triad.

But my reading of what it means was wrong. The report said the absent lāmed “throws weight onto” the triad — i.e. emphasis. NET gives the grammatical description instead:

“The three nouns that follow ‘self-discipline of prudence’ are adverbial accusatives of manner, describing the ways in which the disciplined prudent activity will be manifested: ‘in righteousness, justice, and equity.’”

That is a normal construction doing ordinary work, not a rhetorical spotlight. [S]

And the corrected reading is homiletically stronger than the one it replaces. On the emphasis reading, the triad is the most important item in a list. On the adverbial reading, the triad is not an item in the list at all — it is the mode in which everything else in the list is exercised. Righteousness, justice and equity are not one more thing Proverbs teaches; they are the manner in which its whole discipline is carried out. That is a better sentence and it is the one a grammarian will stand behind.

Action: corrected in the 1:1–7 report and in the sermon-2 plan.

6 · ⚠ The yirʾat YHWH count — the fourteenth is 23:17

Your thirteen are right and one is missing. Machine-checked against the parsed BHS for 10:1–24:34 and 28:1–29:27:

Proverbs 23:17 — אַל־יְקַנֵּא לִבְּךָ בַּחַטָּאִים כִּי אִם־בְּיִרְאַת־יְהוָה כָּל־הַיּוֹם “Let not your heart envy sinners, but continue in the fear of the LORD all the day.” [T]

That makes fourteen, and it is a substantive addition rather than a technicality — it is the only occurrence inside the Words of the Wise, and it is one of only five verses in that collection’s eighty-two to name YHWH at all.

And there is a fifteenth, depending on how you count. 31:30 — אִשָּׁה יִרְאַת־יְהוָה הִיא תִתְהַלָּל — is the same construct chain, but used attributively (“a woman fearing the LORD”) rather than as the abstract noun. Include it and the count is fifteen; exclude it and it is fourteen.

The methodological point is worth keeping, because it has now happened twice. The antithesis-count discrepancy with Martin (163 vs 149) turned out to be a definitional disagreement, not a textual one. So is this. Whenever two counts of the same thing differ, check the definition before checking the data.

8 · ⬆ Isaiah 11:2–3 ↔︎ Proverbs 1:2, 7 — sourced, and the sermon rests on it

The report called this “this report’s own and moderate… worth checking in a commentary on Isaiah 11 before use.” Two commentators make the connection, one of them citing Prov 1:7 by name.

  • Goldingay: the attributes of Isa 11:2–3 “have been referred to as belonging to God and/or as mis-claimed by human beings. Now they become real in a human figure. … The doubling of the reference to the fear of Yahweh corresponds to that extra stress earlier on misdirected fear (8:12–15). It also emphasizes that this is the feature that explicitly distinguishes true wisdom from wisdom that leaves God out of account (see Prov. 1:7).” [S]
  • Barry: “Wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and fear of Yahweh were key components of righteous living (see Prov 1:7; 2:5; Psa 14:4).” [S]

Verdict: upgrade to moderate–high, [S]-supported. Sermon 2’s Christ-landing is sound.

And Goldingay adds two things the report did not have. First, the fear of the LORD is doubled in Isa 11:2–3 — it appears in v.2’s list and then again in v.3a, “his delight shall be in the fear of the LORD.” The repetition is emphatic, and it is the term the two passages share. Second, Isaiah’s earlier chapters have been tracking misdirected fear (7:4; 8:12–13; 10:24) — so the messianic oracle is resolving a thread, not making an isolated statement. The king’s delight in the fear of the LORD is Isaiah’s answer to a nation afraid of the wrong things.

9 · ⬆ ṣāpan at 2:1 and 2:7 — confirmed, and NET states the point exactly

NET: “The verbal root צָפַן is repeated in 2:1 and 2:7. In 2:1, it is the responsibility of man to ‘store up’ wisdom; but in 2:7, it is God who ‘stores up’ wisdom for the wise person who seeks him.” [S]

That is Headline Finding 2 of the Proverbs 2 run, in a commentator’s words. Upgrade to [S]-confirmed, high. Lange adds that the sense is “to protect, to preserve, after the manner of a treasure or jewel, over which one watches that it may not be stolen” — which sharpens the image.

One incidental: Lange notes a Ketiv/Qere at 2:7 — Ketiv וְצָפֵן (“and so he layeth up”), Qere יִצְפֹּן. He prefers the Ketiv, “confirmed by the LXX and Pesch. as the oldest reading,” while noting most commentators take the Qere. The root identity is unaffected either way, so the finding does not move; worth a footnote only.

10 · ⚠ The imperative question — the answer describes the English, not the Hebrew

This one needs care, because the Logos return contradicts a load-bearing finding.

The return says: “verses 2–4 contain direct commands (‘Make,’ ‘Incline,’ ‘Lift,’ ‘seek,’ ‘search’)… The son is directly commanded to receive words, treasure commandments, make his ear attentive…”

Those English words are the NASB’s rendering of Hebrew imperfects. From the BHS you supplied in this very document:

2:1 בְּ֭נִי אִם־תִּקַּ֣ח אֲמָרָ֑י וּ֝מִצְוֹתַ֗י תִּצְפֹּ֥ן אִתָּֽךְ

Both verbs carry the taw prefix — תִּקַּח and תִּצְפֹּן — which makes them second-person imperfects, not imperatives. The imperatives would be קַח and צְפֹן, without the prefix. [T] — verifiable from the text on the page.

The same holds through vv.2–4: taṭṭeh (v.2), tiqrāʾ and tittēn (v.3), təbaqšennâ and taḥpəśennâ (v.4) are all prefixed forms governed by אִם. (Moderate–high — 2:1 is machine-verifiable from the supplied text; vv.2–4 are read from the forms and should be confirmed against BHS if the point is pressed hard from the pulpit.)

English versions render a Hebrew conditional imperfect as an imperative because that is how English says it. “If you make your ear attentive” is clumsy; “Make your ear attentive” is not. The translation is right and the grammatical description drawn from it is not.

Verdict: the finding stands — there is no imperative addressed to the son in Proverbs 2. And the correction strengthens the point rather than weakening it: an ancient reader would have heard conditions, and a modern reader hears commands. That gap is itself worth a sentence in the sermon, because it is precisely how the five-step-method misreading gets in.

This is the third time in this project that a source has answered a question about the Hebrew from an English text. Worth building into future queries: ask for the parsed form, not the rendering.

11 · ✅ RESOLVED — Proverbs 2’s alphabetic architecture

⚠ Corrected. The first issue of this audit called this “NEW” and said “this was not in the report.” That was wrong. The Proverbs 2 report already carried it at Tool 3(c), “A formal observation, flagged”, and had routed it to Open Questions item 3 as [S] and unverified, with the note “do not preach it as established.” So this is a resolution of a question the report itself asked — and the report’s guess was right on both counts.

What the return adds is detail and sourcing, not the observation. Four sources, in agreement on the data and divided on the label:

  • Twenty-two verses = the number of Hebrew letters, and it is regarded as deliberate — “a deliberate artistic means of communicating wholeness and completeness” (Horne). [S]
  • The strophes open on two letters, and they are the alphabet’s first and middle. Stanzas at 2:1–4, 5–8, 9–11 begin with ʾālep; stanzas at 2:12–15, 16–19, 20–22 begin with lāmed, the twelfth letter (Treier). [S]
  • It is not a true acrostic. Longman: the two-letter pattern “makes it unlikely that a Hebrew acrostic structure was intended.” Schipper: “reminiscent of an acrostic, even though… there is no alphabetic sequence.” [S]
  • Skehan’s strophic symmetry: 4 · 4 · 3 ‖ 4 · 4 · 3 — vv.1–4, 5–8, 9–11 ‖ 12–15, 16–19, 20–22. [S]

What it changes. The sermon-3 structure had movements at 1–4 · 5–8 · 9–19 · 20–22. The first, second and fourth match the strophes exactly; the third lumped three strophes together. Corrected to 1–4 · 5–8 · 9–11 · 12–22, which puts the hinge at 2:11/2:12 — the ʾālep-half to lāmed-half turn — and that is where the chapter turns from what you will understand to what you will be delivered from.

And a series-level observation worth flagging as a candidate. The book’s agenda chapter and the book’s closing poem both use alphabetic architecture — Proverbs 2 by twenty-two verses and two letters, Proverbs 31:10–31 by a complete acrostic. [I] Moderate; offered as resonance, not design, since Longman explicitly doubts an acrostic was intended at ch. 2. It does not clear the macro-synthesis’s ≥3-pericope gate and is not promoted to a macro-argument.

12 · ⬆ Colossians 2:3 ↔︎ Proverbs 2 — three commentators, including Beale

The report offered this as a secondary note. It should be primary.

  • Beale: “in 2:2–3 Paul weds Dn 2 and Pr 2,” quoting Prov 2:3–6 in full. He adds that two Greek versions (Symmachus and Theodotion) read “hidden things” instead of “treasure” at Prov 2:4 — which tightens the verbal link to Col 2:3’s apokryphoi. [S]
  • Wright: Christ “sums up in himself all that the Jews predicated of ‘Wisdom’ (cf. Prov. 2:1–8, whose LXX translation is echoed several times in our present passage).” [S]
  • Dunn: the imagery “evokes Jewish wisdom tradition (Prov. 2:3–6; Sir. 1:24–25; Wis. 6:22…).” [S]

Verdict: upgrade to moderate–high, [S] ×3. Promote to the sermon-3 Christ-landing alongside 1 Cor 1:30. Beale’s Daniel 2 pairing is a bonus — Paul’s “mystery” is Danielic and his “treasures of wisdom” is Proverbial, welded.

Your own added catena — Exod 31:1–3 (Bezalel “filled with the Spirit of God in wisdom, in understanding, in knowledge”), 1 Kgs 7:14 (Hiram), Eph 1:17 — is a genuine observation and is not in any of the three commentators. [I] Moderate. It is worth keeping because it identifies the wisdom–understanding–knowledge triad as Spirit-given craftsmanship language, which connects outward to the ʾāmôn debate at Prov 8:30. Credited as yours; held as inference, not as demonstrated allusion.

13 · ★ NEW — ḥāsîd occurs once in Proverbs, at 2:8

Confirmed by lemma search: the covenant-loyalty word appears exactly once in the book, in “guarding the paths of justice and watching over the way of his saints (ḥăsîdāyw)” (2:8). [T]

Worth having. The book’s one use of the great covenant-loyalty term falls in the chapter that grounds wisdom in God’s giving — and it is plural, which supports the report’s point that v.8’s promise is corporate. A small but clean addition to sermon 3.

14 · ✅ The vocative census — confirmed exactly

15 singular + 4 plural = 19, none in chapter 9. The return lists “my son” at 1:8, 1:10, 1:15, 2:1, 3:1, 3:11, 3:21, 4:10, 4:20, 5:1, 5:20, 6:1, 6:3, 6:20, 7:1 (fifteen) and “my sons” at 4:1, 5:7, 7:24, 8:32 (four). [S] — matching the report’s count reached by reading.

And 8:32 is the one that matters: it is the plural formula in Wisdom’s mouth, and it is Headline Finding 2 of the 1:8–9:18 run — the father’s own vocative migrating to her. Confirmed.

15 · ⬆ Proverbs 9:1–6 ↔︎ Luke 14:16–24 — commentators do draw it

Held at moderate as “canonical reflection… not checked.” Three sources: Blomberg (Prov 9 “probably forms background for Jesus’ parable of the great banquet in Luke 14:16–24”), Kittredge and Wilson (both on the Matt 22 parallel — built house, slaughtered animals, mixed wine, servants sent to call from the highest places). [S]

Upgrade to moderate–high. Note that two of the three route it through Matthew 22 rather than Luke 14; Wilson adds that Matthew “interrupts this tone of warm welcome” with eschatological judgement, which Proverbs 9 does not have. Preach the invitation; do not import Matthew’s judgement scene into Proverbs 9.

17 · ✅ ḥokmôt at 1:20 — four sources, standard explanation verified

Plural of intensity / majesty, with a singular verb, like ʾĕlōhîm (Kidner, Schwab, JFB, NET). Kidner adds a live alternative the report did not have: the form may be a Phoenician singular rather than a Hebrew plural. [S] Worth a footnote; it does not change the reading, since both explanations yield a single personified figure.

18 · ✅ RESOLVED — Proverbs 1:23 is an offer

Waltke: “Wisdom again addresses the overgrown, unresponsive youths, calling them to turn back (or repent, tāšûbû); there is still hope for them, but not forever (see vv. 29–31 and the inclusio ‘turn away’ in v. 32)… She pleads with the heretofore-unresponsive youths to turn back and listen to her rebuke.” [S]

So the ESV’s main text is right and its footnote is the weaker option. The ESV prints “If you turn at my reproof” (conditional offer) and footnotes “Will you turn away at my reproof?” (accusation). Waltke reads it as stronger than a condition — a plea. Moderate–high.

And he supplies a structural datum nobody in this project had: the šûb inclusio.

1:23 tāšûbû — “turn back” · 1:32 mĕšûbat pĕtāyim — “the turning away of the simple will kill them”

Same root, opening and closing Wisdom’s speech, and the polarity reverses. [T] on the root; [S] on the inclusio. Wisdom’s appeal is to turn back; what actually kills the simple is their turning away. That is the sermon-4 spine, and it was invisible until Waltke pointed at it.

Preaching consequence. 1:20–33 is not an indictment with a hopeless verdict. It is an offer with a deadline — “there is still hope for them, but not forever.” The judgement of vv. 24–31 is real and it is what happens when the offer is refused; it is not the speech’s opening posture. Preach v.23 as a plea and vv.24–31 as what follows a refused plea.

The question as it was put again — ✅ and it was answered the next day at §29. Do not re-ask it.

In Proverbs 1:5 — yišmaʿ ḥākām wĕyôsep leqaḥ — the verse breaks the chain of five lāmed-infinitives running through 1:2–4 and 1:6 by switching to two finite verbs. Does Waltke (or Fox) read v.5 as (a) naming a second addressee, so that the book targets both the naïve of v.4 and the already-wise; or (b) an aside, the book being aimed at the naïve with v.5 conceding that the wise will also profit?

✅ ANSWERED — 22 August, and both options are right

(a) “On the other end of the spectrum of addressees stands the wise (ḥākām)” — and of the preamble as a whole, “its addressees, namely, Israel’s educable youth (vv. 4–5).” (b) “The force of the volitional form… functions here as an invitation, since the wise want to obey such a command.” (c) and the thing neither option contained — “the preamble subtly includes the son among the wise who are addressed in the volitional form of this word (see 1:8).”

Two ends of one spectrum, not two audiences. [S] Moderate–high. Full working at §29. Nothing further is needed on this.

20 · ⬆⬆ Proverbs 2’s structure — the alphabetic pattern is not laid over the syntax; it is the syntax

This is the largest gain of the day, and it goes well beyond what Part 1 recorded.

Part 1 had the 22 verses, the ʾālep/lāmed strophe-heads and Skehan’s 4·4·3 ‖ 4·4·3. Waltke has all of that independently — “two equal halves (2:1–11, 12–22), each half consisting of two stanzas of four verses and a climactic third of three verses” — and then adds the thing that matters:

“After the address, each stanza of vv. 1–11 begins with the first letter of the Hebrew alphabet, ʾaleph (ʾim ‘if,’ ʾāz ‘then’ [twice]), and each stanza of the second half with its twelfth consonant, lamed (lehaṣṣîlekā ‘to deliver you’ [twice] and lemaʿan ‘so that’).” [S]

The six stanza-initial letters are the six logical particles.

Half Stanza Opening word Letter Function
ʾālep 2:1–4 ʾim א the condition
2:5–8 ʾāz א first “then”
2:9–11 ʾāz א second “then”
lāmed 2:12–15 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā ל first “to deliver you”
2:16–19 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā ל second “to deliver you”
2:20–22 lĕmaʿan ל “so that”

So the chapter’s logic and its alphabet are the same six words. [T] on the words; [S] on the reading. This report and the sermon plan both treated the letters as a formal overlay on a syntactic skeleton. They are the skeleton. That is a materially better observation and it belongs in the sermon.

And Waltke supplies the internal patterns of both halves

First half — an alternating A-B-C ‖ A′-B′-C′:

A v.5 ʾāz tābîn Summary: religious education
B v.6 kî Substantiation: the LORD gives wisdom
C v.8 nṣr / šmr Result: divine protection
A′ v.9 ʾāz tābîn Summary: ethical education
B′ v.10 kî Substantiation: wisdom enters the heart
C′ v.11 šmr / nṣr Result: character protection

This confirms the report’s “vertical then horizontal” finding and gives it a name. Waltke: “A personal relationship with the LORD (vv. 5–8) entails an intuitive understanding of what is right in human relationships (vv. 9–11).”

And Van Leeuwen’s parallel is a gift for the pulpit: “the movement from the divine to the human is fundamental to biblical faith” — the Decalogue, then the Lord’s Prayer (Matt 6:9–10 God-ward, then 6:11–13 human-ward). One caution: the Exodus reference as transmitted reads “20:1–7 followed by 20:8–11”, which looks like a slip — the divine/human division of the Decalogue is normally 20:1–11 / 20:12–17. The point is sound; check the reference before quoting it.

Second half — the same shape:

A v.12 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā deliver from perverse men
B v.13 ʿzb — “who abandon” straight paths
C vv.14–15 maʿgal their crooked tracks
A′ v.16 lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā deliver from the strange woman
B′ v.17 ʿzb — “who abandons” the partner of her youth
C′ v.18 maʿgal her deadly tracks

“Both stanzas end with the final word ‘tracks.’” [T] — confirmed: v.15 ends bĕmaʿgĕlôtām, v.18 ends maʿgĕlōtêhā.

23 · ⚠ QUALIFICATION — “in the deep structure of meaning, this casuistic construction is equivalent to an imperatival construction”

This is the item that requires most care, because it touches a finding this project has used repeatedly.

Waltke: “The expanded conditional clauses introduced by if govern the glorious consequences developed in the rest of the poem… In the deep structure of meaning, this casuistic construction is equivalent to an imperatival construction; both aim to persuade.” [S] And note he calls 2:1 “this second command to internalize the parental teaching.”

What is unaffected. The grammatical fact stands and Waltke does not dispute it: there is no imperative form addressed to the son in Proverbs 2. Confirmed by morphological search on 17 August and re-confirmed against a contrary claim on 21 August. The verbs are prefixed imperfects under ʾim.

What is qualified — and it is my inference, not the datum. The report and the sermon plan have used the imperative-count as though it showed that the son is not being urged to act. Waltke is right that it shows nothing of the kind: a casuistic construction persuades, and “if you seek it like silver” is an appeal however it is parsed.

The honest reformulation. The anti-moralism argument does not rest on “no commands, therefore no effort required” — the chapter has six effortful verbs and Waltke reads them as functionally imperatival. It rests on the kî of v.6: the conditions are strenuous because the LORD gives, not in order to make him give. The imperative count is a supporting observation about form, not the load-bearing one.

This is exactly the error the aide-mémoire’s “keep layered claims apart” rule was written for, and it is worth recording that the rule caught something within a day of being written. Three layers: (1) no imperative forms — fact; (2) the construction is conditional rather than commanding — fact; (3) therefore the son is not being urged — wrong, and Waltke says so. Layer 3 has been removed from the report and the sermon plan.

25 · ⚠ A caution worth carrying — divine names may be parallelism-driven

Waltke: “No point, however, should be made of the use of ʾelōhîm in 2:5 and 30:9, because the name is the required parallel to LORD, ‘as frequently in the Psalter.’” [S]

Nothing in this project rests on ʾĕlōhîm at either verse, so no finding falls. But the principle is a live check on a kind of argument this project makes often — divine-name distribution as evidence.

Applied to the two places it might bite:

  • Prov 30:9’s šēm ʾĕlōhāy — parallels mî YHWH. Waltke’s caution applies; the report makes nothing of it. ✓
  • Prov 30:5’s ʾĕlôah, the only occurrence in Proverbs — the caution does not obviously apply, because the point there is not the bare presence of a divine name but a substitution: the verse quotes Ps 18:31, where the source reads YHWH, and swaps it. A change against a known source is different evidence from a choice within a parallelism. The finding stands; the caution is recorded beside it.

PART 3 · Waltke on Proverbs 1:1–5 — 22 August

The 1:5 question, put again with the right reference attached. It is answered, and the answer is that the question offered a false choice.

29 · ✅ RESOLVED — Proverbs 1:5, and both options were right

The fork this project posed was: (a) a second addressee, or (b) an aside/invitation. Waltke has both, and shows why they are not alternatives.

Waltke Verdict
(a) a second addressee “On the other end of the spectrum of addressees stands the wise (ḥākām).” And of the preamble: “its addressees, namely, Israel’s educable youth (vv. 4–5).” Right. The wise are named, not overheard.
(b) an invitation “By using the volitional form of the word ‘to hear’… The force of the volitional form, which varies from command to request, functions here as an invitation, since the wise want to obey such a command.” Right, and the verb-form is why.
(c) the thing neither option contained “By using the volitional form… the preamble subtly includes the son among the wise who are addressed in the volitional form of this word (see 1:8).” The two are one spectrum, not two audiences.

[S] throughout. Moderate–high.

So the report’s lean to (b) is confirmed on the verb-form and corrected on its implication. The wise are not eavesdropping on a book written for somebody else. They stand at the far end of the addressee-spectrum, and are invited rather than commanded because they would come anyway.

Homiletically this is warmer than the reading it replaces, and the sermon-2 plan has been corrected: version 1 said v.5 “lets them in only as learners, not as the target audience.” Too grudging. They are the target audience, at the other end of it.

30 · ⬆⬆ And the structural key the question had been groping for

Waltke: “The inclusio of ‘wisdom’ and ‘wise’ in vv. 2a and 5a and of ‘insight’ and ‘insightful’ in vv. 2b and 5b moves the unit from the substance of wisdom to the person who incarnates it.” [S]

v.2 v.5
a-colon ḥokmâ — wisdom ḥākām — the wise man
b-colon bînâ — insight nābôn — the insightful man

vv.2–5 are a bracketed unit, and the movement across the bracket is from a quality to a person. [T] on the four words. So v.5 is not the interruption this report called it — it is the closing bracket. The report had two inclusios (the lĕhābîn bracket at 2b/6a and the ḥokmâ ûmûsār bracket at 2a/7b); this is a third, and it is the one that explains v.5.

Plus a catchword chain Waltke supplies and the report did not have [S]: mûsār closes v.2a and stands second in v.3a · daʿat links vv.2a and 4b · the root lqḥ links v.3 (“accept”) and v.5 (“learning”). An alternating stitch — 2→3, 2→4, 3→5 — inside the 2∥5 inclusio.

And a small grammatical gift: the infinitives hang off v.1 because a verb is elided — “The proverbs of Solomon … [were collected] to know wisdom…” [S] That explains how a title governs five purpose-clauses with no finite verb in sight.

31 · ★ Two further structural findings

The preamble is itself a janus. “Since the preamble implicitly commends the work, it forms a janus (or transition) to the prologue (1:8–9:18).” [S] Waltke uses the same term of Proverbs 2 — so on his reading the book opens with two consecutive transitional poems, 1:1–7 and chapter 2. A pattern worth watching; it does not yet clear the ≥3-pericope gate.

And the hinge-word is named. “The catchword ‘listen’ in v. 5 and v. 8 strengthens the connection between the preamble and the prologue.” [S] v.5’s yišmaʿ → 1:8’s šĕmaʿ, same root, same volitional register. [T] This is the mechanism behind “subtly includes the son among the wise” — and a Move 4 link the report lacked. Sermon 2 can end on it and the congregation hears sermon 4 coming.

32 · ⬆ A third reading of 1:3b — and it completes rather than competes

Part 1 corrected this report’s “emphasis” reading to NET’s adverbial accusatives of manner. Waltke supplies the reason the specification was needed:

“Since wisdom and its co-referential terms can be malevolent, not benevolent, v.3b qualifies the instruction as that which serves and heals the community: what is right, and just, and fair.” [S]

NET says how the discipline is exercised; Waltke says why it had to be said. They complement.

And note what Waltke has just done. “Wisdom and its co-referential terms can be malevolent” — that is his own statement of the two-edged-vocabulary finding, made about these very verses. He glosses ʿormâ as “shrewd, or cunning” and mĕzimmâ as “discretion, or caginess.” A third independent line of support for the ʿormâ material, from the commentator the project treats as its most authoritative.

33 · ★ The naʿar’s range, with legal markers

[S] The term “places the morally brainless pethî in an age group that extends from infancy (Exod 2:6) to a seventeen-year-old (Gen 37:2), to a thirty-year-old (Gen 41:12) — and so presumably to any age before being reckoned an elder.” Accountable at twenty (Num 1:3); no temple service until thirty (Num 4:3). “In this book the naʿar is on the threshold of maturity.”

So the word marks liminality, not adolescence — which widens the sermon’s addressee well beyond the young, and matches the v.5 finding: the book addresses a spectrum.
