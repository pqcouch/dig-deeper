# The Turn at Proverbs 15:28–16:15

*How the first Solomonic collection hands over from its first half to its second: an exegetical reading of the transition unit, with the vocabulary evidence that identifies it.*

***

## Summary

The first half of the Solomonic collection trains the reader to sort the world into the righteous and the wicked. That idiom then disappears: the righteous/wicked antithesis occurs 28 times in 10:1–15:33 and twice in 16:16–22:16.

This document argues that the antithesis does not fade because its subject is exhausted, but because **its function is transferred**. Across 15:28–16:15 the reader is moved from observer to listener, told that his own perception is unreliable, and shown the work of discrimination passing to YHWH, who weighs spirits, and to the king, who administers judgment. What the second half then supplies is what a reader needs once he can no longer trust his own classifications: discretion, discernment of motive, and competence in a world he cannot read clearly.

The reading is offered as a reading of the final form. It is not demonstrated by the counts; the counts identify where to look.

***

## The Vocabulary Evidence

Two independent quantitative findings locate the transition and establish that something changes there.

**The antithesis is confined to the first half.** Verses in which צַדִּיק stands in one colon and רָשָׁע in the other:

| | Verses | With antithesis | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10:1–15:33 | 184 | 28 | 15.2% |
| 16:1–15 | 15 | 0 | 0.0% |
| 16:16–22:16 | 176 | 2 | 1.1% |

Fisher's exact test on the two halves: p = 3.9 × 10⁻⁷. The wise/fool antithesis follows the same shape, 11 against 1.

Model comparison confirms that a step at 16:1 fits this better than a gradual decline for three of four vocabulary features tested, with a held-out portion (20:5–22:16) reserved and untouched during fitting. Where the changepoint was left free, three of four features placed it in the last third of chapter 15 — slightly before 16:1, and consistent with treating 15:28–33 as transitional rather than as the close of the first half.

**The transition unit is unlike anything around it.** Comparing 15:28–16:15 with the remaining 354 verses of the collection:

| Lemma | In the unit | Elsewhere |
|---|---|---|
| יְהוָה | 11/21 = 52.4% | 44/354 = 12.4% |
| מֶלֶךְ | 5/21 = 23.8% | 9/354 = 2.5% |
| מִשְׁפָּט | 3/21 = 14.3% | 9/354 = 2.5% |
| לֵב | 6/21 = 28.6% | 41/354 = 11.6% |
| שׁמע | 3/21 = 14.3% | 8/354 = 2.3% |

YHWH is four times denser here than anywhere else; king and מִשְׁפָּט are roughly ten times denser. This is the most theologically saturated block in the collection, and the concentration of לֵב and שׁמע alongside it is the clue to what the block is doing.

***

## The Unit in Four Movements

Taking Heim's editorial clusters 8.13 (15:28–33), 8.14 (16:1–9), 8.15 (16:10–11) and 8.16 (16:12–15) together.

### 1. It opens in the old voice — 15:28–29

> לֵב צַדִּיק יֶהְגֶּה לַעֲנוֹת / וּפִי רְשָׁעִים יַבִּיעַ רָעוֹת
> רָחוֹק יְהוָה מֵרְשָׁעִים / וּתְפִלַּת צַדִּיקִים יִשְׁמָע

Both verses run the righteous/wicked antithesis, and 15:29 places YHWH within it — far from the wicked, hearing the prayer of the righteous. These are effectively the last full instances of the idiom that has governed since 10:1. The unit begins by speaking the language it is about to set aside.

### 2. The reader becomes a listener — 15:30–32

The vocabulary switches to reception. אֹזֶן שֹׁמַעַת תּוֹכַחַת חַיִּים — an ear that hears life-giving reproof. Then 15:32: פּוֹרֵעַ מוּסָר מוֹאֵס נַפְשׁוֹ, וְשׁוֹמֵעַ תּוֹכַחַת קוֹנֶה לֵּב — the one who hears reproof *acquires a heart*.

That last phrase matters. Until now the reader has stood outside the material, sorting other people into two classes. Here he is addressed as someone who does not yet possess understanding and must acquire it. The change of position is the hinge of the whole unit.

### 3. His own perception is disqualified — 15:33–16:9

15:33 makes the fear of YHWH the instruction of wisdom and puts humility before honour. Then eight of the next nine verses name YHWH.

The block is framed by an inclusio on לֵב:

> **16:1** לְאָדָם מַעַרְכֵי לֵב / וּמֵיְהוָה מַעֲנֵה לָשׁוֹן
> **16:9** לֵב אָדָם יְחַשֵּׁב דַּרְכּוֹ / וַיהוָה יָכִין צַעֲדוֹ

Both say the same thing: the heart plans, YHWH disposes. Inside that frame stands the decisive verse:

> **16:2** כָּל־דַּרְכֵי־אִישׁ זַךְ בְּעֵינָיו / וְתֹכֵן רוּחוֹת יְהוָה

Every way of a man is pure *in his own eyes*, but YHWH weighs the spirits. The reader who has spent six chapters confidently distinguishing the righteous from the wicked is told that his own self-assessment is not reliable evidence.

### 4. Discrimination passes to YHWH and the king — 16:10–15

The final movement hands the sorting function on. The king's lips do not betray in judgment (16:10); the abomination of kings is doing wickedness, for the throne is established by צְדָקָה (16:12); kings delight in righteous lips (16:13).

The arrangement is deliberate. 16:10 speaks of the king, 16:12–15 of kings — and between them, 16:11:

> פֶּלֶס וּמֹאזְנֵי מִשְׁפָּט לַיהוָה / מַעֲשֵׂהוּ כָּל־אַבְנֵי־כִיס

The balance and scales of judgment are YHWH's. The royal block is built around a YHWH saying, so that the king's discernment is not an alternative authority but a derived one.

Note the imagery running through the unit. YHWH weighs (תֹכֵן) spirits at 16:2; the scales (מֹאזְנֵי) of judgment are his at 16:11. Different roots, one semantic field: the assessment of what cannot be seen from outside, applied first to the human spirit and then to justice in the community.

***

## What the Turn Is For

The righteous/wicked antithesis is an instrument for training perception. It gives a beginner a serviceable map: there are two kinds of people, their paths diverge, and the ends differ. It works precisely because the reader stands outside the picture and sorts others.

The transition unit dismantles that position in three moves. It makes the reader a listener who must still acquire a heart. It tells him his own eyes are not to be trusted. And it shows the work of discrimination being carried out by YHWH, who weighs spirits, and by the king, who administers judgment under him.

**The antithesis therefore does not disappear for want of subject matter. Its function has been reassigned.** In the first half the reader does the discriminating; after 16:1 the discriminating is done to him.

This accounts for what the second half then contains. If you can no longer rely on your own classification, what you need is not a sharper scheme but the skills of someone operating with limited sight: discretion in speech, judgment of motive, care with witnesses and counsel, competence in commerce and the household, and a working understanding of courts and kings. That is precisely the material of 16:16–22:16 — which is why Ansberry can describe the second half as moving from the general to the specific and from the conceptual to the concrete, and why its characteristic settings are judicial and courtly.

***

## Two Details Explained

This reading resolves two features noticed earlier in the project which had no explanation.

**Why 21:2 restates 16:2.** The hinge principle reaches forward into the second half exactly once, and almost verbatim:

> **16:2a** כָּל־דַּרְכֵי־אִישׁ זַךְ בְּעֵינָיו — all the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes
> **21:2a** כָּל־דֶּרֶךְ־אִישׁ יָשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו — every way of a man is right in his own eyes

The two share six of eight content lemmas, differing in זַךְ / יָשָׁר and, in the second colon, רוּחוֹת / לִבּוֹת: YHWH weighs spirits, then hearts. On the present reading this is not decoration. The principle that disqualified the reader's self-assessment has to be reasserted inside the material where it does its work. Heim, arguing from cluster analysis rather than repetition, reads 21:1–4 as a unit in which the LORD probes the hidden thought of the publicly incontestable hypocrite, and describes its concern as appearance against real conviction.

Note also the fuller triad. 12:15a has דֶּרֶךְ אֱוִיל יָשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו — the way of a *fool* is right in his own eyes. In the first half the trait diagnoses folly in someone else. By 16:2 and 21:2 the same predicate is applied to every man, the reader included. That is the transfer of function in a single clause.

**Why the rich man's wall is imagined.** 18:11 repeats 10:15a verbatim — הוֹן עָשִׁיר קִרְיַת עֻזּוֹ — and then adds וּכְחוֹמָה נִשְׂגָּבָה בְּמַשְׂכִּיתוֹ, like a high wall *in his imagination*. Immediately before it, 18:10 makes the name of YHWH a מִגְדַּל־עֹז into which the righteous runs and is safe.

The rich man of 18:11 is a case study in the failure 16:2 names: a man whose security is pure in his own eyes. Murphy holds that 18:11 and 10:15 cannot be read without the points made in 18:10 and 18:12; Whybray took 18:10 as a deliberate correction of an older secular saying; Heim describes the wealth of 18:11 as a figment of the rich person's imagination. Meinhold notes the root-play carrying it into the next verse: נִשְׂגָּבָה (שׂגב, to be safe, literally to be high) against יִגְבַּהּ (גבה, to be proud, literally to be high) in 18:12.

***

## Status, Limits and Alternatives

**What is measured and what is read.** The vocabulary distributions are measured, and the effects are large and reproducible. The account of *why* the vocabulary changes is a reading. The counts identify the transition; they do not establish its purpose.

**An alternative that this does not exclude.** The two halves may have had separate origins, with the observed contrast reflecting the character of two source collections rather than a designed progression. Nothing here rules that out. It would not, however, dissolve the question: the material has been arranged as it stands, and the arrangement can be read whether or not its parts began together.

**Direction of dependence remains undemonstrable.** Nothing establishes which of a repeated pair was composed first.

**One caution on the counts.** מֶלֶךְ and מִשְׁפָּט are dense within the unit but rare in absolute terms elsewhere (9 verses each in 354). The proportions are striking; the underlying numbers are small.

**Relation to the companion study.** A separate document, *Repeated Bicola in Proverbs 10–22*, tests whether the distribution of repeated sayings marks the two-part division. It does not: five tests returned chance results. The present argument rests on vocabulary composition, not on repetition, and is untouched by those negative findings. The two documents should be read together — the earlier one establishes what cannot be claimed, this one what can.
