Point & Purpose: Proverbs — Series Introduction

Sermon one: a whole-book survey, no single base text · Series: nine sermons in all Translation: ESV (Anglicised) · Date: 21 August 2026 · Version 2 Exegesis consumed: ten completed /dig-deeper runs covering every section of Proverbs; proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis; book-overview-proverbs v1.2; proverbs-collections-assessment.

What changed in version 2. The series scheme is now the preacher’s own: 1:1–7 · ch. 2 · chs. 1–9 · Solomon 1 · Words of the Wise · Solomon 2 · the two “kings” · the closing poem, after this introduction. Three consequences, all worked below. The Words of the Wise is restored and the v1 gap-flag is withdrawn. Solomon 1 and Solomon 2 are each preached whole, which changes their internal shape from a problem into a gift. And the Big Question now recurs in every sermon, so each block is given its own answer to it — that is the largest single addition to this document.

Phase 0 — the scope decision

A series of nine. This document produces the governing series backbone, the per-sermon answers to the Big Question, and the full point-purpose for sermon one.

Sermon one is a whole-book survey with no single base text, by request. That is the hardest of the formats and it carries one specific risk, named here so the structure can be built against it: survey sermons drift into lecture. The guard is that this sermon has the same obligations as any other — one dominant idea, a felt need surfaced early, a response pressed at the end. If in preparation it starts to feel like a tour of findings rather than a single argument, that is the drift, and the fix is to cut material rather than reorganise it.

The overlap in the frame, and how to divide the labour

1:1–7 and chapter 2 are each preached separately and are also inside chapters 1–9. That is a deliberate zoom-out, not an accident, but it will produce three overlapping sermons unless the division of labour is fixed in advance. Fix it now:

Sermon Question it owns Material that belongs to it alone
2 · 1:1–7 What is this book for? The purpose statement, the named addressee (the simple, not the sage), the motto at 1:7, and v.6’s warning that the book will need interpreting rather than merely obeying
3 · Chapter 2 How is wisdom got? The chapter as one Hebrew sentence; the kî of v.6 making the gift the ground of the search; the two lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā deliverances that set the agenda for chs. 3–7
4 · Chapters 1–9 Who is calling? The two women and their crossing paths; the three escalating public calls (1:20–21 → 8:1–3 → 9:3); the father falling silent after 7:27; chapter 9 as the hinge with no vocative in it

What it is for · how you get it · who is calling. Kept to those three, the sermons do not compete.

One consequence for sermon one: its opening movement reads 1:1–7, which is sermon two’s text. Make that a handoff rather than a collision — read it, take the motto and the addressee from it, and say plainly that next week it gets worked properly. The congregation hearing the same text twice in two weeks, differently, is a feature.

THE GOVERNING SERIES BACKBONE

Series Fallen Condition Focus

We want to be competent, and we cannot tell whether we are. We assess ourselves by our own eyes — and our own eyes are the one instrument this book says cannot be trusted.

Anchored, and countably so. “In his own eyes” (bəʿênāyw) is forbidden in the second person at 3:7 — “Be not wise in your own eyes” — and then described happening, in the third person, eight times: 12:15 · 16:2 · 21:2 · 26:5, 12, 16 · 28:11 · 30:12. Under the new scheme an instance falls inside six of the eight blocks. This is not a modern felt-need imported into the text; it is a thread the book runs from the frame to the last chapter.

Series Teaching Point

The LORD gives wisdom to people who cannot certify they have it — and the wisdom he finally gave was a person.

Subject: Where does wisdom come from, and how would you know you had it? Complement: It is given by the LORD (2:6), never verified by the self, and its final form is Christ, “the wisdom of God” (1 Cor 1:24, 30).

Series Purpose Statement

To move the congregation from reading Proverbs as a manual for self-improvement to receiving it as a gift that dismantles self-assessment — so that the confident are unsettled before the series begins, the anxious are relieved of the burden of grading themselves, and both are pointed to Christ as the wisdom of God rather than as the standard they must reach.

Differentiated: for the capable, that competence is not the same as wisdom and cannot certify itself; for the struggling, that the book’s own last sage said he had not learned wisdom, and was not thereby disqualified; for the leader, that the book ends by telling a king to open his mouth for people who have none; for the outsider, that a book which sounds like advice turns out to be about a gift.

The Big Question — and how each sermon answers it

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

The request is that it recur in every sermon, so each block is given its own answer below. This is what turns nine sermons into a series rather than nine sermons on the same book: the question is constant, the answer develops, and the congregation can feel the development.

A working discipline. Ask it early in each sermon — ideally in the first two minutes, in the same words each time — and answer it from that week’s text alone. Do not import next week’s answer. The cumulative effect depends on each answer being partial.

# Sermon Text How this week answers the Big Question
1 Introduction whole book You wouldn’t — and that is the point. This book ends in a confession, not a diploma.
2 Why this book exists 1:1–7 You would start by admitting you are the one it is addressed to. It is written for the simple, not the sage — and its motto is the fear of the LORD.
3 The agenda ch. 2 You would not know by checking. You would know by receiving — “for the LORD gives wisdom” (2:6) grounds the search rather than rewarding it.
4 Two voices in the street chs. 1–9 Wisdom is not a quality you audit; it is a voice you obey — and two of them are calling in the same street, both public, both with a house and a table.
5 The Proverbs of Solomon (1) 10:1–22:16 You would want the two-column sort to place you — and the collection breaks it from inside: “there is a way that seems right” (14:12), “who can say, I have made my heart clean?” (20:9), “all a man’s ways are pure in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirits” (16:2).
6 The Words of the Wise 22:17–24:34 You would know by where your trust is placed, not by what you have mastered — wisdom borrowed from Egypt is re-founded at 22:19 on trust in the LORD.
7 The Proverbs of Solomon (2) 25:1–29:27 You would have to judge for yourself — and 25–27 withholds the verdict to make you. Then 28–29 hands the standard back, and it is tôrāh.
8 The two “kings” 30:1–31:9 The wisest man in the book says he does not know — and then receives a word he did not write, and prays.
9 The closing poem 31:10–31 You would never say it of yourself. Somebody else would say it of you — and the poem’s one imperative is “give her praise.”

Two notes the new block scheme makes necessary

Sermon 5 — Solomon 1 whole (10:1–22:16), and the formal data hands you the structure

Three hundred and seventy-five verses in one sermon looks impossible until you see that the collection’s own measurements give it a three-movement shape:

Movement Text The measurement
The world sorted into two columns 10:1–15:33 81.0% antithetical — the highest in the book. ṣaddîq runs 13 · 8 · 8 · 5 · 2 · 3 across the six chapters and then reaches zero. Chapter 13 has no divine name in twenty-five verses — the longest God-free stretch in the sentence literature.
The crest, where God takes over 15:33–16:15 YHWH runs 4 · 2 · 2 · 0 · 3 · 9, and eight of the first nine verses of chapter 16 name him. Four verbatim links cross the seam.
And you cannot place yourself in it 16:1–22:16 20.4% antithetical — the sorting stops. The collection frames itself on self-knowledge: 16:2 ∥ 21:2, near-verbatim, “pure/upright in his own eyes… but the LORD weighs.”

The sermon’s turn is at the crest, and the turn is the series FCF arriving in its sharpest form. [T] on every figure.

A bonus worth knowing. The 15:33/16:1 boundary is the one seam this project could not settle — a certain formal shift but a contested boundary, with no superscription and a significant body of scholarship declining to treat it as a seam. Preaching Solomon 1 whole puts that dispute inside the sermon rather than between two sermons, which is exactly where it can be handled honestly: the texture changes here, and scholars disagree whether that makes it a new collection.

Sermon 7 — Solomon 2 whole (25:1–29:27), and the securest seam in the book falls inside it

Movement Text The measurement
The section that will not tell you the answer 25:1–27:27 7.3% antithetical — the lowest in the book. 30% simile. One divine name in eighty-three verses. The Leitwort is kābēd, honour. 26:4–5 is the section explaining itself: two consecutive verses giving contradictory advice, so the reader must judge.
The section that hands the standard back 28:1–29:27 60% antithetical — a ten-fold jump, the largest single-chapter change anywhere in Proverbs 10–29. Simile drops to 2%. Five occurrences of absolute tôrāh against none in 25–27, framed 28:4 ∥ 29:18.

The 27:27/28:1 seam is the securest boundary in the book — four independent measures break at the same line. Preaching Solomon 2 whole makes that break the sermon’s hinge. And 25:21–22 sits in the first movement: the Covenant Code’s enemy-duty (Exod 23:5, the identical inflected form), quoted by Paul at Rom 12:20 and alluded to by Jesus at Matt 5:44. It answers the question a thoughtful hearer actually asks: where does the Old Testament ever say love your enemy?

SERMON ONE — POINT & PURPOSE

Fallen Condition Focus

As the series FCF, sharpened for the first hearing: everyone in the room has some competence they are quietly proud of and some they are quietly anxious about — and both the pride and the anxiety rest on a self-assessment this book is about to disqualify.

Teaching Point

Proverbs will make you competent and then refuse to let you certify it — because wisdom is a gift the LORD gives, and the wisdom he finally gave was Christ.

Subject: What is this book for, and what will it do to me? Complement: It forms you, and then dismantles your ability to grade the formation — driving you to the giver rather than to the achievement.

Purpose Statement

To set the congregation up to hear the whole series rightly — by naming, in week one and by name, the thing the book does that the standard Proverbs sermon reverses. To send them home unable to read Proverbs as advice, and glad about it.

Candidate Big Question

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

Ask it in these words. It returns in all eight sermons that follow, and week one is where the congregation learns that the question is serious.

Proposed structure — four movements

The sermon has no single base text, so its structure is the book’s own shape. That is what keeps it a sermon rather than a survey: the movement is the book’s movement, not a tour of topics.

Movement Text Function
1. The book tells you what it is for 1:1–7 (read in full) The stated purpose, the named addressee — the simple, not the sage — and the motto. Note v.6: it announces it will need interpreting. A book that opens by promising riddles is not a rule-book. Hand off explicitly: “we will take this properly next week.” Surfaces the Big Question.
2. And it delivers 1:8–9:18 in summary; a short catena from 10–29 Honour what the book actually gives. Nine chapters of formation and roughly nine hundred sayings, and they work. Do not spend this movement apologising for Proverbs’ practicality — the congregation must feel the gift before the turn. Preview the eight sermons here, briefly.
3. Then it takes away your ability to grade yourself 3:7 → 12:15 → 16:2 / 21:2 → 26:12 → 28:11 → 30:12 The turn, and the sermon’s centre. Read the thread aloud in sequence — the frame forbids it in the second person, the collections then describe it happening eight times. Land on 16:2. The FCF becomes acute here.
4. So the book ends where it ends 30:2–3, 5–6 · 31:8–9 · 31:30–31 A confession of ignorance from the last sage; a word he did not write and may not add to; a mouth opened for people who have none; and one imperative — give her praise. Then Christ as the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24, 30; Matt 12:42). Press the PS.

Suggested public reading: 1:1–7 in full, then a short catena of 16:2 · 30:2–3 · 31:30. Three texts from the three ends of the book; it takes ninety seconds and it previews the whole sermon.

Keep honest

1 · The survey-becomes-lecture trap — the specific risk of this format. The temptation is to show the congregation the findings: the marker densities, the antithesis percentages, the Septuagint arrangement. None of that belongs in the pulpit. It is the evidence for the claim, not the claim. One statistic at most, and only if it lands emotionally rather than academically. (The percentages in the two notes above are for sermon preparation — they belong in your study, not in your mouth.)

2 · Moralism, one level up. The obvious moralism is “be wise.” The subtler and likelier failure here is preaching movement 3 as “so don’t be arrogant” — which turns a claim about the impossibility of self-assessment into a new self-assessment task. The corrective is 2:6, and it is next-but-one week’s text: “For the LORD gives wisdom”, placed inside the conditional sentence as its ground, not its reward. The chapter that sets the syllabus has no imperatives addressed to the son at all.

3 · Proverbs read as promises. The congregation will arrive with 3:5–6 and 22:6 in their pockets, and probably with a disappointment attached to one of them. Name the genre in movement 1 — generally-true observations about how God’s world runs, not guarantees — or the series will spend eight weeks fighting it. 1:6’s “riddles” is the text that authorises the point.

4 · Do not make Solomon the hero. 1:1 invites it, and the canonical freight is double: 1 Kgs 3’s granted wisdom and 1 Kgs 11’s wisdom not kept. The four typology tests were run across all ten dig-deeper reports and returned trajectory and contrast almost everywhere, typology almost nowhere. The honest Christ-connection is 1 Cor 1:24, 30 and Matt 12:42 — Christ as the wisdom of God and the greater-than-Solomon — not Solomon as a type.

5 · The scare quotes on “the two kings” are doing real work. Lemuel is a king; Agur is not — and the series title should not accidentally teach otherwise. What Agur does have is the formula nəʾum haggeber, “the man declares”, which occurs elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible only of Balaam (Num 24:3, 15) and of David’s last words (2 Sam 23:1). An outsider who is not a king speaks in the diction of a foreign seer and of Israel’s dying king, and the book lets him. Worth a sentence in sermon 8, and worth not fumbling in sermon 1’s preview.

Anti-moralism gate

Test Result
Is the application “be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes. No human figure to imitate; the payoff is that self-certification is impossible and wisdom is given.
Is Christ the subject, or merely an example? Passes. The TP’s actor is the LORD who gives, and the complement lands on Christ as the wisdom of God — the subject, not an analogy.
Is the good news grounded in Christ’s saving work, not just information about God’s character? Passes, with a note. Movement 4 must actually reach Christ, not stop at “the book ends humbly.” If the sermon ends at 31:31 it has not cleared this gate. The last two minutes are load-bearing.

Provenance and hand-off

Every element is traceable to the completed exegesis and none is newly derived here. The FCF is macro-argument 2 of proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis, resting on the eight-pericope bəʿênāyw thread. The TP draws on the convergent Headline Findings of the 1:1–7, 2, 16:1–22:16 and 30:1–31:9 runs. The PS comes from those runs’ So What sections. The per-sermon Big-Question answers are drawn from each block’s own run. The two structural notes use the formal measures of the collections assessment and the 10–15, 16–22:16, 25–27 and 28–29 runs. The keep-honest items come from the runs’ Preaching Pitfalls.

Hand-off: the Big Question and the four-movement structure are the inputs to the one-sentence-sermon step and the manuscript. No outstanding decisions — the version 1 block-scheme gap is resolved by the restoration of the Words of the Wise.
