## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

1. **The section's Leitwort is *kābēd* — glory, honour, weight — and it runs from the first saying to the last.** `[T]` Nine occurrences across eight verses, evenly spread: the glory of God and of kings (25:2 ×2), the glory that is not glorious to seek (25:27 ×2), the honour that does not fit a fool (26:1, 8), the fool's provocation heavier than stone and sand (27:3 ×2), the servant who will be honoured (27:18). Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Structure (25:2 ∥ 25:27 is an inclusio built on it), Original Audience. *High confidence — countable.* **This, not "comparison," is what Proverbs 25–27 is about**: who has honour, who may seek it, who may not receive it, and who confers it.

2. **The strongest structural boundary in these three chapters is 27:22/27:23 — and it is the only one attested by both the composition and the transmission.** `[T]` The received Hebrew carries four *petuchah* markers (25:13, 25:20, 26:21, 27:22). The last of them sets off 27:23–27, and the resulting Masoretic paragraph **runs on past 27:27 into chapter 28, closing only at the *petuchah* after 28:4.** Independently, two lexical frames close at 27:21–22 (√*hll* at 27:1, 2, 21; *ʾĕwîl* at 27:3, 22). Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Vocabulary, and the checking stage. *High confidence on the data; moderate–high on the reading.* **✅ The one thing that could have undone this has been checked: BHS carries no *setumah* markers in Proverbs 27–28, so the *petuchah* map is complete where the finding rests.** **This vindicates the structural half of Ansberry's janus — and reverses a correction this project made against him on 17 August.** See Tool 3 and Book-Overview Tensions.

3. **The dominant comparative form in these chapters is the *unmarked* juxtaposition, not the simile — and the ESV's "like" is supplied.** `[T]` Of roughly 37 comparative sayings, only nine carry a comparative particle (*kə-* and/or *kēn*): 25:13; 26:1, 2, 8, 11, 18–19, 22; 27:8, 19. Every other comparison is two noun-phrases set side by side with nothing between them — "Apples of gold in settings of silver; a word spoken at its right time" (25:11). Surfaced by: Parallels, Translations, Genre, Original Languages. *High confidence — countable.* **The Hebrew makes the reader complete the comparison; the English does it for him.** This is a category-1 translation loss under the Three-Way Triage, and it has direct homiletical consequence (see Preaching Pitfalls).

4. **Proverbs 25–27 names God once in eighty-three verses — and it is the section that teaches the reader to judge for himself.** `[T]` *ʾĕlōhîm* at 25:2, YHWH at 25:22, and nowhere else. Set beside six antithetical sayings in eighty-two (against thirty-three in the fifty-five verses of 28–29), a picture emerges: the section systematically withholds the two things that had been settling cases for the reader — the divine verdict and the two-column sort. Surfaced by: Repetition, Parallels, Positional Necessity, Author's Purpose. *High confidence on the counts; moderate–high on the reading.* **26:4–5 sits at the centre of it, and is the section's own explanation of itself.**

5. **Proverbs 25:21 is the Covenant Code's enemy-duty, and it is where both Jesus and Paul go for "love your enemies."** `[T]` on the Hebrew; `[S]` on the NT links. Exod 23:5 commands help for the beast of **שֹׂנַאֲךָ** — "the one who hates you" — inside a block of courtroom law; Prov 25:21 takes **the identical inflected form** and commands feeding him, inside a cluster about lawsuits and false witness. Paul quotes it (Rom 12:20); Jesus alludes to it (Matt 5:44), where he answers a tradition that had added *"and hate your enemy"* to a Torah that never said it. Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Quotation/Allusion (full triad plus NT reception), Structure, So What. *High on the verbal data; moderate–high on the chain.* **It answers the question a thoughtful hearer actually asks: where does the Old Testament ever say love your enemy?**

*(The metallurgical thread — √*ṣrp* at 25:4 and 27:21, *sîgîm* at 25:4 and 26:23, one refining saying per chapter — was the fifth headline in the first issue of this report. Its lexical data are `[T]` and striking, and it is worked in full under Tool 7; but a library query has since confirmed the inclusio claim is unattested, so it is held at `[I]` moderate and gives up its place to a finding that has been checked.)*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level:** worked once in `dig-deeper-proverbs-sweep` and assumed here — the formation of a person who fears the LORD, addressed to "my son."

**Passage level.** Two purposes operate, and they are in tension in a way that is itself the point.

**The stated purpose is transmission.** 25:1 is the only superscription in the book that names a *process* rather than only a source: "These also are proverbs of Solomon **which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied** (*heʿtîqû*)." `[T]` The verb is not "wrote" or "spoke" but "moved across" — the collection announces, inside itself, that it has been handled. That is a purpose statement of a kind: this material is being *passed on*, and the passing-on is worth recording.

**The operative purpose is the formation of judgement.** This is `[I]`, but it rests on four countable facts, each of which is worked below:

| Fact | Count | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Divine-name sayings | **2 in 83 verses** (25:2 *ʾĕlōhîm*; 25:22 YHWH) | 11% of verses in 10–15; ~18% in 16–22:16 |
| Antithetical sayings | **6 of 82** (7%) | 33 of 55 in 28–29 (60%) |
| Comparative sayings | **~37 of 82** (45%) | rare in 28–29 |
| Comparisons carrying a particle | **9 of ~37** (24%) | — |

Put together: a section that mostly declines to name God, mostly declines to sort the world into righteous and wicked, mostly speaks in images, and mostly does not even tell you that an image *is* an image. Every one of those four is a withholding. What is withheld is the thing that would settle the case for the reader.

And the section says so. **26:4–5** places two contradictory admonitions in consecutive lines, each with its own motive clause, and the pair can only be deliberate: the second colon of v.4 and the second colon of v.5 are the only things that differ, and both are *pen*-clauses. `[T]` *High.* The reader who has been handed contradictory instructions must judge which situation he is in. That is what the whole section is training him to do.

**Book-overview note:** the overview's microscript holds. This extends it by naming the *pedagogical* function of the collection's formal profile rather than treating that profile as stylistic variation.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 22:17–24:34 (the Words of the Wise, in two collections) closes with the sluggard's vineyard, 24:30–34 — the book's second and last example story, told in the first person: *"I passed by… I saw… Then I saw and considered it; I looked and received instruction."* `[T]` That is not a proverb; it is a demonstration of how to derive one. And 24:21–22 has just placed the reader under "the LORD and the king," while 22:29 has told him a skilled man "will stand before kings."

So the preceding collection has done two things: it has put the reader in proximity to power, and it has *modelled the act of drawing a lesson from an observed scene*.

**Necessity answer.** Proverbs 25–27 is the exercise for which 24:30–34 was the worked example. `[I]` *Moderate–high.* It opens with seven verses on standing before a king (25:2–7), which is where 22:29 and 24:21 have deposited the reader — the sweep established this and it stands. But the deeper positional answer is formal: having shown the reader how to look at a field and draw a conclusion, the book now hands him eighty-two observations with the conclusions largely undrawn. The bare juxtaposition of 25:11 — *apples of gold in settings of silver; a word spoken at its right time* — is 24:32 ("I looked and received instruction") turned into a genre.

**Why not earlier, and why not later.** The sequence of the sentence literature is legible as a curriculum: 10–15 sorts the world into two columns and names the LORD often; 16:1–22:16 complicates the sort with divine sovereignty; 22:17–24:34 gives vocational instruction and teaches observation; **25–27 removes both the sort and the divine verdict and requires the reader to judge**; 28–29 restores the antithesis and the LORD — and adds absolute *tôrāh*, which occurs nowhere else in 10–29 — to a reader who can now do more than apply a rule. `[I]` *Moderate.* The section could not stand before 22:17–24:34, because the reader would have no method; it could not stand after 28–29, because 28–29 is the resolution.

**Implication for this passage.** A sermon on any single proverb in 25–27 is preaching a text whose position is part of its meaning: it is deliberately under-determined, and the preacher who supplies all the determination is undoing the passage's own work.

#### Immediate context

**Before:** 24:30–34, the sluggard's field, and 24:23–29 ("these also are sayings of the wise"). **After:** 28:1, "the wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are bold as a lion" — an antithesis with the righteous/wicked pair, on the first line, which is precisely the register 25–27 has withheld.

**Within the book:** the second Solomonic collection, 25:1–29:27, of which this is the first half. The seam at 27:27/28:1 is examined under Tool 3 and in Book-Overview Tensions; it is not as simple as the project's earlier documents assumed.

### 3. Structure

Two independent structures cross-cut these chapters, and keeping them apart is the main structural work of this report.

#### (a) The transmission structure: four *petuchot*

The Hebrew as supplied carries four *petuchah* markers (פ), at **25:13, 25:20, 26:21 and 27:22**. `[T]` on their presence in this text; `[S]` on what the convention signifies.

> **✅ Verified against BHS, 20 August: there are no *setumah* (ס) markers in Proverbs 27–28.** `[S]` The risk was that the Lexham export had dropped *setumot*, leaving the paragraph map incomplete — and specifically that a *setumah* at 27:27 would restore a transmission-level boundary at the "common division". **There is none.** The *petuchah*-only map is complete across the stretch on which the finding rests. *Chapters 25–26 were not separately checked; that would bear on the fine-grained map below, not on the boundary claim.* They yield:

| Paragraph | Verses |
|---|---|
| 1 | 25:1–13 |
| 2 | 25:14–20 |
| 3 | 25:21–26:21 |
| 4 | 26:22–27:22 |
| 5 | **27:23 – 28:4** |

The fifth is the finding. **There is no marker at the end of 27:27.** The next marker in the transmitted text falls after 28:4 — as recorded independently in the Solomon 2B report, whose paragraph table opens with "28:1–4". Those are not two paragraphs; they are one, and it begins at 27:23.

> **Correction to `dig-deeper-proverbs-28-1to29-27`.** That report's paragraph table lists **28:1–4** as its first unit. That was an artefact of the passage boundary: the report had chapters 28–29 in view and could not see that the paragraph opens at 27:23. The correct span is **27:23–28:4**. The report's *conclusions* are untouched — its argument was about 28:28/29:1, where the paragraphing runs straight through, and that stands. But the table should be amended.

### 4. Linking Words

For the sentence collections the governing connector is the parallelism itself, treated under Tool 5. Three classes of connector nonetheless do real work here, and one of them is easy to miss.

**Motive *pen* ("lest") — the section's characteristic connector.** 25:8, 25:10, 25:16, 25:17, 26:4, 26:5. `[T]` Six occurrences, and note the pattern: they cluster in the admonitions, and they come in *pairs* whose parallelism is the linking device — 25:16 *pen-tiśbāʿennû* / 25:17 *pen-yiśbāʿăkā* (you have your fill of it / he has his fill of you), and 26:4 *pen-tišweh-lô* / 26:5 *pen-yihyeh ḥākām* (lest you be like him / lest he be wise). In both pairs the *pen*-clause is where the two verses diverge. The connector is doing the structural work.

**Motive *kî* ("for") — sparse, and load-bearing where it appears.** 25:7 (grounding the prohibition of 25:6), 25:22 (grounding the feeding of the enemy), 26:25 (grounding the refusal of trust), 27:1 (grounding the ban on boasting), 27:24 (grounding the shepherd's diligence). `[T]` Five in eighty-three verses. Each stands at the head of a motive that the preacher should not skip: *because* it is better to be told "come up"; *because* the LORD will reward you; *because* there are seven abominations in his heart; *because* you do not know what a day may bring; *because* riches do not last for ever.

**The correlative *kēn* ("so") — only six times.** 26:1, 2, 8, 19; 27:8, 19. `[T]` This is the explicit "as X … so Y" formula, and it accounts for fewer than one comparison in six. See Tool 5.

**What the English adds.** In roughly twenty-eight comparative sayings the ESV supplies "like" or "as … so" where the Hebrew has no connector at all. Those supplied words are the most consequential "linking words" in the passage — and they are not in the text. See Tools 5, 8 and 12.

Everything else is bare juxtaposition. 25:11 in Hebrew is *"Apples of gold in settings of silver — a word spoken at its right time."* 25:12: *"A gold ring and an ornament of fine gold — a wise reprover to a listening ear."* 25:19: *"A bad tooth and a slipping foot — trust in a treacherous man in the day of trouble."* 27:17: *"Iron with iron grows sharp — and a man sharpens the face of his friend."* No verb, no particle, no copula. Two pictures set side by side and a silence between them.

**Why this matters.** Emblematic parallelism is normally described as "image in one line, application in the other." That description is correct but it conceals the crucial fact that in these chapters the *application* is not applied. The reader is handed both halves and must supply the third thing — the respect in which they are alike. That is the definition of an act of judgement, and it is what 26:4–5 says the reader must learn to perform.

Two further observations:

- **The comparisons are frequently absurd on purpose**, and the absurdity is the argument: snow in summer (26:1), a lame man's dangling legs (26:7), a thorn in a drunkard's hand (26:9), a dog seized by the ears (26:17), a stone bound into a sling (26:8). `[T]` Sustained from the sweep.
- **The *kə-* of 26:4–5 is not comparative.** *kəʾiwwaltô* is "according to his folly" — the *kaph* of norm or standard, not of similitude. `[T]` A sermon that renders it "like his folly" has misparsed the pair.

### 7. Vocabulary

#### *kābēd / kābôd* — the section's Leitwort

Nine occurrences across eight verses, one to three per chapter, spread evenly from the first saying to within five verses of the last: `[T]`

| Verse | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| 25:2 | *kəbōd … ûkəbōd* | the **glory** of God · the **glory** of kings |
| 25:27 | *kəbōdām … kābôd* | their **glory** · **glory/glorious** |
| 26:1 | *kābôd* | **honour** is not fitting for a fool |
| 26:8 | *kābôd* | giving **honour** to a fool |
| 27:3 | *kōbed … kābēd* | a stone is **heavy** · the fool's provocation is **heavier** |
| 27:18 | *yəkubbād* | he who guards his master will be **honoured** |

The root's two English faces — *weight* and *honour* — are one word in Hebrew, and the section exploits both. 27:3 uses the physical sense (a stone's *heaviness*) three verses after 27:2 has forbidden self-praise; the fool's exasperation is *weighty* in the same word by which honour is *weighty*. That is a category-1 translation loss: no English version can carry it, and the ESV does not try. *High confidence.*

**What the Leitwort does.** Read across its occurrences, the section makes a single sustained argument about honour:

1. God's glory is in *concealing*; a king's is in *searching out* (25:2). Glory belongs to both, differently.
2. Do not put yourself forward before the king; better to be told "come up" (25:6–7).
3. To search out one's own glory is not glorious (25:27) — the inclusio's closing move, and the reason 25:27 is textually hard: it is doing compressed frame-work.
4. Honour does not *fit* a fool, and giving it to him is like binding a stone into a sling (26:1, 8).
5. Let another praise you, and not your own mouth (27:2).
6. A man is assayed by the praise he receives (27:21).
7. The one who *guards his master* is honoured (27:18).

Honour is real, it is worth having, and it cannot be obtained by seeking it. It is conferred — by the king, by another's mouth, by God — never claimed. **This is the section's centre of gravity and the source of its Fallen Condition Focus.**

#### The refining thread

*ṣrp* (refine) — **25:4** *laṣṣōrēp*, "for the smith"; **27:21** *maṣrēp*, "crucible." The only two occurrences. `[T]`
*sîgîm* (dross) — **25:4**, **26:23**. The only two. `[T]`
*kesep* (silver) — 25:4, 25:11, 26:23, 27:21. Three of the four sit in this thread. `[T]`

One metallurgical saying per chapter, and the outer two share a root that occurs nowhere else in the section:

> **25:4–5** Take away the *dross* from the *silver*, and the *smith* has a vessel — take away the wicked from before the king, and his throne is established.
> **26:23** *Silver of dross* spread over earthenware — burning lips and an evil heart.
> **27:21** A *crucible* for *silver* and a furnace for gold — and a man according to his praise.

The movement is exact: refining makes a *vessel* fit for use (25:4); false plating makes a vessel *look* fit (26:23); the crucible reveals which a man is (27:21). And 27:21's second colon lands on *mahălālô* — closing the √*hll* frame of chapter 27. **The metallurgical thread and the praise-frame arrive at the same word.** `[T]` for the lexical data; `[I]` *moderate* for the claim that 25:4 ∥ 27:21 is a deliberate section inclusio — this is not attested in Ansberry and is routed to Open Questions.

#### Other terms

**Proper-noun inventory.** *Solomon* (25:1), *Hezekiah king of Judah* (25:1), *Sheol and Abaddon* (27:20). Nothing else in eighty-three verses — no place names, no gentilics, no personal names beyond the superscription. `[T]`

- **Solomon / Hezekiah (25:1).** Canonical freight, and it is not neutral. The canonical reader knows how Solomon's story ends (1 Kgs 11) and knows Hezekiah as the reforming king of 2 Kgs 18–20 — including the reform's own ambiguous close, when he shows the Babylonian envoys his treasure-house and is told it will all be carried away (2 Kgs 20:12–19). A collection of proverbs about not seeking one's own glory, transmitted by the men of *that* king, is at minimum a suggestive placement. `[I]` *Moderate — the connection is available to the canonical reader but the text does not press it; I have not re-read 2 Kgs 18–20 for this run, so it is flagged rather than pressed.* **The load-the-story rule is honoured by declining to make it load-bearing.**
- **Sheol and Abaddon (27:20).** Not routine scenery. *ʾăbaddôn* occurs six times in the Hebrew Bible; the pair *šəʾôl waʾăbaddôn* is a Job–Proverbs formation (Job 26:6; Prov 15:11; 27:20). Its canonical neighbourhood is the Writings' meditation on what lies beyond human searching — which is exactly 25:2–3's concern. `[I]` *Moderate–high.* See Tool 11, Move 4.

**Familiar words used in a Bible-specific way:**

- ***rēaʿ*** (25:8, 9, 17, 18; 26:19; 27:9, 10, 14, 17). Nine occurrences and the section's most frequent relational noun. ESV renders it "neighbour" and "friend" in the same chapters. It is neither exactly — it is the person you are in ordinary dealings with, whose house you can visit too often (25:17) and against whom you may be tempted to testify falsely (25:18). *High.*
- ***mədônîm*** (quarrelsome, contentious) — **three occurrences, one per chapter: 25:24, 26:21, 27:15.** `[T]` Two are *ʾēšet mədônîm*, a quarrelsome **wife** (25:24, 27:15); one is *ʾîš mədônîm*, a quarrelsome **man** (26:21). This matters pastorally and is worked under Difficult Verses.
- ***bəʿênāyw*** ("in his own eyes") — 26:5, 12, 16. `[T]` The phrase that binds chapter 26's first sixteen verses. Its force is self-assessment, and the section is systematically dismantling self-assessment (27:2, 27:21).
- ***nēzer*** (27:24) — not "wealth" but the consecrated diadem: the word for the high priest's plate (Exod 29:6; Lev 8:9) and for the royal crown at the coronation of Joash (2 Kgs 11:12) and in Ps 89:39; 132:18. *High on the word's range.* Its presence in a poem about goats is what generates the janus question. See Tools 3 and 11.
- ***pānîm*** — twelve occurrences, and three of them cluster at the chapter's close: 27:17 (*a man sharpens the **face** of his friend*), 27:19 (*as water, **face** to **face***), 27:23 (*know well the **face** of your flock*). `[T]` The ESV renders the last of these "the condition of your flocks," which is the sense but loses the word. See Christological Reading.

**Paronomasia check** (triggered by any word repeated three or more times): the *kābēd* cluster is the live case, and it is a genuine double-sense exploitation rather than a root-echo across semantic domains. *ḥēqer* (25:2, 3, 27) is straight repetition. No consonant-reversal claim is made — none clears the corroboration test.

### 8. Translations

#### 25:11 and the supplied "like" — the section-wide issue

| | 25:11 |
|---|---|
| **Hebrew** | *tappûḥê zāhāb bəmaśkiyyôt kāsep — dābār dābūr ʿal-ʾopnāyw* |
| **ESV** | "A word fitly spoken **is like** apples of gold in a setting of silver." |
| **NASB** | "**Like** apples of gold in settings of silver **is** a word spoken in right circumstances." |
| **KJV** | "A word fitly spoken **is like** apples of gold in pictures of silver." |

All three literal versions supply both a copula and a simile marker; the Hebrew has neither, and it puts the image first. The same holds at 25:12, 19, 25, 26, 28; 26:3, 6, 7, 9, 14, 17, 20, 21, 23, 27; 27:3, 9, 15, 17, 18, 21.

**This is not a translation error — it is unavoidable.** English cannot juxtapose two noun-phrases and leave the relation unstated; it must supply a verb. Under the Three-Way Triage this is **category 1, translation loss**: a feature of the Hebrew that no translation into any language can carry, and one the Greek loses as thoroughly as the English. It is reported here as a fact about the Hebrew, not as a reliability judgement.

**But it has a preaching consequence**, which is why it earns Headline status: the translations have already performed, on the reader's behalf, the interpretive act the Hebrew withholds. A preacher who has only the English will not notice that he is being handed a conclusion.

#### 26:23 — the revocalisation, and what it costs

| | |
|---|---|
| **MT (consonants and vowels)** | *kesep sîgîm məṣuppeh ʿal-ḥāreś* — "silver of dross spread over earthenware" |
| **ESV** | "Like the **glaze** covering an earthen vessel" — with the footnote, *"By revocalization; Hebrew silver of dross"* |

The ESV reads the consonants כספסגים as a single word, commonly explained from a cognate meaning "glaze". `[S]` *Moderate.*

> **✅ Ancient versions checked, 20 August — and both read two words.** `[S]`
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **LXX** | ἀργύριον διδόμενον μετὰ δόλου ὥσπερ ὄστρακον ἡγητέον — "silver given with deceit is to be reckoned as a potsherd" |
> | **Vulgate** | *Quomodo si **argento sordido** ornare velis vas fictile* — "as if you would adorn an earthen vessel with **impure silver**" |

>
> **The Vulgate is the clean witness:** *argento sordido* renders *kesep sîgîm* exactly — two words, silver and its impurity. The LXX also saw two elements (ἀργύριον … ὄστρακον, matching *kesep* … *ḥereś*), rendering *sîgîm* interpretively as "with deceit". **Neither ancient version supports a single word meaning "glaze."**
>
> **And modern scholarship has been turning back.** The emendation rests on Ugaritic *spsg*; the Zondervan Encyclopedia records that the phrase "has traditionally been emended to 'like glaze'… more recent scholarship has challenged this emendation, with the TNIV restoring the traditional rendering of 'silver dross'." `[S]`
>
> **So the ESV's footnote is best read as recording a conjecture, not a correction.** The MT vocalisation now has two ancient versions, a modern scholarly turn, and the structural argument below behind it. **Upgraded from moderate to moderate–high.** *The Peshitta was not checked.*

**The exegetical cost is structural, and it has not been noticed in this project's earlier work.** *sîgîm* occurs exactly twice in Proverbs 25–27: at 25:4 (*sîgîm mikkāsep*, "dross from silver") and here (*kesep sîgîm*, "silver of dross") — **the same two words in reverse order.** `[T]` On the MT vocalisation, 26:23 is a deliberate inversion of 25:4: there, dross removed from silver yields a vessel; here, silver-that-is-dross is spread over a *ḥereś*, a potsherd. The revocalisation dissolves the link.

The sense of the verse is much the same either way — a cheap coating over cheap material — so nothing doctrinal turns on it. But the *structural* argument for retaining the MT vocalisation is real and, so far as I can find, unmade. `[I]` *Moderate.*

> **Checked 20 August: still unattested — but the conclusion now has company.** A library search confirms *sîgîm* at 25:4 and 26:23 and finds no commentator arguing from the inversion. **The argument appears to be mine.** But the position it supports — retain the MT — is independently held by the ancient versions and by the scholarship that has moved against the emendation (see above). *A lone structural argument reaching the same verdict as two ancient translators and a modern reversal stands in a much better place than a lone structural argument.*

#### 25:27b and 25:7c–8a — two acknowledged difficulties

**25:27b.** The ESV footnotes: *"The meaning of the Hebrew line is uncertain."* The Hebrew is *wəḥēqer kəbōdām kābôd* — "and the searching of their glory is glory" — with no antecedent for the suffix on *kəbōdām*. Ansberry proposes reading it as *"and the searching out of difficult things is without glory,"* which makes the inclusio with 25:2 explicit. `[S]` *Moderate.* The ESV's "nor is it glorious to seek one's own glory" reaches a compatible sense by a different route. **Either way the verse is doing frame-work**, and its difficulty is best explained by the compression that frame-work imposes — which is an argument the structural finding supplies. `[I]`

**25:7c–8a.** The ESV footnotes an alternative division: *"presence of a noble, as your eyes have seen. ⁸ Do not go hastily out to court."* The clause *ʾăšer rāʾû ʿênêkā* may close v.7 or open v.8. Nothing large turns on it, but the preacher who quotes v.7 should know the last clause is disputed.

#### 27:13

ESV footnotes: *"Hebrew a foreign woman; a slight emendation yields (compare Vulgate; see also 20:16) foreigners."* A genuine ancient-versions datum. **Category 2** under the triage — a substantive variant with the Vulgate behind the emendation. Minor; the verse is a doublet of 20:16 and nothing here depends on it. *Uncertain — consider verifying against BHS.*

#### Ancient versions check

**Trigger met at 25:21–22**: ESV, NASB and KJV substantively agree on "heap burning coals on his head," and the rendering carries real exegetical weight.

**Result: no split, and a category-3 finding.** The LXX renders the verse in the form Paul quotes at Rom 12:20 (*ἄνθρακας πυρὸς σωρεύσεις ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοῦ*). `[S]` *High on the fact of the citation; moderate on my recall of the LXX wording — NETS would settle it.* Under the triage this is **category 3, the Greek as the NT's own text**: it stands whichever text is prior, and it is reported under Tool 11 rather than as a variant.

**One category-1 loss inside the same verse.** The Hebrew of 25:21 is *ʾim-rāʿēb **śōnaʾăkā*** — "if **the one who hates you** is hungry." The Greek renders *ὁ ἐχθρός σου*, "your enemy," which is also what the ESV gives. That flattening matters, because *śōnaʾăkā* is the distinctive term of Exod 23:5 (see Tool 11). The Greek and the English both lose the verbal thread to the Covenant Code. `[T]` on the Hebrew form; `[I]` on the consequence.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Dry, observant, and frequently very funny — and the humour has a target.**

The register is *unhurried epigram*. There is no urgency here of the kind that drives chapters 1–9; no vocative except one, no "hear, my son," no escalating warning. The speaker is not pleading. He is pointing.

**The devices that establish it:**

- **Deadpan juxtaposition.** The absence of a connective *is* the tone. "A bad tooth and a slipping foot — trust in a treacherous man in the day of trouble" (25:19). The English's "is like" adds a helpfulness the Hebrew withholds.
- **Physical comedy, sustained.** The sluggard revolving on his bed like a door on its hinge (26:14); the same man exhausted by the journey from dish to mouth (26:15); the man who cannot get his hand out of the bowl but is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who can answer sensibly (26:16). Four consecutive verses, each funnier than the last, and the fourth turns it: the joke is about *self-assessment*.
- **The over-loud blessing at dawn** (27:14) — a joke about social miscalibration that still works exactly as written, three thousand years on.
- **Two vomiting images** (25:16, 26:11), the second of them the section's most quoted line.
- **A single change of gear at 27:23.** The infinitive absolute *yādōaʿ tēdaʿ* ("know, you shall know") opens a passage of sustained, warm, second-person instruction after eighty-two verses of third-person observation. The temperature rises at exactly the point where the section stops being a collection and becomes a poem.

**Tonal shifts worth marking:** 25:2–7 is formal and hierarchical (court register). 25:8–28 is wry. Chapter 26 is close to satire throughout. 27:1–22 turns markedly warmer — the friend, the wounds, the counsel, the son — and 27:23–27 is pastoral in both senses.

**Soundtrack:** a solo woodwind with a raised eyebrow. Something like a bassoon, until 27:23, where a cello comes in.

**Note for the preacher:** the sweep's book-level judgement stands and applies here more than anywhere. *A sermon on Proverbs 26 that is never once funny has flattened the register.* The humour is not decoration; it is how the passage disarms a hearer who would otherwise apply every one of these verses to somebody else — which is exactly the manoeuvre 26:12 exists to catch.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources for Proverbs**, carried from the sweep: Genesis 1–3, Deuteronomy (four strands), Job (chiefly 28 and 38), Psalms, the Solomon tradition, and the Egyptian instruction tradition. **Standing negative finding:** Proverbs contains no formula citations. > *The form is not unique in the Hebrew Bible, so this remains a converging argument rather than a knock-down one: exact form + shared scenario (duty to the one who hates you) + selection from the source's own pair. **Upgraded from moderate–high to high on the data.** Intentionality stays `[I]` moderate–high.*

*Source context.* Exodus 23:1–9 is the Covenant Code's block on judicial integrity: no false report, no joining a crowd to do evil, no partiality to a poor man in his lawsuit, no perverting the justice due to the needy, no false charge, no bribe, no oppressing the sojourner. **Set inside that block, at vv.4–5, are two positive duties toward a personal adversary** — return his straying ox, help him raise his fallen donkey. The placement is the point: the Covenant Code puts enemy-duty in the middle of courtroom law, because the man you meet in court is the man whose donkey you must help.

*The verbal anchor.* Exod 23:5 reads *ḥămôr **śōnaʾăkā*** — "the donkey of **the one who hates you**." Proverbs 25:21 reads *ʾim-rāʿēb **śōnaʾăkā*** — "if **the one who hates you** is hungry." `[T]` The term is not the ordinary *ʾōyēb* (which Exod 23:4 uses in the previous verse); Proverbs takes the rarer of the two words Exodus supplies. *Uncertain on the Exodus wording — I did not have Exodus in hand for this run; routed to Open Questions.*

*Book usage.* Torah legal material is an established live source in Proverbs: the weights-and-measures sayings (11:1; 20:10, 23) draw on Lev 19:35–36 / Deut 25:13–16, and the boundary-stone sayings (22:28; 23:10) on Deut 19:14. The Covenant Code specifically has not been tracked elsewhere in the sweep, so this would be a new strand. `[I]` *Moderate.*

*OT-to-OT.* Exod 23:4–5 and Deut 22:1–4 are the same law in two codes, already in canonical conversation, and Deuteronomy's version *drops the enemy* ("your brother's ox"). Proverbs sides with Exodus in keeping the adversary in view.

*What it adds.* This is the finding that reframes 25:21–22 for preaching. Its immediate cluster (25:8–10 lawsuits, 25:18 false witness, 25:23 the backbiting tongue) is *the same subject-matter as Exod 23:1–9*. So the enemy-feeding saying is not free-floating kindness; it is the Covenant Code's courtroom ethic, relocated into a collection about disputes. And the *pen*-clauses of 25:8–10 make the stake explicit: what you do to your adversary out of court determines what happens to you in it.

**Numbers 22–24 (the Balaam narrative) → Proverbs 26:2** *(moderate confidence — conceptual allusion)* `[S: ESV apparatus, declared]` for the candidate; `[I]` for the reading

*Declared:* the ESV cross-reference apparatus lists Num 23:8, Deut 23:5 and 2 Sam 16:12 at this verse, and was visible in the supplied text. I did not reach Numbers independently. The verse's *structural* oddity — it is the one verse in 26:1–12 without *kəsîl* — I did reach independently.

*Source context.* Balak hires Balaam to curse Israel. Balaam cannot: *"How can I curse whom God has not cursed?"* (Num 23:8). Three times he opens his mouth to curse and blessing comes out. The entire narrative is the story of a causeless curse that failed to land — and Deut 23:5 supplies the verdict on it: *"the LORD your God turned the curse into a blessing for you."*

*What it adds.* Proverbs 26:2 states as a bare principle — *"a curse that is causeless does not alight"* — what the Balaam narrative dramatises at length. The bird image is exact: the curse is airborne but finds no perch. Preached with Numbers behind it, the verse becomes pastoral rather than merely observational: it is addressed to someone who has been cursed and is afraid.

*Move 2 / Move 3:* no earlier use of Numbers in Proverbs is tracked in the sweep, and no pairing in this passage. Moderate-confidence allusion, so Move 1 only, per the significance rule.

**Ezekiel 34 and the shepherd tradition → Proverbs 27:23–27** *(moderate confidence — conceptual, carried by *nēzer* and by *pānîm*)* `[I]`

*Source context.* Ezekiel 34 indicts Israel's shepherds for feeding themselves and not the flock — *"the weak you have not strengthened, the sick you have not healed… you have ruled them with force and harshness"* — and promises that God himself will shepherd, and will set up "one shepherd, my servant David."

*What it adds.* Proverbs 27:23–27 is the positive of Ezekiel's negative: *know the face of your flock, set your heart to the herds*, and the household — down to the servant-girls — will be fed. Two textual features carry the link beyond mere shepherd-imagery: **(a)** *nēzer* at v.24, the consecrated diadem, which puts a crown in a poem about goats; **(b)** the sequence of the poem, which ends not with the owner's profit but with *food for your household and maintenance for your **girls*** — the lowest member of the establishment. `[T]` The poem's own logic is that attentive stewardship feeds those at the bottom, which is precisely Ezekiel's charge against the shepherds who did not.

*Move 2.* The shepherd image has not been a tracked strand earlier in Proverbs. This is its one substantial appearance. `[T]`

*Caution.* The metaphorical reading of the poem (flocks = subjects) is contested; see Difficult Verses and Book-Overview Tensions. The Ezekiel link does not depend on settling it — the poem coheres with Ezek 34 whether the flock is livestock or a people, because Ezekiel's own point is that the two are analogous.

**Proverbs 25:21 → Matthew 5:44, with Exodus 23:4–5 behind both** *(moderate–high; added 20 August)* `[S: Logos]` for the Matthew link; `[T]` for the Hebrew data

**This is the strongest single-verse result in the section, and it only became visible when the Exodus anchor was confirmed.**

*The chain.* Exodus 23:5 commands help for the donkey of **שֹׂנַאֲךָ**, "the one who hates you", inside the Covenant Code's block of courtroom law (23:1–9). Proverbs 25:21 takes **the identical form** and commands feeding him, inside a cluster about lawsuits and false witness (25:8–10, 18, 23). The New Testament then reaches back to that verse twice — **Paul quotes it** at Rom 12:20, and **Jesus alludes to it** at Matt 5:44.

*Why the Matthew link matters more than it looks.* Matt 5:43 is the notorious one: *"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbour **and hate your enemy**.'"* The second half is **nowhere in the Old Testament**. Leviticus 19:18 commands neighbour-love; nothing commands enemy-hatred.

**Exodus 23:4–5 and Proverbs 25:21 are the texts that prove it isn't there** — the Torah requires you to raise the fallen beast of the man who hates you, and Proverbs requires you to feed him. So when Jesus says *"Love your enemies"*, he is **not correcting Moses; he is recovering him against a tradition that had added a clause.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.* And Paul, quoting the same verse in the same argument at Rom 12:19–21, is doing the same thing.

*What it adds for the preacher.* It answers the question a thoughtful hearer actually asks — *where does the Old Testament ever say love your enemy?* — with two texts rather than an apology. And it means 25:21–22 should be preached not as an isolated flash of grace in a hard book but as **the wisdom literature's restatement of a Covenant Code duty, which Jesus and Paul both pick up.**

*Confidence note.* The Matthew allusion is `[S]` from the Logos index, not derived here; the Exodus form-match is `[T]` and verified; the Romans quotation is beyond dispute. **The chain is as strong as its weakest link, which is Matt 5:44 — but Matthew's argument requires* some *OT text commanding kindness to an enemy, and these are the two candidates.**

### 12. Genre

Hebrew wisdom literature; within it, the **sentence collection** — but a sentence collection with two distinctive features that change the reading rules.

**First, the dominant sub-form is the comparison, and it is usually unmarked.** The reading rule that follows is not the usual one. For a marked simile ("like X, so Y") the reader is told what to compare; for a bare juxtaposition he is told only *that* two things stand together. The genre therefore requires an act the reader must perform, and the text will not perform it for him.

**Second, the collection contains its own reading instruction, at 26:4–5.** Two consecutive admonitions give opposite advice about the same situation, each with a reason. Neither is qualified. The only possible reading is that both are true and the reader must discern which case he is in. `[T]` *High.* This is the hinge of the whole book's homiletics: **a proverb is a generally-true observation, not a law, and its application requires judgement.** Preach that pair once and a congregation will read the rest of Proverbs correctly.

**A sub-genre at the end.** 27:23–27 is not a sentence but an **admonitory poem** — imperative (23), motive (24), temporal condition (25), consequence (26–27) — with a shape found elsewhere in the book only at the close of major units (9:1–6; 24:30–34; 31:10–31). `[S: Ansberry]`/`[T]` Its genre difference is part of the evidence for the boundary at 27:22/23.

### 13. Copycat

**N/A** for the sentence material — there are no narrative characters here, only character-types. The sluggard, the fool, the whisperer, the quarrelsome spouse and the faithful messenger are portraits, not people; they function as mirrors, not biographies.

One qualification worth stating, because it is where copycat error actually enters this section. The admonitions (fifteen of them) *are* direct commands, and they are prescriptive — but they are prescriptive as *proverbs*, which is to say generally and with judgement, not as statute. 26:4 and 26:5 make that explicit by being mutually exclusive as statutes and jointly true as proverbs. **The copycat question for Proverbs 25–27 is not "should I imitate this character?" but "is this the situation this saying is for?"** — and the section is training that discrimination.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** In its self-presentation: sayings attributed to Solomon (united monarchy, tenth century) transmitted by a named body under Hezekiah (late eighth century). `[T]` on the attributions as *stated*; the historical reconstruction behind them is `[S]` and nothing in this report depends on it. Date of final form is disputed; irrelevant here.

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross, resurrection and ascension, awaiting consummation.

**What has happened in between that changes the reading.** Three things, and the first is decisive for this section specifically:

1. **The honour argument has been settled by an event.** 25:6–7 counsels taking the lower place; Luke 14:11 makes it eschatological; Philippians 2:5–11 records that the one who did not grasp equality with God took the lowest place and *was* told "come up here" — *"therefore God has highly exalted him."* The section's central concern is not merely illustrated but *enacted* on the timeline. See Christological Reading.
2. **The concealing God has disclosed himself.** 25:2 says the glory of God is to conceal a matter (*dābār*). The apostolic vocabulary for the gospel is "the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now revealed" (Col 1:26; Rom 16:25–26; Eph 3:9). `[I]` *Moderate* — this is canonical reflection rather than a claim about the proverb's authorial intent, and it should be offered as such.
3. **The one shepherd has come.** 27:23's "know the face of your flock" stands in a canonical line that runs through Ezek 34 to John 10:14, *"I know my own and my own know me."*

**And one guard.** Proverbs is not a mechanism, and the timeline is what prevents it becoming one: at the cross the wholly wise and wholly righteous man received everything this book assigns to the fool and the wicked. Any sermon from 25–27 that implies competent living secures honour has to answer Golgotha.

### 15. Who Am I?

**The default identification is the addressee — the one who does not yet have wisdom.** Book-level finding, and it bites here. The characteristic misreading of Proverbs 26 is to identify with the observer rather than the observed.

**The passage forecloses it.** 26:1–11 accumulates eleven images of the fool, and then v.12 turns: *"Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him."* `[T]` The run has been softening the reader up for a verse aimed at him. And the same phrase returns at v.16 to skewer the sluggard, and at v.5 to justify answering the fool — so *"wise in his own eyes"* has been in the reader's ear for eleven verses before it lands on him.

| Figure | Function |
|---|---|
| The fool, the sluggard, the whisperer, the meddler, the hypocrite | Warnings — and the section's own device (26:12) makes the reader ask whether he is one |
| The faithful messenger (25:13), the wise reprover (25:12), the friend who wounds (27:6) | Positive types. Not types of Christ in the strict sense; but see Christological Reading on 27:6 and 27:23 |
| The king (25:2–7) | Neither example nor type here — he is the *environment* the addressee must navigate |
| The quarrelsome wife / quarrelsome man (25:24, 26:21, 27:15) | Warnings, and the section applies the same word to both sexes — see Difficult Verses |
| The shepherd-owner (27:23–27) | The addressee himself, given responsibility — and, on the trajectory reading, a figure the true Shepherd fulfils |

**Where "Moses-is-me" error enters this section**: not through a hero, but through the *sage's chair*. The preacher who stands in the position of the one who already discerns, dispensing these observations about other people, has taken a seat the text has just removed (26:12).

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** Not primarily an action but a *capacity*: the ability to look at a situation and judge what it is like. The section teaches by withholding — no divine verdict, few antitheses, comparisons without particles — and asks the reader to complete what it leaves open. Alongside that, one sustained substantive claim: **honour is real, and it cannot be self-conferred.**

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

| Domain | Content |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | The world is legible but not self-interpreting. God conceals; kings search out; and the searching of one's *own* glory is not glorious (25:2, 27). There is a limit to what may profitably be investigated — and the self is on the far side of it. |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Putting yourself forward (25:6). Praising yourself (27:2). Boasting about tomorrow (27:1) or about a gift you have not given (25:14). Wearing out a friendship by over-visiting (25:17). Meddling in a quarrel not your own (26:17). Repeating what you were told in confidence (25:9). Calling malice a joke (26:19). |
| **Behaviour — start** | Arguing your case with the person himself (25:9). Feeding the one who hates you (25:21). Receiving open rebuke (27:5–6). Knowing the face of what is entrusted to you (27:23). |
| **Motivation** | Not prudence but the five *kî*-clauses: *because* it is better to be told "come up"; *because* the LORD will reward you; *because* there are seven abominations in his heart; *because* you do not know what a day may bring; *because* riches do not last and no crown endures for ever. |

**Four audiences.**

| Audience | What this section says |
|---|---|
| **For me** | 26:12 is aimed at me, and 27:2 forbids the reflex by which I would deflect it. The question is not whether I can spot the fool but whether I can bear to be assayed by praise I did not solicit. |
| **For a Christian friend** | 27:5–6 — open rebuke is better than hidden love, and a friend's wounds are faithful. This is the section's warrant for saying the hard thing, and 27:9 supplies the manner: counsel from the *nepeš*, out of genuine concern. |
| **For the church** | 26:20–22 and 26:17. A congregation is destroyed by whisperers and by people who take up other people's quarrels, and the passage says so with unusual bluntness. Also 25:9 — take it to the person. |
| **For an unbeliever** | 27:1 and 27:20. You do not know what tomorrow will bring, and the eye is never satisfied. That is not moralism; it is an accurate description of a life spent acquiring — and it is where James takes 27:1 (Jas 4:13–14) and where John takes 27:20 (*"the lust of the eyes"*, 1 John 2:16, in a passage about the world passing away). |

**Prayer in response.**

- *Sorry* for the seat I have taken — assessing others while exempting myself (26:12).
- *Thank you* that the verdict on me does not come from my own mouth (27:2), and that in Christ it has already been spoken from outside.
- *Please* give me the honesty to receive a friend's wound (27:6), and the attentiveness to know the face of what you have put in my care (27:23).
- *Mercy* on the one who hates me, and on me as I feed him (25:21–22).

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **Verbless juxtaposition** is the section's signature construction and the single most important grammatical fact about it. Roughly twenty-eight sayings consist of two noun-phrases with no verb, no copula and no particle. `[T]` The relation is left to the reader. See Tools 5, 8, 12.
- **The *kaph* of norm at 26:4–5.** *kəʾiwwaltô* is "according to his folly," not "like his folly" — a different function of the same preposition from the comparative *kə-* elsewhere in the chapter. `[T]` *High.*
- **Infinitive absolute at 27:23**: *yādōaʿ tēdaʿ*, "knowing you shall know" — emphatic, and the strongest imperative construction in the section, opening the closing poem. `[T]`
- **Anaphora at 25:4–5**: *hăgô … hăgô*, the same infinitive absolute verse-initial in consecutive verses, the only two occurrences in the section. `[T]` This is the linking device Ansberry also names.
- **Chained *pen*-clauses** at 25:8–10 and in the two motive-pairs (25:16–17; 26:4–5). `[T]`

#### Wordplay and sound

- ***kābēd***: the weight/honour double sense, exploited at 27:3 where a stone's *kōbed* and a fool's *kābēd* provocation stand in one verse. Category-1 translation loss. *Moderate–high* that this is deliberate.
- **√*śbʿ***: "be sated" and "seven" share the consonants, and both senses appear within the section (25:16, 17; 26:16, 25; 27:7, 20). *Moderate* — the two are distinct roots in the lexicon (*śbʿ* / *šbʿ*, with different sibilants), so this is **assonance, not a shared root**, and should be flagged as such rather than presented as paronomasia. Stated here with the correction rather than omitted.
- **25:11 *ʿal-ʾopnāyw***: a hapax-adjacent expression usually rendered "fitly" or "at its right time"; the etymology is disputed. *Uncertain — consider a lexicon.*

BDB/HALOT on *ʾopnāyw* (25:11), *maśkiyyôt* (25:11), *mərgēmâ* (26:8), and the *kesep sîgîm* / *kəsapsîgîm* question at 26:23. NETS for the LXX of 25:21–22 and 25:27.

Under the Three-Way Triage, this section carries **category 2 only**. Category-1 losses (the unmarked juxtaposition; the *kābēd* double sense; the *śōnaʾăkā* flattening) are reported under Tools 7–8 and Original Languages; the category-3 case (Rom 12:20) under Tool 11.

**26:23 — *kesep sîgîm* or *kəsapsîgîm*?**

- **The issue:** whether the consonants are two words ("silver of dross") or one ("glaze," from a proposed cognate).
- **Evidence:** the MT vocalises as two words. The ESV revocalises and footnotes the fact. `[S]` *Moderate.*
- **Ancient versions (checked 20 August):** **Vulgate** *argento sordido* and **LXX** ἀργύριον … ὄστρακον both attest a **two-word** reading; neither supports "glaze". `[S]` Peshitta not checked.
- **Position:** the sense is close either way, so nothing doctrinal turns on it. **Retain the MT vocalisation.** Three independent supports converge: the two ancient versions above; the modern turn against the Ugaritic emendation (Zondervan Encyclopedia; TNIV restoring "silver dross"); and the structural argument, which appears to be unmade — *sîgîm* occurs only twice in these three chapters, at 25:4 and here, **with the word order reversed**, so that on the MT reading 26:23 inverts 25:4 (dross removed from silver / silver that is dross), and on the revocalisation the link vanishes. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Preaching implication:** minimal for the verse itself; real if the preacher wants to run the refining thread across the section. Mention the footnote; don't build on it.
- **Confidence:** *moderate–high*, upgraded 20 August on the ancient-version evidence.

**27:13 — *nokrîyâ* or plural "foreigners"?**

- **The issue:** the ESV footnote records a slight emendation, comparing the Vulgate, yielding a plural.
- **Preaching implication:** none. The verse is a doublet of 20:16.
- **Confidence:** *uncertain — consider verifying.*

**25:27b — a difficulty, not a variant.** The MT is grammatically hard (the suffix on *kəbōdām* has no antecedent) and many emendations have been proposed. This is an internal Hebrew problem, not a version split; it is treated under Tool 8.

No other significant variants.

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time period.** The material is presented as Solomonic and its transmission as Hezekian — a span of roughly two and a half centuries between source and editorial body, stated in the text itself (25:1). `[T]` on the presentation; `[S]`/`[I]` on any historical reconstruction of either stage.
- **Location.** Judah; and specifically a **court** environment. `[I]` The evidence is the material's own furniture: the king's presence and the place of the great (25:6), the noble (*nādîb*, 25:7), the ruler (*qāṣîn*, 25:15), the messenger sent to masters (25:13), the smith and his vessel (25:4), the assembly (*qāhāl*, 26:26), the household with flocks, herds and servant-girls (27:23–27).
- **Political context.** A monarchy with a bureaucracy — a hierarchy in which advancement, placement and the securing of a hearing are live daily concerns.

#### What the original audience knew

- **Seating was rank.** 25:6–7 assumes an occasion at which physical position encodes status publicly, and at which a misjudgement is corrected *in front of everyone*. The shame in v.7 is not private embarrassment; it is public demotion. `[I]` *High.* This is why Jesus' version at Luke 14:8–11 needs no explaining to his hearers either.
- **The messenger was the message.** 25:13's faithful envoy "refreshes the *nepeš* of his masters" because in a world without written dispatch a sender's business, and reputation, travelled in another man's mouth. 25:13 and 26:6 ("sends a message by the hand of a fool cuts off his own feet") are two sides of one anxiety. `[I]` *High.*
- **A curse was an *act*, not a wish.** 26:2 answers a real fear. In this culture words spoken against a person were held to be effective; the proverb's claim — that an unwarranted curse does not land — is a *theological* claim of some boldness, and the Balaam narrative is its precedent. `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
- **Litigation was public and local.** 25:8–10 assumes a dispute heard among people who all know each other, where losing means a reputation that "has no end." `[I]` *High.*
- **Refining was the everyday image of testing.** The removal of *sîgîm* from silver was a familiar operation; and plating base pottery with a silvery slip was a familiar fraud (26:23). `[I]` *Moderate–high.*
- **A household with *nəʿārôt* was substantial.** 27:27's "your girls" implies a retinue; the same term appears at 9:3 and 31:15. `[S: Ansberry]` *Moderate–high — the inference is his, the term is checkable.* This is a genuine argument that 27:23–27 is not a smallholder's manual.
- **The lot, the gate, weights, surety** — the wider furniture of Proverbs, worked at book level in the sweep and assumed here.

#### What this changes about how we read

**25:6–7 is not modesty advice.** It is career advice in a system where the wrong seat is a public, correctable error — which is why it works so well when Jesus repurposes it for the kingdom's table. Preached as "be humble," it loses both its shrewdness and its sting.

**26:23–28's hypocrite is a courtier.** The vocabulary is of *lips*, *heart*, *assembly*, and *deception uncovered in the *qāhāl** — a public body. This is a portrait of someone whose survival depends on being believed, drawn for readers whose survival depends on knowing whom to believe.

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section:** the Writings. The canonical reader of Proverbs arrives having read the Torah and the Prophets — which is to say, arriving **after** the exile and after Malachi, holding promises that are not yet resolved.
- **What that reader knows:** the whole Solomon story, including 1 Kgs 11; the whole monarchy, including its end; the shepherd-indictment of Ezekiel 34 and the promise of one shepherd; the Covenant Code and Deuteronomy. He reads "proverbs of Solomon, copied by the men of Hezekiah" knowing what became of both kings and of the kingdom.
- **Canonical register:** Job, Psalms and Ecclesiastes are the immediate neighbours. *šəʾôl waʾăbaddôn* at 27:20 points first to Job (26:6); the limits-of-searching theme at 25:2–3, 27 sits alongside Job 28 ("But where shall wisdom be found?").

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were:** young men of standing being formed for service — men with access to a court, the means to employ others, and a household to run. `[I]` *Moderate–high*, from the material's own assumptions.
- **Their situation:** advancement depended on being *noticed correctly*. Too forward and you are demoted publicly; too retiring and you are overlooked. Reputation was the working capital, and it could be destroyed by one badly handled quarrel or one repeated confidence.
- **What they brought:** an assumption that honour is real, public, conferred by others, and worth pursuing.

#### Where the passage fits their world

The section meets that assumption and does something unexpected with it. It does not deny that honour matters — it affirms it more strongly than they might have expected, tracing it up to God himself (25:2). What it denies is that honour can be *taken*. Every one of the section's honour sayings moves in the same direction: honour comes from outside — from the king who says "come up here," from another mouth, from the crucible of what people actually say about you.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking:** *"If your enemy is hungry, give him bread"* (25:21). In an honour-shame world, feeding the man who hates you is not merely generous; it is a public act with public meaning, and the motive clause makes it stranger still.
- **Surprising:** 26:12. Eleven verses have invited the hearer to enjoy the spectacle of the fool. The twelfth asks whether he has been laughing at himself.
- **Comforting:** 26:2 — the causeless curse does not land. And 27:10, the near neighbour better than the distant brother, in a culture that prized kinship above all.
- **Disturbing:** 27:2. Self-commendation was the ordinary mechanism of advancement; the section forbids it and offers no replacement except the assessment of others (27:21). That is a genuinely uncomfortable place to be left.
- **Funny:** the sluggard's four verses (26:13–16); the over-loud morning blessing (27:14); the man who cannot get his hand out of the dish yet is wiser than seven counsellors.

#### What we bring that they didn't

- **A suspicion of the word "honour."** We hear it as vanity and so pre-emptively defuse the section's whole argument. They heard it as a real and necessary good, which is why the prohibition on seeking it landed as a *cost*.
- **Individualism.** These sayings assume a face-to-face community where reputation is a shared possession. Read individually, 25:9–10 becomes advice about discretion; read corporately, it is about how a community's trust is destroyed.
- **A therapeutic instinct** that reads 27:5–6 ("faithful are the wounds of a friend") as permission to be blunt. The section's own qualification is 27:9 — counsel that comes *from the nepeš*, out of genuine concern — and 25:9, take it to the person himself.
- **Self-promotion as a professional duty.** We live in the one culture in history where 27:2 is a career risk. That is worth naming from the pulpit rather than smoothing.

#### Implication for the sermon

The section's own emotional architecture runs from *the glory of God and of kings* (25:2) to *a man is tested by his praise* (27:21). Preached without that arc, the individual sayings become etiquette. Preached with it, the section is an extended dismantling of self-assessment — which is the shape of the doctrine of justification long before the doctrine is available.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge.

#### (1) Glory and the limits of human searching

- **Where this passage sits.** 25:2–3 states the settled OT position: God's glory lies in concealing; the king's in searching out; and even the king's own heart is beyond his subjects' reach. 25:27 closes the frame by ruling one object of enquiry out of bounds — one's own glory.
- **What comes before.** Genesis 3, where the reach for a knowledge not given is the fall. Job 28 ("where shall wisdom be found?") and Job 38–41, where God answers the man who searched by asking what he can possibly know. Deuteronomy 29:29, *"the secret things belong to the LORD our God"* — which the ESV apparatus, rightly, sets against 25:2.
- **What comes after.** The apostolic language of the *mystery*: hidden for ages, now disclosed (Rom 16:25–26; Col 1:26; Eph 3:9); and Rom 11:33, *"how unsearchable his judgements"* — the same complex of ideas, now with a revelation inside it. The concealing God has searched himself out and told us.
- **What this contributes.** It supplies the section's epistemology, and it guards the preaching: this material is not a manual for mastering life. It opens by saying there are matters God has hidden, and closes its first movement by saying self-examination for the sake of glory is not among the things worth searching.
- **Confidence:** *high* on the OT trajectory; *moderate* on the mystery connection, which is canonical reflection.

#### (2) The shepherd

- **Where this passage sits.** 27:23–27 — attend to the flock, and the household eats. With *nēzer* in v.24, the poem holds a crown and a flock in five verses.
- **What comes before.** The patriarchs and Moses as shepherds; David taken from the sheepfolds (2 Sam 7:8; Ps 78:70–72, "he shepherded them with upright heart"); the LORD as shepherd (Ps 23).
- **What comes after.** Ezekiel 34's indictment of shepherds who fed themselves, and the promise of one shepherd; Zech 11; then John 10 — *"I know my own and my own know me"* — and 1 Pet 5:2–4, where elders shepherd the flock and await the chief Shepherd's crown (*stephanos*, and Prov 27:24 asks whether a *nēzer* endures).
- **What this contributes.** It is what makes the poem more than husbandry without forcing allegory: the canon itself has made shepherding a category for rule, and Proverbs 27:24 puts the crown in the poem.
- **Confidence:** *moderate–high.*

#### Confluence

**Climactic convergence.** The two themes meet in a person. The section says God's glory is to conceal and that no one may search out his own glory; and it ends by telling a man to know the face of his flock, because no crown lasts. The New Testament's answer is one figure who is both — the hidden God disclosed, and the shepherd whose crown does endure, who took the lowest place and was told "come up here."

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: trajectory (primary), with one authorised transposition and one contrast.**

#### 1. The authorised transposition — Luke 14:8–11 on Proverbs 25:6–7

This is not typology; it is Jesus quoting the section and telling us what to do with it. He reproduces both the counsel and its reasoning at a wedding feast, then generalises: *"For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted."* `[T]`/`[S]` *High.*

**What that licenses.** Jesus has taken a courtier's rule about seating and made it an eschatological principle about the last day. That authorises reading the whole 25:2–7 unit — and, through the Leitwort, the whole honour argument of the section — forward to the kingdom's table. The preacher does not have to *construct* the Christological move here; the Gospels have made it.

#### 2. The enactment — Philippians 2:5–11

**Tests passed:** *theological-category* (honour and exaltation, not incidental detail) ✔ · *escalation* (the pattern is fulfilled at infinite scale) ✔ · *NT precedent* (Luke 14 makes the same move on the same text) ✔ · *authorial pattern* — the section itself raises the honour question repeatedly and never resolves who confers it ✔. **All four: *high confidence*, and this is trajectory arriving at its terminus rather than typology strictly speaking.**

Christ did not grasp what was his; he took the lowest place; *therefore* God highly exalted him and gave him the name above every name. That is Proverbs 25:6–7 performed at the scale of the cosmos, and it supplies what the section conspicuously lacks: an answer to *who says "come up here."*

#### 3. The contrast — 25:2 and the disclosed mystery

The section opens by saying God's glory lies in concealing. The gospel is announced as the mystery hidden for ages and now made known. The relation is not contradiction but consummation: the concealing God has, at his own initiative, searched out and disclosed the one thing 25:2–3 declares unsearchable — himself. `[I]` *Moderate.* Offer it as canonical reflection, not as the proverb's own claim.

#### 4. The shepherd — 27:23–27 → John 10

The Hebrew of 27:23 is *dāʿ pənê ṣōʾnekā*, "know the **face** of your flock." John 10:14: *"I am the good shepherd. I know my own and my own know me."* And 10:3, *"he calls his own sheep by name."* `[T]` on the Hebrew; `[I]` *moderate–high* on the connection, which runs through Ezek 34.

The poem's own end is that the household — down to the servant-girls — is fed. The chief Shepherd's crown (1 Pet 5:4) answers 27:24's question about whether a *nēzer* endures to all generations.

#### Moralism check

**The "be like X" temptation here is unusually strong**, because the section is fifteen admonitions and seventy observations about how competent people behave. Preached flat, it becomes an etiquette manual with a religious veneer: be discreet, be humble, don't over-visit, feed your enemy, mind your flock.

**Where the gospel grounding actually sits.** In the section's own systematic dismantling of self-assessment:

- 25:27 — searching out your own glory is not glorious.
- 26:12 — the man wise in his own eyes is beyond hope, worse off than a fool.
- 27:2 — the verdict must come from another mouth, not yours.
- 27:21 — you are assayed by the praise you receive, not the praise you give yourself.

Read together, these say: **a man cannot certify himself.** That is not a moral lesson; it is the anthropology on which justification rests. The preacher's move is not "try to be humble" but "you were never going to be able to pronounce on yourself — and there is a verdict from outside, pronounced over the one who took the lowest place, and shared with everyone in him."

**Christ as hero, not example.** In 25:6–7 Christ is not primarily the model of taking the lower seat (though he is that). He is the one who *was told "come up here"* — and the section's whole argument is that the invitation must come from someone else.

**Confidence:** *high* on the Luke 14 / Phil 2 line; *moderate–high* on the shepherd; *moderate* on the mystery.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 26:4–5 — the contradiction

- **Category:** apparent contradiction (apologetic), and the most productive one in Scripture.
- **The difficulty:** consecutive verses give opposite instructions with no qualifier.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** the pair sits inside the fool-run, bound to it by *kəsîl kəʾiwwaltô* and to each other by matched *pen*-clauses. It is not an editorial accident; it is the run's methodological centre.
- **Common mishandling:** harmonising ("sometimes this, sometimes that"). That is not the resolution — it is the *lesson*, and stating it as a resolution defuses the pedagogy.
- **Honest exegesis:** the reader is being taught that a proverb is not a rule. Both verses are true; discerning which applies is the skill the whole section is training.
- **Handling:** make it the sermon, once, early in any series on Proverbs.

#### 25:22 — "burning coals on his head"

- **Category:** ethical / interpretive.
- **The difficulty:** does the kindness shame the enemy into repentance, or store up judgement against him? Both readings are ancient.
- **Rhetorical function:** it is the *kî*-clause motivating the admonition of v.21 — so whatever it means, it is offered as an *incentive*.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text is genuinely ambiguous. **Paul is not.** Rom 12:20 quotes it and immediately concludes, *"Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good"* — which settles the preaching sense. And the Exod 23:4–5 background (Tool 11) points the same way: the Covenant Code's enemy-duty is restorative.
- **Handling:** acknowledge the ambiguity in a sentence; land on Paul.

#### 25:24 and 27:15 — the quarrelsome wife

- **Category:** pastoral, and increasingly apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** two sayings compare living with a contentious wife unfavourably to a roof-corner and a leaking roof; a third (27:16) says she cannot be restrained. Modern hearers reasonably ask why the fault is always the woman's.
- **The corrective is inside the section.** *mədônîm* occurs three times in these chapters — 25:24 and 27:15 of a **wife**, and **26:21 of a man**: *"as charcoal to embers and wood to fire, so is a quarrelsome **man** for kindling strife."* `[T]` The same noun; the same destructive function. The book is not making a claim about women; it is making a claim about contention, and it illustrates it from both sexes within three chapters.
- **Handling:** preach all three occurrences together, or none of them. Preaching 25:24 alone, without 26:21, gives the congregation a distorted text — and hands an unnecessary objection to anyone in the room already inclined to make it. Note also that 27:15–16 is addressed to the *husband*, and what it says to him is that she cannot be controlled — which is at least as much a limit on him as a complaint about her.
- **Pastoral landmine:** someone in the room is in a hard marriage. The section offers no counsel to them, and it is more honest to say so than to soften the verses into something they can use.

#### 26:1–12 preached at other people

- **Category:** the standard sermon cliché for this chapter.
- **The difficulty:** eleven vivid images of fools invite a congregation to think of somebody else.
- **The corrective:** v.12 is the run's own punchline and turns it on the hearer — *and on the preacher first*. Follow the text's structure and the sermon's shape is given to you.

#### 27:23–27 — literal or metaphorical?

- **Category:** interpretive, with structural consequences.
- **The difficulty:** a poem about goats containing the word *nēzer* (crown), placed at a collection boundary.
- **The options:** (a) literal husbandry, with *nēzer* a metonym for wealth; (b) royal metaphor, flocks as subjects; (c) Ansberry's both/and — the poem addresses one kind of person, an aristocratic landholder with a retinue, for whom the literal and the figurative are the same counsel. `[S]`
- **What this run adds:** the structural question and the metaphorical question are separable, and the evidence bears on them differently. The *petuchah* at 27:22 and the two lexical frames closing at 27:21–22 establish 27:23–27 as a **distinct closing unit** — that much is now well attested. Whether the flock is figurative is untouched by that evidence.
- **Handling:** preach it as what it plainly is — attentive stewardship of what is entrusted to you, motivated by the impermanence of wealth and crowns — and let the shepherd-king resonance be heard through Ezek 34 and John 10 rather than asserted from *nēzer* alone.

#### 27:17 — "iron sharpens iron"

- **Category:** weaponised / popular misuse.
- **The difficulty:** the verse has become a slogan for men's fellowship groups, meaning something like mutual encouragement.
- **Honest exegesis:** the Hebrew is *"iron with iron grows sharp, and a man sharpens the **face** of his friend"* (ESV footnote: "Hebrew *sharpens the face of another*"). Sharpening iron on iron involves friction, heat and the removal of metal. And the chapter's own context is **rebuke** — 27:5–6 three verses after the frame opens, and 27:9's counsel that comes from the *nepeš*.
- **The corrective:** the popular use is not wrong, but it is thinner than the text. Preach the friction. And note *pānîm* here alongside 27:19 ("as water, face to face") — the two verses together are about what it costs to see yourself accurately in another person.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: explaining every comparison

- **What it looks like:** "Now, what does the writer mean by apples of gold in a setting of silver? He means that the right word at the right moment is beautiful and valuable, just as…" — and the congregation relaxes.
- **Why it's wrong:** the Hebrew does not say "is like." It sets two pictures side by side and leaves the space between them empty. The interpretive act the sermon has just performed is the act the text was asking the *hearer* to perform. Doing it for them is not exposition; it is removing the exercise.
- **The corrective:** read the juxtaposition as the Hebrew has it — *"Apples of gold in settings of silver; a word spoken at its right time"* — and let it sit for three seconds before saying anything. Then, if you explain, explain that the text is making them do the work, and why.

### Pitfall: inserting God into every proverb

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on 25:11 or 26:14 that arrives, in each case, at a paragraph about God's sovereignty or Christ's example — because a sermon must be about God.
- **Why it's wrong:** the section names God twice in eighty-three verses, and that reticence is deliberate and theological. 25:2 says as much: the glory of God is to conceal. A homiletic that will not tolerate a text about the ordinary is at odds with a section that is largely about the ordinary.
- **The corrective:** preach the section's arc rather than baptising each saying. The gospel connection is real and it is structural — the honour argument and its terminus in Luke 14 and Phil 2 — not verse-by-verse.

### Pitfall: harmonising 26:4–5

- **What it looks like:** "There's no contradiction here — sometimes you answer and sometimes you don't."
- **Why it's wrong:** that is the correct content stated as though it were a defensive manoeuvre. It is not a problem to be solved; it is the book teaching its own genre, deliberately, in its most quotable pair.
- **The corrective:** make it a positive claim. Proverbs are not statutes. The pair exists so that a reader who applies proverbs mechanically will fail at exactly this verse and learn something.

### Pitfall: preaching 26:1–12 at the congregation

- **What it looks like:** an entertaining catalogue of fools, with the preacher and the hearers on the same side of the glass.
- **Why it's wrong:** v.12 turns the whole run on the hearer, and the phrase it uses — *wise in his own eyes* — has been planted at v.5 and will return at v.16.
- **The corrective:** structure the sermon as the chapter is structured: accumulate, then turn. And turn it on yourself first.

### Pitfall: "iron sharpens iron" as a fellowship slogan

- **What it looks like:** a men's-breakfast text about mutual encouragement.
- **Why it's wrong:** the image involves friction and loss of metal, the Hebrew says "sharpens the *face*," and the chapter's own material on friendship is about *rebuke* (27:5–6) and hard counsel (27:9).
- **The corrective:** preach the friction, and pair it with 27:6 and 27:19.

### Pitfall: 25:21–22 as sanctified passive-aggression

- **What it looks like:** "kill them with kindness" — you feed your enemy and the coals do the rest.
- **Why it's wrong:** it turns a command into a tactic, and it reads the ambiguous *kî*-clause in the worst available direction. Paul quotes the verse and concludes "overcome evil with good."
- **The corrective:** set it in its own cluster (lawsuits, false witness, the backbiting tongue) and, if you have it, in Exod 23:4–5 — where the duty to the man who hates you sits in the middle of courtroom law.

### Pitfall: 27:23–27 as a stewardship-and-prosperity text

- **What it looks like:** "Mind your business well and God will provide" — flocks as investments, v.26–27 as the return.
- **Why it's wrong:** the poem's own motive clause runs the other way. *"For riches do not last for ever; and does a crown endure to all generations?"* (v.24) is a warning against trusting accumulation, and it is the reason given for the diligence, not a promise attached to it.
- **The corrective:** preach the *kî*. The passage's logic is that because nothing you can pile up survives, attend instead to what has been entrusted to you — which ends, in v.27, with the household's most junior members being fed.

### Pitfall: preaching 25:24 without 26:21

- **What it looks like:** a light-hearted aside about the quarrelsome wife.
- **Why it's wrong:** the section uses the same word of a quarrelsome *man* (26:21), and preaching one without the other misrepresents the text and hands an objection to the room for free.
- **The corrective:** all three occurrences or none. See Difficult Verses.

## Book-Overview Tensions

### 1. The 27:27/28:1 seam — the assessment should be qualified, not overturned

`proverbs-collections-assessment` calls 27:27/28:1 "the strongest of the two reconstructed seams," on four converging distributional measures (antithesis, righteous/wicked, similes, divine name) and four witnesses, one of whom — Fox — calls it "the common division."

**Nothing in this run contradicts the distributional evidence. It confirms it.** The Hebrew antithesis count for 25–27 (six of eighty-two) lands almost exactly where the assessment's ESV count did (five), from an independent method. The divine-name figure (two in eighty-three) is confirmed exactly.

**But the structural evidence points somewhere else.** The received Hebrew paragraphing places no boundary at 27:27/28:1 and runs a single paragraph from 27:23 through to 28:4. Two lexical frames close at 27:21–22. The genre changes at 27:23. **Every structural marker available points to 27:22/27:23; the distributional markers point to 27:27/28:1.**

**These are not in conflict — they are measuring different things.** A *petuchah* marks where a unit of reading ends. A change in antithesis density marks where a body of material changes character. There is no reason those must coincide, and here they do not: the last unit of Solomon 2A begins at 27:23, and the material begins to change character four verses later.

**Which is exactly what a janus looks like.** Recommended amendment to the assessment: retain the 27:27/28:1 seam as the *distributional* boundary; add 27:22/27:23 as the *structural* boundary; and state that the gap between them is the janus, rather than treating the two as rivals.

### 2. Ansberry's janus — a correction of this project's correction

On 17 August the assessment recorded: *"An earlier version of this section called Ansberry's janus at 27:23–27 'the right instinct' and built on it. That was too generous."* The correction was made because the *metaphorical* reading (flocks as subjects) is a minority position, and because the sweep had examined and declined it.

**That correction conflated two claims of Ansberry's that should have been kept apart:**

- **the structural claim** — that 27:23–27 is a discrete closing unit which also functions as an introduction to 28:1–29:27;
- **the metaphorical claim** — that the flock stands for the king's subjects.

**The structural claim is now well attested and was never a minority position:** Ansberry's own section divisions are 25:2–27 · 25:28–26:28 · 27:1–22 · **27:23–27**, and Van Leeuwen independently divides 25 · 26:1–16 · 26:17–28 · 27:1–22 · **27:23–29:27**. The two disagree about much and agree about this. The *petuchah* at 27:22 is a third, independent witness at transmission level.

**The metaphorical claim remains contested**, and the sweep's caution about it stands untouched.

**What went wrong:** the correction was made against a *summary* of Ansberry's position rather than his argument. His actual case is a both/and — that the poem addresses one kind of person, an aristocratic landholder with *nəʿārôt*, for whom literal husbandry and royal responsibility are the same counsel — and it rests on two text-level observations (*nēzer* at v.24; *nəʿārôt* at v.27, with 9:3 and 31:15 as comparanda) that deserved engagement. **This is error 19 for the project record: criticising a position from a paraphrase of it.**

### 3. The sweep's structure of chapter 26 should be revised

The sweep gives 26:1–12 (the fool) and 26:13–16 (the sluggard) as separate sections. **The *bəʿênāyw* triad at 26:5, 12 and 16 binds them.** The phrase occurs three times in the section and nowhere else; it appears in the middle of the fool-pair, at the close of the fool-run, and at the close of the sluggard-run. The sluggard is introduced precisely so that the phrase can be used a third time.

**Recommended: 26:1–16 as one movement with an internal topic-shift at v.13** — which is also Van Leeuwen's division, reached independently.

### 4. The simile count in the assessment is an ESV artefact and should be corrected

The assessment's evidence table gives similes per chapter as **25 → 11, 26 → 13, 27 → 1**, and concludes: *"On the imagery criterion alone, the break falls at 26/27, not 27/28."*

**Counting from the Hebrew changes this materially.** The table counts the English word "like" and the phrase "as … so". In Hebrew:

| | marked comparisons (*kə-* / *kēn*) | all comparative sayings |
|---|---:|---:|
| ch. 25 | **1** | 13 |
| ch. 26 | **6** | 16 |
| ch. 27 | **2** | 8 |

Two consequences. **(a)** Chapter 25's apparent eleven similes are almost entirely unmarked juxtapositions to which the ESV has supplied "like"; the marked similes are concentrated in chapter 26. **(b)** Chapter 27 does decline, but from 16 to 8, not from 13 to 1. **The competing break at 26/27 that the assessment's figure suggested does not survive the Hebrew count** — which, if anything, strengthens the case for 27:27/28:1 by removing a rival.

The assessment's wider conclusion — that chapter 27 is transitional — survives, but on a much smaller margin than "1 against 13" implied, and it should be restated.

### 5. What the overview gets right and this run confirms

- The book-level claim that emblematic parallelism is dominant in 25–27 — **confirmed**, and now quantified from the Hebrew (39 of 82 sayings comparative in some form, 48%).
- The characterisation of chapter 26 as the fool chapter — **confirmed** (*kəsîl* 11×, all in vv.1–12).
- The Move-4 finding that 27:11's "my son" is the frame's voice reaching into the collection — **confirmed and sharpened**: it is the only vocative in eighty-three verses.
- The book-level judgement that Proverbs is funnier than it is usually preached — **confirmed with interest**; chapter 26 is the evidence.
- 26:4–5 as the book's own reading instruction — **confirmed**, and this run adds a positional reason for its placement *here*.
