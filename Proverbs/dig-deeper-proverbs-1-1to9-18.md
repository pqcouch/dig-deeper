## Headline Findings

1. **The father stops speaking, and Wisdom finishes his sentence for him.** `[T]`, WLC. בְּנִי ("my son") occurs fifteen times in chs 1–9 and **its last occurrence is 7:1**; the plural בָּנִים ("sons") occurs four times as a vocative, at 4:1, 5:7, 7:24 and 8:32. The formula וְעַתָּה בָנִים שִׁמְעוּ־לִי ("Now then, my sons, listen to me") stands **three times and only three times in the book** — 5:7 and 7:24 in the father's mouth, and **8:32 in Wisdom's**. After 7:27 the father never addresses the son again; chapters 8 and 9 belong to the two women. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tone, Who Am I?, Tool 11 Move 4. *High confidence.*

2. **The three public calls are one escalating sequence, and the escalation is verifiable word by word.** `[T]`, WLC. 1:20–21 and 8:1–3 share **seven lemmas** — רנן ("to cry aloud"), קוֹל ("voice"), נתן ("to give"), רֹאשׁ ("top"), שַׁעַר ("gate"), פֶּתַח ("entrance"), קרא ("to call") — across five verses (1:20–21; 8:1–3), and they share the exact phrase **תִּתֵּן קוֹלָהּ ("she gives her voice"), found only at 1:20 and 8:1 in the canon**; the Greek adds three wordings unique to these verses `[S: audit 2]`. The sequence then runs to 9:3, where Wisdom no longer shouts but *sends servants*, and to 9:14–15, where **Folly takes over her setting** — מְרֹמֵי קָרֶת ("the heights of the city", 9:3 and 9:14 only) — **but sends no one; she sits and calls** `[S: audit 2]`. Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Tool 11, Vocabulary. *Moderate–high confidence* `[S: audit 2]` *— on the phrase, not the bare lemma count.*

3. **The two invitations in chapter 9 are the same sentence, and the frame around them is not.** `[T]`, WLC. 9:4 מִי־פֶתִי יָסֻר הֵנָּה חֲסַר־לֵב אָמְרָה לּוֹ and 9:16 מִי־פֶתִי יָסֻר הֵנָּה **וַ**חֲסַר־לֵב **וְ**אָמְרָה לּוֹ. **The invitation — the four words the two women actually say — is word-for-word identical. The narrator's frame around each speaker is not: 9:16 adds two conjunctions.** The book's central problem is stated as a doublet, and the doublet is exact where it matters. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Parallels, Tool 11 Move 4. *High confidence. (This corrects the fuller "word for word identical" claim carried in the earlier file — see Book-Overview Tensions.)*

4. **Chapters 1–9 and chapters 10–31 speak different dialects, and the numbers are stark.** `[T]`, WLC. מַעְגָּל ("course, track") stands in **seven verses of chs 1–9 and none at all in chs 10–31**; אֹרַח ("path") is four times denser in the frame (4.7% of verses against 1.1%); נְתִיבָה ("pathway") five against one. Running the other way, צַדִּיק ("righteous") is in 1.6% of the frame's verses and 9.4% of the field's, רָשָׁע ("wicked") 2.7% against 10.6%, כְּסִיל ("fool") 1.6% against 6.8%. **The frame teaches the reader to read the field using words the field does not use.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Genre, Author's Purpose. *High confidence on the counts.*

5. **The divine name is *less* dense in chapters 1–9 than in the rest of the book — which cuts against the obvious assumption.** `[T]`, WLC. יְהוָה stands in 19 of the frame's 256 verses (7.4%) and in 68 of the remaining 659 (10.3%). Whatever makes chs 1–9 the theological frame, it is not frequency of the divine name. Surfaced by: Repetition, Author's Purpose. *High confidence on the count; the inference is the preacher's to draw.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**At book level**, the overview's microscript holds `[S: overview]`: wisdom is acquired and self-certification is what the book removes.

**At unit level, chapters 1–9 exist to make the rest of the book readable.** That is not a modern description; it is what the unit does, and three text-features show it `[T]`:

- **It supplies the categories the sentence collections will use without explaining.** פֶּתִי ("the naive") is defined in the frame and then simply appears in the field. The frame is where the cast is introduced.
- **It supplies the reading posture.** 1:6 announces that the material will need interpreting; chapter 2 supplies the conditions on which it can be got; chapters 3–7 rehearse the two roads until the reader has a map.
- **It supplies the alternative.** The field never argues with a rival voice. The frame does nothing else: from 1:11 the book is staging competing invitations, and by 9:4/9:16 the two sound identical.

**The unit's own purpose statement is 1:2–6, and its motto is 1:7** — both established in the solo run on 1:1–7 and not re-derived here `[S: 1:1–7 dig]`.

**What the unit is *not* doing.** It is not arguing for the existence of wisdom, and it is not establishing God's authority — the divine name is less frequent here than later (Headline 5). It is doing something narrower and more interesting: *forming a hearer*. `[I]`, high confidence.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** The unit opens the book, so the preceding movement is the canon: Psalms and Job, and behind them the Torah and the Prophets `[T]` on the canonical order. The solo run on 1:1–7 established the Job 28:28 relation — the fear of the Lord as the terminus of a failed search in Job, and as the *beginning* of a successful one here `[S: 1:1–7 dig]`.

**Necessity answer, at unit level.** Chapters 1–9 exist *before* 10:1 because a reader who met 10:1 first would have no way to read it. The evidence is distributional and is Headline 4: the sentence collections run on a vocabulary of settled types — righteous, wicked, fool — and the frame is where those types are populated and where the reader is told which one he is. **Remove chapters 1–9 and the book becomes an anthology of verdicts with no account of how anyone arrives at them.** `[I]`, high confidence, resting on `[T]` counts.

**And there is a second, sharper answer the unit supplies itself.** The frame ends at 9:18 with a man who does not know where he is: וְלֹא־יָדַע כִּי־רְפָאִים שָׁם ("But he does not know that the dead are there") `[T]`. The last thing the frame says before the proverbs begin is that a person can be at the wrong table and not know it. **That is why the field follows: nine hundred sentences of discrimination for a reader who has just been shown that he cannot tell the two houses apart.** `[I]`, moderate-to-high confidence.

**Implication.** The sermon's job is not to summarise nine chapters. It is to bring the congregation to 9:18 in the state 9:18 assumes.

#### Immediate context

**Before:** nothing. **After:** 10:1, מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה — the second superscription, and with it the formal break of the book `[T]`. WLC prints a mid-verse petuchah at 10:1, immediately after the superscription and before "A wise son makes a father glad" `[T]`, WLC — the only mid-verse marker in the book, and a reading-tradition feature rather than an authorial one.

**Internal boundaries.** WLC marks **24 paragraph markers in chs 1–9** — 1:7, 1:19, 1:33, 2:22, 3:4, 3:10, 3:18, 3:35, 4:19, 5:6, 5:23, 6:5, 6:11, 6:15, 6:19, 6:26, 6:35, 7:23, 7:27, 8:21, 8:31, 8:36, 9:9, 9:18 — one per 10.7 verses, against one per 94 in 10:1–22:16 `[T]`, WLC. The frame is the most heavily articulated prose in the first two-thirds of the book. Again: a reading tradition, not an authorial signal, but it tracks the syntax closely and every marker falls at a place the sense also breaks.

### 3. Structure

**The unit's own signals are three, and they do not perfectly coincide** — which is why proposals differ `[T]`:

1. **The vocatives.** בְּנִי fifteen times (1:8, 10, 15; 2:1; 3:1, 11, 21; 4:10, 20; 5:1, 20; 6:1, 3, 20; 7:1) and בָּנִים four (4:1; 5:7; 7:24; 8:32).
2. **The speaker changes.** Father → Wisdom (1:20) → father (2:1) → … → father's last words (7:27) → Wisdom (8:1) → narrator (9:1–3) → Wisdom quoted (9:4–6) → aphorisms (9:7–12) → Folly (9:13–18).
3. **The paragraph markers.** Twenty-four, listed above.

**A speaker map, which is the structure that matters for this sermon:**

| Verses | Speaker | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–7 | Editorial voice | Superscription and purpose; no vocative, no imperative `[S: 1:1–7 dig]` |
| 1:8–19 | **Father** | First address; quotes the sinners' invitation (1:11–14) |
| 1:20–33 | **Wisdom** | First public call; ends in refusal and threat |
| 2:1–22 | **Father** | One conditional sentence; no imperative `[S: ch. 2 dig]` |
| 3:1–4:27 | **Father** | The imperative-dense heart: 11 imperatives in ch. 3, 25 in ch. 4 |
| 5:1–23 | **Father** | First full strange-woman address; 5:7 vocative plural |
| 6:1–19 | **Father** | Surety, sluggard, the numerical saying |
| 6:20–35 | **Father** | Second strange-woman address |
| 7:1–27 | **Father** | Third, and narrated: "I looked out my window" |
| **7:27** | — | **The father's last words in the book** |
| 8:1–36 | **Wisdom** | Second public call; the longest first-person speech |
| 9:1–6 | Narrator + **Wisdom** | She builds, sends, and calls |
| 9:7–12 | Aphoristic voice, blended | Commands a "you" (9:8–9); 9:11 "by me" is Wisdom's, its "years of life" the father's (3:2; 4:10) `[S: audit 2]` |
| 9:13–18 | **Folly** | The mirror call |

**The device: a handover.** The unit is not a chiasm and not a linear argument. It is a **transfer of voice**. The father speaks for six chapters, is interrupted twice by Wisdom, and then stops — and Wisdom takes his closing formula with her. The measurement that shows it is the first/second-person density per chapter `[T]`, WLC, counting person-marked verbs and pronominal suffixes:

| | ch1 | ch2 | ch3 | ch4 | ch5 | ch6 | ch7 | **ch8** | ch9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1st-person markers** | 27 | 3 | 6 | 17 | 14 | 3 | 24 | **39** | 3 |
| **2nd-person markers** | 26 | 21 | 55 | 64 | 34 | 46 | 20 | **9** | 19 |

Chapter 8 inverts the book's normal ratio: thirty-nine first-person markers against nine second-person. **It is the only chapter in the unit where the speaker is more present than the addressee** `[T]`, and it is the chapter where Wisdom speaks for herself.

### 4. Linking Words

Over 256 verses a connector-by-connector trace is not useful; three patterns are `[T]`:

- **The unit's argument runs on purpose and condition, not on inference.** אִם ("if") governs the whole of ch. 2 and returns at 1:10, 3:24, 4:16, 6:1, 9:12; לְמַעַן ("so that") and לְ + infinitive carry the purposes. There is very little "therefore" in chs 1–9: the father does not deduce, he lays out.
- **כִּי ("for") is the workhorse.** It stands in **42 of the unit's 256 verses** `[T]`, WLC — 1:9, 16, 17, 29, 32; 2:3, 6, 10, 18, 21; 3:2, 12, 14, 25, 26, 32; 4:2, 3, 8, 13, 16, 17, 22, 23; 5:3, 21; 6:3, 23, 26, 30, 34, 35; 7:6, 19, 23, 26; 8:6, 7, 11, 35; 9:11, 18. The pattern established in the ch. 2 run holds across the whole unit: **promises are grounded, not asserted**. Note where the last one falls — 9:18, the narrator's comment.
- **וְעַתָּה ("now then") is the unit's turn-marker**, and it occurs three times, always with the plural vocative — 5:7, 7:24, 8:32 `[T]`. It is how a speaker moves from illustration to appeal.

### 5. Parallels

Bicolon parallelism is continuous. Three pairings carry the unit:

**1:20–21 ‖ 8:1–3 — the same call, escalated.** Not a parallel within a verse but between two openings, and it is verified lemma by lemma `[T]`, WLC: רנן ("cry aloud", 1:20, 8:3), נתן קוֹל ("lift up voice", 1:20, 8:1), רֹאשׁ ("top", 1:21, 8:2), שַׁעַר ("gate", 1:21, 8:3), פֶּתַח ("entrance", 1:21, 8:3), קרא ("call", 1:21, 8:1). **Seven lemmas across five verses** — and the exact phrase תִּתֵּן קוֹלָהּ ("she gives her voice") at 1:20 and 8:1 only `[S: audit 2]`. What changes is the posture: in ch. 1 she is in the noisy street and is refused; in ch. 8 she stands on the heights, beside the road, at the crossroads, and argues.

**9:1–6 ‖ 9:13–18 — the mirror.** Both women have a house (בַּיִת, vv.1, 14), both are at a high place (מְרֹמֵי קָרֶת, vv.3, 14 — identical phrase), both address the same person in the same words (vv.4, 16), both offer food and drink `[T]`. The differences are more than three `[S: audit 2]`: Wisdom **builds** (בָּנְתָה), slaughters, mixes, **sets a table and sends**; Folly **sits** and calls, and sends no one; Wisdom speaks in imperatives (vv.5–6), Folly in a claim (v.17); Wisdom's food is slaughtered and mixed, Folly's is stolen (מַיִם־גְּנוּבִים, v.17); and the one ends in "live" (v.6), the other in v.18.

**Antithetical parallelism is rare here and that is a finding.** The form that will dominate from 10:1 is almost absent from the frame; the frame prefers synonymous and synthetic lines `[T]`. The one place it arrives in force is 2:21–22, at the close of the chapter, which the ch. 2 run identified as the field's form arriving early `[S: ch. 2 dig]`.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**Rarer than speech, and two of them matter.** The unit is mostly speech; narration introduces Wisdom (1:20–21; 8:1–3) and stages chapter 9 (9:1–3, 13–15, with the frames at 9:4, 16) `[S: audit 2]`. Two moments carry weight `[T]`:

- **7:6–9.** The father steps out of exhortation into first-person narrative: כִּי בְּחַלּוֹן בֵּיתִי בְּעַד אֶשְׁנַבִּי נִשְׁקָפְתִּי ("For at the window of my house I looked out through my lattice"). It is the only sustained narrative in the unit, and it exists so that the son sees the young man from outside, as the father saw him.
- **9:18.** וְלֹא־יָדַע כִּי־רְפָאִים שָׁם בְּעִמְקֵי שְׁאוֹל קְרֻאֶיהָ ("But he does not know that the dead are there, That her guests are in the depths of Sheol"). A piece of information given to the reader and withheld from the character — **and it repeats the father's own sentence**: 7:23 closes his window-story with וְלֹא־יָדַע כִּי־בְנַפְשׁוֹ הוּא ("and he does not know that it will cost him his life"); the clause וְלֹא־יָדַע כִּי stands only at 7:23, 9:18 and 28:22, each time chapter-final in the frame `[S: audit 2]`. **The father has fallen silent, and the narrator ends the frame in his words.** It is the last sentence before the proverbs begin, and it is the reason they are needed. *High confidence on the echo; v1.0's "only true narrator's comment" is withdrawn* `[S: audit 2]`.

### 7. Vocabulary

**The way-words.** דֶּרֶךְ ("way") in 28 verses of the unit, אֹרַח ("path") in 12, מַעְגָּל ("course") in 7, נְתִיבָה ("pathway") in 5 `[T]`, WLC. **מַעְגָּל occurs in no verse of chs 10–31 at all**, and נְתִיבָה in one. The frame's governing image is a road, and two of its four road-words effectively belong to it alone. *High confidence.*

**חָכְמוֹת (1:20; 9:1).** The odd plural form, five occurrences in the whole WLC — four in Proverbs and one at Ps 49:4 `[T]`. Within the unit it marks the first and third public calls; 8:1 uses the ordinary חָכְמָה. *High confidence on the count; the significance of the alternation is an open question.*

**פֶּתִי ("the naive").** Eight verses in the frame against six in the rest of the book, and in a unit a third the length `[T]`. The frame's addressee is the field's occasional subject. *High confidence.*

**אִשָּׁה זָרָה / נָכְרִיָּה ("strange woman / adulteress").** זָר in seven verses of the frame, נָכְרִי in five `[T]`. The figure carries four passages — 2:16–19; 5:1–23; 6:20–35; 7:1–27 — and the ch. 2 run established that 2:16 and 7:5 are verbatim identical `[S: ch. 2 dig]`. The words themselves mean *outsider*, not *immoral*; the moral colouring is contextual.

**Proper-noun inventory (Tool 7c).** Across 256 verses the unit names: שְׁלֹמֹה, דָּוִד, יִשְׂרָאֵל (all at 1:1 only) and יְהוָה (19 verses) `[T]`. **No place, no other person, no people, no number with canonical freight.** Nothing else in the unit is named at all. The three names at 1:1 were treated in the 1:1–7 run, where the Queen-of-Sheba riddle link was worked `[S: 1:1–7 dig]`; no further name-hyperlink check is triggered. *That the longest instructional unit in the Old Testament contains no proper noun after its first verse is itself worth a sentence* `[T]`.

### 8. Translations

**Pulpit divergence note.** On this unit NIV84 does the preacher a favour at the one place that matters most for the sermon's spine: its uniform rendering of וְעַתָּה בָנִים שִׁמְעוּ־לִי at 5:7, 7:24 and 8:32 makes the handover audible in English. Say the three references aloud and the congregation will hear it without being told. Elsewhere the two versions' differences are local and are covered in the consumed runs.

**Ancient versions check.** The moderns agree substantively on 1:20–21, 8:1–3 and 9:1–6, and those renderings carry weight, so the check runs.

- **1:20 — Swete has ὑμνεῖται ("is hymned/praised") for תָּרֹנָּה, and BHS proposes reading תִּרְנֶה or רִנָּתָהּ on the Greek's evidence** `[S: BHS, apparatus at 1:20]`. **Triage: category 2, minor.** The Hebrew's plural verb with the odd plural subject is awkward and the versions felt it. No preaching consequence, but it is part of why חָכְמוֹת is an open question.
- **1:21 — Swete has τειχέων ("of walls") where the Hebrew has הֹמִיּוֹת ("the noisy streets"), and BHS records it as reflecting חֹמוֹת ("walls")** `[S: BHS, apparatus at 1:21]`. **Triage: category 2.** The Hebrew is the harder reading and is to be preferred; but note that the Greek puts Wisdom on the city wall rather than in the din, which changes the picture a congregation would form. *Moderate confidence.*
- **8:22 — Swete has Κύριος ἔκτισέν με ("the Lord created me") for קָנָנִי** `[T]`, Swete. **Triage: category 2 on the lexical choice, and category 3 in its effects**, since this rendering carried the fourth-century Arian argument. Treated at book level in the overview `[S: overview]`; the sermon needs to know it exists and should not preach ch. 8 as though the identification with Christ were straightforward.
- **9:18 — Swete adds a plus** after the Hebrew's close `[T]`, Swete. **Triage: category 2.** The Hebrew ends on the reader's knowledge of what the young man does not know; the Greek continues. **Preach the Hebrew's ending — it is the frame's last word and the Greek blunts it.**

### 9. Tone and Feel

**The unit has three distinct registers and moves between them deliberately** `[T]`:

- **Warm and urgent** in the father's addresses — vocatives, imperatives, promises, the accumulation of second-person forms (ch. 3: 55 markers; ch. 4: 64).
- **Public and forensic** in Wisdom's speeches. 1:20–33 is a prophetic oracle in everything but name: a call, a refusal, a threat, and a closing promise. 8:1–36 is an argument — claims, warrants, a cosmology, an appeal.
- **Cool and narrative** at 7:6–23 and 9:1–18, where the father and then the narrator simply describe.

**The tonal fact a preacher should build on** is the change of temperature at ch. 9. After nine chapters of address, the unit ends in the third person, watching two women set two tables — and then, in one flat sentence, tells the reader what the guest does not know. **The frame does not end by pleading. It ends by showing.** `[I]`, high confidence.

**Soundtrack:** a voice growing more insistent for six chapters; then a second, larger voice taking the same melody in ch. 8; then, in ch. 9, the voices stop and you hear only two doors opening onto the same street.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources** `[S: overview]`: Genesis 1–3, Deuteronomy, Job 28, Psalms, the Solomon tradition, Amenemope. No citation formula occurs anywhere in Proverbs `[T]`.

**Genesis 1–3 → 3:18–20; 8:22–31** *(high confidence on the vocabulary; moderate on the intent)*

*Source context:* Genesis 1 establishes creation by ordered speech over an unformed deep; Genesis 2–3 sets a tree of life in a garden and introduces a creature who is עָרוּם ("crafty").

*Book usage:* the tree of life is a live chain — עֵץ חַיִּים at 3:18, 11:30, 13:12, 15:4, verified, and the phrase occurs in the WLC only in Genesis and Proverbs `[S: 1:1–7 dig, confirmed corpus-wide]`. תְּהוֹם ("deep") stands at 3:20 and at 8:24, 27, as at Gen 1:2, verified by lemma `[T]`, WLC.

*OT-to-OT:* Job 28 asks where wisdom is found and searches the deeps; Prov 8 answers from the deeps' own making. The two are in conversation independently of Proverbs 3 `[I]`, moderate confidence.

*What it adds:* the frame grounds its road-and-two-women argument in creation twice — once briefly (3:19–20) and once at length (8:22–31). **The point is not cosmology for its own sake but authority: the voice calling in the street was there when the world was made.** That is the argument of ch. 8 and it is why ch. 8 sits where it does.

**Deuteronomy 6:6–9 (with 11:18–20) and Exodus 31:18 → 6:20–22; 7:3; 3:3** *(high confidence on the verbal link, strongest at 7:3 and 6:20–22)* `[S: audit 2]`

*Source context:* the Shema's command to bind the words on hand and forehead and write them on doorposts.

*Book usage:* קשר ("bind") at 3:3, 6:21, 7:3 and כתב ("write") at 3:3, 7:3 — verified by lemma against Deut 6:8–9 `[T]`, WLC. **Binding and writing a teaching occur together in the canon only in Deut 6:8–9, Deut 11:18–20, Prov 3:3 and Prov 7:3** `[S: audit 2]`, and the writing surface is the לוּחַ ("tablet") of the heart (only 3:3; 7:3; Jer 17:1), לוּחַ being the Sinai word. **7:3 is the closest**: אֶצְבַּע ("finger") + לוּחַ ("tablet") + כתב ("write") stand together only at Exod 31:18, Deut 9:10 and Prov 7:3 `[S: audit 2]`. **6:22** adds the Shema's day — "when you walk… when you lie down… when you wake" (walk + lie down + rise only Deut 6:7; 11:19; 1 Sam 3:6) `[S: audit 2]`. Two cautions: at **3:3** the things bound are חֶסֶד וֶאֱמֶת ("kindness and truth"), not the father's words; and 3:3's tablet line is absent from Swete and Rahlfs (BHS: "> 𝔊^BS, dl (ex 7,3)"). The Greek uses Deuteronomy's ἀφάπτω ("fasten") at 3:3 and 6:21 (only Deut 6:8; 11:18; Prov 3:3; 6:21 in Swete) and has no "tablet" anywhere in Proverbs `[S: audit 2]`.

*OT-to-OT:* Jer 31:33 writes the torah on the heart with the same two lemmas, כתב and לֵב, verified `[S: overview, confirmed]`.

*What it adds:* the frame is not offering advice; it is claiming, by borrowed posture, the authority of instruction that is binding. **And it does so for a father's words, not for God's** — which is either a striking transfer or the clearest sign that the father speaks as a covenant instructor. *Moderate-to-high confidence.*

### 12. Genre

**Extended paternal instruction, with two embedded first-person speeches by a personified figure and one closing tableau.** The reading rules follow. The instruction material is not proverbial and must not be read as generalisation; it is sustained argument and should be followed as such. **The personification is the genre feature most often mishandled**: חָכְמָה is a figure in a poem, given house, servants, table and voice, and the poem's rules — not systematic theology's — govern what may be inferred from her `[T]`. The unit's one stretch of sentence-literature, 9:7–12, sits oddly and is discussed under Open Questions.

### 13. Copycat

**N/A for most of the unit** — there are no narrative characters whose conduct is being evaluated, only types within instruction.

**One exception, and it is instructive.** 7:6–23 does present a character: the young man crossing the street at dusk. He is neither model nor warning-by-verdict; the father narrates him **so that the son will recognise himself before the event rather than after** `[T]`. The right label is *negative example, observed rather than judged* — the narrator never condemns him, and 7:22 simply says he goes "as an ox goes to the slaughter."

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** The frame of a collection attributed to Solomon `[T]` on the attribution; any date is reconstruction `[S]`. Canonically: Ketuvim, after Psalms and Job.

**Canonical position (five fields, extracted from the overview** `[S: overview]`**).** Section: Ketuvim, third in the BHS order. Reading implication: the frame's confidence is heard after Job's refusal. Presupposes: the Shema's bind-and-write (3:3; 6:21; 7:3); Eden's tree (3:18) and deep (3:20; 8:24, 27); Deuteronomy's covenant curse via 2:22 `[S: ch. 2 dig]`; the Solomon narratives behind 1:1. Handoff: the vocabulary and the cast that 10:1–31:31 will use without explaining. Neighbours: Job before, Ruth after.

**Where the reader sits.** This side of the cross, and the unit's two most preachable features point forward differently. The **handover of voice** (Headline 1) finds its analogue in a Son who speaks the Father's words (John 12:49) `[I]`, moderate confidence — noted as resonance, not as a claimed allusion. The **personified Wisdom** of ch. 8 is drawn on by the New Testament for its language rather than exegeted (Col 1:15–17; 1 Cor 1:24, 30; John 1:1–3) `[S]`.

**How that reframes the reading.** The frame's question is *whose voice will you obey*, and the New Testament's answer is a person rather than a principle. The timeline's work here is mostly to stop the sermon treating ch. 8 as a christological proof-text.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** To be formed into someone who can tell two identical invitations apart. Not more information; a trained ear. The unit gives its reader nine chapters of the same contest so that when he meets the sentence collections — and the street — he already knows the shape of it.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | There are two voices, they are equally public, and they say the same thing. The difference between them is not volume, plausibility or location — it is what is on the table and where the guests are |
| **Behaviour — start** | Listen to the voice that has a history — who built the house, who paid for the food. Wisdom's credentials in ch. 8 are the whole answer to why one of the two identical invitations should be taken |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Assuming that a bad invitation will sound bad. 9:4 and 9:16 are the end of that assumption |
| **Motivation** | Not fear of the consequences — though 9:18 supplies them — but the fact that one of the two speakers was there when the world was made (8:22–31) |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me:** I am being addressed as the son, and the unit will not let me take the father's chair even while I preach it.
- **For a Christian friend:** for anyone who thinks discernment is obvious, 9:4 and 9:16 are the two verses to put side by side.
- **For the church:** the frame is corporate formation, not private study. A congregation that has heard chs 1–9 whole can read the rest of the book; one that has heard three favourite verses cannot.
- **For an unbeliever:** the unit's claim is that there are two invitations and that they sound the same — which is a claim anyone can test against their own experience before they are asked to believe anything else.

**Prayer in response.** Thanks for a book that says the two voices sound alike rather than pretending otherwise. Sorry for assuming I would know. Please give the ear the frame is trying to form, and keep me from the table at 9:18.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** No narrative setting; the unit names no place and no event `[T]`. What it assumes is a household with a father who teaches, a son old enough to be recruited and propositioned, a city with streets, gates and a market, and a social world in which a woman could invite a passer-by into a house.

**What the original audience knew.**

- **That instruction literature had this shape** — a named authority, a named addressee, a purpose statement — and that it was international `[S]`, with BHS itself cross-referencing Amenemope at 22:18, 22:20 `[S: BHS]`.
- **What the city gate was.** Not an entrance but the civic space — where cases were heard, business done, elders sat. Wisdom crying at the gate (1:21; 8:3) is claiming the place of public authority, not begging in a doorway `[I]`, moderate-to-high confidence.
- **What a built house with hewn pillars implied** (9:1): substance, permanence, cost. Folly has a house too but does not build it `[T]` on the verbs.
- **What the clean-and-costly meal against stolen water and secret bread would have signalled** — provision honestly obtained against provision taken `[I]`.

**What this changes.** The two invitations are not a respectable voice against a disreputable one. **Both are public, both are at the high point of the city, both are hospitable.** A sermon that makes Folly seedy has removed the problem the chapter exists to state.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Ketuvim `[T]`; the reader arrives after the Torah and the whole of the Prophets, and therefore after the exile `[I]`, high confidence. Canonical register: Job and the wisdom psalms are the nearest neighbours.

**The first hearers.** Young men on the threshold of responsibility, and the households that transmitted this to them `[I]`.

**Where the passage fits their world.** They would have recognised the instruction form and the gate as the place of authority; they would have expected a father to command; and they would have found the personification of wisdom as a woman calling in public unusual enough to attend to.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Surprising:** that the father stops. Six chapters of address and then nothing.
- **Shocking:** 9:4 and 9:16. Two women, one sentence.
- **Comforting:** 8:22–31 — the voice competing for you is older than the world.
- **Disturbing:** 9:18, and its placement. The unit's last word is not an appeal but an obituary.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A private, textual model of "reading Proverbs" where the unit assumes a street, a gate and a voice. A discomfort with the gendering of the two figures which a first hearer would not have shared — a pastoral matter for the sermon, not a textual one. And an assumption that wrong invitations advertise themselves, which is exactly the assumption 9:4/9:16 exists to destroy.

**Implication for the sermon.** The work is not to warn against folly — everyone already agrees folly is bad. It is to show that folly says the same thing wisdom says, and then to give the congregation the one thing that distinguishes them: who built the house.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**(1) The two ways.**
*Where the unit sits:* the OT's fullest development of the motif, carried by a road-vocabulary largely confined to these nine chapters `[T]`. *Before:* Deut 30:15–20 (life and death set before them); Ps 1 (the two ways, with דֶּרֶךְ). *After:* Prov 10–31 uses the categories without the roads; and in the NT, the two gates and two ways (Matt 7:13–14) `[S]`. *Confidence: high on the OT chain; the Matthew link is standard and is `[S]`.*
*Contribution:* the frame's roads are what make the field's verdicts intelligible.

**(2) Wisdom personified and present at creation.**
*Where the unit sits:* 3:19–20 and 8:22–31 `[T]`. *Before:* Gen 1:2's תְּהוֹם, shared by lemma `[T]`; Job 28's search of the deeps. *After:* the NT's wisdom language for Christ `[S]`; and the Arian history that shows how far the identification can be pushed `[S]`.
*Contribution:* the argument of ch. 8 is an argument from *authority by antiquity*, and it is the answer to 9:4/9:16.

**(3) The word written on the heart.**
*Where the unit sits:* 6:20–22; 7:3 (and 3:3), with Deuteronomy's two verbs, the Shema's day, and Sinai's finger and tablet `[S: audit 2]`. *Before:* Deut 6:6–9; 11:18–20; Exod 31:18; Deut 9:10. *After:* Jer 31:33 `[T]`; and 2 Cor 3:3 `[S]` — which draws on Exodus, Ezekiel and Jer 38:33 LXX, not on the Greek of Proverbs, which has no "tablet" `[S: audit 2]`.
*Contribution:* the father's instruction is given the posture of covenant law, which is either a large claim or a clue about who is really speaking.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: trajectory and contrast. No typology is claimed.** The unit has no person, institution or event that could serve as a type; personified חָכְמָה is a figure in a poem, and treating her as a type would fail the category test at the first step.

**How the unit points to Christ.**

- **The voice with a history (8:22–31).** The unit's answer to two identical invitations is that one speaker was present at the making of the world. The New Testament names Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24, 30), and its nearest language to Prov 8:22 is Col 1:15–18 ("firstborn of all creation… all things were created through him") and Rev 3:14 — without exegeting Prov 8 `[S: audit 2]`. *v1.0 cited John 1:3 here; the audit puts John 1:1–3 low–moderate for Prov 8: John builds on Gen 1:1, and "all things came into being through him" has no counterpart in Prov 8* `[S: audit 2]`. In the Gospels Jesus also speaks in Wisdom's place (Luke 11:49 // Matt 23:34; moderate), though not as the figure of Prov 8–9 (low) `[S: audit 2]`. **Preach the trajectory: the world was made wisely, and the wisdom by which it was made has come to us in a person.** *Moderate-to-high confidence on the trajectory; the identification is a category error and the Greek's ἔκτισέν με shows why.*
- **The handover of voice (Headline 1).** The father speaks, then falls silent, and the voice that continues is Wisdom's, using his words. The resonance with a Son who says οὐκ ἐλάλησα, ἀλλ᾽ ὁ πέμψας με πατὴρ ("I did not speak on My own initiative, but the Father who sent Me", John 12:49) is real and should be named as resonance, not as allusion `[I]`, moderate confidence. *It is homiletically strong and exegetically modest; say which it is.*
- **The contrast — the guest at 9:18.** The frame ends with a man who does not know he is among the dead, in the father's own words from 7:23. Nothing in chs 1–9 retrieves him — and the frame says so: כָּל־בָּאֶיהָ לֹא יְשׁוּבוּן ("none who go to her return", 2:19) `[T]`. Wisdom offers a turning before the door (1:23); no one comes back out of the other house. **The unit's most Christ-shaped feature is the shape of the hole it leaves.**

**Trajectory.** *Anticipates:* a voice that can be trusted because of who it is, and a discernment the hearer does not have. *Christ fulfils:* the wisdom of God in person, and the Spirit who gives the ear. *Remains:* two invitations, still public, still similar.

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** here it is "listen to Wisdom, not Folly" — which sounds like a gospel and is not one, because the unit has just said the two sound the same. Preached flat, it is an exhortation to do the very thing the text says the hearer cannot do unaided.
- **The gospel grounding:** ch. 8's answer. The ground for trusting one voice is not that it sounds better but that it was there first — and for the Christian hearer, that it has since become flesh.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** the hero of the unit is the voice that calls, not the son who listens.

**Confidence:** *moderate-to-high* on the trajectory; *no typological claim advanced*; the John 12:49 resonance is explicitly `[I]`.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

- **8:22–31.** The *locus classicus*. קָנָנִי ("possessed me" / "created me") and אָמוֹן (8:30, "master workman" / "nursling") are both genuinely contested, and the Greek's ἔκτισέν με is why the fourth century argued about it. The overview holds the section at moderate confidence `[S: overview]`; the sermon should say plainly that this is a poem's personification and not a description of the pre-incarnate Son.
- **9:7–12.** Six verses of sentence-literature in the middle of the tableau, addressed to no one *in the scene* but to a "you" (9:8–9, 11–12), with 9:10's motto at their centre and a voice that blends Wisdom's "by me" with the father's promise (9:11) `[S: audit 2]`. Whether they are an interlude, a later insertion, or an aside is genuinely open; the Masoretic break falls after 9:9, not 9:6 `[S: audit 2]`. *See Open Questions.*
- **The strange-woman passages (2:16–19; 5; 6:20–35; 7).** Four passages, roughly 70 verses, in a unit of 256. Pastorally this needs deliberate handling: the text's emphasis falls on her **speech** and her **abandonment**, and the unit's symmetry gives the perverse *man* equal weight at 2:12–15. Preach the pair.
- **1:20–33.** Wisdom's threat — "I will laugh at your calamity" (1:26) — is the hardest thing in the unit to read aloud. It is a prophetic-oracle register, and **the frame answers it itself**: the seeking that fails at 1:28 succeeds at 8:17, in the same two words (Move 4). Preach 1:26 with 8:17 in hand, not on its own.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: Making Folly obviously bad

- **What it looks like:** Wisdom dignified and Folly tawdry; two invitations the congregation is expected to tell apart at a glance.
- **Why it's wrong:** 9:4 and 9:16 are **the same four words** `[T]`. Both women have a house, both are at the high point of the city — the identical phrase, 9:3 and 9:14 — and both open with the same words. The invitations are indistinguishable by their sound. *(v1.0 added "both set a table, both send or call": false — only Wisdom sets a table and sends; Folly sits and calls* `[S: audit 2]`*.)*
- **The corrective:** read 9:4 and 9:16 one after the other, aloud, and let the room sit with it. Then ask what does distinguish them — and answer from the text: what each host does, serves and leads to (9:1–6 against 9:13–18), the fear of the LORD between them (9:10), and ch. 8's voice that was there first; not from tone of voice `[S: audit 2]`.

### Pitfall: Preaching the unit as nine chapters of advice

- **What it looks like:** a survey — "chapter 3 on trust, chapter 4 on the path, chapter 5 on purity…"
- **Why it's wrong:** the unit is one movement with a shape, and the shape is the point. The father speaks, is interrupted twice, hands over his formula and stops; then two women set two tables. A survey loses every one of those.
- **The corrective:** preach the movement. The series rationale — that people hear the book in fragments — applies to this sermon more than to any other in the series.

### Pitfall: Taking the father's chair

- **What it looks like:** a sermon delivered from 1:8 — the preacher as the instructing father, the congregation as the son.
- **Why it's wrong:** the unit removes that seat. The father falls silent after 7:27 and the voice that continues is not his `[T]`. The preacher is a son being addressed, and saying so is both true and disarming.
- **The corrective:** preach from inside the vocative, not from above it.

### Pitfall: Preaching Proverbs 8 as Christ

- **What it looks like:** "Here the pre-incarnate Son tells us he was with the Father at creation."
- **Why it's wrong:** it flattens a poem's personification into a christophany, and the Greek's ἔκτισέν με ("created me") is the historical proof of what that costs. The NT uses the language; it does not exegete the chapter `[S]`.
- **The corrective:** preach the trajectory — the world was made wisely, and that wisdom has come to us in a person — and say which move you are making.

### Pitfall: Stopping at 9:6

- **What it looks like:** ending the sermon on Wisdom's invitation, because it is the warm place to end.
- **Why it's wrong:** the unit does not end there. It ends at 9:18 with a man at a table who does not know where he is — the father's sentence from 7:23, now spoken over Folly's guests, and the reason the rest of the book follows `[T]` `[S: audit 2]`.
- **The corrective:** end where the frame ends — but do not leave the appeal unpressed. The text applies itself at 8:32–36 and 9:6 ("leave your simple ways and live"); press that call, and let 9:18 be the last sentence.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

- **What is 9:7–12 doing?** Six aphoristic verses inside the tableau, addressed to no one in the scene but to a "you", with no marker of their edges. Interlude, insertion or aside — the text does not say, and the corpus cannot settle it. Two commentaries. *Audit 2 adds three data* `[S: audit 2]`: the WLC/BHS petuchah falls after **9:9**, not 9:6 (so the Masoretic division is 9:1–9 / 9:10–18); 9:11's speaker is blended (Wisdom's "by me", the father's "years of life"), and BHS notes the Greek's different reading there; and BHS records one manuscript omitting 9:9–10 and one 9:10–12.
- **Why חָכְמוֹת at 1:20 and 9:1 but חָכְמָה at 8:1?** The form occurs five times in the WLC `[T]`; the alternation within the unit is unexplained, and the Greek's ὑμνεῖται at 1:20 suggests the versions found the form difficult too.
- **אָמוֹן at 8:30.** "Master workman" or "little child"? A genuine crux, held at moderate confidence in the overview `[S: overview]`, and one the sermon can name rather than resolve.
- **Is the ten-lecture/five-interlude analysis of 1:8–9:18 the text's own division?** It is the standard critical account `[S]` and the vocatives partly support it, but the vocative count (15 singular, 4 plural) does not map cleanly onto ten. The report has used the speaker map instead, which is derived from the text alone.
