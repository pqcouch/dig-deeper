## How to use this

Each question is written to be **pasted straight in**. Under each: what depends on it, which document it belongs to, and — the part that matters most — **what a negative answer would require me to change**. A “no” is as useful as a “yes”; several of these are my own proposals and I would rather they were killed than quietly preached.

**Match the question to the right Logos tool.** This is the single biggest efficiency gain, and it is why the tiers are ordered as they are:

| **Tier** | **Question type** | **Best tool** | **Consumes AI credits?** |
| - | - | - | - |
| 1 | Load-bearing lexical facts | **Exegetical Guide** or **Bible Word Study** | No |
| 2 | Counts and distributions | **Search** (Bible search, lemma or morph) | No |
| 3 | Interpretive cruxes | **Study Assistant** | Yes |
| 4 | Proposed connections | **Study Assistant** | Yes |

### 1.4 · Proverbs 1:4 — ʿormâ and the serpent

> What does the Hebrew noun ormah (prudence) mean in Proverbs 1:4, and do any commentators connect it with the adjective arum used of the serpent in Genesis 3:1? Is the shared root taken as deliberate?

**Prudence** (עָרְמָה, derived from עָרַם) signifies properly nakedness, smoothness (comp. theadj. עָרִוּם \[“subtle” E.V.\], naked, *i.e*., slippery, crafty; used of the serpent, Gen. 3:1); therefore metaphorically “the capacity for escaping from the wiles of others” (Umbreit), “the prudence which guards itself against injury” (22:3; 1 Sam. 23:22)

Lange, J.P. et al. (2008) A commentary on the Holy Scriptures: Proverbs. Bellingham, WA: Logos Bible Software, p. 44.

The Hebrew word for **shrewdness** always has a positive connotation in the book of Proverbs (the adjective is translated “shrewd” in 12:23 and “sensible” in 12:16; 13:16; 14:8, 15, 18; 15:5; 22:3; 27:12). Such a person foresees and deals with difficulties (22:3). However, in other books it usually means “cunning” or “craftiness” that is in direct defiance of God’s will (Gn 3:1; Jb 5:12).

Stabnow, D.K. (2017) “Proverbs,” in Blum, E.A. and Wax, T. (eds.) CSB Study Bible: Notes. Nashville, TN: Holman Bible Publishers, p. 954.

- **Depends on it:** `dig-deeper-proverbs-1-1to7.md` Headline 2 — half of it. The *mĕzimmâ* half is already confirmed.

- **Status:** **this is my own proposal and it is entirely unsourced.** It has never been put to the library.

- **If the answer is no:** downgrade to a suggestion, and rewrite Headline 2 to rest on *mĕzimmâ* alone — which the 9 August check confirmed.

### 2.4 · *lēb* and *ḥālaq* in Proverbs 1:8–9:18

> How many times does leb (“heart”) occur in Proverbs 1:8 to 9:18, and at which verses? Separately, how many times does the root halaq (“smooth”) occur in the same span?

> לֵב appears 19 times – 2:2, 10; 3:1, 3, 5; 4:4,, 23; 5:12; 6:14, 18, 21, 32; 7:3, 7, 10, 25; 8:5; 9:4, 16

> לֵבָב appears twice – 4:21; 6:25

> חלק appears twice – 2:16; 7:5

> חָלָק appears once – 5:3 

> חֶלְקָה appears once – 6:24 

> חֵ֫לֶק appears once – 7:21

> So, yes, the root of *ḥālaq* appears five times in total

- **Claimed:** *lēb* 16× (2:2, 10; 3:1, 3, 5; 4:4, **21**, 23; 5:12; 6:14, 18, 21, 25; 7:3, 10, 25); *ḥālaq* 5× (2:16; 5:3; 6:24; 7:5, 21).

- **Used in:** `1-8to9-18.md` Tools 7(c) and 10; the “persuasion is the battlefield” finding.

- ✅ ***Ḥālaq* confirmed** at five.

- ❌ ***Lēb* was wrong, and wrong in two directions.** The correct figure is **19**, with *lēbāb* twice more. I had wrongly counted **4:21 and 6:25** as *lēb* when both are the byform *lēbāb*; and I had **missed five** genuine occurrences — 6:32; 7:7; **8:5**; **9:4**; **9:16**.

- 💡 **The correction is a finding, not just a fix.** Three of the five missed occurrences fall in chapters 8–9, precisely where the report observes that the vocative “my son” stops. Two of those three are the *same phrase*: *ḥăsar-lēb*, “whoever lacks sense”, spoken by **Wisdom at 9:4 and by Folly at 9:16 in identical words**. So the closing chapters do not drop the heart-language — they transfer it from the father to the two women, who then use identical words as each other at the decisive moment. This **strengthens** both the “father falls silent” reading and the Wisdom/Folly parallel at chapter 9. Folded into `1-8to9-18.md` Tool 7(c).

### 5.1 · LXX Proverbs 1:4

### 5.5 · Proverbs 7:22

> At Proverbs 7:22, the ESV reads “as a stag is caught fast”, following the LXX, Vulgate and Syriac, while the MT reads something like “as a chain to the discipline of a fool”. What is the evidence, and which reading do commentators prefer?

> A selection of commentators… 

> Kidner: The Heb. text of 22b is very doubtful (RV preserves it; AV transposes it; ancient versions find a dog, a stag (both in LXX) and a lamb (Vulg.), in texts not unlike the existing Hebrew; Toy glimpses a calf, but the general sense of 22, 23 is clear.

> Kidner, D. (1964) Proverbs: An Introduction and Commentary. Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press (Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries), p. 72.

> Schwab: Lit., “as \[one in\] fetters to punishment \[goes\] a fool” (’ewil). NLT emends ’ewil to ’ayyal (stag). The LXX reads “dog”; Vulgate, “lamb.”

> Schwab, G.M. (2009) “The Book of Proverbs,” in Cornerstone Biblical Commentary, Vol 7: The Book of Psalms, The Book of Proverbs. Carol Stream, IL: Tyndale House Publishers, p. 509.

> Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible: 

> Suddenly he follows her As an ox goes to the slaughter, Or as one in fetters to the discipline of a fool, 

> The LXX, Syr., and Tg. have “and as a dog to bonds” in place of “and like a stag to the instruction of a fool.” The Hebrew of this final line is very difficult and uncertain.

> Brannan, R. and Loken, I. (2014) The Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible. Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press (Lexham Bible Reference Series), p. Pr 7:22.

> Barnes: Lit. “As a fetter to the correction of a fool,” the order of which is inverted in the A.V. The LXX., followed by the Syriac Version, has another reading, and interprets the clause: “As a dog, enticed by food, goes to the chain that is to bind him, so does the youth go to the temptress.” None of the attempts of commentators to get a meaning out of the present text are in any degree satisfactory.

### 5.6 · Proverbs 30:1

> At Proverbs 30:1, the ESV reads “I am weary, O God, and worn out”, which rests on a revocalisation; the MT reads “to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal”, apparently proper names. What is the evidence for each?

> Ansberry seems to have the best word on this:

> While the expression לְאִֽיתִיאֵ֑ל לְאִ֖יתִיאֵ֣ל וְאֻכָֽל׃ may refer to addressees of the piece, or function as an agnostic, even atheistic confession (“There is no God, there is no God. How can I prevail?”), it appears the expression represents an assertion concerning the futility of human efforts to attain knowledge of God (“I am weary, O God, I am weary, O God, but I can prevail”). 

> This proposal is consistent with the remainder of the monologue. The enigmatic confession summarises Agur’s argument in verses 2–6, which moves from the epistemological limits of human wisdom (30:2–4) to the divine solution to human ignorance (30:5–6). Agur expresses the tension between human competence and incompetence to illuminate the locus of true wisdom. In so doing, he supplements the search for wisdom with a profound theological statement; he modulates the promise of wisdom by describing its limits (30:2–4) and links the acquisition of wisdom with Israel’s covenantal tradition (30:5–6).

> Within the larger context, the obscure expression seems to combine human exhaustion with confidence in the divine. This combination serves as a distillation of Agur’s address, which balances human weakness (30:2–4) with divine enablement (30:5–6) and sets the trajectory for the rhetorical flourish that follows.

- **Why it matters:** the ESV’s whole opening tone for Agur depends on an emendation, and a preacher should know.
