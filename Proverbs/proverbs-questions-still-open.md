## Group A · Lookups you can do yourself

### 0 ✅ · The LXX of Proverbs 22:8a — **CLOSED 20 August**

> **22:8a** ἄνδρα **ἱλαρὸν** καὶ **δότην** εὐλογεῖ **ὁ θεός** — *"God blesses a cheerful man and a giver."*
> **2 Cor 9:7** **ἱλαρὸν** γὰρ **δότην** ἀγαπᾷ **ὁ θεός** — *"for God loves a cheerful giver."*

**The Greek carries an entire additional verse with no Hebrew equivalent**, and Paul draws on it. Three words shared verbatim; **one changed — εὐλογεῖ → ἀγαπᾷ** — so it is close adaptation, not formula quotation.

**And Paul takes both cola.** 2 Cor 9:6's *"he who sows … will also reap"* has the construction of LXX 22:8; 9:7 has 22:8a. Consecutive verses in Paul from consecutive lines in Greek Proverbs. *(The 9:6 link is moderate on its own — sowing/reaping is a widespread topos — so it is the adjacency that carries it.)*

**Attested by Kruse, Keener and Martin.** **The most quoted verse in Christian teaching on giving is a verse of Greek Proverbs** — worth saying plainly. Full working: `dig-deeper-proverbs-16-1to22-16`, Tool 11, and `proverbs-question-record` §7.5.

### 5 · Proverbs 26:23 — ✅ **CLOSED 20 August. Retain the MT.**

**Both ancient versions read two words.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Vulgate** | *argento sordido* — "impure silver", i.e. *kesep sîgîm* exactly |
| **LXX** | ἀργύριον … ὄστρακον — silver and potsherd, matching *kesep* … *ḥereś*, with *sîgîm* rendered "with deceit" |

**Neither supports a single word meaning "glaze."** And modern scholarship has independently turned: the Zondervan Encyclopedia records that the Ugaritic emendation "has been challenged", **with the TNIV restoring "silver dross"**.

**So the ESV footnote records a conjecture, not a correction** — and the structural argument (the reversed *sîgîm* order at 25:4 ∥ 26:23) survives, which it would not have done on the revocalisation. That argument is still unattested and appears to be mine, but it now reaches the same verdict as two ancient translators. *Peshitta unchecked — a loose end, not a live question.*

### 6 · Ben Sira and the dating of 28–29 — ✅ **CLOSED with a caveat, 20 August**

**The circularity is still unaddressed in print** — the commentaries note the Torah-piety of these chapters without defending the dating logic that would expose the loop.

**But the inference turns out not to be one-way.** **Morris** (1942, 1:377): *"the meaning given to tôrāh accords better with a **pre-exilic** than a post-exilic date."* At least one commentator reads the same evidence toward an *earlier* date.

**That is enough to close it for preaching purposes.** A preacher who repeats "late" is repeating a **contested** claim, not walking a circle — which is all the question needed to establish. And the seam argument was never touched by it: the distribution of absolute *tôrāh* is a fact of the Hebrew whatever the date.

**Two bonuses applied to the 28–29 report.** **Pauw** independently confirms the semantic shift both runs derived from the morphology — *tôrâ* is parental teaching earlier in Proverbs, "in chapters 28–29 it refers primarily to God's teaching and law". **Van Leeuwen** (NIB) supplies the right canonical neighbour: "the Torah piety of the Psalms" — Ps 1, 19, 119, not a legal code.

**Say the six; drop the superlative.** Six in eighty-three verses is a good density, but it is not evidence of a maximum — Proverbs 3 alone supplies several NT quotations, and 10:12 and 11:31 sit outside this section. **I had held the superlative at *moderate–high*; that was not defensible, and it is retracted rather than left open.**

**The gain is Matthew 5:44, and it is considerable.** Matt 5:43 quotes a tradition — *"love your neighbour **and hate your enemy**"* — whose second clause appears nowhere in the Old Testament. **Exod 23:4–5 and Prov 25:21 are the texts that show it isn't there**, and both use the same word for the enemy, *śōnaʾăkā*. So Jesus is not correcting Moses but recovering him against an addition; and Paul, quoting the same verse at Rom 12:20 in the same argument, does the same. **This is now Headline Finding 5 of the 2A report.**

**And a withdrawal.** 25:27 → 2 Cor 10:12, 18 is not in the index — and rightly so: **the phrase it depends on, "to seek one's own glory", sits in the line the ESV itself footnotes as uncertain.** An allusion resting on a disputed rendering rests on the translator. *(27:2 → 2 Cor 10:18 survives as an unlisted resonance on a textually secure verse — but not as an allusion to preach.)*

Read as *Isaiah*, which is the obvious parse. **Logged as error 20**, with the lesson: write every question so it can be pasted into a search tool without ambiguity, and never open one with a bare "Is".

### 8 · The metaphorical reading of 27:23–27

Whether the flock stands for the king's subjects. **Structurally the unit is now settled (see above); this is the separable second question.** Ansberry's case rests on two checkable text-level observations — *nēzer* at v.24 (the consecrated diadem, not a general word for wealth) and *nəʿārôt* at v.27 (a retinue implying substance, cf. 9:3; 31:15). Most commentators read the poem as literal husbandry.

**The preaching guidance does not wait on it:** preach the poem as attentive stewardship of what is entrusted to you, motivated by v.24's warning that neither riches nor crowns endure, and let the shepherd-king resonance be heard through Ezekiel 34 and John 10 rather than asserted from *nēzer* alone.

### 9 · Hezekiah's canonical freight

The canonical reader meets a collection about not seeking one's own glory, transmitted by the men of the king who showed the Babylonian envoys his treasure-house (2 Kgs 20:12–19). **I did not re-read 2 Kgs 18–20 for the 2A run**, so the observation is flagged and not pressed — the load-the-story rule requires the source narrative to be read before a hyperlink is made load-bearing.
