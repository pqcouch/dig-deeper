Phase 0 — scope

A single sermon on a seven-verse unit. No scope question arises: 1:1–7 is a self-contained preamble — one Hebrew sentence with one interruption — bracketed by חָכְמָה וּמוּסָר at v.2 and v.7, closed by the connectorless motto at v.7, and followed at v.8 by the first vocative and the first imperative of the book [T].

Its place in the series, and the boundary it must respect. This sermon owns what is this book for? Chapter 2 (sermon 3) owns how is wisdom got? and chapters 1–9 (sermon 4) owns who is calling? The temptation here is to answer all three — the preamble touches all of them — and the sermon will collapse into a general introduction if it does. Stay on purpose, addressee, posture, and the warning about riddles. Hand the rest forward by name.

One further boundary: sermon one has already read this text. Treat that as an asset. The congregation has heard 1:1–7 read; now it gets worked. Open by saying so.

Fallen Condition Focus

We want the benefits of wisdom without occupying the position wisdom is given from — and the position is the one we would least choose for ourselves.

Anchored in the text, and the anchor is grammatical. The book’s stated beneficiaries are the פְּתָאיִם (“the naive, the simple”, v.4) and the נַעַר (“the youth”). The wise man of v.5 is not addressed at all — he is described, in the third person, as someone who will profit if he listens [T]. The book is written for the simple and overheard by the wise, and every reader arrives at it standing in the wrong verse.

What the sermon does: it moves the congregation from v.5 to v.4. That is the whole work.

How this relates to the series FCF. The series concern is we cannot tell whether we are wise. This sermon is that concern at the door: before I can ask whether I am wise, I have to accept the seat the book has given me. The book turns it inward later — “all a man’s ways are pure in his own eyes” (16:2), which is sermon 5.

What version 3 had here now goes forward. “We are more persuadable than we think, and we cannot tell a good voice from a bad one” is a true and preachable concern — and it is sermon 4’s, where the two women call the identical sentence to the identical person (9:4, 9:16). Do not spend it here.

Teaching Point

Proverbs is written for the gullible, and what it offers is not a technique but a gift from the LORD — which is why it begins where knowing itself begins, in the fear of him.

Subject: Who is this book for, and what does it offer them? Complement: The easily-led; and a gift, grounded in the fear of the LORD — a gift the New Testament says has a face.

Purpose Statement

To move the congregation from approaching Proverbs as advice for the competent to receiving it as a gift for the simple — so that the self-sufficient hear themselves named as the people this book is not addressed to, the easily-led hear that it is addressed precisely to them, and both are pointed to the one on whom the Spirit of wisdom, understanding, knowledge and the fear of the LORD rests.

Differentiated: for the capable, that they are genuinely included — Waltke: “on the other end of the spectrum of addressees stands the wise” [S] — but as overhearers, by invitation rather than by address, and to add to what they have rather than to arrive; for the anxious and easily-led, that being called פְּתִי (“simple”) is a summons, not a slight — 1:32 shows the alternative; for the preacher, that the sage’s chair in v.5 is not his — “I will read this book as a preacher, which is to say as the man of v.5, and the text has put me at v.4”; for the outsider, that the passage makes a publicly falsifiable claim — a life not founded on the fear of the LORD is not merely less devout but less knowing.

Big Question

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s answer: You would start by admitting you are the one it is addressed to. This book is written for the simple, not the sage — and its motto is not a technique but the fear of the LORD.

Ask it in the same words as last week, early. The congregation is learning that the question is serious and that the answers develop.

Proposed structure — four movements

Tracking the passage’s own syntax: superscription · purpose-chain · interruption · purpose resumed · motto. The chain is five ל + infinitive-construct phrases — לָדַעַת (“to know”), לְהָבִין (“to discern”, v.2), לָקַחַת (“to receive”, v.3), לָתֵת (“to give”, v.4), לְהָבִין again (v.6) — and v.5 is not one of them [T].

⚠ Re-cut, 18 September. Version 3 ran movement 2 across vv.2–5. The syntax will not carry that: v.5 has three finite verbs (יִשְׁמַע, וְיוֹסֶף, יִקְנֶה) and a subject of its own, and the purpose-chain resumes at v.6 as though nothing had happened [T]. Verse 5 now stands with v.6 as the sermon’s turn, which is where the congregation’s actual self-image sits. Waltke’s lexical inclusio across vv.2 and 5 — חָכְמָה to חָכָם, בִּינָה to נָבוֹן — is still worth saying [S]; it describes the parenthesis, it does not dissolve it.

Movement Text Function
1. Whose book is this — and where does it stand? v.1 Solomon — and the canonical reader knows both halves: 1 Kgs 3, the wisdom asked for and granted, and 1 Kgs 11, “his wives turned away his heart.” The book opens a gap on its first line: here is the wisdom of Solomon, and here is the condition Solomon failed. And note where the book sits: after Psalms and Job [T]. A reader arrives at this confident preamble having just watched Job’s search for wisdom fail — which is precisely why 1:2–6’s promises cannot be heard as naive. Do not resolve the Solomon gap yet. Sets up movement 4 and the Christ-landing.
2. Who is it for, and what will it give? vv.2–4 Four infinitives hanging off v.1 — so this is one sentence, not four topics. Two things to land. The centre is justice (v.3b — צֶדֶק, מִשְׁפָּט, מֵישָׁרִים, “righteousness, justice and equity”): the Hebrew has no preposition before the triad, and the grammarians read the three nouns as adverbial accusatives of manner [S: NET] — how the book’s discipline is exercised, not one more item on the list. Waltke gives the reason the specification was needed: “since wisdom and its co-referential terms can be malevolent, not benevolent, v.3b qualifies the instruction as that which serves and heals the community” [S]. And that is exactly what v.4 then demonstrates. The book proposes לָתֵת (“to give”) the naive עָרְמָה (“prudence”) — a word that occurs five times in the whole Hebrew Bible, and two of those five are premeditated murder (Exod 21:14) and the Gibeonites’ deception (Josh 9:4) [T]. The book hands over a dangerous competence, names the people least able to handle it, and then tells you what keeps it pointed the right way. Surfaces the FCF.
3. Where are you standing — and what will the book ask of you? vv.5–6 The turn, and it is grammatical. The chain stops. Three finite verbs, a new subject, and the subject is the man who already knows — described, not addressed [T]. The competent are not the target; they are overhearers who profit. Ask the congregation, plainly, which verse they came in at. Then v.6, and the book announces that it must be interpreted, not merely obeyed: מָשָׁל (“proverb”), מְלִיצָה (“a figure”) and חִידָה (“riddle”) — the three stand together in the Hebrew Bible only here and at Hab 2:6, and מְלִיצָה occurs in only those two verses [T]. Pair it with 26:4–5, two consecutive verses giving opposite advice, which proves the point better than any explanation. This movement is the series’ licence for everything it will later do with genre — and its second half is where the whole series is protected.
4. Where does knowing begin? v.7 Asyndetic — the Hebrew has no connector at all in the whole unit, and none here [T]. The motto is not derived from vv.2–6; it is set down underneath them. Preach both cola: the second says what the first looks like in practice, and it is negative — fools have despised (a perfect: some hearers are already on the wrong side of a completed verb). And close the bracket: חָכְמָה וּמוּסָר, the pair that opened the purpose at v.2, is the pair the fools have thrown away at v.7 — and the two verses are two of only three in the Hebrew Bible that carry it [T]. Preach v.7 as foundation and summit, not an elementary stage. Then close the gap opened in movement 1: Isaiah 11:2–3. Presses the PS.

The Christ-landing, and why it is this one

Isaiah 11:2–3 on the coming king: “the Spirit of wisdom and understanding… the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD. And his delight shall be in the fear of the LORD.”

That single oracle names all four of the preamble’s key terms — חָכְמָה (“wisdom”) and בִּינָה (“understanding”) from v.2, דַּעַת (“knowledge”) and יִרְאַת יְהוָה (“the fear of the LORD”) from v.7 — and rests them on a king. [T] It is built from the passage’s own vocabulary rather than laid over it, which is why it is the landing.

Sourced at the August Logos pass, and it holds. Goldingay on Isa 11: these attributes “have been referred to as belonging to God and/or as mis-claimed by human beings. Now they become real in a human figure… this is the feature that explicitly distinguishes true wisdom from wisdom that leaves God out of account (see Prov. 1:7).” Barry: “Wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and fear of Yahweh were key components of righteous living (see Prov 1:7; 2:5).” [S] ×2. Moderate–high. The 18 September dig did not reach Isaiah 11 independently — it is a secondary-source link, and worth knowing as one.

Two things Goldingay adds. The fear of the LORD is doubled in Isa 11:2–3, and the repetition is emphatic. And Isaiah has been tracking misdirected fear since chapter 7 (7:4; 8:12–13; 10:24), so the king’s delight in the fear of the LORD is Isaiah’s answer to a nation afraid of the wrong things. That is a sermon sentence.

And the New Testament makes the Solomon move itself, which is stronger than making it for them. Matthew 12:42 — πλεῖον Σολομῶνος (“something greater than Solomon”) [T]. v.1 gives a wise king whose wisdom did not hold; v.7 gives the condition he failed; Isaiah 11 promises a king on whom all four terms rest and whose delight is in the very thing Solomon lost; and Matthew says he has come. Solomon is the book’s signature and its cautionary case. He is not its hero.

And the motto’s own destination: the formula runs 1:7 → 9:10 → 15:33 → 31:30, where it stops being a principle and becomes a description of a person [T]. In the New Testament the formula as a formula disappears — replaced by union with the Christ who is wisdom (1 Cor 1:24, 30) [T]. The book’s motto ends in a person twice over.

Keep honest

1 · Preaching 1:7 as an imperative, or as a stand-alone maxim. “So the first thing you must do is fear the LORD” — or a sermon on “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” that never quotes the second half. v.7a is a verbless clause and v.7b is a perfect [T]. There is no imperative anywhere in vv.1–7; the first one arrives at v.8, which is next week’s neighbourhood. The Hebrew makes a statement about where knowledge starts and a report about what fools have already done. Turning the motto into a command hands the hearer a task where the text has given him a location — and it is exactly the self-certification the book spends thirty-one chapters removing. Preach both cola, and preach v.7 last, where the passage puts it.

2 · “The beginning” as the elementary stage. “Start here, then move on to the deeper things.” רֵאשִׁית carries both the temporal sense and the sense of chief part (Num 18:12; Amos 6:6), and the book does not settle it: five occurrences in Proverbs (1:7; 3:9 firstfruits; 4:7; 8:22; 17:14), and the motto is restated at 9:10 with a different noun, תְּחִלַּת [T]. This is the unit’s one genuine crux, and the sermon is stronger for naming it than for picking one sense and sounding certain. “Beginning” may mean where it starts, its first principle, or the best of it. And structurally the book refutes the elementary reading anyway: the phrase returns at the hinge (9:10) and lands in the final line of praise (31:30). Foundation and summit.

3 · Quoting Psalm 111:10 as though it were this verse. It is not. Ps 111:10 reads רֵאשִׁית חָכְמָה יִרְאַת יְהוָה, which matches Prov 4:7 exactly — not 1:7, which has רֵאשִׁית דָּעַת (“the beginning of knowledge”), and not 9:10, which has תְּחִלַּת [T], WLC. A preacher who equates Ps 111:10 with 1:7 or 9:10 is quoting the wrong verse. One clause of care, and the point survives.

4 · Skipping v.5, and so skipping the turn. An outline that runs 1 · 2–4 · 6 · 7, with v.5 folded in as more of the same. v.5 is the only place in the unit where both the grammar and the subject change [T], and the work it does is to put the competent in the position of overhearers. It is where the congregation’s actual self-image sits, and the text has quietly declined to address it. Make it the turn.

5 · Skipping v.6, and so skipping the genre. A series that goes from “this book gives you wisdom” straight to a topical sweep of money, sex and speech, without ever teaching how a מָשָׁל works. A congregation not taught to read a proverb will read every proverb as either a promise or a rule, and both misreadings do pastoral damage — 22:6 preached as a guarantee, 13:24 preached as a method. Do not cut this for time.

6 · Preaching from the sage’s chair. The preacher as the wise man of v.5, dispensing to the simple. The tell is an application entirely about other people’s gullibility. Verse 4 names the target audience, and the preacher is in it.

7 · Making Solomon the hero of the series’ opening. An introduction built on the dream at Gibeon, the two mothers, the Queen of Sheba. v.1 is an attribution, not a commendation, and the canon that supplies the riddles supplies 1 Kgs 11. Name Solomon once, as the text does, and let Matt 12:42’s “greater than” do the work.

8 · Softening v.4 — and over-reading it. Two failures, opposite directions.

Claim Status Use
עָרְמָה shares its root with עָרוּם, the serpent of Gen 3:1 — and Proverbs offers the quality to the naive as a good Sourced, and now counted. Five occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, two of them Exod 21:14 and Josh 9:4 [T]. Barnes: the teacher offers a subtlety that “may turn to evil… and become as the wisdom of the serpent (Gen. 3:1), but which also takes its place… among the highest moral gifts (Matt. 10:16).” [S] Preach it — but say “the same root”. עָרְמָה and עָרוּם are different lemmas [T]
מְזִמָּה is positive throughout the frame and negative throughout the sentence collections, by design Distribution [T]; design claim still unsourced Suggestion only. “It is at least worth noticing…”, never “the author deliberately…”

And the Greek saw the edge too. Swete renders עָרְמָה by πανουργία — a word that is pejorative every time it appears in the New Testament, and the word Paul uses of the serpent: “as the serpent deceived Eve by his craftiness” (2 Cor 11:3) [T]. A second translator, working independently, reached for exactly the ambivalence the Hebrew carries. Convergence, not citation — Paul is not quoting Proverbs. But it is the best single piece of evidence that the edge in v.4 is real and was felt.

Barnes hands the sermon its New Testament authorisation: Matthew 10:16 — “be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” Christ commands the serpent’s quality, paired with a dove’s innocence. One precision from Stabnow worth having: עָרְמָה and its adjective are always positive inside Proverbs (12:16, 23; 13:16; 14:8, 15, 18; 15:5; 22:3; 27:12) and usually negative elsewhere. The book has domesticated the word. Genesis 3 is where it was wild.

Anti-moralism gate

Test Result
“Be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes. There is no character to imitate. The subtler moralism — be teachable, be discerning, fear God, preached as three imperatives the passage never issues — is guarded against by movement 2: v.4’s infinitive is לָתֵת (“to give”).
Is Christ the subject, or an example? Passes. The actor is the God who gives wisdom to those who lack it; the landing is Isa 11:2–3 on the king, not Solomon as a model.
Grounded in Christ’s saving work? Passes with a note. Movement 4 must actually make the move from Solomon’s failure to Christ’s delight in the fear of the LORD. If the sermon ends on “so let us fear the LORD,” it has not cleared this gate — that is the exhortation to acquire by effort what v.4 says is given.

Pulpit-text check

Pulpit text: NIV84. Study text NASB95. On this passage the pulpit text serves the sermon better than the study text does, which is not the usual result and is worth knowing.

What it carries NIV84
The spine — the inclusio (vv.2, 7) Carried, and consistently — “for attaining wisdom and discipline” (v.2) against “fools despise wisdom and discipline” (v.7). The congregation can see the bracket in the pew Bible. (NASB95 keeps it too: “wisdom and instruction” at both.)
The addressee (v.4) Carried, and better worded — NIV84 has “the simple”, NASB95 “the naive”. פְּתָאיִם is the open and unformed, not the stupid, and “simple” is the better pulpit word. If reading NASB95 aloud, say “the simple” when expounding it
The turn (v.5) Carried, and read as an invitation — NIV84: “let the wise listen and add to their learning, and let the discerning get guidance.” NASB95 reads it as a prediction: “A wise man will hear.” The Hebrew forms are ambiguous [T] and the dig routes this to Open Questions — but note that the pulpit text takes Waltke’s side, and that the invitation reading makes movement 3 land more gently
The motto’s contrast (v.7) Made explicit — NIV84 supplies “but fools despise”. The Hebrew has no connector anywhere in vv.1–7 [T], so this is an interpretation, and a fair one. Do not say “the text says ‘but’”. Say the Hebrew simply sets the two side by side and lets you feel the drop
The handover to sermon 3 (v.3 → 2:9) Carried — and the dig’s warning is withdrawn. NIV84 dissolves the three nouns into “doing what is right and just and fair” — but it dissolves them identically at 2:9, “Then you will understand what is right and just and fair”. The repetition is audible in the pulpit text without any help. The dig’s Tool 8 note that “NIV84 will not carry it on its own” was written from the 1:3 rendering alone and is corrected here [T], Logos NIV84 export. (NASB95 and the ESV keep the nouns and also match.)
v.6’s vocabulary Carried — “proverbs and parables, the sayings and riddles of the wise”. “Parables” for מְלִיצָה is looser than NASB95’s “a figure”, and “riddles” — the word the movement turns on — is in both

If this sermon is later preached at home from the ESV: the inclusio holds (“instruction” at v.2 and v.7); v.3’s nouns are kept and match 2:9; v.4 has “the simple”; v.7 has no connector, like the Hebrew.

Hands forward to sermon 3 (chapter 2): the question where does this gift actually come from? — answered at 2:6. And v.3b’s triad — צֶדֶק, מִשְׁפָּט, מֵישָׁרִים — is answered at 2:9, the only other verse in Proverbs where the three stand together [T]. Say so at the end of this sermon: it costs one sentence and it gives the congregation the join a week before they need it.

Hands forward to sermon 4 (chapters 1–9): the voices. The פְּתִי of v.4 is the one both women call to, in the same words, at 9:4 and 9:16 — that is sermon 4’s concern and version 3’s FCF has been moved there. And v.6’s riddle-warning is paid off across the whole series.

Hands forward to sermon 6 (the Words of the Wise): חָכְמָה וּמוּסָר, the prologue’s bracket, has one other home in the Hebrew Bible — 23:23, “Buy truth, and do not sell it; get wisdom and instruction” [T]. The abstraction of the prologue becomes a purchase deep inside the collection. Worth a sentence there, not here.
