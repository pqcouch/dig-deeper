Point & Purpose: Proverbs 1:1–7

Sermon 2 of 9 — Why this book exists · Translation: ESV (Anglicised) · Date: 21 August 2026 Exegesis consumed: dig-deeper-proverbs-1-1to7 (full solo run) · proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis · book-overview-proverbs v1.2 · proverbs-question-record

Phase 0 — scope

A single sermon on a seven-verse unit. No scope question arises: 1:1–7 is a self-contained preamble, closed by the asyndetic motto at v.7 and followed at v.8 by the first vocative of the book.

Its place in the series, and the boundary it must respect. This sermon owns what is this book for? Chapter 2 (sermon 3) owns how is wisdom got? and chapters 1–9 (sermon 4) owns who is calling? The temptation here is to answer all three — the preamble touches all of them — and the sermon will collapse into a general introduction if it does. Stay on purpose, addressee, motto, and the warning about riddles. Hand the rest forward by name.

One further boundary: sermon one has already read this text. Treat that as an asset. The congregation has heard 1:1–7 read; now it gets worked. Open by saying so.

Fallen Condition Focus

We are more persuadable than we think, and we do not know how to tell a good voice from a bad one.

Anchored in the text. The addressee is named pethî (v.4) — not “stupid” but open, persuadable, the person who will believe whoever spoke last. The remedies promised are ʿormâ and mĕzimmâ, the capacities to see through a proposal. And the book demonstrates the problem nine verses later by quoting a gang’s recruitment speech at length, and again at 9:4 / 9:16, where Wisdom and Folly call out the identical sentence to the identical person.

How this relates to the series FCF. The series concern is we cannot tell whether we are wise. This sermon is that concern in its first, outward-facing form: I cannot assess the voices. The book turns it inward later — “all a man’s ways are pure in his own eyes” (16:2), which is sermon 5. Say the outward form now; the inward form is coming.

Teaching Point

Proverbs is written for the gullible, and what it offers is not a technique but a gift from the LORD — which is why it begins where knowing itself begins, in the fear of him.

Subject: Who is this book for, and what does it offer them? Complement: The easily-led; and a gift, grounded in the fear of the LORD — a gift the New Testament says has a face.

Purpose Statement

To move the congregation from approaching Proverbs as advice for the competent to receiving it as a gift for the gullible — so that the self-sufficient hear themselves named as the people this book is not addressed to, the easily-led hear that it is addressed precisely to them, and both are pointed to the one on whom the Spirit of wisdom, understanding, knowledge and the fear of the LORD rests.

Differentiated: for the capable, that v.5 lets them in only as learners, not as the target audience; for the anxious and easily-led, that being called pethî is a summons, not a slight — 1:32 shows the alternative; for the preacher, that the sage’s chair in v.5 is not his; for the outsider, that the passage makes a publicly falsifiable claim — a life not founded on the fear of the LORD is not merely less devout but less knowing.

Big Question

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s answer: You would start by admitting you are the one it is addressed to. This book is written for the simple, not the sage — and its motto is not a technique but the fear of the LORD.

Ask it in the same words as last week, early. The congregation is learning that the question is serious and that the answers develop.

Proposed structure — four movements

Tracking the passage’s own shape: superscription · purpose-chain · interruption · purpose resumed · motto.

Movement Text Function
1. Whose book is this? v.1 Solomon — and the canonical reader knows both halves: 1 Kgs 3, the wisdom asked for and granted, and 1 Kgs 11, “his wives turned away his heart.” The book opens a gap on its first line: here is the wisdom of Solomon, and here is the condition Solomon failed. Do not resolve it yet. Sets up movement 4 and the Christ-landing.
2. Who is it for, and what will it give? vv.2–5 The five purpose-infinitives, all hanging off v.1 — so this is one sentence, not five topics. Two things to land: the centre is justice (v.3b — righteousness, justice and equity, and in the Hebrew the expected preposition is missing there, throwing weight onto it); and the named addressee is the naïve (v.4), to whom the book proposes to give what they do not have. Then v.5 interrupts the chain — the already-wise may come in, but as learners. Surfaces the FCF.
3. What will it ask of you? v.6 The book announces that it must be interpreted, not merely obeyed. Ḥîdôt — “riddles”, the word for Samson’s riddle and the “dark sayings” of Psalm 78:2 — in a purpose statement. Pair it with 26:4–5, two consecutive verses giving opposite advice, which proves the point better than any explanation. This movement is the series’ licence for everything it will later do with genre.
4. Where does knowing begin? v.7 Asyndetic — no connector at all, dropped in like a foundation stone. Preach both cola: the second tells you what the first looks like in practice, and it is negative — not despising instruction. Preach it as foundation and summit, not an elementary stage. Then close the gap opened in movement 1: Isaiah 11:2–3. Presses the PS.

The Christ-landing, and why it is this one

Isaiah 11:2–3 on the coming king: “the Spirit of wisdom and understanding… the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of the LORD. And his delight shall be in the fear of the LORD.”

That single oracle names all four of the preamble’s key terms — ḥokmâ and bînâ from v.2, daʿat and yirʾat YHWH from v.7 — and rests them on a king. [T] It is the strongest Christological link available in this passage, and better than a general appeal to 1 Cor 1:30 because it is built from the passage’s own vocabulary rather than laid over it.

✅ Sourced 21 August — this was version 1’s largest unverified claim, and it holds. Goldingay on Isa 11: these attributes “have been referred to as belonging to God and/or as mis-claimed by human beings. Now they become real in a human figure… this is the feature that explicitly distinguishes true wisdom from wisdom that leaves God out of account (see Prov. 1:7).” Barry: “Wisdom, understanding, knowledge, and fear of Yahweh were key components of righteous living (see Prov 1:7; 2:5).” [S] ×2. Upgraded to moderate–high.

Two things Goldingay adds. The fear of the LORD is doubled in Isa 11:2–3 — once in the list, then again in “his delight shall be in the fear of the LORD” — and the repetition is emphatic. And Isaiah has been tracking misdirected fear since chapter 7 (7:4; 8:12–13; 10:24), so the king’s delight in the fear of the LORD is Isaiah’s answer to a nation afraid of the wrong things. That is a sermon sentence.

The move to make: v.1 gives you a wise king whose wisdom did not hold. v.7 gives you the condition he failed. Isaiah 11 promises a king on whom all four terms rest and whose delight is in the very thing Solomon lost — and Matthew 12:42 says he has come. Solomon is the book’s signature and its cautionary case. He is not its hero.

Keep honest

1 · Preaching 1:7 as a stand-alone maxim. A sermon on “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” that never quotes the second half. The verse is an antithesis, and the second colon supplies the content of the first. It is also the conclusion of a six-verse purpose statement, so the “knowledge” is not knowledge in general but the specific programme of vv.2–6 — including the ability to read a riddle. Preach both cola, and preach v.7 last, where the passage puts it.

2 · “The beginning” as the elementary stage. “Start here, then move on to the deeper things.” Rēʾšît carries both the temporal sense and the sense of chief part (Num 18:12; Amos 6:6), and nothing in the verse suggests a stage to be outgrown. The book refutes it structurally: the same phrase returns at the hinge (9:10) and lands in the final line of praise (31:30). Foundation and summit. If the growth question comes up, note that 9:10 uses a different, unambiguously temporal noun — the book distinguishes the two ideas rather than confusing them. ✅ Confirmed against BHS, 21 August: 1:7 reads רֵאשִׁית, 9:10 reads תְּחִלַּת. The two nouns genuinely differ, so this is a real distinction and not stylistic variation — 1:7 says something 9:10 does not. [T]

3 · Skipping v.6, and so skipping the genre. A series that goes from “this book gives you wisdom” straight to a topical sweep of money, sex and speech, without ever teaching how a proverb works. A congregation not taught to read a māšāl will read every proverb as either a promise or a rule, and both misreadings do pastoral damage — 22:6 preached as a guarantee, 13:24 preached as a method. Movement 3 is where the whole series is protected. Do not cut it for time.

4 · Preaching from the sage’s chair. The preacher as the wise man of v.5, dispensing to the simple. The tell is an application entirely about other people’s gullibility. Verse 4 names the target audience, and the preacher is in it.

Claim Status Use
ʿormâ (v.4) shares its root with ʿārûm, the serpent of Gen 3:1 — and Proverbs offers the quality to the naïve as a good Sourced. Barnes: the teacher offers a subtilty that “may turn to evil… and become as the wisdom of the serpent (Gen. 3:1), but which also takes its place… among the highest moral gifts (Matt. 10:16).” NET: “they need to be wise as serpents.” [S] Preach it.
mĕzimmâ is positive throughout the frame (1:4; 2:11; 3:21; 5:2; 8:12) and negative throughout the sentence collections (12:2; 14:17; 24:8), by design Distribution [T]; design claim still unsourced — no commentator, not even Fox Suggestion only. “It is at least worth noticing…”, never “the author deliberately…”

And Barnes hands the sermon its New Testament authorisation: Matthew 10:16 — “be wise as serpents and innocent as doves.” Christ commands the serpent’s quality, paired with a dove’s innocence. That is a better landing for movement 2 than an observation about roots, and it removes any need to lean on the mĕzimmâ design claim at all.

One precision from Stabnow worth having: ʿormâ and its adjective are always positive inside Proverbs (12:16, 23; 13:16; 14:8, 15, 18; 15:5; 22:3; 27:12) and usually negative elsewhere. The book has domesticated the word. Genesis 3 is where it was wild.

Anti-moralism gate

Test Result
“Be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes. There is no character to imitate. The subtler moralism — be teachable, be discerning, fear God, preached as three imperatives the passage never issues — is guarded against by movement 2: v.4’s infinitive is lātēt, “to give.”
Is Christ the subject, or an example? Passes. The actor is the God who gives wisdom to those who lack it; the landing is Isa 11:2–3 on the king, not Solomon as a model.
Grounded in Christ’s saving work? Passes with a note. Movement 4 must actually make the move from Solomon’s failure to Christ’s delight in the fear of the LORD. If the sermon ends on “so let us fear the LORD,” it has not cleared this gate — that is the exhortation to acquire by effort what v.4 says is given.
