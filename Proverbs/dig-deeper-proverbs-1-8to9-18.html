Dig Deeper: Proverbs 1:8–9:18

Warrant tags: [T] derivable from the text itself · [I] reasonable inference from the text · [S] supplied by a secondary source, held provisionally.

The Passage

Too long to quote in full: 1:8–9:18 runs to 250 verses. The section’s own seams, quoted at the points where the argument turns, appear throughout the report. Read the whole section in one sitting before using this document — the findings below are cumulative and several of them are invisible chapter by chapter.

The four hinge-texts, quoted here because the report returns to them:

1:8 Hear, my son, your father’s instruction, and forsake not your mother’s teaching.

5:7 / 7:24 / 8:32 And now, O sons, listen to me…

7:27 Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the chambers of death.

9:4 / 9:16 “Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!” To him who lacks sense she says…

Headline Findings

  1. Chapter 2 is a programme, and chapters 3–7 execute it in order. [T]/[S] The second lecture announces four concerns — right relation to the LORD (2:5–8), communal relations (2:9–11), the danger of wicked men (2:12–15), and the strange woman (2:16–19) — and the lectures that follow develop them in that sequence: 3:1–12, 3:21–35, 4:10–27, and then 5:1–23 · 6:20–35 · 7:1–27. Surfaced by: Structure, Author’s Purpose, Positional Necessity. The observation is Ansberry’s; it is verifiable from the text and it holds. High confidence.

  2. Wisdom takes over the father’s own vocative formula, word for word. [T] The father twice says “And now, O sons, listen to me” (5:7; 7:24). At 8:32 Wisdom says it — the identical formula. Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Move 4, Structure. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that the lectures and the interludes are one composition rather than two anthologised strands. High confidence on the English identity; moderate–high on the Hebrew (wĕʿattâ bānîm šimʿû-lî) — verify against BHS.

  3. The father falls silent after 7:27, and the section’s last two chapters belong to the women. [T] Nineteen vocatives run from 1:8 to 8:32; not one appears in chapter 9. The father’s last words are the epilogue to the strange-woman narrative; then Wisdom speaks alone (ch. 8), and then the two women speak while the narrator only stages them (ch. 9). Surfaced by: Structure, Tone, Repetition, Narrator’s Comment. The son is left to choose with his father’s voice no longer in his ear. High confidence.

  4. The two women cross over. [T]/[I] The strange woman travels from abstraction to flesh and back to abstraction: four verses of warning (2:16–19), a chapter of consequence (5), an economics (6:20–35), a full narrative with a named hour and a lattice (7), and then her disclosure as Folly, a personification, in 9:13–18. Wisdom travels the other way: a voice in the street (1:20–33), a cosmic self-presentation (8), and finally a house with seven pillars, slaughtered meat, mixed wine, a laid table and servants sent out (9:1–6). Surfaced by: Structure, Genre, Move 4, Tone. They pass each other in chapter 9. Moderate–high.

  5. The lectures happen indoors and the interludes happen outdoors — and chapter 9 fuses the two. [T]/[I] Every lecture is domestic address (“my son”, “your father”, “your mother”); every Wisdom speech is civic — street, market, the head of the noisy streets, the city gates, the heights, the crossroads, the entrance of the portals. In 9:1–6 Wisdom builds a house and sends servants out to the highest places. Surfaced by: Structure, Historical Background, Tone, Move 4. The section’s geography is its argument: what is learned in a house must be able to stand in the street. Moderate–high.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

The book-level purpose is settled in the overview. What this section contributes is the lens — and the section’s own purpose is best stated as a job description rather than a theme.

1:8–9:18 exists to do four things that neither the preamble nor the sentence collections can do.

It supplies motivation. Nothing in 10:1–22:16 argues that wisdom is worth having; the sayings assume it. The frame is where the case is made — wisdom is a tree of life (3:18), more precious than jewels (3:15; 8:11), the thing by which the LORD founded the earth (3:19), the thing kings reign by (8:15).

It supplies the cast. The wise, the fool, the scoffer, the simple, the righteous, the wicked, the sluggard, the adulteress — every character-type the collections will use in single lines is introduced here at length. A reader who meets “the simple” first at 14:15 has met a label; a reader who meets him at 1:4 and watches him take the road to her house at 7:8 has met a person.

It supplies the metaphors. The way, the path, the two houses, the tree of life, the fountain, the lamp, the garland. The collections trade on these without explaining them.

It forces a decision. This is the distinctive one, and it is why the section ends where it does. The frame does not conclude with a summary; it concludes with two identical invitations (9:4, 9:16) and no narratorial verdict between them. [T]

Book-overview note: the overview describes 1–9 as supplying “the lens and the cast.” That is confirmed, and this run adds the fourth item: the frame is also a decision procedure, and its structure is built to end at the moment of choice.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 1:1–7: a superscription, a purpose statement in five infinitives, and a motto. The preamble has named an addressee — “the simple,” “the youth” — and a starting point, and has warned (1:6) that the book will contain riddles. It has given no content whatever.

Necessity answer. Three answers, in ascending order.

The reader has been diagnosed but not treated. 1:4 identifies the pethî as persuadable. A diagnosis without a demonstration is inert, so the very next thing the book does is quote a criminal gang (1:10–14) — the first of the four persuasive speeches this section will reproduce.

The sentence literature is unreadable without it. 10:1 begins 375 unconnected sayings whose vocabulary, character-types and metaphors are all established here. Put chapter 10 immediately after chapter 1 and the reader has a heap of advice; put nine chapters of framing between them and he has a description of one of two houses.

The section must end at the point of decision, and it is built to. This is the strongest positional argument, and it is what settles the boundary at 9:18. The frame’s whole rhetorical architecture converges on chapter 9: three escalating public calls (1:20–21 → 8:1–3 → 9:3), two women who have been travelling in opposite directions (Headline 4), a father whose voice has fallen silent (Headline 3), and finally two invitations in identical words with no narrator to adjudicate between them. [T] The reader crosses into 10:1 having chosen — or having refused to.

Implication. Preaching any part of 1:8–9:18 without the destination in view flattens it. Every lecture is an argument for an allegiance that will be demanded at 9:4. And the section cannot be ended at 9:10: the motto there is real and it is a genuine bracket with 1:7, but Folly’s counter-invitation is the point toward which the whole frame has been moving.

Revisited after Tool 11 Move 2. The book-usage tracking below shows Deuteronomy entering at 2:21–22 and being worked at length at 6:20–23. That sharpens the positional answer: the frame is not only preparing the reader for the collections, it is establishing that this book’s instruction stands in the same relation to the son as Torah stands to Israel.

General contextual notes

Immediately before: 1:7, the motto — attached to what follows by no connector. Immediately after: 10:1, “The proverbs of Solomon,” a superscription and a total change of form.

The section’s internal boundary that matters most: 9:18/10:1. It is the largest formal break in the book, and unusually it falls at a chapter division.

Widely read out of context? Yes, in a specific way: chapters 1–9 are routinely mined for individual texts (3:5–6; 4:23; 8:22–31) with no reference to the argument they serve.

3. Structure

Two structures operate, and both are needed.

(a) Lectures and interludes

The section alternates between parental lectures (each opening with a vocative and an imperative to hear or keep) and interludes (no vocative from the father; Wisdom or the narrator speaks). Ten lectures and five interludes: [T] on the units; [S] on the terminology and the count. The one genuinely awkward unit is 6:1–19. It carries a vocative at 6:1 and 6:3, which argues for lecture; but it is four short vignettes rather than a sustained argument, and it interrupts the strange-woman sequence between chapters 5 and 6:20. [T] The honest position is that it does not fit the pattern cleanly, and that its function is best explained positionally (see the sweep, Unit 7): its two body-part catalogues (6:12–14; 6:16–19) set up 6:25’s anatomy of desire.

(b) The programme of chapter 2

This is the section’s most important structural fact and it is easily missed. Lecture 2 announces four concerns; lectures 3–10 develop them in the same order. [S: Ansberry], verified [T]:

Announced in ch. 2 Developed in
Right relation to the LORD — “then you will understand the fear of the LORD… for the LORD gives wisdom” (2:5–8) 3:1–12 (trust, honour, accept his discipline)
Communal relations — “righteousness and justice and equity, every good path… discretion will watch over you” (2:9–11) 3:21–35 (do not withhold good from your neighbour; do not plan evil against him)
The wicked man — “delivering you from the way of evil, from men of perverted speech… who walk in the ways of darkness” (2:12–15) 4:10–19, 20–27 (do not enter the path of the wicked; the two paths)
The strange woman — “so you will be delivered from the forbidden woman” (2:16–19) 5:1–23; 6:20–35; 7:1–27

High confidence. Chapter 2 is a single Hebrew sentence (see the sweep, Unit 3) and it is also a table of contents. That double function is why it feels both dense and oddly abstract on first reading: it is not yet teaching, it is announcing.

(c) The devices

A converging structure. Not linear argument and not a chiasm, but a funnel: ten lectures on scattered topics, punctuated by interludes of rising scale, converging on a single choice.

Three escalating public calls. 1:20–21 (street, markets, head of the noisy streets, entrance of the city gates) → 8:1–3 (heights, beside the way, crossroads, gates, portals) → 9:3 (servants sent out, highest places in the town). Each is more public, more organised, and more provisioned than the last. [T]

A silence. No vocative in chapter 9 (Headline 3).

4. Linking Words

For a section this long, tracing every connector would be noise. Four patterns carry the argument.

The conditional chain of chapter 2. ʾim… ʾim… ʾim (2:1, 3, 4) answered by ʾāz… ʾāz (2:5, 9), with a kî at 2:6 grounding the whole conditional in a gift: “for the LORD gives wisdom.” [T] Flip the kî into a “therefore” and the sentence reads: the LORD gives wisdom; therefore seek it like silver. This is the section’s theological engine and it is grammatical.

The command-plus-motive pattern of 3:1–12. Six imperatives, six reasons, no bare commands (see the sweep, Unit 4). It establishes the section’s habit: Proverbs argues, it does not merely instruct.

The purpose-infinitives of deliverance. lĕhaṣṣîlĕkā, “to deliver you,” at 2:12 and 2:16 — wisdom is for rescue, and the two rescues named are from the crooked man and the strange woman, which are then the two threads of lectures 6–10. [T]

“And now” (wĕʿattâ) as a closing formula. 5:7; 7:24; 8:32 — each introduces the peroration of a unit, and all three carry the identical vocative (Headline 2). [T] This is the section’s most consistent structural connector, and it is the one that ties the interludes to the lectures.

One conspicuous absence. There is no connector at all between 9:12 and 9:13 — Folly is introduced with a bare nominal clause (“The woman Folly is loud”), exactly as Wisdom was at 9:1. The two panels are set side by side with nothing joining or contrasting them. [T] The reader supplies the “but.”

5. Parallels

The genre-dominant tool for the section, since all 250 verses are poetry. Three observations at section level rather than a catalogue.

The lectures favour synonymous and synthetic parallelism; the collections will favour antithetic. In 1:8–9:18 the second colon typically develops the first (“Hear, my son, your father’s instruction / and forsake not your mother’s teaching”; “bind them around your neck / write them on the tablet of your heart”). From 10:1 the dominant form flips to antithesis. [T] Moderate–high; countable. The formal difference tracks the functional one: the frame persuades, the collections sort.

The section’s few antitheses are load-bearing. 3:33 (the LORD’s curse / he blesses); 4:18–19 (the path of the righteous like the light of dawn / the way of the wicked like deep darkness); 9:8 (reprove a scoffer, he will hate you / reprove a wise man, he will love you). Each marks a place where the frame stops persuading and states a division.

The deliberate break: 9:4 ∥ 9:16. Not parallelism but identity — the same sentence twice, in different mouths. In a section built on paired cola that differ, the one place where two lines are the same is the point the whole thing has been made for. [T] High.

6. Narrator’s Comment

Live at two points, and only two — which is itself a finding, since the section is almost entirely speech.

7:6–23 — the book’s only extended narrative, with two authorial intrusions: “I have seen among the simple… a young man lacking sense” (7:7), a verdict delivered before the action, and “he does not know that it will cost him his life” (7:23). Worked fully in the sweep, Unit 8.

9:1–3, 9:13–15, 9:18 — the staging of the two banquets. The narrator sets each scene, reports each invitation, and then delivers the section’s final line: “But he does not know that the dead are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol” (9:18). [T]

That last is significant beyond its own verse. The section’s closing sentence is a narrator’s comment, and it is the same comment as 7:23 — he does not know. [T] Moderate–high. The frame ends where its one narrative ended: with a man walking in ignorance, and a narrator telling the reader what the man cannot see. That is the last thing said before the collections begin, and it is the reason the collections exist.

Three things follow. Fifteen singular, four plural — and the plural is not casual: three of the four carry the identical closing formula. The formula migrates: what the father says at 5:7 and 7:24, Wisdom says at 8:32. And the address stops: chapter 9 has no vocative at all.

(b) wĕʿattâ bānîm šimʿû-lî — the migrating formula. Headline 2. Two readings are available and both are worth preaching. Either the sage is signalling that Wisdom’s voice and the father’s are the same voice — Ansberry’s “Wisdom speaks through the voice of the father” [S] — or Wisdom is displacing the father, taking over his formula at the moment he is about to fall silent. The second reading has the sequence in its favour: 8:32 is the last vocative in the section, and the father never speaks again. [I] Moderate.

(c) The heart (lēb). The section’s most frequent anthropological term: 2:2, 10; 3:1, 3, 5; 4:4, 23; 5:12; 6:14, 18, 21, 32; 7:3, 7, 10, 25; 8:5; 9:4, 16. Nineteen occurrences, with the byform lēbāb twice more at 4:21 and 6:25 — twenty-one heart-words in all. [T] High; confirmed by concordance, 15 August. Note the distribution: the son’s heart is to receive (2:2), keep (4:21), be guarded (4:23) and not turn aside (7:25); the wicked man’s heart devises (6:14, 18); the strange woman is “wily of heart” (7:10). The same organ is the battlefield for both sides.

Correction, 15 August. An earlier draft gave sixteen occurrences and made two mistakes in opposite directions. It counted 4:21 and 6:25 as lēb when both are lēbāb; and it missed five genuine occurrences — 6:32; 7:7; 8:5; 9:4; 9:16.

The five missed occurrences are a finding, not just a correction. Three of them fall in chapters 8 and 9 — precisely where Tool 7(a) below observes that the vocative “my son” stops. And two of those three are the same phrase: ḥăsar-lēb, “he who lacks heart/sense”, spoken by Wisdom at 9:4 and by Folly at 9:16 in identical words. So the section’s closing chapters do not abandon the heart-language; they transfer it. The father falls silent, and the two women pick up his vocabulary and address his audience with it — using, at the decisive moment, exactly the same words as each other. That sharpens the “father falls silent” reading rather than weakening it, and it strengthens the Wisdom/Folly parallel at chapter 9.

(d) The road-words. Derek, ʾōraḥ, nātîb, maʿgāl — the section’s governing metaphor, densest in 2:8–20 and 4:10–27. The frame is a book about which way to walk; and then, at 9:1, the metaphor changes to a house, and walking stops. [T] Moderate–high. 7:27 is the hinge where both are held together: “Her house is the way to Sheol.”

(e) The women’s vocabulary is shared. Ḥokmâ is called “sister” (7:4) in the same breath as the warning against the ʾiššâ zārâ; both women are loud (7:11 of the strange woman; 9:13 of Folly, the same root hmh); both stand at the high places of the town (8:2; 9:3; 9:14); both address the pethî in identical words (9:4, 16). [T] The vocabulary is deliberately insufficient to tell them apart — which is the section’s whole problem.

Ancient versions check. Two triggers fire.

1:20, “Wisdom cries aloud.” The Hebrew noun is the plural ḥokmôt with a singular verb — an unusual construction, usually explained as a plural of majesty or an abstract plural. [T] on the form; moderate on the explanation. The versions render singular and no modern translation flags it. Not a variant; a category 1 translation-loss item, and worth a sentence in the pulpit only if the sermon is on personification.

8:22, qānâ. LXX ektisen (“created”); Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion ektēsato (“acquired”); Vulgate possedit. [S] High on the renderings. Translation-tradition split: the moderns follow the Hebrew’s ambiguity toward “possessed”; the Old Greek resolved it toward “created,” and that resolution became the Arian party’s principal Old Testament text. Classified category 1-adjacent — an interpretive decision on an ambiguous verb, not a Vorlage variant. Full treatment in the sweep, Unit 9.

9. Tone and Feel

The section’s tonal architecture is a decrescendo of the father and a crescendo of the women, and it is worth mapping because a sermon series should follow it.

Chapters 1–4: urgent, warm, paternal. Vocatives, imperatives, body-language (neck, heart, flesh, bones), the memory of the speaker’s own father (4:3–4). The warmest passage in the book is here.

Chapters 5–7: darkening. The register turns bodily in a different sense — honey and wormwood, fire in the lap, the ox to the slaughter. Chapter 7 is told with something close to novelistic relish, and its pacing (slow through the approach, five verbs in a rush at v.22, then the arrow) is the section’s finest piece of writing.

Chapter 8: expansive, then joyful. Wisdom’s self-presentation moves from a herald’s shout through a merchant’s valuation to something like a hymn, and lands on the only unguarded delight in the book — “rejoicing… rejoicing… delighting” (8:30–31).

Chapter 9: ceremonious, then flat. Wisdom’s banquet is formal and provisioned. Folly’s is squalid — she sits, she offers what is stolen, her guests are already dead. Having shown how attractive folly feels from inside (ch. 7), the frame now shows how it looks from outside. [I] Moderate–high.

Soundtrack: a father’s voice over a long walk, rising to a public fanfare, ending in a room where two doors stand open and nobody is speaking.

10. Repetition

The three cries — 1:20–21; 8:1–3; 9:3. Escalating in location, organisation and provision.

The three “And now, O sons” — 5:7; 7:24; 8:32.

“Do not” — the section’s dominant grammatical mood in the lectures: do not consent, do not walk, do not forget, do not despise, do not lose sight, do not forsake, do not enter, do not swerve, do not desire, do not let your heart turn aside.

The strange woman’s smooth speech — ḥālaq at 2:16; 5:3; 6:24; 7:5, 21. Five occurrences. [T] Moderate–high. The recurring danger in this section is not beauty but rhetoric, which is why the antidote is a rival rhetoric rather than a rule.

Who the ʾiššâ zārâ is — settled 17 August

The question was never asked directly and has now been put to the library. There is a clear consensus, and it is not the obvious one.

Zārâ does not mean “foreigner” here. The root zûr can denote ethnic outsiders (Isa 1:7; Hos 7:9), but of a woman it means one estranged from the covenant community, not from the nation. Snijders and McKane are the standard references; the NET notes put it plainly: “It does not mean that she is a foreigner but that she is estranged from the community with its social and religious values… Here an Israelite woman is in view because her marriage is called a ‘covenant with God.’” [S]

Keil and Delitzsch draw the distinction between the two words that this report had treated as near-synonyms: zār is alienus — “him who does not belong to a certain class… the person or thing not belonging to me” — so ʾiššâ zārâ is the non-married wife; whereas nokrî is peregrinus and “scarcely anywhere divests itself of the essential mark of a strange foreign origin”, so nokrîyâ designates her as non-Israelite. On that analysis the two terms in 2:16 are not a hendiadys but a pair with different force. [S]

Waltke: the “unchaste wife”, “an outsider” who “remains at heart a prostitute.” Kidner, following Toy: “one who is outside the circle of a man’s proper relations.”

Goldingay holds the multivalence open and is worth quoting for the caution: the woman “is such a major concern of chs. 1–9… that there seems likely to be more to it than” straightforward adultery. He suggests marital unfaithfulness may be a parable of unfaithfulness to God, and that “alien woman” may point to those “committed to foreign gods and foreign wisdom” — but concedes that 2:17 “most naturally suggests an Israelite woman who has abandoned the husband to whom she made her vows before the God of Israel.” [S]

What this settles for this report. Section 7(e) above observes that the two women’s vocabulary is “deliberately insufficient to tell them apart.” That stands, and now has a firmer base: the strange woman is defined by covenant abandonment rather than by ethnicity, which is exactly what makes her a structural counterpart to Wisdom rather than merely a moral hazard. Both women are insiders addressing insiders.

And it removes a preaching trap I had not identified. Rendering ʾiššâ zārâ as “foreign woman” invites a sermon about outsiders, marriage across boundaries, or cultural contamination. The text is doing something else: she is one of us who has forgotten the covenant of her God (2:17). The danger is apostasy from within, not infiltration from without. [S] Moderate–high.

(See proverbs-2.md for Atkinson’s further step — that this covenant framing is what links 2:16–19 to the land sanctions of 2:21–22.)

Sheol — 1:12; 2:18 (rĕpāʾîm); 5:5; 7:27; 9:18 (rĕpāʾîm). Five occurrences, opening in the mouth of the gang and closing in the narrator’s verdict on Folly’s guests. [T] The section is bracketed by the grave.

The tree of life — 3:18 only, in this section; it recurs three times in the collections (11:30; 13:12; 15:4). Planted here.

Deuteronomy 6:4–9 → Proverbs 6:20–23 (with 3:3 and 7:3) (high confidence)

Source context. The Shema. Israel is to love the LORD with all the heart; “these words that I command you today shall be on your heart. You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise. You shall bind them as a sign on your hand… and write them on the doorposts.” Its function is to make covenant loyalty total and domestic — the whole of life, transmitted to the next generation.

Book usage. Deuteronomy is the most-used scriptural source in Proverbs, and within this section it is worked three times: the land-tenure formula at 2:21–22, the Deut 8 cluster behind 3:1–12 (remember, length of days, prosperity, fatherly discipline), and then Deut 6 at length in 6:20–23 — its second use of Deut 6 specifically, since 3:3 has already taken the binding-image. [T] The section does not dip into Deuteronomy at random; it works one chapter twice and another at length.

OT-to-OT. Deut 6 and Jer 31:33 are already in canonical conversation — the externalised law promised as internalised. “Write them on the tablet of your heart” (3:3; 7:3) sits inside that conversation, and it is no accident that the phrase appears at exactly the two Deuteronomic high points of the section. [I] Moderate–high.

What it adds, and the crux it raises. Deuteronomy says bind the LORD’s words; Proverbs says bind your father’s commandment and your mother’s teaching, and then calls them miṣwâ and tôrâ — Deuteronomy’s own two nouns (6:23). Three construals: identification (parental instruction in a covenant household simply is the delivery-system of Torah); analogy (the sage claims Torah-like authority by borrowing its clothes); subordination (the parents’ word is authoritative because it transmits the law). The third is confirmed by the book’s own later development at 28:4–9 and 29:18, where absolute tôrâ finally appears. Moderate–high. For a section sermon, this is the passage where the frame’s authority-claim is made explicit.

Genesis 1 → Proverbs 3:19–20 and 8:22–31 (high confidence)

Source context. Genesis 1 narrates ordering by separation and boundary — the expanse dividing waters, the seas gathered so dry land appears, limits set. Its verdict-refrain is delight: “and God saw that it was good.”

Book usage. Within this section the source is used twice, and the second use expands the first. 3:19–20 asserts in two verses that the LORD founded the earth by wisdom, established the heavens by understanding, broke open the deeps by knowledge. 8:22–31 turns those two verses into ten and adds the one thing they lacked: a speaker. [T] High. This is the clearest instance in Proverbs of the frame developing its own material.

OT-to-OT. Genesis 1 and Job 38:4–11 are already in conversation — Job 38 is Genesis 1 turned into an interrogation (“Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?… who shut in the sea with doors… and prescribed limits for it”). Psalm 104 belongs to the same conversation. Proverbs 8 enters a three-way exchange rather than reaching back to Genesis alone. [I] Moderate–high.

What it adds. Job’s question is designed to silence a man; Wisdom’s answer is designed to authorise her. The thing you lacked when God made the world is the thing now standing in the street offering itself to you. That is the section’s largest theological claim and it is why chapter 8 sits where it does — immediately after the seduction narrative and immediately before the choice.

Deuteronomic land tenure → Proverbs 2:21–22 (moderate–high). Worked in the sweep, Unit 3. OT-to-OT: the phrasing is closest to Psalm 37 (vv.9, 11, 22, 29) — a wisdom psalm working the same two-ways problem in the same canonical section. Proverbs joins a conversation Psalm 37 has begun; both feed Matt 5:5.

Genesis 2:9; 3:22 → Proverbs 3:18 (high on the source; moderate on the load). “Tree of life” occurs in the Hebrew Bible only in Genesis and Proverbs. Worked in the sweep, Unit 5.

Job 28:28; Psalm 111:10 → Proverbs 9:10 (moderate–high). The motto’s second statement. Note that 9:10 uses tĕḥillat where 1:7 used rēʾšît — see the 1:1–7 solo report, Tool 7(h).

12. Genre

ANE instruction, in three sub-forms: the parental lecture (exordium, lesson, conclusion — a scheme Ansberry traces from 1:8–19 through the rest [S]), the personification speech (1:20–33; 8; 9:1–6, 13–18), and one example story (7:6–23).

Three reading rules, and the first is the one most often broken.

This is not a collection of proverbs. 1:8–9:18 is sustained rhetoric with a cumulative argument. It should be read and preached as argument — which means a series should not skip lectures, because chapter 2 has programmed what follows.

The personifications are personifications. Woman Wisdom is a rhetorical figure of the sages, elevating an abstraction to a symbolic register [S: Ansberry]. Reading her as a hypostasis or a christophany creates the Arian problem at 8:22 and is not required by anything in the text.

The example story is an example story. 7:6–23 is designed to be watched. Its narrator supplies the verdict (7:7, 23) so that the reader is never in doubt while the young man is.

13. Copycat

Largely N/A — there is one narrative and its protagonist is explicitly a negative example (7:7, “a young man lacking sense”; 7:23, “he does not know”).

The tool is worth running for one section-level observation. The frame offers the reader no positive human exemplar at all. The father is a voice, not a portrait; the son is silent throughout — he never speaks a word in nine chapters [T]; the young man of chapter 7 is a warning. The only figures held up for emulation are personifications. [I] Moderate–high. The book will not supply a human model until 31:10–31 — which is one more reason to read that poem as the frame’s answer.

14. Bible Timeline

Where the section sits. Presented within the Solomonic frame (1:1) and read by a post-exilic canonical audience who know Deuteronomy, the monarchy’s failure, and Solomon’s own end.

What has happened since, that changes the reading. Three items.

Solomon failed. The frame’s warnings about the strange woman (chs. 5–7) are signed by the man of whom 1 Kings 11:1–4 says “his wives turned away his heart.” The canonical reader cannot hear chapter 5 innocently. And Proverbs itself closes by warning a king, “Do not give your strength to women, your ways to those who destroy kings” (31:3).

Wisdom has been personalised. 1 Cor 1:24, 30; Col 2:3; Matt 12:42. The frame’s central figure — a wisdom that calls publicly, gives to rulers, and delights in humanity — has been given a name.

The invitation has been reissued. Wisdom’s banquet (9:1–6) has a canonical successor: “Come, for everything is now ready” (Luke 14:17), in a parable Jesus tells at a meal, and which he shapes using Proverbs 25:6–7 four verses earlier. [I] Moderate.

The correction this section needs. Chapters 1–9 are frequently preached as timeless moral advice for young men. They are that, but they are also the introduction to a book, in a canon, with a destination. Preach the frame as a frame.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. Not a list of behaviours but an allegiance, and the section is built to extract it: get wisdom (4:5, 7), give me your heart (23:26 states later what the frame is doing throughout), and — at the end — go into one house rather than the other.

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.

Worldview. Three claims the section presses. Persuasion is the real battlefield: five occurrences of “smooth” speech, four quoted seductions, and a rival rhetoric as the antidote. Wisdom is prior to the world, not derived from it (3:19–20; 8:22–31) — so living wisely is living with the grain of what is actually there. And the two ways are two houses: the frame ends not with a road but with a threshold.

Behaviour — stop. Stop assuming that a good offer is good because it is attractive. Stop treating the frame’s warnings as period-specific: the recruitment speech of 1:11–14 (“throw in your lot among us; we will all have one purse”) is a description of every syndicate since.

Behaviour — start. Learn the rival rhetoric — which means reading these chapters until Wisdom’s voice is as familiar as the other one. Receive correction (the mûsār thread). Guard the heart (4:23), which the section defines as the source from which conduct flows, not as emotional self-protection.

Motivation. The section supplies its own and it is not fear of consequences: it argues from value (3:13–18; 8:10–11, 18–21), from creation (3:19–20; 8:22–31), and from love (3:12, “the LORD reproves him whom he loves”). And the ground beneath all of it is 2:6 — “for the LORD gives wisdom.” Application from this section must run through receiving before pursuing, or it becomes the self-improvement programme the whole book resists.

Four audiences.

  • For me. Whose voice have I been listening to longest? The section’s assumption is that the last voice wins with the pethî.
  • For a Christian friend. Anyone being talked into something — a scheme, an affair, a resentment. Chapter 7 is the most pastorally useful passage in Proverbs for that conversation, precisely because it does not moralise.
  • For the church. A congregation is a room of people being persuaded all week. Wisdom’s public cry (1:20–21; 8:1–3) is the model for a church that speaks in the street rather than only in the house.
  • For the unbeliever. 9:4 and 9:16 are the same sentence. That is a claim about the world that can be tested against experience, and most people have a story about the time they could not tell the difference.

Prayer in response. Thanks that wisdom is given (2:6) and not merely demanded. Confession of the voices we have found persuasive. Petition for the ear that can hear two identical invitations and know which door to walk through — and thanks for the Wisdom of God who has come out into the street.

Textual Variants

The Hebrew of 1:8–9:18 is generally stable. Three items reach the threshold, and only one is category 2.

7:22 — category 2, substantive. ESV “or as a stag is caught fast” follows the LXX, Vulgate and Syriac; the MT reads, roughly, “as a chain to the discipline of a fool” (so KJV). [S] Moderate–high on the state of the evidence. The exegetical stake is small — the young man is trapped either way — but a preacher quoting the ESV should know it is not the Hebrew. The report takes no position on which is original.

8:22 — not a variant but a rendering. LXX ektisen; Aquila/Symmachus/Theodotion ektēsato; Vulgate possedit. Category 1-adjacent (an interpretive decision on an ambiguous verb) with enormous doctrinal consequence. Full treatment in the sweep, Unit 9.

1:23 — a minor ambiguity. ESV “If you turn at my reproof” with a footnote offering “Will you turn away at my reproof?” The two readings make Wisdom’s line either an offer or an accusation. Moderate. Worth knowing before preaching 1:20–33, since it affects whether v.23 is the last offer before the verdict or part of the indictment.

No category 3 case arises in this section: the New Testament nowhere quotes Proverbs 1–9 directly, though 3:34 (just outside, in the same lecture-block) is quoted from the LXX at Jas 4:6 and 1 Pet 5:5.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting. A town with a gate that functions as court, market and civic square; a household economy; an honour–shame culture; a monarchy in the book’s own frame.

What the original audience knew that we miss.

  1. The instruction genre was international, and its shape was recognisable. Egyptian and Mesopotamian courts produced father-to-son instruction texts for centuries. [S: Ansberry, ch. 2] High on the existence of the tradition. A first hearer would have recognised 1:8’s “Hear, my son” as this kind of book immediately — and would have been listening for what was different.
  2. Two things were different, and both are in this section. Ansberry names them: the mother is invoked alongside the father (1:8; 6:20), which the ANE instructions do not do; and Woman Wisdom is an autonomous figure who extends the material into the communal and cosmic realms. [S] Moderate–high. Neither has a close parallel in the comparative literature.
  3. Education happened in a house. In both Egypt and Mesopotamia instruction took place in the master scribe’s own home, with teacher-as-father and pupil-as-son as the governing idiom, and the biological and pedagogical senses shading into one another. [S: Ansberry] Moderate–high. So “my son” need not settle whether the addressee is a biological son or a pupil — the idiom covers both, which is why the question is genuinely open.
  4. The city gate. Where Wisdom cries (1:21; 8:3) is where legal cases were heard, business transacted and elders seated. Her presence there is a claim about jurisdiction, not just about volume. [S: Ansberry]/[I] Moderate–high.
  5. Standing surety (6:1–5) could cost the guarantor his person and his household — which is why that vignette reads like a fire drill.
  6. Peace-offering meat had to be eaten within two days (Lev 7:16), which is why the woman of 7:14 has fresh meat and a pious pretext for being out.

What this changes. Two things. Recognising the genre lets you see where Proverbs departs from it — and the departures (the mother’s voice; Wisdom as a public figure) are precisely what a sermon on this section should be built on. And recognising the gate turns Wisdom’s cry from a metaphor about being noisy into a claim that wisdom belongs in the courts and the market, not only in the home.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience. Readers of the Writings, post-Torah and post-Prophets, with a post-exilic horizon. They arrive knowing: Genesis 1–3 (the ordering of the world; the ʿārûm serpent; the tree of life and its barring); Deuteronomy, including the Shema and the land sanctions; the whole Solomon narrative, gift and apostasy; Job’s failed search and its one-line answer; and Psalm 1’s two ways. They read Proverbs with Job and Psalms as canonical neighbours.

The first hearers. - Who: covenant Israelites in a household-based, honour–shame society, where instruction was oral and a young man’s ruin was a family’s ruin. - Their situation: a generation being handed responsibility in a society whose institutions could be corrupted at every point the book later names. - What they brought: familiarity with the instruction genre; the assumption that wisdom is a public and political good; and, from the Prophets, the knowledge that Israel’s leaders had failed.

Where the section fits their world. - The genre was familiar; the foundation was not. Verse 1:7 has already grounded an internationally recognisable programme in the fear of YHWH. - “The simple” was a recognised social type, not an insult — one with a known trajectory (1:32). - Wisdom’s public cry would have registered as a jurisdictional claim (see Background).

Surprises, shocks, comforts. - Surprising: that the mother’s word stands beside the father’s, and is called tôrâ (1:8). - Shocking: 1:26, “I also will laugh at your calamity” — Wisdom taking the posture Psalm 2 gives to the LORD enthroned. - Comforting: 2:6, “the LORD gives wisdom” — the search is not a lottery. - Familiar: the accumulation of near-synonyms; the two-ways framework already known from Deuteronomy 30 and Psalm 1.

What we bring that they did not. - Individualism — we hear the lectures as private advice; they heard a household’s survival strategy. - Suspicion of authority — mûsār includes correction and the rod; modern resistance to being corrected is itself what 1:7b names. - A therapeutic reading of “fear.” - Sexual privacy — the strange-woman material was, for them, a matter of public covenantal consequence (5:14, “in the assembled congregation”).

Biblical-Theological Themes

Three themes; the third is included because the section’s structure demands it.

1 — Wisdom

  • Where the section sits: the canon’s fullest presentation of wisdom as a person to be chosen rather than a quality to be acquired.
  • Before: Eden, where wisdom was sought on the wrong terms from the wrong voice (Gen 3:5–6). Deut 4:6, where wisdom is Torah-keeping visible to the nations. Solomon’s request and gift (1 Kgs 3). Job 28’s exhausted search.
  • After: Ecclesiastes presses wisdom’s limits; the New Testament identifies wisdom with a person (1 Cor 1:24, 30; Col 2:3; Matt 12:42).
  • Contribution: the section supplies the canon’s answer to Genesis 3 in kind. Eden’s disaster was a persuasive voice offering wisdom on other terms; Proverbs 1–9’s remedy is a rival persuasive voice offering it on God’s terms. Wisdom does not argue the serpent down; she out-speaks him in the same street. [I] Moderate–high.
  • Confidence: high on the trajectory.

2 — The two ways

  • Where the section sits: the fullest development of the motif in Scripture.
  • Before: Deut 30:15–20 (“I have set before you life and death”); Ps 1.
  • After: Matt 7:13–14 (the narrow and wide gates); and, in this section’s own idiom, two doors rather than two roads.
  • Contribution: the section’s distinctive move is the shift of metaphor at chapter 9. For eight chapters the choice is a road; at the end it is a threshold. Roads can be walked back; a meal accepted cannot. [T]/[I] Moderate–high. That shift is what makes chapter 9 the section’s climax rather than its summary.
  • Confidence: high on the motif; moderate–high on the road-to-house reading.

3 — Word and invitation (the banquet)

  • Where the section sits: at the origin of a canonical thread that runs to the gospels.
  • Before: creation by word (Gen 1); Sinai’s words; the covenant meal on the mountain (Exod 24:9–11).
  • After: Isaiah’s banquet for all peoples (Isa 25:6); Wisdom’s servants and full table reissued in Jesus’ parable — “Come, for everything is now ready” (Luke 14:17), in a chapter that also draws on Prov 25:6–7; the marriage supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9).
  • Contribution: it explains why the section ends at a table rather than with a summary exhortation. [I] Moderate. Flagged as canonical reflection rather than textual claim: Proverbs does not cite Isaiah, and Luke does not cite Proverbs 9. But the shape — a prepared feast, servants sent out, an invitation to the unlikely — is the same shape, and it is the most preachable Christological line in the section.
  • Confidence: moderate.

Confluence

The three relate by (c) climactic convergence, and chapter 9 is where they converge. The wisdom theme supplies who is calling; the two-ways theme supplies what is at stake; the banquet theme supplies the form the choice finally takes. A sermon that keeps them apart will preach chapter 9 as a summary; a sermon that names the convergence will preach it as the destination.

Christological Reading

Type of connection

Trajectory (primary), contrast (secondary), and an explicit refusal of the two typological readings most often attempted here.

Running the four tests on Woman Wisdom as a type of Christ:

  1. Theological-category test — passes. Wisdom is an established canonical category.
  2. NT-precedent test — passes for the category, fails for this figure. The New Testament applies wisdom language to Christ (1 Cor 1:24, 30; Col 2:3) and John 1 and Col 1:15–17 echo the language of Prov 8; but no New Testament text identifies Woman Wisdom as Christ.
  3. Escalation test — passes. “Greater than Solomon.”
  4. Authorial-pattern test — fails. Nothing in the section signals that the personification awaits a personal fulfilment; she is a rhetorical figure with a female persona, set against a female Folly.

Score: two and a half of four → trajectory, not typology. [I] High confidence in that verdict. The early church almost unanimously read Prov 8’s wisdom with John’s logos [S], and the reading is not illegitimate — but it is a canonical reading applied backwards, and pressing it as typology is what hands the Arian his text at 8:22.

How the section points to Christ

1. The wisdom that calls in the street has been given a name. The section’s largest claim is that wisdom was present at creation and now stands in public offering itself (8:22–36). The New Testament’s claim is that this has come to us as a person in whom “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Col 2:3). High on the NT texts; moderate–high as trajectory.

2. The banquet has been reissued. Wisdom builds a house, slaughters her beasts, mixes her wine, sets her table, and sends servants out to call the simple (9:1–6). Jesus tells of a man who prepared a great banquet and sent his servant to say “Come, for everything is now ready” — and, when the invited would not come, sent him to the poor and the crippled and then out to the highways (Luke 14:16–23). Moderate. The shape is the same and the reissue is the sermon: the invitation that went out to the simple now goes out to those who were not even in the street.

3. The Son who kept the whole instruction. Nine chapters of a father saying “my son, keep my words,” addressed to a son who never speaks. The canon supplies a Son who said “I have kept my Father’s commandments” (John 15:10). [I] Moderate. Offered as trajectory, not typology — but it is the natural answer to the section’s most conspicuous silence (Tool 13).

4. Contrast, and it is needed. The section promises that the wise will dwell secure (1:33), that wisdom’s ways are pleasantness and all her paths peace (3:17), that the path of the righteous shines brighter until full day (4:18). The canon’s own wisdom incarnate walked a path that got darker, and the one who was the wisdom of God was despised. [I] Moderate–high. Preached alongside, the contrast keeps the frame’s promises from hardening into a mechanism — which is what Job and Ecclesiastes exist to prevent.

Trajectory

  • Anticipates: a wisdom that can actually be found and heard; a son who keeps the whole instruction; a decisive invitation.
  • Christ fulfils: wisdom given rather than found; the Son who kept it; the banquet thrown open.
  • Not yet: the two invitations still sound the same; the simple are still being taken in.

Moralism check

  • The temptation: nine chapters of imperatives make “try harder to be wise” almost irresistible, and it is the standard sermon on this material.
  • What the text supplies against it: 2:6 — “for the LORD gives wisdom; from his mouth come knowledge and understanding” — placed inside the conditional sentence as its ground, not its reward. The section’s own grammar makes the gift prior to the search.
  • Christ as hero: the hero of these chapters is not the father, not the son, and not the reader. It is the wisdom of God, which calls publicly, delights in humanity, and has since been given a name and a table.

Confidence

High on the trajectory through the wisdom category. Moderate on the banquet line (canonical reflection, not citation). Moderate–high on the contrast. Typology of Woman Wisdom: rejected, with the reception history noted.

Difficult / Contested Verses

1. 8:22 and Arianism. (Doctrinal / apologetic.) The section’s biggest. Address it briefly and directly — a congregation that meets the verse first on the doorstep should have met it in the pulpit. Full material in the sweep, Unit 9.

2. 1:26, “I also will laugh at your calamity.” (Pastoral landmine.) Someone in the room is living with the consequences of a bad decision. Two things hold it: the speaker is personified Wisdom in a prophetic-lawsuit form, not a bare statement of God’s disposition toward suffering; and the speech’s own last word is not the laugh but v.33’s offer of security. Do not preach v.26 without v.33.

3. 1:28 against 8:17. (Apparent contradiction.) “They will seek me diligently but will not find me” (1:28) beside “those who seek me diligently find me” (8:17). Same verb, opposite outcome. The distinction the text draws is between seeking wisdom and seeking escape from consequences — the seeking of 1:28 begins only “when terror strikes.” Preach them together.

4. 3:5–6. (Popular misuse — the most misused verse in the book.) Yāšar means “make level/straight,” not “make successful”; and “in all your ways” is a statement about the whole of life, not a technique for choosing between two jobs.

5. The strange-woman material addresses men only. (Pastoral.) Name the rhetorical situation explicitly — a father to a son — rather than letting the sermon imply that adultery is a male problem or that women are its cause. Note that 6:32–35 puts the responsibility squarely on the man.

6. 5:18–19’s frankness in a mixed congregation. (Pastoral.) The frankness is the point and should not be sanitised; but it will land hard on the single, the widowed and the unhappily married. Say so.

7. 9:7–8, “Do not reprove a scoffer.” (Pastoral.) Read carelessly this licenses giving up on difficult people. Its context (v.9) makes it a statement about method and expectation, not about writing anyone off.

8. 6:16–19, the seven abominations. (Doctrinal.) Worth flagging only because it is often preached as a list of “sins God hates most,” which the numerical form does not support — the x/x+1 pattern is a rhetorical device, and the climax is the last item (sowing discord), not the aggregate.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: preaching the lectures as free-standing moral talks

  • What it looks like: a series in which each chapter is a topic — money week, sex week, friendship week — with no cumulative argument.
  • Why it’s wrong: chapter 2 has programmed chapters 3–7, and the whole section converges on a single decision in chapter 9. Detach the lectures and you have advice; keep them together and you have a case being built.
  • The corrective: preach chapter 2 as the programme it is, and tell the congregation at the outset where the section is going.

Pitfall: stopping before chapter 9

  • What it looks like: a series on “the wisdom chapters” that ends with the glories of chapter 8.
  • Why it’s wrong: chapter 8 is Wisdom’s credentials, not the section’s conclusion. Everything — the three escalating cries, the two women’s converging journeys, the father’s silence — is built to arrive at two identical invitations. Ending at 8:36 or 9:10 leaves the reader admiring wisdom without having chosen a house.
  • The corrective: make 9:1–18 the destination from week one.

Pitfall: allegorising Woman Wisdom

  • What it looks like: “In Proverbs 8 the pre-incarnate Christ describes his eternal generation”; the seven pillars decoded; Wisdom’s banquet preached as the Lord’s Supper.
  • Why it’s wrong: the typology tests fail on the authorial-pattern criterion, the figure has a female persona set against a female Folly, and the flat identification hands the Arian his proof-text at 8:22.
  • The corrective: preach the trajectory — the wisdom by which God made the world delights in humanity and stands in the street calling, and that has come to us in a person (1 Cor 1:30; Col 2:3). The banquet line to Luke 14 is the strongest available and needs no decoding.

Pitfall: the strange-woman chapters preached as a sex talk

  • What it looks like: chapters 5–7 as a purity series, with the woman as the hazard.
  • Why it’s wrong: the recurring marker in this material is ḥālaq, “smooth” — five times, always of speech. The section’s subject is persuasion, of which seduction is the sharpest instance. 6:32–35 puts the responsibility on the man; 7:6–23 makes the young man the negative example and says so twice.
  • The corrective: preach the rhetoric. It also makes the material usable by the whole congregation rather than half of it.

Pitfall: preaching from the father’s chair

  • What it looks like: the preacher as the wise father, the congregation as sons. Signalled by an application entirely about other people’s gullibility.
  • Why it’s wrong: the reader’s assigned role is the son’s, and specifically the silent son’s — he does not speak once in nine chapters. The frame’s addressee is the pethî, and 9:4 and 9:16 are both addressed to him.
  • The corrective: locate yourself in 1:4 out loud, early.

Pitfall: “wisdom” preached as a quality rather than a person to be chosen

  • What it looks like: an abstraction — good judgement, prudence, skill in living — commended in general terms.
  • Why it’s wrong: the section’s whole rhetorical strategy is to make wisdom a voice with a rival. Reduce her to a quality and there is nothing to choose between; the two invitations of chapter 9 become unintelligible.
  • The corrective: keep both women in view every week. The section is a contest, not a curriculum.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. The wĕʿattâ bānîm šimʿû-lî identity (Headline 2).   4. The lecture/interlude division. Ten and five is a standard analysis [S: Ansberry] and the units are text-observable, but the treatment of 6:1–19 is genuinely contested — it carries a vocative yet behaves like an interlude. Named as unresolved rather than decided. Resolved 17 August, and more firmly than expected.

    • Ten lectures and five interludes is not merely “a standard analysis” — it is the standard analysis, widely adopted in modern critical scholarship (Whybray, Fox, Clifford, Crenshaw among others). The lecture list returned matches this report’s table exactly: 1:8–19; 2:1–22; 3:1–12; 3:21–35; 4:1–9; 4:10–19; 4:20–27; 5:1–23; 6:20–35; 7:1–27. The interludes likewise: 1:20–33; 3:13–20; 6:1–19; 8:1–36; 9:1–18. Upgrade from [S: Ansberry] to [S] with a broad base.
    • 6:1–19 is Interlude C on the majority view — classified as an interlude despite its vocative, on exactly the grounds this report gave independently: it is a composite of four short units (surety 1–5; sluggard 6–11; the worthless man 12–15; the seven things 16–19) rather than a sustained didactic speech, and the “my son” is occasional rather than structuring.

The “genuinely awkward” framing can be softened: 6:1–19 is formally awkward but not contested — the awkwardness is a real feature of the unit, and the standard analysis handles it the same way this report did. The positional explanation offered above (the two body-part catalogues setting up 6:25) remains this report’s own contribution and is still [I].

  7. The Luke 14 banquet line. Whether commentators draw Prov 9:1-6 to Luke 14:16-24 explicitly has not been checked. RESOLVED 21 August — three commentators draw it. [S] Blomberg: Lady Wisdom “probably forms background for Jesus’ parable of the great banquet in Luke 14:16-24, ‘emblematic of life, health, and celebration’.” Kittredge and Wilson both draw it to Matthew 22 — the built house, the slaughtered animals, the mixed wine, the servants sent to call from the highest places. Upgraded from moderate to moderate-high.

    One caution from Wilson, and it should govern the sermon. Matthew’s parable “interrupts this tone of warm welcome” at 22:11-14 with eschatological judgement, which Proverbs 9 does not have. Preach the invitation; do not import Matthew’s judgement scene into Proverbs 9. (Proverbs 9 has its own ending at 9:18, and it is quite dark enough.)

  8. 1:23’s ambiguity — offer or accusation. ✅ RESOLVED 21 August — Waltke reads it as an offer, and supplies an inclusio this project did not have.

    Waltke: “Wisdom again addresses the overgrown, unresponsive youths, calling them to turn back (or repent, tāšûbû); there is still hope for them, but not forever (see vv. 29-31 and the inclusio ‘turn away’ in v. 32)… She pleads with the heretofore-unresponsive youths to turn back and listen to her rebuke.” [S] Moderate-high.

    So the ESV’s main text is right and its footnote is the weaker option — and Waltke reads v.23 as stronger than the ESV’s conditional: not “if you turn” but a plea, turn back.

    And the structural datum, which is new:

    1:23 tāšûbû — “turn back” · 1:32 mĕšûbat pĕtāyim — “the turning away of the simple will kill them”

    The same root opens and closes Wisdom’s speech, and the polarity reverses. [T] on the root; [S] on the inclusio. Wisdom’s appeal is to turn back; what kills the simple is their turning away.

    Preaching consequence, and it changes the shape of the sermon. 1:20-33 is not an indictment with a hopeless verdict. It is an offer with a deadline. The judgement of vv.24-31 is what follows a refused plea; it is not the speech’s opening posture.

  9. The ḥokmôt plural at 1:20. The explanation is standard but unverified for this run. RESOLVED 21 August — four sources, and one adds an alternative this report did not have. [S] Schwab: “a feminine plural parsed as a singular… similar to the plural elohim… She, Wisdom, is a larger-than-life single entity, omnipresent and inescapable.” NET: “a plural of intensity, stressing the all-embracing, elevated wisdom.” JFB: “indicative of the great excellency of wisdom.”

    And Kidner supplies a live alternative: the form “is either a Phoenician form of the singular or a Heb. plural. If it is the latter, it is to express intensity and fullness.” So the plural-of-majesty reading is the majority but not the only one. It does not change the exegesis — both explanations yield a single personified figure — but a preacher who says “the Hebrew is plural, which shows her greatness” should know a respectable alternative says it is simply a singular form.
