Point & Purpose: Proverbs 1–9

Sermon 4 of 9 — “Two voices in the street” · Version 2, 18 September 2026, refreshed against the new solo dig · Study text: NASB95 · Pulpit text: NIV84 (first engagement; the home church’s ESV noted where it bears) Exegesis consumed: dig-deeper-proverbs-1-1to9-18 (18 September 2026, full solo run) · book-overview-proverbs v2.0 · point-purpose-proverbs-series-introduction v3 · the 1:1–7 and chapter 2 runs, consumed within the unit run

What changed in this refresh

The sermon’s thread — who is calling? — held, and so did the four movements. One of its load-bearing claims was wrong, and four findings are new.

Version 1, 26 August Version 2
The unit 1:8–9:18, to avoid colliding with sermon 2 Chapters 1–9 whole (1:1–9:18), matching the dig and the series scheme. The collision is handled by discipline, not by amputation: 1:1–7 was preached two weeks ago and is referred to, not re-worked
9:4 ‖ 9:16 — the sermon’s hinge “The identical sentence” Corrected, and it sharpens. The invitation is word-for-word identical — מִי־פֶתִי יָסֻר הֵנָּה, the four words the two women actually say. The narrator’s frame is not: 9:16 adds a waw to חֲסַר־לֵב and another to אָמְרָה [T], WLC. Say “the four words they say are the same”, not “the two verses are identical”
The handover “Nineteen vocatives… the father’s last words at 7:27” Counted and closed. בְּנִי (“my son”) fifteen times, last at 7:1; בָּנִים (“sons”) four times, at 4:1, 5:7, 7:24 and 8:32. The formula וְעַתָּה בָנִים שִׁמְעוּ־לִי (“Now then, sons, listen to me”) stands three times in the whole book — twice the father’s, once Wisdom’s [T]
The escalation Asserted Verified word by word. 1:20–21 and 8:1–3 share seven lemmas — רנן (“cry aloud”), קוֹל (“voice”), נתן (“give”), רֹאשׁ (“top”), שַׁעַר (“gate”), פֶּתַח (“entrance”), קרא (“call”) — across two two-verse spans [T]
New — the hardest verse answered Absent 1:26 has an answer inside the unit. At 1:28 Wisdom says of those who refused her יְשַׁחֲרֻנְנִי וְלֹא יִמְצָאֻנְנִי (“they will seek me diligently but not find me”); at 8:17 she says וּמְשַׁחֲרַי יִמְצָאֻנְנִי (“those who diligently seek me will find me”) — the same two lemmas, negated in the first speech and affirmed in the second [T]. Never preach 1:26 without 8:17 in hand
New — why this unit is preached whole Rationale was pastoral Now countable. The frame and the field speak different dialects: מַעְגָּל (“course, track”) stands in seven verses of chapters 1–9 and not once in chapters 10–31; אֹרַח (“path”) is four times denser here; and צַדִּיק, רָשָׁע and כְּסִיל are nearly absent from the frame [T]. The frame teaches the reader to read the field in words the field does not use
New — the close 9:18 as the ending And it answers the opening. The gang at 1:12 boast נִבְלָעֵם כִּשְׁאוֹל חַיִּים (“let us swallow them alive like Sheol”); the unit ends with Folly’s guests בְּעִמְקֵי שְׁאוֹל (“in the depths of Sheol”, 9:18) [T]. What the gang offered to do, Folly has already done

Phase 0 — the scope decision

A single sermon on 256 verses. Four times the length at which this work would normally split a unit, and it is the right call for one reason: this is the third visit to the frame, not the first. The series has fixed the division of labour and it holds:

Sermon The question it owns Material that is its alone
2 · 1:1–7 What is this book for? The purpose statement, the named addressee, the motto — and the position: the book is written for the simple and overheard by the wise
3 · ch. 2 How is wisdom got? The one-sentence chapter; the כִּי (“for”) of 2:6; the two deliverances
4 · chs 1–9 Who is calling? The two women and their crossing paths · the three escalating public calls (1:20–21 → 8:1–3 → 9:3) · the father falling silent after 7:27 · chapter 9 as the hinge, with no vocative in it

So this sermon does not survey chapters 1–9. It follows one thread through them — the voices — and leaves the lecture content to the two sermons that have already done it. What it is for · how you get it · who is calling. Kept to those three, they do not compete.

On 1:1–7. The unit now runs from 1:1 because that is where the frame begins and what the dig covers. In the pulpit it is a reference, not a text: name the purpose and the addressee in one sentence — “the book told us three weeks ago who it is written for; now we find out who is calling” — and move on.

Consequence for the preparation: you will read all 256 verses and preach perhaps thirty. The four movements below name the verses that carry the thread; everything else is summarised in transit.

Fallen Condition Focus

We assume we would recognise the wrong voice — and the one thing this unit will not grant us is that assumption.

Anchored, and countably so. [T] The four words Wisdom speaks at 9:4 and the four words Folly speaks at 9:16 are identical — מִי־פֶתִי יָסֻר הֵנָּה (“Whoever is naive, let him turn in here”). Two women, two houses at the high point of the same city, two tables, two sets of servants, one sentence. Around it: the smooth speech of the strange woman, four quoted seductions, and a rival rhetoric offered as the only antidote. The unit’s working assumption about the פְּתִי, the simple, is that the last voice wins.

This is the shared concern, not an imported felt-need. The first hearers were a generation being handed responsibility in a society whose institutions could be corrupted at every point the book later names. Ours is a congregation that has been persuaded of several things since Monday.

Where the sermon’s work actually lies: not in warning against folly — everyone already agrees folly is bad — but in showing that folly says the same thing wisdom says, and then giving the congregation the one thing that distinguishes them: who built the house.

Teaching Point

Two voices call the simple from the same street in the same words — and only one of them was there when the world was made, and has since come out into the street in person.

Subject: Who is calling, and how would you know which voice to obey? Complement: Two, indistinguishable by their invitations — and the one that gives life is the Wisdom of God, present at creation and now come to us as a person.

Why this is the dominant idea and not one of five. It is where the convergent findings meet. Wisdom takes the father’s own vocative formula word for word (8:32 ∥ 5:7, 7:24 — three occurrences in the book) [T]; the father falls silent after 7:27 and the last two chapters belong to the two women; the lectures are indoors and the public calls outdoors, and chapter 9 fuses the two — a house and a street. And chapter 8 is the only chapter in the unit where the speaker is more present than the addressee: thirty-nine first-person markers against nine second-person, when every other chapter runs the other way [T]. The unit is built to end with the reader standing between two doors.

Purpose Statement

To move the congregation from trying to assess offers by how they sound, to knowing the voice that is speaking — so that the self-assured stop trusting their own ear, the person currently being talked into something can name which house they are walking towards, and everyone hears that Wisdom’s banquet has been reissued and the servants are already out.

Differentiated:

  • For the capable — the sermon’s sharpest edge. You are good at reading people. 9:4 and 9:16 are the same four words; your ear is not the instrument you think it is.
  • For the person being persuaded — a scheme, an affair, a resentment. Chapter 7 is the most pastorally useful passage in Proverbs for that conversation precisely because it does not moralise. Let it do its work without adding to it.
  • For the church — Wisdom cries in the street (1:20–21; 8:1–3), not only in the house, and by chapter 9 she has stopped shouting and sent servants. A congregation that only ever speaks indoors has half her practice.
  • For the enquirer — 9:4 and 9:16 are a claim about the world that can be tested against experience, and most people have a story about the time they could not tell the difference.
  • For the preacher — “I am being addressed as the son, and the unit will not let me take the father’s chair even while I preach it.”

Candidate Big Question

The series question, asked in these words in every sermon:

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s form, and its answer:

“Two invitations, word for word the same — whose voice have you been listening to longest?”

Answer: You wouldn’t know by auditing yourself. Wisdom is not a quality you check; it is a voice you obey — and the way you tell the voices apart is not by how they sound but by who is speaking.

Flagged as candidate: the final wording belongs to the manuscript stage.

Proposed structure — four movements

Tracking the unit’s own shape, which the dig establishes from the speaker map rather than from the ten-lecture convention: the father speaks, is interrupted twice by Wisdom, hands over his formula and stops; then two women set two tables.

1 · The first cry, and nobody stops · 1:8–33 Function: surface the need. The gang’s recruitment speech (1:11–14 — “throw in your lot with us, we shall all have one purse”) set beside Wisdom’s first public cry (1:20–33). Two invitations, one street, and the unit’s opening verdict on the simple: they are already being recruited, and persuasion is the real battlefield. Read 1:26 if you read the passage — “I will laugh at your calamity” — but do not leave it standing alone; movement 3 answers it from the text.

2 · The father talks — in covenant posture — and then stops · 2:1–7:27 Function: establish that the human voice runs out. Move through the addresses at pace, showing they are one cumulative argument rather than ten topics (chapter 2 announced a programme; chapters 3–7 execute it in order). Two things carry the movement. First, the father gives his own words the posture of covenant law: at 3:3 and 7:3 he tells the son to קשר (“bind”) them and to כתב (“write”) them on the לוּחַ (“tablet”) of his heart — the verbs of Deut 6:8–9 and the noun of Sinai (Exod 31:18), verified by lemma [T]. He is not offering advice. Second, he runs out: fifteen בְּנִי and four בָּנִים run from 1:8 to 8:32, and the father’s last words are at 7:27 [T]. He has said everything he has, and it has not settled the matter. This is where the sermon refuses to end.

3 · The voice that was there first · 8:1–36 Function: establish the Teaching Point. Wisdom cries from the same public places as in chapter 1 — and the two openings share seven lemmas across four verses [T], so this is a deliberate reprise, not a coincidence. Then she does three things the father never could. She answers her own hardest sentence: those who refused her “will seek me and not find me” (1:28) — and now “those who diligently seek me will find me” (8:17), the same two verbs, negated and then affirmed [T]. She takes his formula: “Now then, sons, listen to me” (8:32), the same four Hebrew words he used at 5:7 and 7:24 [T]. And she grounds her claim not in experience but in creation (8:22–31). The answer to “who is calling?” is: someone who was there before the street was.

4 · Two doors on the same street · 9:1–18 Function: press the response. Two houses, two tables, two sets of servants sent out — and the identical four words at 9:4 and 9:16 [T]. Note what is missing: chapter 9 has no vocative. The father is not telling anyone what to do; the choice has been handed over. And the unit closes its own circle: the gang who wanted to “swallow them alive like Sheol” (1:12) are answered by Folly’s guests who are already “in the depths of Sheol” (9:18) — the same word, the frame’s first boast and its last fact [T]. 9:18 is the only true narrator’s comment in 256 verses, and the sermon lands where the unit lands: not at a table, but in the street with two doors open.

Where Christ comes in, and how

Trajectory and contrast. No typology is claimed — and the distinction is load-bearing. Running the four tests on Woman Wisdom as a type of Christ: theological-category passes; NT-precedent passes for the category but fails for this figure (no New Testament text identifies Woman Wisdom as Christ); escalation passes; authorial-pattern fails. Two and a half of four. The early church read Proverbs 8 with John’s λόγος (“word”) almost unanimously, and the reading is not illegitimate — but it is a canonical reading applied backwards, and pressing it as typology is what hands Arius his argument. The Greek shows the cost: Swete has Κύριος ἔκτισέν με (“the Lord created me”) at 8:22 [T], and that rendering carried the fourth-century case.

So preach it as trajectory, in three moves, all in the second half of the sermon:

  1. The wisdom that calls in the street has been given a name. Wisdom was present at creation and now stands in public offering herself (8:22–36). The New Testament’s language for Christ draws on exactly this register — πάντα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο (“all things came into being through Him”, John 1:3) [T] — without exegeting Proverbs 8. Say “the wisdom of God has come out into the street”. Do not say “Proverbs 8 is Jesus.”
  2. The banquet has been reissued. Wisdom builds her house, slaughters her beasts, mixes her wine, sets her table and sends servants to call the simple (9:1–6). Jesus tells of a man who prepared a great banquet, sent his servant to say “Come, for everything is now ready”, and — when the invited would not come — sent him out into the streets to bring in anyone he could find (Luke 14:21–23). The invitation of 9:4 is still open, and it is still addressed to the simple.
  3. And the hole the unit leaves is the most Christ-shaped thing in it. 9:18 ends with a man at a table who does not know where he is, and nothing in chapters 1–9 retrieves him. The frame can tell you which door; it cannot get anyone back out of the wrong house. That is the silence the gospel speaks into, and naming it is better than filling it early.

A resonance worth having and worth labelling as one: the father speaks, falls silent, and the voice that continues uses his words — and a Son later says οὐκ ἐλάλησα, ἀλλ᾽ ὁ πέμψας με πατὴρ (“I did not speak on My own initiative, but the Father who sent Me”, John 12:49). [I] Homiletically strong, exegetically modest. Say which it is.

This is what keeps the sermon off moralism. If the payoff were “listen more carefully”, the sermon would have failed on its own terms, because the two women say the same four words and careful listening cannot separate them. The resolution is not a better ear; it is that one of the two speakers has come out and made herself known.

Keep honest

1 · Making Folly obviously bad. Wisdom dignified and Folly tawdry, with the congregation expected to tell them apart at a glance. Both women have a house, both are at the high point of the city, both set a table, both send or call, and both say the same four words [T]. Read 9:4 and 9:16 one after the other, aloud, and let the room sit with it. Then ask what does distinguish them — and answer from chapter 8, not from tone of voice.

2 · Do not stop before chapter 9 — and do not stop at 9:6. Two versions of the same failure. A sermon that ends at chapter 8 has cut off the ending the unit was built for; a sermon that ends on Wisdom’s invitation at 9:6, because it is the warm place to end, has done it more subtly. The unit ends at 9:18, with the only narrator’s comment in 256 verses [T]. And note: the Greek adds a plus after the Hebrew’s close [T], Swete — preach the Hebrew’s ending; it is the frame’s last word and the Greek blunts it.

3 · Preaching the unit as nine chapters of advice. A survey — “chapter 3 on trust, chapter 4 on the path, chapter 5 on purity…” The unit is one movement with a shape, and the shape is the point. A survey loses the handover, the escalation and the two doors. The series rationale — that people hear this book in fragments — applies to this sermon more than to any other.

4 · Taking the father’s chair. The preacher as the instructing father, the congregation as the son. The unit removes that seat: the father falls silent after 7:27 and the voice that continues is not his [T]. Preach from inside the vocative, not from above it — and say so; it is both true and disarming.

5 · Preaching Proverbs 8 as Christ. “Here the pre-incarnate Son tells us he was with the Father at creation.” It flattens a poem’s personification into a christophany, and ἔκτισέν με is the historical proof of what that costs. The New Testament uses the language; it does not exegete the chapter [S]. And אָמוֹן at 8:30 — “master workman” or “little child” — is a genuine crux the sermon can name rather than resolve.

6 · Preaching 1:26 without 8:17. “I will laugh at your calamity” is the hardest thing in the unit to read aloud, and a sermon that leaves it standing has misrepresented the book. The frame answers it itself: the seeking that fails at 1:28 succeeds at 8:17, in the same two words [T]. Preach the two together, and the register — prophetic oracle, not divine amusement — becomes audible.

7 · Turning chapters 5–7 into a sex talk. They are in this sermon as the second voice acquiring a body — the strange woman moving from abstraction (2:16–19) to flesh (chapter 7) and back to abstraction as Folly (9:13–18). The unit’s own symmetry gives the perverse man equal weight at 2:12–15; preach the pair. The text’s emphasis falls on her speech and her abandonment. Chapter 7 works pastorally because it does not moralise; do not repair that.

8 · Saying the frame is “the theological part” because God is mentioned more. He is not. יְהוָה stands in 7.4% of the frame’s verses and 10.3% of the rest of the book [T]. Whatever makes chapters 1–9 the theological frame, it is not the frequency of the divine name — it is that these chapters supply the vocabulary and the posture with which the rest is to be read. If a reason is wanted, the dialect split is the one that will bear weight: מַעְגָּל stands seven times here and not once in chapters 10–31 [T].

Anti-moralism gate

Test Result
Is the payoff “be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Trust. The payoff is a door, and the one who opens it is the Wisdom of God who has come. Not “listen harder” — the text forecloses that at 9:4 ∥ 9:16.
Is Christ the subject or merely an example? Subject. Movements 3 and 4 make the actor the one who was there at creation and who now sends servants into the street.
Is the good news grounded in Christ’s saving work? Yes — the reissued banquet (Luke 14:21–23), where the invitation goes out to those who were not looking; and the man at 9:18 whom the frame cannot retrieve.

Passes.

Pulpit-text check

Pulpit text: NIV84. Study text NASB95. On this unit the pulpit text carries the sermon’s spine better than the study text, which is worth knowing before the reading is chosen.

What it carries NIV84
The handover (5:7, 7:24, 8:32) Carried — and NIV84 is the better witness. The Hebrew is identical in all three: וְעַתָּה בָנִים שִׁמְעוּ־לִי. NIV84 renders all three the same way — “Now then, my sons, listen to me” — so the congregation can hear Wisdom take the father’s formula without being told. NASB95 varies “then”/“therefore” and adds “O” at 8:32, which slightly obscures it. Say the three references aloud from the pulpit text
The two invitations (9:4, 9:16) Carried. Both versions render the four words identically at both verses, which is what the movement needs. The two waws that distinguish the narrator’s frames are invisible in any English — that is a study-desk fact, not a pulpit one
The reversal (1:28 → 8:17) Carried — “look for me but will not find me” against “those who seek me find me”. The verb pairing survives in English
The Sheol inclusio (1:12 → 9:18) Check the reading. NIV84 has “the grave” at both — with “Hebrew: Sheol” footnoted at both, which is the preacher’s way in; NASB95 has “Sheol”. If the inclusio is preached, “the grave… the grave” still works — say that it is the same word, and the footnote backs you up
1:20, 8:1, 9:1 Neither version marks the חָכְמוֹת/חָכְמָה alternation, and neither can. A translation loss, noted and not pressed — and the form is an open question in the dig anyway
Chapter 8’s crux (8:22, 8:30) Both versions take defensible lines. Do not build on either. The sermon names the crux and moves through it

If this sermon is later preached at home from the ESV: the handover formula is rendered consistently (“And now, O sons, listen to me” at 5:7, 7:24 and 8:32), so the spine holds; 9:4 and 9:16 match; “Sheol” is kept at 1:12 and 9:18, which makes the closing inclusio easier to hear than in NIV84.

Provenance and hand-off

Every element is drawn from dig-deeper-proverbs-1-1to9-18 (18 September 2026): the FCF verbatim from its candidate; the TP from Headline Findings 1, 2 and 3 converging with the person-marker profile; the PS from its So What; the structure from its speaker map; the keep-honest items from its five Preaching Pitfalls, with item 6 from its Tool 11 Move 4 and item 8 from Headline Findings 4 and 5. The Luke 14 banquet move is the preacher’s [I]; the John 1:3 trajectory and the Arian caution are the dig’s.

Received from sermon 2: the position — the book is written for the simple and overheard by the wise — and the voices concern itself, which version 3 of that plan carried as its FCF and has now handed here. The פְּתִי of 1:4 is the one both women call to, in the same words, at 9:4 and 9:16.

Received from sermon 3: the two deliverances of 2:12 and 2:16, which named the agenda; this sermon shows it executed — 3:1–12, 3:21–35, 4:10–27, then 5 · 6:20–35 · 7, in that order. And 2:16’s half-line recurs verbatim at 7:5 [T].

Hands forward to sermon 5 (Solomon I, 10:1–22:16): the dialect changes at 10:1 and the congregation should be told before it happens. The frame’s path-words disappear and the field’s צַדִּיק/רָשָׁע (“righteous”/“wicked”) antithesis takes over [T]. Chapters 1–9 are the lens; chapters 10–22 are what is seen through it — and that is the sentence that makes sermon 5 preachable.

Not decided here: how many of the father’s addresses to name aloud in movement 2 (the risk is a catalogue), and whether 3:13–20 or 4:23 earns a mention — both are strong, and neither is on this sermon’s thread.
