# Point & Purpose: Proverbs 31:10–31

**Sermon 9 of 9** — *"The closing poem"* · **Translation:** ESV (Anglicised)
**Exegesis consumed:** `dig-deeper-proverbs-31-10to31` · **Series backbone:** `point-purpose-proverbs-series-introduction` (v2)
**Date:** 26 August 2026

---

## Phase 0 — the scope decision

**A single sermon on 22 verses** — the shortest unit in the series, and the only one whose length is fixed by its form: **a complete, unbroken alphabetic acrostic, א to ת, one verse per letter, with no line missing.** `[T]` Unlike Psalm 145, which lacks *nun*, or the irregular acrostics of Lamentations.

**The completeness is itself the argument.** A book that opened by offering instruction to the naïve closes with a form that says: *this is the whole of it, A to Z, nothing left out.* **You cannot preach a selection from an acrostic without contradicting its shape.**

> **This sermon carries the weight of the series' ending, not just its own text.** Eight weeks have argued that wisdom cannot be self-certified. This is where that argument is cashed — and the poem does the cashing itself, in its final verse.

---

## Fallen Condition Focus

> **We want to be able to say of ourselves that we have turned out well — and the one verdict that matters is one we are structurally unable to pronounce.**

**Anchored, and the anchor is grammatical.** `[T]` **There is exactly one imperative in twenty-two verses; it is the last word of the book; and it is not "be like her."** Every verb from v.11 to v.30 is indicative or participial — description, not instruction. **Verse 31 alone commands, and it commands the reader to *speak about someone else*:** *tĕnû*, *"**Give** her of the fruit of her hands, and let her works praise her in the gates."*

**So the poem's own grammar forbids the sermon most often preached from it.** The book has spent thirty-one chapters training an eye, and the final test is not whether you can *be* the woman in the poem. It is whether you can **recognise** her — which is precisely what the *pethî* of 1:4 could not do.

---

## Teaching Point

> **The book that set out to make the simple wise ends by asking not "have you become this?" but "can you see it when it is in front of you — and will you say so out loud?"**

**Subject:** How does a book about acquiring wisdom finish?
**Complement:** With one command — to recognise wisdom in another and pay it publicly — because the verdict on a life is never self-issued.

**Why this is the series' ending and not merely this passage's point.** The Big Question has been *"How would you know if you were actually wise?"* for eight weeks. **Here is the answer the book gives: you wouldn't. Somebody else would say it of you.** That is why the poem is in the third person from v.11 to v.30, and why v.31 is the only imperative in it.

---

## Purpose Statement

> **To bring the congregation to the end of a book about becoming wise and hand them, instead of a self-assessment, a command to praise somebody else** — so that the anxious are released from a verdict they were never able to give themselves, the capable are turned outward, and hidden competence in that room is named aloud this week.

**Note what the command actually asks for.** `[T]` v.31 is **remuneration language, not applause**: *give her of the fruit of her hands.* The poem treats silent appreciation as a failure — its one command is to **say** something, and its setting is **the gates**, which is public.

**Differentiated:**

- **For the anxious** — you have spent eight weeks wondering whether you would pass. The book's last word takes the pen out of your hand and gives it to someone else.
- **For the capable** — the education has been training your eye, not your CV. If you cannot see wisdom in the room, the book has not worked on you.
- **For husbands** — vv.28–29 put words in a husband's mouth. **Do not aim this sermon entirely at wives in a room containing husbands.**
- **For the church** — hidden work is real work and the poem insists it be recognised at the gate. Congregations are full of it.

## Candidate Big Question

**The series question, asked in these words in every sermon — and this week it is answered:**

> **"How would you know if you were actually wise?"**

**This week's form:**

> **"Who would have to say it, for it to be true?"**

> *Answer:* **You would never say it of yourself. Somebody else would say it of you — and the book's one closing command is that you be the somebody else for another.**

*Flagged as candidate: final wording belongs to the manuscript stage.*

## Proposed Structure

**Three movements. The poem is a single sentence-form, so the movements are angles on it rather than divisions of it.**

**1 · The question the book has been asking all along · 31:10, with 8:35 and 18:22**
*Function: open the frame.* *"An excellent wife who can find?"* — and **Proverbs says the same thing, in the same words, about finding Wisdom and finding a wife.** 8:35, of Wisdom: *"whoever finds me finds life and obtains favour from the LORD."* 18:22, of a wife: *"He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favour from the LORD."* **The poem is asking chapter 8's question one last time**, in the last place a reader would look for it.

**2 · Twenty verses of unhurried competence · 31:11–29**
*Function: make the congregation actually look — and resist the urge to hurry.* Buying a field, planting a vineyard, the lamp not going out, the arms made strong, the hand to the needy, the scarlet, the sold linen. **This is the movement most sermons skip**, and skipping it turns the sermon into a short talk on v.30 with a long introduction.
**Two details worth naming.** `[T]` At **31:16** she *"considers a field and buys it"* — *zāmĕmâ*, from *zmm*, **the root of the *mĕzimmâ* that 1:4 promised the simple.** The thing offered to the naïve in the book's fourth verse is being *performed* here. And at **31:26** she *"opens her mouth with wisdom"* — *pîhā pātĕḥâ*, **the same verb and noun as the command to the king four verses earlier**, *pĕtaḥ pîkā* (31:8, 9). **Last week the king was told to open his mouth; this week somebody does it.**

**3 · The one command · 31:30–31**
*Function: land the series.* v.30 grounds twenty verses of enterprise in the fear of the LORD — **the phrase the book opened with at 1:7, arriving at its destination.** Then the single imperative: *give her, and let her works praise her in the gates.* **The book ends with a mouth opened in praise of someone else's wisdom.**

---

## Where Christ comes in, and how

**⚠ Typology is not available here, and the negative verdict is firm.** `[I]` *High confidence.* Running the four tests on *the ʾēšet ḥayil as a type of Christ*: **theological category fails** (there is no canonical category "the valiant wife"); **NT precedent fails** (the New Testament never cites or alludes to this passage); **escalation is not applicable**; **authorial pattern fails**. **Score: none of four.**

**"She is a picture of Christ's love for the church" is a not-uncommon move on this text and it has no warrant at all.** Neither has "the scarlet is the blood" or "her two hands are law and gospel". *Say nothing of the kind.*

**What is available is trajectory, and it is enough.**

**1 · The fear of the LORD arriving.** v.30 is 1:7 at the far end of the book, now attached to a life rather than a maxim. The whole education has been to produce someone of whom this could be said.

**2 · The one place Christ belongs, and the reason.** `[S]` The poem's final command is to praise another's competence publicly. **The reason a Christian can do that without envy is that his own standing does not rest on his own competence** — *"Christ Jesus, who became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption"* (1 Cor 1:30; cf. Col 2:3). **Preach it there, at the point of application, as the ground of generous praise** — not as an allegorical key to the poem.

---

## Keep Honest

**1 · Do not preach the specification sermon.** The poem as a standard for Christian womanhood, twenty-two verses turned into headings or a checklist, often on Mothering Sunday. **The poem's own grammar refuses it: one imperative, and it is addressed to the observer.**

**2 · Do not preach the apology either.** The mirror-image failure — handling the passage defensively, explaining it away, treating it as a culturally-bound embarrassment. **The woman in this poem buys land, runs a business and speaks with authority; she does not need rescuing from her own text.**

**3 · Do not race to verse 30.** Twenty verses of inventory treated as scenery. The detail *is* the argument: this is what wisdom looks like on a Tuesday.

**4 · Do not preach it as a law of outcomes.** *Work like this and your household will prosper, your children will bless you, and you will laugh at the future.* Proverbs has spent eight sermons complicating exactly that inference — and 16:2, 20:9 and 30:8–9 are all still true.

**5 · Do not leave the husband's passivity unremarked.** He sits in the gate (v.23) while she does what the poem describes; then he speaks (vv.28–29). **An application aimed entirely at wives, in a room containing husbands, has missed who the imperative is for.**

---

## Anti-Moralism Gate — checked

**This passage carries the series' most notorious moralism trap, and the text supplies its own guard.**

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Is the payoff "be like X" or "trust the Christ whom X points to"? | **Neither — and that is the finding.** The payoff is *recognise and praise*, because the poem's single imperative is *tĕnû*, addressed to the observer, not to the woman. "Be like her" is grammatically excluded. |
| Is Christ the subject or merely an example? | **Subject where he appears** — 1 Cor 1:30, as the ground on which a hearer can praise another's competence without envy. He is not read *into* the poem. |
| Is the good news grounded in Christ's saving work? | **Yes** — and precisely as the release from self-assessment that the whole series has been building towards. |

**Passes.** *Note that the usual gate is inverted here: the danger is not that the sermon fails to reach Christ but that it reaches him by allegory. The four-test result is none of four.*

## Hand-off

The Big Question and the three movements feed the one-sentence-sermon and manuscript stage. **This sermon closes the series**, so the manuscript should carry a deliberate backward glance — the question asked in week one, answered here.

**Not decided here:** whether vv.28–29 are performed aloud (they are the only direct speech in the poem and would preach well as such), and how explicitly to name the 31:8–9 ∥ 31:26 link, which is strong but requires last week's sermon to be fresh in the congregation's mind.
