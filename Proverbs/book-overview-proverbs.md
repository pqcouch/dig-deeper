## Canonical Position

**Section.** Ketuvim, third in the BHS order — Psalms, Job, **Proverbs**, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles. In the English order it stands third among the "poetical books", between Job and Ecclesiastes, with Ruth pulled back to follow Judges. A reader arriving by either route meets a different pair of neighbours, and the difference is not cosmetic.

**Reading implication.** In the Tanak, Proverbs follows the two books that press hardest on whether the world is morally legible. The Psalter ends its laments unanswered; Job ends in a whirlwind that refuses to explain. Proverbs then opens by promising that a young man can be given עָרְמָה ("prudence") and מְזִמָּה ("discretion") — 1:4 `[T]`. The canonical order does not let the confidence come first. Whatever Proverbs means by its promises, it means them *after* Job, not instead of him. `[I]`, high confidence — the order is textual, the inference from it is mine.

**Handoff.** Proverbs hands the Writings a vocabulary — חָכְמָה, מוּסָר, יִרְאַת יְהוָה — and one specific phrase. אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל ("an excellent wife") occurs in the whole WLC only three times: Prov 12:4; 31:10 and Ruth 3:11. It hands Ecclesiastes its problem: a book that has counted the returns on wisdom and reports יִתְרוֹן ("advantage") where Proverbs promised it.

**Neighbours.** Job before, Ruth after. The adjacency with Ruth is doing work and can be checked: Proverbs closes with the אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל nobody has yet found (31:10, מִי יִמְצָא — "who can find?"), and the next book in the order calls Ruth אֵשֶׁת חַיִל to her face (Ruth 3:11) and its male lead גִּבּוֹר חַיִל (Ruth 2:1). Both halves of the pairing are in the corpus; the pair is not a homiletical flourish. `[T]` on the occurrences, `[I]` on the editorial intent, moderate confidence — three occurrences is a small base, and the direction of the arrangement is not recoverable from the text.

Where canonical and compositional order diverge, both stand unresolved: 25:1 states that men of Hezekiah transcribed material attributed to Solomon, so the book itself reports a composition history later than its first attribution `[T]`, while its canonical placement is a separate fact about the collection `[S]`.

**A third divergence, added at v3.0, and it changes the Handoff field depending on the tradition in view.** The Greek does not merely translate this book in a different order; **it puts the Hezekiah collection after the Agur and Lemuel material, so a Greek reader meets 25:1–29:27 immediately before the acrostic** `[T]`, Swete; `[S: BHS]` at 30:1ᵃ (§7). *Both traditions end the book with the acrostic; what differs is what the reader has just been reading.* Reported and unresolved, per the standing rule — and it bears directly on sermons 7, 8 and 9, because the unit each of them hands on to is not the same in the two traditions.

## Presenting Situation

**Author.** The book names its sources and does not claim one author. שְׁלֹמֹה appears exactly three times, and only in superscriptions — 1:1, 10:1, 25:1 `[T]`. דָּוִד and יִשְׂרָאֵל each appear once, both at 1:1 `[T]`. The other headings attribute material to חֲכָמִים ("the wise", 22:17; 24:23), to אָגוּר בִּן־יָקֶה (30:1), and to the mother of לְמוּאֵל (31:1); 31:10–31 carries no attribution at all `[T]`. 25:1 goes further and reports its own transmission: אֲשֶׁר הֶעְתִּיקוּ אַנְשֵׁי חִזְקִיָּה מֶלֶךְ־יְהוּדָה ("which the men of Hezekiah, king of Judah, transcribed") `[T]`.

Who wrote, compiled or edited any of this, and when, the book does not say. Solomonic authorship of the whole, an eighth-century Hezekian edition, a post-exilic final form: all are reconstructions `[S]`, and none of them is what the superscriptions state. What the superscriptions state is attribution and transmission — which is a claim about the material's *provenance*, not a colophon signed by a compiler `[T]`.

**Audience.** A young man, addressed as בְּנִי ("my son"). The vocative occurs 22 times in the book, distributed unevenly: 15 in chs 1–9 (1:8, 10, 15; 2:1; 3:1, 11, 21; 4:10, 20; 5:1, 20; 6:1, 3, 20; 7:1), one in 10:1–22:16 (19:27), five in 22:17–24:22 (23:15, 19, 26; 24:13, 21) and one in 25–29 (27:11); the plural בָּנִים ("sons") adds 4:1; 5:7; 7:24; 8:32 `[T]`, WLC. Lemuel's mother uses the Aramaic בַּר three times in a single verse (31:2) `[T]`.

The addressee is not a child. He is old enough to stand surety for a stranger (6:1), to be recruited by an ambush (1:10–14), to be propositioned in the street at dusk (7:7–20), to dine at a ruler's table (23:1–3) and to stand before kings (22:29) `[T]`. The audience the book constructs is a young man at the point of leaving supervision.

The canonical audience is wider — the collection stands in Israel's scriptures, not in a family archive `[I]`.

**Occasion.** None is stated. The book gives a purpose statement instead (1:2–6) and a motto (1:7) `[T]`. An overview that supplies a crisis here is inventing one.

**Pressure.** The dominant tension is stated by the book in its own words, and it is not "folly versus wisdom" — that is the contrast the book uses, not the pressure it feels. The pressure is the gap between *being* wise and *thinking* you are. The phrase בְּעֵינָיו / בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("in his/your own eyes") governing a self-assessment occurs nine times: 3:7; 12:15; 16:2; 21:2; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11; 30:12 `[T]`, WLC — verified by phrase search with maqqef, paseq and sof pasuq normalised, and separated from the seven occurrences where the eyes are someone else's (1:17; 3:4; 6:13; 17:8; 20:8; 21:10; 24:18). Of the nine, one is second-person and prohibitive — 3:7 אַל־תְּהִי חָכָם בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("do not be wise in your own eyes") — and eight are third-person descriptions of somebody who ignored it. In five of the nine the adjective is חָכָם itself (3:7; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11); the other four vary it (יָשָׁר at 12:15 and 21:2, זַךְ at 16:2, טָהוֹר at 30:12) `[T]`.

**Purpose.** To make a young man wise by making self-certified wisdom impossible: to hand him the vocabulary, the categories and several hundred concrete tests, and to close every route by which he might grade his own paper `[I]`, high confidence — every element of the inference is a counted feature of the text.

## Structural Arc Map

### The seams the book marks for itself

Seven superscriptions divide the book, and they are the only structural signals it gives in its own voice `[T]`:

| Ref | Superscription | Renders (NASB95) |

| 1:1 | מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה בֶן־דָּוִד מֶלֶךְ יִשְׂרָאֵל | "The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel" |
| 10:1 | מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה | "The proverbs of Solomon" |
| 22:17 | דִּבְרֵי חֲכָמִים | "the words of the wise" — embedded in a clause, not free-standing |
| 24:23 | גַּם־אֵלֶּה לַחֲכָמִים | "These also are sayings of the wise" |
| 25:1 | גַּם־אֵלֶּה מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה אֲשֶׁר הֶעְתִּיקוּ אַנְשֵׁי חִזְקִיָּה מֶלֶךְ־יְהוּדָה | "These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah, king of Judah, transcribed" |
| 30:1 | דִּבְרֵי אָגוּר בִּן־יָקֶה הַמַּשָּׂא | "The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, the oracle" |
| 31:1 | דִּבְרֵי לְמוּאֵל מֶלֶךְ מַשָּׂא | "The words of King Lemuel, the oracle" |

Two observations that only the Hebrew yields. First, מַשָּׂא ("oracle") stands in both of the non-Solomonic headings and nowhere else in the book — 30:1; 31:1 `[T]`, WLC — so the two closing collections are linked to each other by a word, not merely by position. Second, 22:17 is not formatted as a heading: the phrase sits inside "incline your ear and hear the words of the wise". BHS proposes on the Greek's evidence that it should be lifted out and written above the section as its title (apparatus at 22:17^{b–b}, "eiciendum et ut titulus 17sqq supra scribendum est, cf 𝔊") `[S: BHS]`. The collection boundary at 22:17 is therefore real but differently marked from the others — a text-critical judgement, not a plain reading.

WLC prints 52 paragraph markers in Proverbs — 50 verse-final פ (petuchah), one mid-verse פ at 10:1 immediately after the superscription, and one ס (setumah) at 24:22 `[T]`, WLC. This is a feature of the manuscript's layout, a reading tradition rather than an authorial signal, and it is reported here as WLC prints it, not as "the Masoretic text" at large.

### Two counted gradients

Two measures, taken independently of each other and of any prior claim, fall out at the same place.

**Divine name density.** יְהוָה occurs in 87 verses `[T]`, WLC, distributed: 19 of 256 in chs 1–9 (7.4%); 55 of 375 in 10:1–22:16 (14.7%); 5 of 70 in 22:17–24:22 (7.1%); none in 24:23–34; 6 of 138 in 25–29 (4.3%); one each in chs 30 and 31. אֱלֹהִים occurs in only five verses (2:5, 17; 3:4; 25:2; 30:9) and אֱלוֹהַּ once (30:5) `[T]`. Within 10:1–22:16 the density is not flat: the name stands in 13 of the 26 verses from 15:25 to 16:11 — the densest concentration in the book — and then in none of the 65 consecutive verses from 25:23 to 28:4, the longest silence `[T]`, WLC.

**Lexical opposition.** Counting bicola in which a fixed pair of opposed lemmas (צַדִּיק/רָשָׁע, חָכָם‑class/כְּסִיל‑class, אהב/שׂנא, חַיִּים/מָוֶת, אֱמֶת/שֶׁקֶר, חָרוּץ/עָצֵל, עָשִׁיר/רָשׁ‑class) is split across the atnach `[T]`, WLC:

| Section | Rate |
|---|---|
| 1:1–9:18 | 5 of 256 (2.0%) |
| **10:1–15:33** | **58 of 184 (31.5%)** |
| **16:1–22:16** | **10 of 191 (5.2%)** |
| 22:17–24:34 | 1 of 82 (1.2%) |
| 25:1–29:27 | 13 of 138 (9.4%) |
| 30:1–31:31 | 0 of 64 |

This is a narrower measure than "antithetic parallelism" as commentators use the term — it counts only lexical opposition across the caesura, and it will miss an antithesis carried by syntax alone. It is reported because it is reproducible: the same script run on the same edition will return the same figures. On this measure the drop at 15:33/16:1 is a factor of six, and ch. 16 scores zero.

### The movements

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–9:18 | **The Long Address** | Sets the terms: the purpose statement (1:2–6), the motto (1:7), a sequence of paternal speeches — conventionally counted as ten lectures with interludes, which is the standard critical analysis `[S]` and not a division the text marks — and two women who both stand in the street and call. Nothing here is a proverb; everything is an argument for wanting proverbs. |
| 10:1–15:33 | **The Two Ways in Two Lines** | The book's contrast engine. Opposition is the default form (31.5%); the righteous and the wicked, the wise and the fool are set against each other line by line until the categories feel self-evident. |
| 16:1–22:16 | **YHWH, the King, and the Limits of Planning** | The form loosens and the subject changes. Opposition collapses to 5.2%; the divine name spikes; מֶלֶךְ ("king") clusters — 12 of the 14 king-verses in 10:1–22:16 fall here, five of them in the block 16:10–15; and the recurring shape becomes *a man plans, YHWH disposes* (16:1, 9, 33; 19:21; 20:24; 21:1, 30–31 — each verse checked to contain יְהוָה). |
| 22:17–24:34 | **The Words of the Wise** | Two short collections in the instruction form of chs 1–9, with the vocative "my son" returning five times. Explicitly presented as received material (24:23 "these also"). |
| 25:1–29:27 | **The Hezekiah Transcription** | Attributed to Solomon but framed as transcribed by someone else, and markedly less theological in vocabulary: 4.3% YHWH density, the 65-verse silence, and no occurrence of מוּסָר or of the noun יִרְאָה at all. Antithesis gives way to simile: כֵּן ("so") completing a comparison stands in 7 of these 138 verses against 18 in the whole book, concentrated at 26:1–27:19, and דַּעַת ("knowledge") occurs once in the whole collection (29:7). |
| 30:1–31:9 | **Two Oracles from Outside** | Both headed מַשָּׂא. Agur begins by denying he has wisdom (30:2–3) and asks a question nobody in the book answers (30:4); Lemuel's material is a mother's instruction to a king. The numerical saying, used once at 6:16–19, becomes the governing form (30:15, 18, 21, 24, 29). |
| 31:10–31 | **The Alphabet Closes** | Twenty-two verses, one per letter, in strict order and without a superscription. The book's last word on wisdom is not a maxim but a household. |

**Binding device.** An anthology of superscribed collections, held inside two frames that the text marks and that can be counted. The **motto frame**: יִרְאַת יְהוָה at 1:7 and the adjectival יִרְאַת־יְהוָה at 31:30, with 9:10 and 15:33 as its internal restatements `[T]`. The **attribution frame**: Solomon at 1:1, 10:1, 25:1 — "king of Israel" at the first, "king of Judah" (Hezekiah) at the last `[T]`. And a **figure frame**: a woman who calls in the street and at the gates (1:20–21; 8:1–3; 9:1–6) answered by a woman whose works praise her in the gates (31:31); שַׁעַר ("gate") stands at 1:21; 8:3; 31:23 and 31:31 `[T]`, WLC.

The movements do not build an argument. They stack: an argument for wisdom (1–9), two large deposits of it (10–22:16; 25–29) with two smaller ones between and after, and a closing portrait. The forward motion is generic, not logical — and a series that preaches Proverbs as though ch. 15 followed from ch. 12 will be preaching a book that is not there.

**Confidence note on the 15:33/16:1 seam.** Within the MT the seam is strongly marked: two independent counted measures turn there, and the paragraph layout gives no competing signal nearby. It is nevertheless an MT-specific seam. In the Greek the sequence at exactly this point is different — BHS records "in 𝔊 v 6 post 15,27, v 7 post 15,28, v 8sq post 15,29" (apparatus at 16:6ᵃ), and Swete confirms it: Swete's 15:27–29 carry the material of MT 16:6, 7, 8, Swete's 16:1 carries MT 16:9, and Swete's 16:2–4 carry MT 15:30, 32, 33 `[T]`, Swete; `[S: BHS]`. Whether that reflects a different Vorlage or the translator's own reordering is not decidable from the corpus. The honest statement: **the MT shows a designed hinge here; the Greek does not preserve the MT's order at the hinge.** Moderate-to-high confidence on the MT claim, and the divergence is stated rather than suppressed.

**Confidence note on the shape of the whole.** The seven-movement arc above is the shape of the *Masoretic* book. The Greek arranges the same material differently and the difference is not trivial: BHS records that in 𝔊 "30,1–14 post 24,22; 30,15–30 (ad 31,1–8) post 24,34" (apparatus at 30:1ᵃ) and that "10–31 in 𝔊 post 29,27" (apparatus at 31:10ᵃ). Swete prints exactly this: MT 30:1–14 as Swete 24:24–37, MT 24:23–34 as Swete 24:38–49, MT 30:15–33 as Swete 24:50–68, MT 31:1–9 as Swete 24:69–77, and MT 31:10–31 as Swete 29:28–49 `[T]`, Swete; `[S: BHS]`. In the Greek there is no closing appendix of foreign sages: Agur's and Lemuel's material is folded into the middle of the book, and the acrostic follows the Hezekiah collection directly. The fear-of-YHWH frame and the acrostic close survive in both arrangements; the "outside voices penultimate" shape does not.

## Microscript

Wisdom in Proverbs is something you *acquire* — and the one thing you cannot do is certify that you have acquired it, because the first instruction the father gives about wisdom's own object is "do not be wise in your own eyes" (3:7), and eight further times the book shows a man who is. So it hands the son the vocabulary, several hundred tests he cannot mark himself, and finally a life rather than a maxim: a woman whose works do the praising for her, because the fear of YHWH is where wisdom starts (1:7) and praise from someone else is where it ends (31:30–31).

**Test against the movements.** 1–9 argues for wanting it; 10–15 supplies the categories by which a man is graded; 16–22:16 removes the grader's own authority by putting YHWH behind the plan; 22:17–24:34 re-opens the address and warns against envy of the successful wicked; 25–29 is the longest stretch in which the reader is left to assess cases with the divine name almost absent — the exam with the invigilator out of the room; 30:1–31:9 has two outsiders confess the limits (30:2–4) and instruct a king; 31:10–31 shows what a wise life looks like from outside. Every movement serves the spine.

**For the series as a whole:** the nine runs describe a book with one problem and one pedagogy. **The problem is that a man cannot certify his own wisdom** — stated in the prologue, illustrated nine times, asked as a question at 20:9 that is never answered, and confessed by the book's last sage in the frame's own vocabulary. **The pedagogy is the withdrawal of help**: the father stops speaking a third of the way in, the collections address nobody, the imperative rate falls to 1.8%, the vocative retires at 27:11, and the closing poem gives its subject no command and its reader one. *Proverbs teaches judgement by progressively refusing to exercise it for you* — which is why the collections look shapeless to a reader expecting a syllabus, and why the shapelessness is the point. Three things follow: **the frame is the lens, not the introduction** (the book returns to its vocabulary in the last twenty-two verses); **the gospel need not be imported** (20:9, 30:4 and 31:28–31 are the book's own openings); and **the poor are how the book keeps the whole thing honest**, the theme turning covenantal exactly where the book stops teaching.

## Intertextual Map

**The headline finding is negative, and dig-deeper needs it.** Proverbs contains no citation formula, no marked quotation, and no appeal to an earlier book by name. תּוֹרָה occurs in twelve verses (1:8; 3:1; 4:2; 6:20, 23; 7:2; 13:14; 28:4, 7, 9; 29:18; 31:26) `[T]`, WLC, and in six of them it is the teaching of a human instructor — תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ ("your mother's teaching", 1:8; 6:20), תּוֹרָתִי ("my teaching", 3:1; 4:2; 7:2), תּוֹרַת חָכָם ("the teaching of the wise", 13:14). The four absolute uses at 28:4, 7, 9 and 29:18 may point to Israel's law, but the book never says so `[I]`, moderate confidence. No named person from Israel's history appears except Solomon, David and Hezekiah in superscriptions `[T]`.

## Christological Trajectory

**Category: trajectory and contrast, not prophecy.** Proverbs contains no messianic prediction, and the attempt to find one usually lands on 8:22–31, where it does the most damage.

**1. The unanswered questions (the strongest doorway, and it is the book's own).** Twice the book asks something it cannot answer.

- 20:9 — מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי טָהַרְתִּי מֵחַטָּאתִי ("Who can say, 'I have cleansed my heart, I am pure from my sin'?"). The question stands unanswered in a book otherwise full of answers `[T]`.
- 30:4 — מִי עָלָה־שָׁמַיִם וַיֵּרַד … מַה־שְּׁמוֹ וּמַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ כִּי תֵדָע ("Who has ascended into heaven and descended? … What is His name or His son's name? Surely you know!") `[T]`.

Agur's confession is the frame for it: כִּי בַעַר אָנֹכִי מֵאִישׁ … וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה ("Surely I am more stupid than any man … Neither have I learned wisdom", 30:2–3) `[T]`. The book that opened promising wisdom ends its penultimate collection with a sage saying he has none and asking who has been up and come back. That gap is where Christ stands, and it does not require a single allegorical move. High confidence — the material is text-internal.

**Added at v3.0 — the shape-argument that makes 30:4 the strongest doorway in the book.** §8 established that the question asks for **one person who both went up and came down**, and that this is the discriminating detail: Swete reads τίς ἀνέβη εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν **καὶ κατέβη**, and **the two nearest rivals do not have it** — Deut 30:12 has an ascent only, and Bar 3:29 brings *wisdom* down `[T]`, Swete (`[S: §8]`). **John 3:13: οὐδεὶς ἀναβέβηκεν εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εἰ μὴ ὁ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβάς, ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** `[T]`, SBLGNT — **and 30:4's last question, מַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ, is answered in the same clause.** *The caution goes with it and should never be dropped:* **the singular "his son" is the Masoretic reading; the Greek has τοῖς τέκνοις αὐτοῦ, and *BHS* records it** (apparatus 30:4ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`. Anyone checking a Septuagint will not find the singular, and saying so makes the point stronger rather than weaker.

**And the close supplies a verdict rather than a type.** §9 established that the acrostic is neither a picture of Christ nor of the church, and that what it stages is **a life assessed, in public, by somebody with the standing to do it** — the shape of Matt 25:21's εὖ, δοῦλε ἀγαθὲ καὶ πιστέ, spoken about work, out loud, by someone else `[T]`, SBLGNT (`[S: §9]`). **The ground is that the praise in 31:31 is earned and the verdict a Christian waits for is not** (Rom 4:5). *The licensed canonical trajectory is the book's own: personified Wisdom (chs 1–9) → a life (31:10–31) → σοφία in person (1 Cor 1:30; Col 2:3) — abstraction, embodiment, person, with the poem as the middle term.* Moderate-to-high as a trajectory; it is a reading and not a citation.

**2. Wisdom personified (8:22–31) — handle with care.** The NT never exegetes Prov 8; it *uses* the vocabulary of personified wisdom for Christ (Col 1:15–17; 1 Cor 1:24, 30; Jn 1:1–3) `[S]`. Two cautions the Hebrew supplies. First, קנה at 8:22 belongs to a book-wide chain in which the verb means *acquire*: 1:5; 4:5, 7 (twice); 8:22; 15:32; 16:16; 17:16; 18:15; 19:8; 20:14; 23:23 `[T]`, WLC — the son is told four times over to קנה wisdom before wisdom says YHWH קָנָנִי. Second, Swete renders it Κύριος ἔκτισέν με ("the Lord created me"), which is not what the Hebrew requires, and that rendering carried the fourth-century Arian argument `[T]`, Swete. **Preach 8:22–31 as the trajectory it is** — wisdom is not an afterthought but present at the world's making, and the one in whom all things hold together is therefore not an intruder in his own creation — **and do not preach it as a description of the pre-incarnate Son.** Moderate confidence on the trajectory; the identification is a category error.

**3. Contrast.** The book's own test is failed by everyone in it. Nine times a man is wise in his own eyes (3:7; 12:15; 16:2; 21:2; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11; 30:12), and 26:12 concludes that there is more hope for a fool than for him `[T]`. The אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל of 31:10 is introduced with מִי יִמְצָא ("who can find?") — the same interrogative shape as 20:6's אִישׁ אֱמוּנִים מִי יִמְצָא ("a trustworthy man, who can find?") `[T]`. The book ends by displaying a life it has just said is hard to find.

**Moralism check.** The trap is obvious and nearly universal: *be the wise son; be the Proverbs 31 woman; work like the ant*. **The sweep refused all three, and the refusals are textual rather than pastoral** `[S: macro-synthesis §9]`: 2:6's כִּי grounds the search in the gift rather than rewarding it; §3 declined the identification of Wisdom with Christ while keeping the trajectory; and §9 answered the Proverbs-31 trap from the poem's own genre, grammar and vocabulary (see trap 2). Every one of those sermons is available from the text and every one of them ignores 20:9 and 30:2–4. The book supplies the standard and withholds the power; it is diagnostic before it is prescriptive. The gospel grounding is not that Christ helps us keep Proverbs but that Christ is the one who can say what 20:9 asks and the one whose name answers 30:4 — and only then does the book's wisdom become liveable rather than accusing.

## Preaching Traps

**1. The promise-mechanism.** *Sounds like:* "God's promise is that if you train them up, they will not depart." *Why it is wrong:* a מָשָׁל is a generalisation about how the world usually runs, not a covenant undertaking; the book itself supplies the counter-cases (13:23 a poor man's field yields much food and is swept away by injustice; 16:33; 21:31 the horse prepared and the victory belonging to YHWH). *Where it bites:* 22:6 above all, then 3:9–10 and 10:22, and anywhere in 10:1–22:16 where prosperity is predicated of the righteous.

**2. Proverbs 31 as a wife-template.** *Sounds like:* a checklist sermon on Mother's Day. *Why it is wrong:* the poem is an alphabetic acrostic, addressed in a book whose named audience is a son, and it caps a sequence that began with 12:4's אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל and ran through the אִשָּׁה זָרָה of chs 2, 5, 6, 7 and the אֵשֶׁת כְּסִילוּת of 9:13. It is the resolution of a contrast, not a job description. *Where it bites:* 31:10–31; secondarily 5:15–19.

   **Three textual defences, added at v3.0 from §9 — the trap now carries its own answer.** **Genre:** an acrostic that runs the alphabet is making a formal claim about *completeness*, not issuing twenty-two requirements. **Grammar:** the poem gives her **no command at all** — every verb about her is indicative or participial — and gives the audience one, תְּנוּ־לָהּ at 31:31, plural `[T]` (`[S: §9]`). **Vocabulary:** the poem is written in **personified Wisdom's** words, on five lemma-verified chains (פְּנִינִים 3:15, 8:11 → 31:10; שַׁעַר 1:21, 8:3 → 31:23, 31; שׂחק 1:26, 8:30–31 → 31:25; אשׁר piel and תמך, both in 3:18 → 31:28, 31:19; מצא 3:13, 8:17, 8:35 → 31:10), **so the subject is wisdom-embodied, not wifely performance** `[T]` (`[S: §9]`). *A useful sentence from the front: "the only person commanded to do anything in this poem is you."*

   **And a fourth thing worth knowing before preaching it.** **Swete ends the book "give her of the fruit of her *lips*, and let *her husband* be praised in the gates"** `[T]`, Swete (`[S: §9]`). **The impulse to redirect the praise is two thousand years old and the Hebrew resists it.** *If a sermon lands on the husband, it has quietly taken the Greek's ending.*

**3. Preaching 8:22–31 as the pre-incarnate Christ.** *Sounds like:* "Here is the eternal Son speaking." *Why it is wrong:* see the trajectory section; the Greek's ἔκτισέν με shows exactly how far the identification can be pushed against orthodoxy. *Where it bites:* ch. 8, and any series sermon on chs 1–9 as a unit.

**4. Preaching the collections as an argument.** *Sounds like:* "Having established X in chapter 12, Solomon now turns to Y." *Why it is wrong:* the Leningrad layout treats 10:1–22:16 as one undifferentiated block with four markers in 375 verses, and the book's own headings present the material as gathered, not composed (25:1). A sermon on a "section" of chs 10–29 must justify its boundaries from something, and usually cannot. *Where it bites:* every sermon covering more than about ten verses of 10:1–22:16 or 25–29 — which, in a nine-part series, is most of them.

**5. Moralism.** *Sounds like:* "Be wise." *Why it is wrong:* 20:9 and 30:2–4. *Where it bites:* the series introduction and any sermon on chs 1–9.

**6. The rod verses.** *Sounds like:* a confident application of 13:24; 22:15; 23:13–14; 29:15 to modern child-rearing. *Why it is wrong, or at least why it is dangerous:* 23:13–14's "you shall strike him with the rod and deliver his soul from Sheol" is proverbial hyperbole in a culture of corporal discipline, and the congregation includes people for whom this was not proverbial. *Where it bites:* 22:15; 23:13–14; 29:15.

**7. Reading the "strange woman" as a person rather than as the book's counter-figure.** *Sounds like:* a sermon on adultery. *Why it is partial:* אִשָּׁה זָרָה / נָכְרִיָּה is the structural opposite of personified חָכְמָה — both call, both are in the street, both set a table (9:1–6 against 9:13–18). Adultery is the vehicle; the alternative to wisdom is the tenor. *Where it bites:* chs 5, 6:20–35, 7.

**8. Preaching 31:10–31 as the book's nice afterthought.** *Sounds like:* "and the book closes with a poem about a good wife." *Why it is wrong:* **seven of the book's chains make their last appearance in these twenty-two verses** — בטח, תּוֹרָה, חֶסֶד, הלל, מַעֲשֶׂה, הֶבֶל and the adjective יָרֵא `[T]` (`[S: §9]`); the motto lands here; 27:2 is performed here; and 31:10's question is the book's own from 20:6. **This is the designed ending.** *Where it bites:* sermon 9, and any series that budgets its shortest slot for it.

**Notes on the units.**

- **Unit 4** as framed covers 249 verses and includes 8:22–31, the single densest passage in the book. It needs its own solo dig regardless of the series' pace; consider whether ch. 8 can be given a separate hearing inside the sermon.
- **Units 5 and 7** are the two large sentence collections. A sermon on 375 verses cannot expound them; what it *can* do is teach the reader how to read a מָשָׁל, and the counted evidence above gives it the material — the opposition rate, the YHWH gradient, the 65-verse silence. That is a sermon about how the book works, preached from the book's own shape rather than from three favourite verses.
- **Units 5 and 7 — added at v3.0.** §4 and §5 together show that 10:1–22:16 is **one unit with two distinct methods**, and that **the join is made by the text itself at 16:25, which reprints 14:12** `[S: §5]`. The first half is the contrast engine (60.3% of verses carrying a character word, 86.9% waw at the caesura); the second relocates the seat of judgement (מִן+יְהוָה in five of its eight book-wide occurrences, the man/YHWH couplet in seven sentences, three chapters at zero on the opposition measure). *That is a fact about the material rather than a criticism of the plan, and a sermon on unit 5 can use it as its own outline.*
- **Unit 7** is the place to say plainly that 25–29 has no occurrence of מוּסָר, none of the noun יִרְאָה, one of דַּעַת, and only six verses naming YHWH in 138. That is not a defect; it is the book handing the reader the cases and standing back. **Two further absences, added at v3.0** `[S: §7]`: **לֵץ ("scoffer") stands in seventeen verses of Proverbs and in none of these 138**, and **the book's last יִרְאַת יְהוָה is 23:17 — thirty-four verses before the collection opens.** *The second is the sharper of the pair, because it dates the withdrawal rather than merely observing it.* And **the collection divides at 27:27 on seven measures**, with 25–27 at 4.8% lexical opposition against 28–29's 18.2% — so a sermon on unit 7 should preach two movements, not one.
- **Unit 8** carries the Christological weight of the series (30:2–4), and 31:1–9 is the natural place for the mother-teacher thread that began at 1:8. **Made specific at v3.0** `[S: §8]`: the weight is carried by **the 9:10 → 30:3 revocation** (דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים in two verses of the Hebrew Bible) and by **a question whose shape John 3:13 matches and whose two nearest rivals do not.** *Carry the caution with it:* **the singular "his son" at 30:4 is the Hebrew's; the Greek has the plural, and *BHS* records it.** And 31:1's אֲשֶׁר־יִסְּרַתּוּ אִמּוֹ is **the only place the book supplies the mother's teaching 1:8 promised**.
