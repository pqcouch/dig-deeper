Proverbs: the Greek Pluses and Minuses — a Book-Wide Survey

Greek: Rahlfs, complete Proverbs, supplied verbatim from Logos, 26 August 2026 · Hebrew reference: BHS verse-counts Date: 26 August 2026 · Mode: Textual survey, whole book Warrant tags: [T] text-derivable · [I] inference · [S] secondary source

Why this document exists

Until today only two of the project’s twelve runs had ever had a Greek text in hand — Solomon I and Solomon II, both surveyed on 26 August. The other ~540 verses had been worked from the Hebrew and English alone. This survey closes that gap by reading the whole book against the Greek at once.

It does two things no single run could. It puts every Greek expansion in the book on one page and shows that they are not evenly spread; and it looks at the Greek where it is shorter than the Hebrew, which no run had done at all.

⚠ And it qualifies a headline finding in three documents. See §5.

1 · The count

[T] Derived by parsing the supplied Rahlfs text and comparing verse-by-verse against BHS chapter-and-verse counts.

Hebrew verses (BHS) 915
Greek verse-units, total 939
— of which plain (answering a Hebrew verse) 891
— of which lettered pluses (no Hebrew counterpart) 48
Hebrew verses with no Greek counterpart 24

Greek Proverbs is a longer book than Hebrew Proverbs by twenty-four verse-units — but the arithmetic hides the real story, which is that neither the additions nor the omissions are spread evenly. Both cluster, and they cluster in different places.

Method note. “Plus” here means a verse-unit that Rahlfs numbers with a letter — 9:18a, 24:22c — which is the standard editorial signal that the Greek has material the Hebrew verse-numbering cannot accommodate. “Minus” means a BHS verse-number with no corresponding Greek verse. Both are formal, countable judgements about the two printed texts; neither is a claim about which reading is older. [T] on the counts; questions of priority are [S] and are not attempted here.

Three things follow, and the third is the one worth preaching-time attention.

(a) The two figures this project had already established are confirmed exactly. Solomon I’s nineteen and Solomon II’s six were counted on 26 August from separately supplied texts; the whole-book parse reproduces both, 19 and 6, with no discrepancy. [T] That is a genuine independent check, since the parsing path was different.

(b) The frame is the most expanded part of the book. Eighteen pluses in 256 verses, a higher rate than any sentence collection. And they are not spread through the frame either — chapter 9 alone carries eight of them in eighteen verses, one per 2.25, which is by a wide margin the densest chapter in Proverbs. See §3.

(c) Proverbs 30–31 has no pluses at all. [T] Not one lettered verse in sixty-four. This matters because 30–31 is precisely the part of the book the Greek most drastically rearranges — the five-block transposition verified on 20 August, where 30:1–14 is lifted between 24:22 and 24:23, and 30:15–33 plus 31:1–9 follow before Solomon II. The section the Greek most reorders is the section it leaves alone. [I] Moderate–high. Rearrangement and expansion are independent phenomena in Greek Proverbs, and a preacher who has heard that “the Greek of Proverbs is expansionist” should know it is not expansionist here.

3 · The three concentrations

Forty-eight pluses, but twenty-one of them sit in three places.

Chapter 9 — eight pluses in eighteen verses

[T] 9:10a · 9:12a, b, c · 9:18a, b, c, d. The chapter that closes the frame is, in Greek, half as long again as in Hebrew.

  • 9:10a “for to know the law is the mark of a good mind” (τὸ γὰρ γνῶναι νόμον διανοίας ἐστὶν ἀγαθῆς) — attached to “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom.” The Greek glosses the fear of the LORD with Torah-knowledge. [T] on the text; [I] on the significance. This is worth noting beside the Solomon II finding that tôrâ is absent from 25–27 and returns at 28:4: the Greek tradition reaches for nomos at precisely the junctures the Hebrew does not.
  • 9:12a–c — three verses on the man who leans on lies, herds winds, chases birds, and wanders through waterless desert. A wisdom-poem with no Hebrew counterpart.
  • 9:18a–d — four verses closing the Folly scene: “leap away, do not linger in the place, do not set your eye on her”, then the water imagery of 5:15–20 reprised — “pass over strange water, keep away from a strange spring, that you may live long.”

The frame does not end where the Hebrew ends it. [I] In Greek, the last thing said before Solomon I begins is a fourfold warning with an explicit promise of long life — which makes the seam at 9:18/10:1 read very differently. A preacher working the frame whole (sermon 3) should know the Greek gives that seam a peroration the Hebrew does not.

Proverbs 24:22a–e — the longest continuous expansion in the book

[T] Five consecutive verses on the king’s word and the king’s wrath: “let no lie be spoken from the tongue to the king” · “the king’s tongue is a sword and not of flesh” · “if his anger is sharpened he consumes men with sinews, and devours the bones of men and burns them like a flame, so that they are not fit to be eaten by the young of eagles.”

And its position is the point. [T] This block sits at exactly the seam where the Greek arrangement breaks — 24:22 is the last verse before 30:1–14 is interposed. The largest expansion in the book stands at the largest structural join in the book. [I] Moderate. Whether the expansion motivated the transposition, marks it, or merely accompanies it, this survey cannot say; but the coincidence is exact and should be recorded.

Chapters 15–16 — the seam the project could not settle

[T] Five pluses in chapter 15 (15:18a, 27a, 28a, 29a, 29b) and four minuses in 16:1–6 (16:1, 3, 4, 6 have no Greek counterpart). This is treated separately at §5, because it bears on a finding already in print.

4 · Where the Greek is shorter

[T] Twenty-four Hebrew verses have no Greek counterpart, and they are not scattered:

Location Verses Note
20:14–22 9 The largest single minus in the book — the Greek runs 20:13 straight to 20:23
16:1, 3, 4, 6 4 Four of the chapter-16 divine-name cluster — see §5
18:23–24 + 19:1–2 4 A single run of four, crossing the chapter division
4:7 · 8:33 · 11:4 · 15:31 · 21:5 · 22:6 · 23:23 7 Isolated

Two observations. [I] Moderate.

First, the minuses concentrate in Solomon I — twenty-one of the twenty-four fall between 11:4 and 23:23, and none at all in 1–3, 5–7, 9–10, 12–14, 17, 24–31. The frame is expanded and never abridged; the sentence collection is both expanded and abridged.

Second, 20:14–22 is a coherent block to lose: the buyer who disparages and then boasts, the lips of knowledge, the garment taken in pledge, bread gained by deceit, plans by counsel, the talebearer, the lamp put out for cursing father and mother, the inheritance gained hastily, and “do not say ‘I will repay evil.’” [T] Three of these reappear as pluses elsewhere in the Greek — the lamp and the cursing of father and mother at 20:9a, the hastily-gained portion at 20:9b, and “do not say I will repay the enemy, but wait for the Lord” at 20:9c. The material is not absent from Greek Proverbs; it is relocated fourteen verses earlier. [I] High confidence on the correspondence, which is verbatim in substance. That is the same redistribution pattern the Solomon I and Solomon II runs found — the Greek tradition moves material as readily as it adds it.

5 · ⚠ The qualification this survey forces

A headline finding in three documents is a fact about the Hebrew text, and has not been saying so.

The claim, in its several forms: ṣaddîq falls to zero and YHWH peaks at chapter 16; “eight of the first nine verses of chapter 16 name him”; the two vocabularies cross there; the collection “opens sorting people into two columns and arrives at talking about God.”

In Greek, four of those nine verses are not present at all. [T] 16:1, 16:3, 16:4 and 16:6 have no Greek counterpart — and in the Hebrew all four name YHWH. So the cluster on which the finding rests is, in the Greek tradition, less than half of what it is in the Masoretic text.

The finding is not wrong, and it is not weakened as an observation about the book we preach from. The ESV, and every English version, follows the Hebrew. But it must be stated as an observation about the Masoretic text, not about Proverbs simpliciter — because a hearer who checks a Septuagint will not find it.

And the survey adds something the finding did not have. [T] Chapters 15 and 16 are, between them, the point of sharpest divergence between the two texts anywhere in Solomon I: five pluses in chapter 15 and four minuses in 16:1–6, nine differences across thirty-nine verses. Nowhere else in the 375 verses of Solomon I do the two texts diverge at that rate.

This is independent evidence bearing on the one seam this project could not settle. [I] Moderate. The 15:33/16:1 boundary was left open — a certain formal shift, a contested boundary, with the collections assessment recording that Heim’s own analysis yields a unit straddling it. Now a third witness speaks: the Greek and Hebrew traditions disagree with each other more at this seam than anywhere else in the collection. Textual instability at a proposed boundary is not proof of a boundary — later hands may simply have worked hardest where the material was already loose — but it is a fact about the transmission that any account of the seam now has to accommodate. Recorded as evidence, not as a verdict.

7 · Two that a preacher will want

22:8a — behind “God loves a cheerful giver.” [T] on the Greek; [S] on the New Testament link. The plus reads ἄνδρα ἱλαρὸν καὶ δότην εὐλογεῖ ὁ θεός — “God blesses a cheerful man and a giver.” Paul at 2 Corinthians 9:7 has ἱλαρὸν γὰρ δότην ἀγαπᾷ ὁ θεός.

Three of the four significant words are shared — ἱλαρόν, δότην, ὁ θεός — and the verb is not. [T] So this is a close adaptation rather than a verbatim quotation, and it should be described that way: Paul is not citing the Hebrew Proverbs, which does not contain the saying at all, and he is not reproducing the Greek word for word either. Moderate–high that the LXX plus stands behind him; the combination ἱλαρός + δότης + θεός is distinctive enough to make coincidence unlikely.

The pulpit point survives the qualification and is worth thirty seconds. A congregation that knows 2 Corinthians 9:7 by heart is echoing a sentence of Proverbs that is not in their Bible — because Paul read the Old Testament in Greek, and the Greek Proverbs had a verse the Hebrew did not.

26:11a — the Sirach overlap. [T] on both Greek texts; [S] on the relationship. Recorded in the Solomon II run and repeated here for completeness: a verse standing in Greek Proverbs and in Ben Sira and in the Hebrew of neither. Direction of dependence unresolved — see Open Questions.

8 · What this changes, and what it does not

Changes:

  • The chapter-16 divine-name finding is now labelled as Masoretic. §5, propagated to dig-deeper-proverbs-10-1to22-16, dig-deeper-proverbs-10-1to15-33 and proverbs-dig-deeper-macro-synthesis.
  • The 15:33/16:1 seam has a third witness, and it is a witness to instability. §5.
  • The claim that Greek Proverbs is expansionist now has a shape: expansionist in the frame, moderately so in the sentence collections, not at all in 30–31.

Does not change:

  • Every count in every existing report. Solomon I’s nineteen and Solomon II’s six are confirmed exactly.
  • The LXX arrangement, verified 20 August and re-confirmed here by the source’s own block headings (CHAPTER 24 (VS 1-22E), CHAPTER 30 (VS 1-14), and so on).
  • Any Hebrew-derived structural finding — paragraph markers, Leitwort threads, the nested silences of Solomon II. None of these depends on the Greek.

Open Questions

  1. Which way does the 26:11a / Sirach 4:21 dependence run? Carried from the Solomon II run. Moderate priority.
  2. Is the 20:14–22 minus / 20:9a–c plus relationship a relocation or two independent traditions? The correspondence is close in substance; a commentary on Greek Proverbs would settle it. Moderate.
  3. Does any source connect the 24:22a–e expansion with the transposition at that same seam? Moderate — it would turn an [I] into something firmer.
  4. Is the chapters 15–16 divergence rate noticed in the literature on Greek Proverbs? Worth one query before it is used in a sermon.
  5. Why does the Greek not expand 30–31 at all? Low priority; possibly a question about the translator’s units of work.

Text-First Declaration

Every figure in this document was derived by parsing the supplied Rahlfs text programmatically and comparing it against BHS chapter-and-verse counts, before any secondary source was consulted. The parse was corrected once during the run: an initial pass silently dropped six of the thirty-four chapter-blocks, because the blocks belonging to the transposed section carry range annotations in their headings (CHAPTER 24 (VS 1-22E)) which the first pattern did not match. That error would have lost the five pluses at 24:22a–e and reported them at 23:22. It was caught by comparing the block list against the file’s own chapter headings, and the figures above are from the corrected parse.

Warrant summary: all counts, the plus inventory, the minus list and the by-collection rates are [T]. The reading of chapter 9 as a Greek peroration, the 20:14–22 relocation, the significance of the 24:22 position, and the seam argument at §5 are [I]. The 2 Corinthians 9:7 and Sirach links, and all questions of textual priority, are [S] or left open.
