Dig Deeper: Proverbs 10:1–15:33

Translation: ESV · Hebrew: BHS (Elliger–Rudolph–Weil), supplied verified Pulpit translation: ESV (Anglicised) — same text as the report default Date: 20 August 2026 Book-overview context: book-overview-proverbs (v1.1, upgraded 17 August) — in project Series context: Solomon 1A. The third of five section runs completing Proverbs, and the largest — 184 verses, plus 16:1–15 read as a shared hinge at the brief’s direction.

Warrant tags: [T] derivable from the text itself · [I] reasonable inference from the text · [S] supplied by a secondary source.

A note on method, stated at the top because it bears on how the findings should be weighed. This is a text-first derivation: the Hebrew was parsed and the structure worked out from the words before any secondary source was consulted. It is not a blind test. Heim’s cluster map for these chapters is in the project and its section boundaries were already known to me. I did not consult it while deriving — but I could have, so the agreements reported at the checking stage are corroborations, not controls. Where a finding is genuinely independent of a source, that is stated at the finding. This is the ordinary situation of any expositor who comes to a text carrying previous study; the tagging is what keeps it honest.

Headline Findings

  1. Two vocabularies change places across the collection: the righteous/wicked pair falls away almost exactly as the divine name takes over — and the antithetical form loosens with them. [T] Counted by verses: ṣaddîq runs 13 · 8 · 8 · 5 · 2 · 3 across chapters 10–15 and reaches zero in 16:1–15; YHWH runs 4 · 2 · 2 · 0 · 3 · 9 and reaches 9 in the fifteen verses of the hinge. A third gradient runs with them: antithetical parallelism peaks at 92% in chapter 13 — the chapter with no divine name — and falls to 66.7% in chapter 15, its lowest, where God is named nine times. Surfaced by: Repetition, Parallels, Structure, Author’s Purpose, Positional Necessity. High confidence — all three countable. The collection opens by sorting the world into two kinds of people and ends by talking about God, and the crest is 15:33, “the fear of the LORD is instruction in wisdom.”

  2. Chapter 13 contains no divine name in twenty-five verses — the longest God-free stretch in the sentence literature. [T] High. It is the trough of the gradient above, and it sits immediately before the collection’s only internal petuchah (13:25). Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure. A preacher working chapter 13 should know he is in the one place in Proverbs 10–15 where the LORD is not named, and that the text resumes naming him immediately afterwards.

  3. The collection frames itself with a verbatim repetition, and the frame is stronger than the sweep recorded. [T] bēn ḥākām yəśammaḥ-ʾāb — “a wise son makes a father glad” — stands as the first line after the superscription (10:1b) and returns at 15:20, four words verbatim, with the mother in the B-colon both times. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Move 4. Upgraded from the sweep’s moderate–high to high on the verbatim data. This is independent text-first support for the traditional division at 15:33/16:1.

  4. 13:14 and 14:27 are the same saying with the subject changed — and the substitution is the collection’s theology in miniature. [T] Five words verbatim: məqôr ḥayyîm lāsûr mimmōqšê māwet, “a fountain of life, to turn from the snares of death.” The subject at 13:14 is the teaching of the wise; at 14:27 it is the fear of the LORD. With 10:11 (the mouth of the righteous) that is one fountain-of-life saying per movement. Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Move 4, Biblical Theology. High on the data; moderate–high on the reading. Sapiential instruction and the fear of the LORD are given the identical predicate.

  5. Four verbatim links cross 15:33/16:1, and the hinge is real. [T] yād ləyād lōʾ yinnāqeh (11:21 ∥ 16:5) · ṭôb məʿaṭ (15:16 ∥ 16:8) · sûr mērāʿ (13:19 ∥ 16:6) · bəlōʾ mišpāṭ (13:23 ∥ 16:8). Together with the divine-name crest (nine YHWH sayings in fifteen verses), this is text-first evidence that 16:1–15 is not a new collection’s opening but this one’s destination. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Positional Necessity. High on the links; moderate–high on the reading.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level: worked once in dig-deeper-proverbs-sweep and assumed — the formation of a person who fears the LORD, addressed to “my son.”

Passage level. The sweep named the section’s purpose as filling in the characters the frame had named. That stands. This run adds the shape of the filling-in, and it is a shape with a direction.

The section moves from anthropology to theology, and the move is countable. [T] Set the two governing vocabularies side by side:

verses naming ṣaddîq 13 8 8 5 2 3 0
verses naming YHWH 4 2 2 0 3 9 9
(verses in chapter) 32 31 28 25 35 33 15

Read down the columns: the righteous man is the subject at the start and God is the subject at the end. The two curves cross somewhere in chapter 14. [T] High on the counts.

What that makes the section’s purpose. [I] Moderate–high. Not “here are two kinds of people” but “here are two kinds of people — and here is who decides which you are.” The reader is given the categories in their crispest form while he still needs them, and then walked toward the one who sees the heart (15:11), weighs the spirit (16:2) and establishes the steps (16:9). The bifurcation is not withdrawn; it is grounded.

Book-overview note: this extends rather than contradicts the overview. The overview’s microscript holds; what is new is that the section has an internal direction rather than being a heap of antitheses with a motto at the end.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. Chapters 1–9 close with chapter 9’s two banquets and a forced choice between two houses. The frame has named the wise, the fool, the righteous and the wicked; it has not shown them.

Necessity answer. The sweep’s answer stands: having chosen a house, the reader needs to know what living in it looks like, and the frame cannot supply that. [T]/[I] This run adds a second, formal answer.

Why this material first, before 16:1–22:16. Because the reader needs the categories before he can be told their limits. [I] Moderate–high. The evidence:

  • 10:1–15:33 is the collection’s most bifurcated stretch — ṣaddîq and rāšāʿ appear in 39 and 45 verses respectively, concentrated in chapters 10–12.
  • The bifurcation is then broken from inside, four times, by the yēš-sayings (see Tool 5): 11:24, 12:18, 13:7, 14:12. Every one is about the gap between how a thing looks and what it is.
  • And the divine-name gradient rises as the character-vocabulary falls, so that by 16:1–9 the reader has been handed the categories, shown that he cannot always apply them, and pointed to the one who can.

Why the passage exists here and not elsewhere. [I] A sequence is legible across the sentence literature, and this run supplies the first term of it: 10–15 gives the sort · 16:1–22:16 subordinates it to divine sovereignty · 22:17–24:34 teaches observation · 25–27 removes the sort altogether and requires judgement · 28–29 restores it with tôrāh. Solomon 1A cannot stand anywhere but first, because everything after it either complicates or withholds what it establishes.

Implication for this passage. The sweep’s advice holds and sharpens: preach these chapters by cluster, never verse by verse — and preach the direction, not only the contrasts. A series that stops at “two ways” has preached chapters 10–12 and ignored 14–15.

Immediate context

Before: 9:13–18, the woman Folly’s banquet, and 9:10, “the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom.” After: 16:1, “the plans of the heart belong to man, but the answer of the tongue is from the LORD.”

The two boundaries are not alike. 9:18 to 10:1 is a genre change (personification speech → sentence) and a superscription and a paragraph marker: a hard break. 15:33 to 16:1 is none of those — no marker, no superscription, no change of form, and four verbatim links running across (Headline 5). The collection has a hard front edge and a soft back edge. [T]

Twenty units for 184 verses — an average of nine verses each. That is coarser than Heim’s stated norm of three to eight sayings, and the checking stage below reports what that costs.

(c) The formula that does the most structural work

The full two-part tôʿăbat YHWH … rəṣônô formula — abomination and delight, both halves — occurs exactly four times: [T]

11:1 a false balance … a just weight is his delight 11:20 crooked heart … blameless ways are his delight 12:22 lying lips … those who act faithfully are his delight 15:8 the sacrifice of the wicked … the prayer of the upright is his delight

Three further verses carry tôʿēbâ without the rəṣônô half (15:9, 15:26, 16:5), and one applies it to kings rather than the LORD (16:12). The four full occurrences track the section’s own subjects in order — trade, character, speech, worship — which is a progression from the marketplace to the sanctuary. [I] Moderate–high. And the last of them, 15:8, sits in the stretch where the divine name is densest.

4. Linking Words

For a sentence collection the governing connector is the parallelism itself, treated under Tool 5. Three observations earn their place.

The wāw rendered “but” is the section’s engine, and it is nearly universal. In a stretch where roughly three sayings in four are antithetical, the connector is the form. It should not be traced verse by verse; it should be preached as a shape.

Motive kî is almost absent. [T] The section states rather than argues. Where kî does appear it is worth noticing — 16:12, “for the throne is established by righteousness”, is one of the few grounded assertions in the whole stretch, and it stands in the hinge.

And the connectors that matter most are the ones that break the pattern. Four verses open with yēš, “there is” — 11:24, 12:18, 13:7, 14:12 — and one with hălôʾ, “do they not” (14:22). In a run of flat contrasts these are the marked openings, and they carry the section’s most uncomfortable content. See Tool 5.

The direction of his claim is right; the “full circle” is not. See Book-Overview Tensions.

What is countable, and what it shows

  • Marked comparisons: six in 184 verses. [T] 10:23, 10:25, 10:26, 12:18, 15:19 — plus 16:15 in the hinge. Against nine in the eighty-three verses of 25–27. A simile density roughly three times lower.
  • And 10:26 is the only full kə…kēn simile in the whole unit — vinegar to the teeth, smoke to the eyes, so is the sluggard. [T] In 184 verses of contrast, one emblematic saying. It is a marked verse and should be preached as one.
  • “Better-than” sayings: three genuine ones — 12:9, 15:16, 15:17 — with 16:8 in the hinge. 15:16 and 15:17 are adjacent, the only such pair, and both set a modest good with something against a great good without it. [T]

The yēš-sayings — where the section breaks its own pattern

Four verses open yēš, “there is”: [T]

11:24 There is one who scatters and yet increases; another withholds what is due, and only comes to want. 12:18 There is one whose rash words are like sword thrusts, but the tongue of the wise brings healing. 13:7 There is one who pretends to be rich yet has nothing; one who pretends to be poor yet has great wealth. 14:12 There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death.

All four are about the gap between appearance and reality, and the yēš opening is what marks them off from the flat two-column sayings around them. [I] Moderate–high.

14:12 is the climax of the set, and it has no second half to reassure the reader. The sweep noted this and it is right; what this run adds is that 14:12 is not an isolated break but the last of four, and that it sits inside the unit (14:9–16) bound by ʾaḥărîtāh — “its end” — at vv.12 and 13, the only two occurrences in the section. The section admits its own limits in a small, marked, deliberate group of verses, and a preacher who finds only 14:12 has found the loudest of them but not the pattern.

Chiasm

10:6–11 is a genuine six-verse chiasm on verbatim colon repetition (Tool 3), with 10:9 at the centre: “Whoever walks in integrity walks securely, but he who makes his ways crooked will be found out.” [T] The centre of a chiasm is its point, and this one’s point is integrity is safety — with the two outer pairs saying, twice each, that the wicked mouth conceals violence and the babbling fool comes to ruin. The structure preaches: what is hidden is found out, and the man who has nothing to hide walks securely.

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A — a sentence collection, not narrative. The book’s one stretch of narrative with authorial intrusion is 7:6–23, worked in its own unit. The editorial notice at 10:1 is treated under Tools 1 and 2.

7. Vocabulary

The two governing vocabularies (see Headline 1)

ṣaddîq / rāšāʿ — 39 and 45 verses. [T] High; counted. Note that they are not perfectly paired: rāšāʿ outlasts ṣaddîq, appearing in six verses of 15:8–16:15 against two. The wicked man is still in view when the righteous man has dropped out of the vocabulary — because by then the subject is God, and what is said of him is said against the wicked (15:8, 15:9, 15:29, 16:4).

məqôr ḥayyîm — the fountain thread

Three occurrences, one per movement: [T]

10:11 — the mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life 13:14 — the teaching of the wise is a fountain of life, to turn from the snares of death 14:27 — the fear of the LORD is a fountain of life, to turn from the snares of death

13:14 and 14:27 share five words verbatim and differ only in the subject. [T] High. The predicate is fixed and the subject climbs: from a righteous man’s speech, to the sage’s instruction, to the fear of the LORD. This is the Leitwort-scale evidence for Headline 1, and it is the single most preachable structural fact in the unit.

(A fourth occurrence follows at 16:22 — “good sense is a fountain of life to him who has it” — outside this unit but inside the hinge’s neighbourhood.) [S: sweep]

yirʾat YHWH — five occurrences, and where they fall

10:27 · 14:26 · 14:27 · 15:16 · 15:33. [T] Three in the bare construct (10:27, 14:27, 15:33) and two prefixed (14:26, 15:16).

Two things are worth the preacher’s attention. First, 14:26 and 14:27 are the only consecutive pair — the phrase is used twice running, at exactly the point where the divine-name gradient begins to climb. Second, 15:33 closes the unit on it, restating the motto of 1:7 and 9:10 in the collection’s own idiom. [T]

Proper-noun inventory

Solomon (10:1) — and nothing else in 184 verses. [T] No place names, no gentilics, no personal names, no numbers with canonical freight. The section is deliberately unlocated: it describes a world, not a place. The Solomon attribution carries the freight worked at book level (the canonical reader knows 1 Kgs 11); nothing here requires it to be load-bearing, and the load-the-story rule is honoured by not making it so.

Familiar words used in a Bible-specific way

  • lēb — 34 verses. The whole inner person. Note its concentration in chapter 15 (nine verses) at exactly the point where God is said to see it (15:11, “Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the LORD; how much more the hearts of the children of man”). The heart-vocabulary peaks where the divine-inspection vocabulary peaks. [T]/[I]
  • ʿōśēhû (“his Maker”, 14:31) — sustained from the sweep as one of the strongest ethical texts in the book: the poor man is not merely unfortunate, he is made, and contempt for him is contempt for the one who made him. [T] High.
  • tôʿēbâ — Deuteronomy’s cultic-legal verdict-word, applied here to trade (11:1), character (11:20), speech (12:22) and worship (15:8). [T] High. See Tool 11.
  • ʾiwwelet — 21 occurrences, concentrated in chapter 14 (seven). It frames 14:1–8 and 14:17–29.

Paronomasia check

Triggered by ʾappayim (14:17, 14:29) — but the pairing is semantic inversion (qəṣar / ʾerek), not sound-play. No consonant-reversal claim clears the corroboration test. None flagged.

8. Translations

13:15 — where the versions genuinely part

Hebrew (MT) wədderek bōgədîm ʾêtān — “and the way of the treacherous is enduring” (or “rugged”)
ESV “but the way of the treacherous is their ruin” — with the footnote: “Probable reading (compare Septuagint, Syriac, Vulgate); Hebrew is rugged, or is an enduring rut”

The ESV is following the ancient versions against the MT here, and says so. [S] Moderate. Under the Three-Way Triage this is a category 2 substantive variant — the versions appear to reflect a different reading, and the MT as pointed yields a sense (“the way of the treacherous endures”) that sits awkwardly against the collection’s whole argument. This is one of the few places in the section where a preacher’s English is not rendering the Hebrew in front of him, and the footnote should be read from the pulpit if the verse is preached. Peshitta, LXX and Vulgate all cited by the ESV; not independently verified here.

14:32 — settled earlier in the project, recorded here

“The wicked is overthrown through his evildoing, but the righteous finds refuge in his death” (MT bəmôtô) against the LXX’s “in his integrity” (bətummô). The library divided four to one for the MT, with Fox alone against on syntactic rather than theological grounds. [S] High. Ancient versions check

Trigger met at 11:31: ESV, NASB and KJV substantively agree — “If the righteous is repaid on earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner!” — and the rendering carries weight.

Result: a category-3 finding. The LXX reads “If the righteous is scarcely saved…”, and that is the form quoted at 1 Peter 4:18. [S] High. Under the triage this stands whichever text is prior: it is a fact about how the New Testament reads Proverbs, and it is reported under Tool 11 rather than as a variant. A sermon on 11:31 should note that Peter’s Greek says something the Hebrew does not.

Category 1 — translation loss

Three findings in this report are features of the Hebrew that no translation carries: the verbatim identity of 10:1b and 15:20a (English must vary “makes a glad father”); the five-word identity of 13:14 and 14:27; and the four-times tôʿăbat YHWH … rəṣônô formula, which the ESV renders with three different words for rəṣônô (“his delight”, “acceptable to him”). Reported as facts about the Hebrew, not as reliability judgements.

9. Tone and Feel

Level, unhurried, epigrammatic — the register of settled observation. Sustained from the sweep, and this run can now say why it feels that way.

The section almost never addresses the reader. [T] In 184 verses there is one imperative: 14:7, “Leave the presence of a fool.” The vocative “my son” does not appear once. The father, mother and son of 10:1 are all third person. This is a book talking about the reader rather than to him, and the effect is of overhearing rather than being taught.

Which makes the two imperatives of the wider unit stand out sharply. [T] 14:7 — leave the fool — and, in the hinge, 16:3 — commit your work to the LORD. Two commands in 199 verses, and they are the negative and positive poles of the whole thing: get away from the wrong company; hand your work to the right person.

Tonal shifts. Chapters 10–12 are flat and forensic. Chapter 13 is the coolest stretch — no divine name, heavy on wealth and appetite. Chapter 14 warms and unsettles by turns (14:10, “the heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy” — the loneliest verse in the book; 14:13, “even in laughter the heart may ache”). Chapter 15 turns pastoral and God-ward, and closes on humility.

Soundtrack: a metronome for chapters 10–12; a solo cello entering at 14:10; something choral by 15:33.

11. Quotation/Allusion

Live sources for Proverbs, from the sweep: Genesis 1–3, Deuteronomy (four strands), Job (28, 38), Psalms, the Solomon tradition, and the Egyptian instruction tradition. Standing negative finding: Proverbs contains no formula citations; its scriptural engagement runs by allusion and transferred idiom, and its argument runs on internal repetition — which is why Move 4 does the heavy lifting.

Deuteronomy 25:13–16 → Proverbs 11:1 (high confidence — verdict-word transfer) [T]/[S: sweep]

Source context. The last case-law in Deuteronomy’s code before the firstfruits liturgy: “You shall not have in your bag two kinds of weights… A full and fair weight you shall have… For all who do such things, all who act dishonestly, are an abomination to the LORD your God.” Its function there is to close the legal material on commercial integrity — and the word tôʿēbâ attaches to it in the source.

Book usage. Deuteronomy is well established as a live source. What is distinctive is that Proverbs takes the verdict-word as well as the content, and returns to weights three more times (16:11; 20:10; 20:23), making it the most frequently revisited legal text in the book. [T] High.

OT-to-OT. Deut 25:13–16, Lev 19:35–36 and Amos 8:5 are already in canonical conversation on this exact issue — the prophets prosecuting what Deuteronomy legislates. Proverbs joins them and supplies the positive half the law does not: “but a just weight is his delight.”

What it adds. This is the anchor of the whole tôʿēbâ group, and the group is a finding in its own right. The full formula appears four times (Tool 3c), moving trade → character → speech → worship. A word Deuteronomy attached to false weights has been extended across the whole of life and finally into the sanctuary — so that by 15:8 the sacrifice of the wicked is what the false balance was. That is Proverbs doing to the cult what Amos and Isaiah do, using Deuteronomy’s own vocabulary.

Genesis 2–3 (the tree of life) → Proverbs 11:30; 13:12; 15:4 (high on the source; moderate on the load) [S: sweep]/[T]

Source context. Eden’s tree of life, barred after the fall (Gen 3:22–24).

Book usage — and this is the finding. The image migrates: wisdom herself (3:18) → the righteous man’s fruit (11:30) → fulfilled desire (13:12) → a gentle tongue (15:4). [T] By 15:4 the Eden image has been domesticated to a manner of speaking. The trajectory is the point, and it belongs beside the məqôr ḥayyîm thread of Tool 7: two life-images, both migrating, both landing in ordinary human conduct.

OT-to-OT. No pairing in this passage. What it adds: preached together, 11:30 and 15:4 say that what was lost in Eden is met — partially, really — in a righteous life and a kind word. That is neither too little nor too much to claim.

12. Genre

Hebrew wisdom literature; the sentence collection in its purest form — 184 two-line sayings with almost no admonition, no vocative, no narrative and no extended argument.

The reading rule that matters here is the one about partiality. A proverb is a generally-true observation, not a promise. This section is the place in the book where that rule is most needed and least obvious, because the antithetical form sounds like a rule: the righteous prosper, the wicked perish. Two features of the text itself supply the corrective — the yēš-sayings (Tool 5), and the 10:15 → 18:11 pair, where the book audibly qualifies itself.

And one sub-genre marker. 10:26 is the section’s only emblematic saying and 14:7 its only imperative. [T] In a collection this formally uniform, a change of form is a signal, and both should be preached as such.

13. Copycat

N/A for the sentence material — no narrative characters, only character-types. The righteous, the wicked, the sluggard, the scoffer and the excellent wife are portraits, not people; they function as mirrors.

One qualification, and it is the one that matters pastorally. The section’s form invites the hearer to place himself in the right column, and that placement is the copycat error specific to these chapters. 20:9 blocks it (“who can say, ‘I have made my heart pure’?”) and 14:12 blocks it from inside the unit. The question these chapters put is not “which of these am I like?” but “how would I know?”

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits. Sayings attributed to Solomon; united monarchy in the book’s self-presentation. [T] on the attribution as stated; the historical reconstruction is [S] and nothing here depends on it.

Where the reader sits. This side of cross, resurrection and ascension.

What has happened in between that changes the reading. Two things, and the first is specific to this section.

  1. The two-column sort has been broken and remade at the cross. These six chapters assert, more insistently than anywhere else in Scripture, that the righteous prosper and the wicked perish. At Golgotha the wholly righteous man received everything this section assigns to the wicked, and Romans 4:5 says God “justifies the ungodly” — which 17:15 calls an abomination. The section is not wrong; it is awaiting the event that will let it be true for people who are not righteous. That tension is the section’s road to the gospel and should not be smoothed.
  2. The fountain has a name. 13:14 and 14:27 give the identical predicate to the sage’s teaching and the fear of the LORD; John 4:14 and 7:38 give it to Christ.

And one guard. 10:22 (“the blessing of the LORD makes rich”) is a prosperity proof-text; the correctives are internal — 11:4, 15:16, 16:8, and 30:8–9 later.

15. Who Am I?

The default identification is the addressee — the one who does not yet have wisdom. The characteristic misreading here is to identify with the righteous man of the A-cola.

The section itself resists that, and it does so quietly. There is no vocative to place the reader; the sayings are all third-person. The reader is not addressed as righteous or wicked — he is shown both and left to work out where he stands, and then told at 14:12 that he may be wrong.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. To be able to tell the difference — between wise and foolish speech, honest and dishonest gain, discipline and its refusal — and then to learn that the ability to tell has a limit, and that the LORD does not share it. The section forms discernment and then humility about discernment, in that order, and 15:33 names the pair.

Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.

Domain Content
Worldview The world is morally legible — actions have shapes and consequences. But it is not transparently legible: there is a way that seems right (14:12), a man who seems rich (13:7), a word that seems helpful (12:18). Legible, and not obvious.
Behaviour — stop Talking too much (10:19). Concealing hatred behind civil speech (10:18, 26:23’s cousin). Slandering (11:13). Hoarding grain (11:26). Despising a neighbour (11:12, 14:21). Trusting riches (11:28). Sparing the rod out of ease (13:24 — with the cautions at Difficult Verses).
Behaviour — start Restraining the lips (10:19). Giving and finding it multiplies (11:24–25). Taking advice (12:15). Answering softly (15:1). Being generous to the poor — which is honouring their Maker (14:31). Leaving the presence of a fool (14:7 — the section’s one command).
Motive Not consequence alone, though the section is full of consequence. The four tôʿēbâ sayings supply the deeper motive: these things are an abomination to the LORD, and their opposites are his delight. Trade, character, speech and worship are all matters of what God finds pleasing.

Four audiences.

Audience What this section says
For me 14:12 is aimed at me, and the section gives me no way of exempting myself. The right response to six chapters of moral clarity is not confidence but the fear of the LORD (15:33).
For a Christian friend 12:25 — “anxiety in a man’s heart weighs him down, but a good word makes him glad.” The section’s tenderest verse, and a mandate. And 15:1: the answer you give is a choice.
For the church 11:9–14. A community is destroyed by the godless mouth and preserved by counsel; 11:14 puts safety in “an abundance of counsellors.” And 15:8 — a congregation can bring worship God finds abominable.
For an unbeliever 14:12, and 15:11: “Sheol and Abaddon lie open before the LORD; how much more the hearts of the children of man.” The claim is not that religion improves you but that you are already seen.

Prayer in response.

  • Sorry for the confidence with which I sort other people into the columns I keep myself out of.
  • Thank you that the fountain of life turned out to have a name, and that the fear of the LORD is not terror but instruction.
  • Please give me a restrained tongue (10:19), a soft answer (15:1) and a good word for someone weighed down (12:25).
  • Mercy on those who are on a way that seems right.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period. Presented as Solomonic. [T] on the presentation; historical reconstruction [S], and nothing here depends on it.
  • Location. Deliberately unlocated — no place name appears in 184 verses. [T] The furniture is generic and domestic: scales and weights (11:1), grain and its hoarding (11:26), the ox and the manger (14:4), fields and harvest (10:5), the household and its bread (12:9), the city and its gates (11:10–11; 14:19).
  • Social world. Agrarian, kin-based, honour–shame, with a functioning market and a city whose fortunes rise and fall with its righteous (11:10–11).

What the original audience knew

  • Weights were carried in a bag and were the ordinary point of commercial fraud. [T]/[I] High. Deuteronomy legislates them; Amos prosecutes them; Proverbs calls them an abomination — cultic language for a trading sin. 11:1 is not a platitude about honesty; it is the law’s own verdict-word transferred to the marketplace.
  • A city’s welfare was visibly tied to its leading men. 11:10–11 assumes a community small enough that the fortunes of the righteous are public news. [I] High.
  • Grain-hoarding in a subsistence economy was a life-and-death matter, which is why 11:26 has the people cursing the man who withholds. [I] Moderate–high.
  • The ox was capital, and a clean manger meant no ox. 14:4 is a joke about the cost of productivity that lands instantly in an agrarian economy and needs explaining in ours. [T]/[I]
  • “His Maker” (14:31) assumed a doctrine of creation the audience held without argument: the poor man is not a social category but a creature. [T] High.

What this changes about how we read

11:1 and the tôʿēbâ group stop being moralism. They are Torah-ethics in another key, and the transfer of Deuteronomy’s cultic verdict-word to trade, character, speech and worship is a theological argument, not a rhetorical flourish.

14:4 stops being obscure. “Where there are no oxen, the manger is clean” is not agricultural trivia; it is a dry observation that a tidy operation may be an empty one — and it sits in a collection otherwise devoted to the tidiness of the righteous life.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: the Writings. The canonical reader arrives having read Torah and Prophets — post-exilic, holding promises unresolved, knowing how Solomon’s story ends.
  • What that reader knows: the whole monarchy and its collapse; Deuteronomy’s blessings and curses; the prophets’ prosecution of a cult without justice. He reads “the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to the LORD” (15:8) with Isaiah 1 and Amos 5 already in his ears.
  • Canonical register: Job and Psalms are the immediate neighbours, and Job is the loudest — because the reader who has read Job has already seen the two-column sort fail in a single devastating case. The canonical reader of Proverbs 10–15 is not naïve about it.

The first hearers

  • Who they were: a household and a community being formed — the material assumes property, dependants, a market and a share in the city’s affairs, but nothing courtly. [I] Moderate. Unlike 25–27, there is no king to navigate: melek appears in just two verses of 184 (14:28, 14:35).
  • Their situation: ordinary life, in which the connection between conduct and outcome is mostly reliable and occasionally not.
  • What they brought: the assumption — which the section largely endorses — that the world is morally ordered.

Where the passage fits their world

It confirms the assumption and then, quietly and in four places, qualifies it. That is a more sophisticated pastoral operation than it looks. A hearer who needed the order confirmed got six chapters of confirmation; a hearer who had watched the order fail got 11:24, 12:18, 13:7 and 14:12, and got the last of them without a reassuring second half.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Comforting: the whole of chapters 10–12, to anyone whose experience matched them. And 12:25 — a good word makes a weighed-down heart glad.
  • Disturbing: 14:12. And 15:8 — that a man’s worship could be the abomination.
  • Surprising: 11:24, that scattering increases and withholding impoverishes. The economics are counter-intuitive and stated flatly.
  • Lonely: 14:10, “the heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy.” In a collectivist society this is a remarkable admission of the privacy of suffering.
  • Funny: 11:22, the gold ring in a pig’s snout. 14:4, the clean manger.

What we bring that they didn’t

  • A therapeutic suspicion of moral categories. We hear “the righteous and the wicked” as judgemental; they heard it as descriptive. The section is not sorting a congregation; it is describing two possible lives — and it declines to tell the reader which he is living.
  • Individualism. 11:10–11 assumes a city whose welfare is bound to its citizens’ character. Read individually it becomes advice; read corporately it is a political claim.
  • A prosperity reflex in both directions — either reading 10:22 as a promise, or reacting against the section wholesale because 10:22 has been abused.

Implication for the sermon

Preach the direction, not only the contrasts. The section’s own architecture moves from the righteous man to the LORD, and a series that stops at “two ways” has preached its first third. The destination is 15:33 — and the fear of the LORD is where discernment turns into humility rather than confidence.

Biblical-Theological Themes

(1) The two ways

  • Where this passage sits. The densest concentration of two-ways material in Scripture — and, at 14:12, its own qualification.
  • Before. Deuteronomy 30:15–20 (life and death set before them); Psalm 1 (the two trees); Jeremiah 21:8.
  • After. Matthew 7:13–27 — two gates, two trees, two houses, and a warning that many will say “Lord, Lord” — which is the yēš-problem of Proverbs 14:12 in the mouth of Jesus. Then Galatians 5 and Romans 8’s two walks.
  • What it contributes. The two-ways scheme is not a Proverbs peculiarity but a covenant structure, and the New Testament keeps it. What Proverbs adds is the honest admission that you may be on the wrong road and not know it — which is exactly what Matthew 7:21–23 turns into a scene.
  • Confidence: high.

(2) The fear of the LORD as the source of life

  • Where this passage sits. 13:14 and 14:27 give the sage’s teaching and the fear of the LORD the identical predicate — a fountain of life, to turn from the snares of death — and 15:33 makes the fear of the LORD “instruction in wisdom.”
  • Before. Deuteronomy 4:10; 6:2; 10:12; Job 28:28; and the book’s own 1:7 and 9:10.
  • After. John 4:14 and 7:38 (living water, from Christ and from the believer); Acts 9:31, the church walking “in the fear of the Lord.”
  • What it contributes. The identical predicate is the argument. Instruction and the fear of the LORD are not two ladders; they are one thing described twice. That guards Proverbs against being read as technique.
  • Confidence: high on the data; moderate–high on the reading.

Confluence

Mutual illumination. The two themes explain each other’s placement. The two-ways material needs a ground outside the reader’s own judgement, or 14:12 destroys it; the fear of the LORD supplies that ground, which is why the divine-name gradient rises exactly as the character-vocabulary falls. The section’s structure is its theology: it teaches you to sort, shows you that you cannot finally sort yourself, and hands you to the one who can.

Christological Reading

Type of connection: trajectory and contrast, with one authorised NT use.

1. The contrast that governs everything else

These six chapters assert the moral order more insistently than anywhere in Scripture: the righteous prosper, the wicked perish, the diligent hand grows rich, the blessing of the LORD adds no sorrow.

At the cross the order inverted completely. The one wholly righteous man received the whole of what this section assigns to the wicked — and Romans 4:5 says God “justifies the ungodly”, which 17:15 calls an abomination. [I] High on the tension; it is the section’s most important theological fact for preaching.

This is not the section being wrong. It is the section being incomplete on its own terms — and it says so, at 14:12. The way that seemed right ended in death; and at Golgotha the way that was right ended in death too, on purpose, so that people who are on the wrong road could be put on the right one.

Tests: theological category (righteousness and its reward) ✔ · NT precedent (Romans 4 makes the connection explicitly) ✔ · escalation (the fulfilment exceeds the pattern by inverting it) ✔ · authorial pattern — the section raises the problem itself at 14:12 and 20:9 ✔. All four; but classify as contrast rather than typology.

2. The fountain

10:11, 13:14, 14:27 — three fountains of life, subject climbing from a righteous mouth to the fear of the LORD. John 4:14: “the water that I will give him will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life.” [I] Moderate–high. The trajectory is real and the image is not incidental — but the connection is canonical reflection, not a claim about what the proverb meant.

3. The tree

3:18 → 11:30 → 13:12 → 15:4, and then Revelation 22:2. The image barred at Genesis 3:24 is handled with increasing ordinariness through Proverbs and finally restored in the city. [I] Moderate–high.

4. Wisdom builds a house (14:1)

Chapter 9’s Wisdom built her house and set her table; 14:1 compresses it; and Hebrews 3:3–6 says Christ is faithful over God’s house as a son, “and we are his house.” [I] Moderate. Offer as resonance, not as the verse’s claim.

Moralism check

The temptation here is overwhelming and the section is almost designed to produce it: 184 verses describing the behaviour of good people, in a form that sounds like a rule.

Where the gospel grounding sits. In the section’s own architecture, not in a verse bolted on:

  • The two-column sort is grounded in God, not in the reader — that is what the divine-name gradient does.
  • The reader’s ability to place himself is denied, at 14:12 and 20:9.
  • The section’s last word is not achievement but humility (15:33), and its motive-clauses are about what God finds abominable and delightful, not about outcomes.

Christ as hero, not example. The righteous man of these chapters is not first a model for the reader; he is a portrait the reader fails to match and Christ fills. The right sermon move is not “be the man of chapter 10” but “here is the man of chapter 10, and here is how you come to be counted as him.”

Confidence: high on the contrast; moderate–high on the fountain and the tree; moderate on the house.

Difficult / Contested Verses

13:24 — “whoever spares the rod hates his son”

  • Category: pastoral landmine and weaponised text. The hardest verse in the unit, and it will be heard by parents, by survivors of abuse, and by people told this verse licensed what was done to them.
  • Rhetorical function: the verse’s own subject is love — the B-colon is “but he who loves him is diligent to discipline him.” The contrast is not between harshness and softness but between two claims to love.
  • What the text supplies: the ESV footnotes an alternative rendering (“who loves him disciplines him early”); the same book insists the purpose is the child’s life, not the parent’s anger (19:18, “do not set your heart on putting him to death”); and 22:6 frames the whole in terms of a way to be trained into, not a behaviour to be beaten out.
  • Handling: name the abuse question rather than avoiding it. Do not preach 13:24 without 19:18. Sustained from the sweep.

14:12 — “there is a way that seems right”

  • Category: doctrinal, and the section’s most important verse.
  • The difficulty is not obscurity but placement. In a run of confident contrasts, this verse has no reassuring second half.
  • Handling: preach it as the section’s own qualification, with 13:7, 12:18 and 11:24 as its companions — and with Matthew 7:21–23, where the same problem becomes a scene.

10:22 — “the blessing of the LORD makes rich”

  • Category: weaponised. A prosperity proof-text.
  • The correctives are internal: 11:4 (riches do not profit in the day of wrath), 15:16 and 16:8 (better a little), 11:28 (trust in riches and fall), and 30:8–9 later.
  • Handling: preach it with 11:4 in the same sermon, or not at all.

13:15 — the version-split

  • Category: textual, with preaching consequence.
  • The ESV renders “the way of the treacherous is their ruin” against an MT that says something closer to “endures.” The versions are followed and footnoted.
  • Handling: read the footnote. Do not build on the second colon.

11:31 — Peter’s Greek

  • Category: apologetic / textual.
  • 1 Peter 4:18 quotes the LXX (“if the righteous is scarcely saved”), which says something the Hebrew does not.
  • Handling: a good opportunity to explain how the NT uses the Greek OT, rather than a problem to conceal.

The antitheses as a whole

  • Category: the section-wide pastoral risk.
  • They invite the hearer to place himself among the righteous. 20:9 blocks it; 14:12 blocks it; the absence of any vocative means the text never actually tells the reader which he is.
  • Handling: say so, early in any series.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: preaching the contrasts and missing the direction

  • What it looks like: a series called “Two Ways” that works through the righteous/wicked antitheses chapter by chapter and finishes where it started.
  • Why it’s wrong: the section moves. The righteous man is named in thirteen verses of chapter 10 and none of 16:1–15; the LORD is named in none of chapter 13 and nine verses of the hinge. The collection’s own architecture takes the reader from character to God.
  • The corrective: structure the series along the gradient. Start with the sort, end with 15:33, and let the crossing be the point.

Pitfall: preaching chapter 13 as though God were in it

  • What it looks like: a sermon on 13:7 or 13:11 that arrives, as sermons do, at a paragraph about the LORD’s provision.
  • Why it’s wrong: chapter 13 names God nowhere in twenty-five verses, and that is not an accident of the material — it is the trough of a gradient that rises immediately afterwards. A preacher who supplies the divine name has smoothed over a deliberate silence.
  • The corrective: preach chapter 13 as the cool, worldly, appetite-and-money stretch it is, and let 14:26–27 arrive as relief.

Pitfall: 13:24 without 19:18

  • What it looks like: “the Bible says spare the rod and spoil the child.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the verse’s own subject is love, the ESV footnotes an alternative rendering, and the book itself sets a limit at 19:18 (“do not set your heart on putting him to death”). Preaching the first without the second has, historically, done real harm.
  • The corrective: both verses, and name the abuse question aloud rather than hoping nobody is thinking it.

Pitfall: 10:22 as a prosperity text

  • What it looks like: “the blessing of the LORD makes rich, and he adds no sorrow” — offered as a promise.
  • Why it’s wrong: the same collection says riches do not profit in the day of wrath (11:4), that a little with the fear of the LORD is better (15:16), and that the man who trusts his riches falls (11:28).
  • The corrective: preach 10:22 and 11:4 together. They are thirty-three verses apart and they are a matched pair — both open lōʾ yôʿîl-shaped claims about wealth.

Pitfall: taking 14:12 as the only crack in the wall

  • What it looks like: a sermon that treats 14:12 as a lone flash of realism in an otherwise confident section.
  • Why it’s wrong: it is the last and loudest of four yēš-sayings — 11:24, 12:18, 13:7, 14:12 — all about the gap between appearance and reality. The section qualifies itself repeatedly and quietly.
  • The corrective: preach the group. It shows that the honesty is structural rather than an aberration.

Pitfall: the sage’s chair

  • What it looks like: describing the two kinds of people to a congregation assumed to be the first kind.
  • Why it’s wrong: the section has no vocative and never tells the reader which he is. 20:9 and 14:12 make self-placement impossible.
  • The corrective: preach from inside the second column looking for a way out. That is where the gospel enters these chapters.

Pitfall: verse-by-verse through 184 sayings

  • What it looks like: a long series treating each proverb as a discrete unit.
  • Why it’s wrong: the section is arranged — chiasm at 10:6–11, inclusio at 12:3–12 and 13:1–8, a four-fold tôʿēbâ spine, a frame at 10:1/15:20. Verse-by-verse destroys all of it.
  • The corrective: preach by cluster. The map at Tool 3 is a starting point, not a straitjacket.
