Point & Purpose: Proverbs 10:1–22:16

What changed in this refresh

The shape held and the evidence underneath it is new. Version 1 was built on a single whole-section run; this version consumes two solo halves and an umbrella pass. Its central judgement — preach the transmitted paragraphs, not the chapter numbers, and do not make 15:33/16:1 a boundary — survives, and is now better founded than it was.

Phase 0 — the scope decision

A single sermon on 375 verses — the longest unit in the series — and the scope is settled, not open.

The governing rationale is the series backbone’s: people hear this book in fragments and so miss the point of its being a book [S: series introduction v3]. The sermon follows the collection’s own movement rather than surveying its contents. Three things now make that viable, and all three are counted.

First, the transmitted text does the dividing, and it divides almost not at all. WLC, printing the Leningrad layout, marks four places in 375 verses [T] — the lowest paragraphing density anywhere in Proverbs:

Paragraph Verses
10:1 — the superscription, set off by itself 1
10:2 → 13:25 115
14:1 → 19:9 162
19:10 → 21:30 80
21:31 → 22:16 17

A transmitted paragraph of 162 verses is itself the argument for preaching this whole. One manuscript, a reading tradition rather than an authorial signal, and its spread unchecked [unchecked — apparatus spread] — but it is the only division the text offers, and it declines to offer more.

Second, the collection has a movement and the movement is the sermon. It opens sorting the world into two columns and ends by telling the reader he cannot sort himself. That arc cannot be heard in a fragment.

Third — and this is new — the text welds its two halves together itself. The verse that breaks the sorting, 14:12, is reprinted word for word at 16:25 [T], and both stand inside the 162-verse paragraph. The preacher does not have to build the bridge; he has to point at it.

⚠ Do not make 15:33/16:1 the sermon’s hinge — and the reason has changed.

Version 1 said no marker falls there, which is true. The new work adds that two counted measures really do turn there — the collection’s method changes, and a preacher who felt a shift was not imagining it. But the text itself refuses to break there. The paragraph runs 14:1 → 19:9 straight through; the longest consecutive run of verses naming יְהוָה anywhere in Proverbs is the eight from 15:33 to 16:7, beginning on one side and ending on the other [T]; and 16:25 reaches back across it. So: a change of method, not a boundary. Chapter 16 is the crest.

Fallen Condition Focus

We want the moral order to place us — to tell us which kind of person we are — and we cannot read our own position in it.

Anchored, and countably so. [T] The collection spends its first half connecting character to consequence and then breaks the instrument from inside, twice in each half:

  • 14:12 — יֵשׁ דֶּרֶךְ יָשָׁר לִפְנֵי־אִישׁ (“there is a way which seems right to a man”) — and its end is the way of death.
  • 16:2 — כָּל־דַּרְכֵי־אִישׁ זַךְ בְּעֵינָיו (“all the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes”) — but the LORD weighs the spirits.
  • 20:9 — מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי (“who can say, ‘I have cleansed my heart’?”).
  • 21:2 — כָּל־דֶּרֶךְ־אִישׁ יָשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו (“every man’s way is right in his own eyes”) — but the LORD weighs the hearts.

And the four are lexically joined. 16:2 and 21:2 are a near-doublet — two words changed [T] — and 20:9’s verb shares its root with 16:2’s adjective: זַךְ (“clean”) and זִכִּיתִי (“I have cleansed”). All four occurrences of that root in Proverbs fall inside this collection — 16:2; 20:9; 20:11; 21:8 [T]. The claim and the question are made of the same word.

This is the series FCF at its sharpest. The spine — בְּעֵינָיו (“in his own eyes”) — runs 3:7 → 12:15 → 16:2 → 21:2 and arrives at 20:9 [T]. The congregation comes wanting the two columns to place them. The collection will not do it.

Teaching Point

The moral order is real and God stands behind it — and the same collection that spends eleven chapters proving it tells you that you are not the one who can read where you stand in it.

Subject: Does the world reward character, and can I tell which side of that I am on? Complement: Yes it does, and no you cannot — because the weighing is the LORD’s.

Why this is one idea and not two. The two claims are the collection’s own two methods, and the second is unintelligible without the first. Eleven chapters of sorting are not undercut by 16:2; they are what gives 16:2 its force. Take away the sorting and “the LORD weighs the spirits” is a platitude; take away 16:2 and the sorting becomes a self-audit. The sermon needs both, in that order — and the text agrees, because it puts 14:12’s problem and 16:25’s reprint inside one paragraph.

Purpose Statement

To move the congregation from using Proverbs as a device for grading themselves and others to receiving it as a true description of a world they do not adjudicate — so that the confident lose the instrument they have been scoring themselves with, the anxious are relieved of a verdict they were never qualified to give, and both are handed to the one who does the weighing.

Note the direction of the relief. The weighing is moved off the hearer’s desk before anything is asked of him. That is relief before it is rebuke, and 21:1 extends it as far as it can go: even the king’s heart is water in that hand [T].

Differentiated:

  • For the capable — you have been reading the two columns and placing yourself in the right one. 21:2 says the instrument is broken, and it is your instrument.
  • For the anxious — you have been reading the two columns and placing yourself in the wrong one. The same verse takes the pen out of your hand too.
  • For the church — the two-column world is corporate: 11:10–11, where the city rejoices and is exalted. This is not a sermon about private morality.
  • For the enquirer — that character and outcome are connected is a claim anyone can test, and most people have found it broadly true and painfully uneven. The collection says both — 10:3 and 13:23 in the same book, eighty verses apart.
  • For the preacher — the collection has no teacher in it. The father has stopped speaking and the sages have not arrived. In these 375 verses you are one of the cases, not the voice.

Candidate Big Question

The series question, asked in these words in every sermon:

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s form, and its answer:

“Which column are you in — and who told you?”

Answer: You would want the sort to place you, and the collection breaks the sort from inside. “All the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirits.”

Flagged as candidate: final wording belongs to the manuscript stage.

Proposed structure — three movements

Following the transmitted paragraphs, not the chapter numbers and not the change of method.

Movement Text Function
1. The world sorted 10:2–13:25 (the first paragraph, 115 verses) Surfaces the FCF — and it must be preached as true. Antithesis at its densest anywhere in the book: 33.2% of these verses set opposed words across the caesura, and 41 of them put צַדִּיק and רָשָׁע inside a single verse [T]. The two columns, line after line. Do not hedge it here because the congregation knows life is untidy — the collection does that work itself in movement 2, and doing it early spoils both. One concession belongs here and only one: 13:23, the poor man’s harvest swept away for lack of justice — because it is in this paragraph and it teaches the genre in a single verse.
2. The crest 14:1–19:9 (the 162-verse paragraph), centred on 14:12 · 16:1–9 · 16:25 Establishes the TP, and the paragraph does the work. Three things happen inside this one transmitted unit. 14:12 breaks the instrument. Then the method changes without the text breaking: צַדִּיק disappears from chapter 16 entirely while the divine name reaches eleven verses there [T], and the recurring sentence becomes a man plans, YHWH decides — 16:1, 16:9, 16:33 [T]. Land on 16:2: the LORD weighs the spirits. Then 16:25 reprints 14:12, and the congregation hears the problem re-posed in the place where the answer stands. That is the sermon’s centre, and it is all one paragraph.
3. The auditor disqualified 19:10–22:16 (the last two paragraphs, 97 verses) Presses the PS. 20:9 — who can say, “I have cleansed my heart”? — asked in the vocabulary of 16:2, and 21:2, the last of the “in his own eyes” sayings [T]. Then three questions in one chapter (20:6, 20:9, 20:24) and no answers. The collection does not resolve into a technique. It stops at 22:16, on oppressing the poor to enrich oneself — reusing 14:31’s own phrase עֹשֵׁק דָּל and having just said at 22:2 that the LORD is the Maker of them all [T]. No closing frame, no summary. Let the sermon feel that it simply stops.

The Christ-landing

Contrast, and it is structural rather than verbal. There is no messianic verse here and reaching for one would be the error. The connection is the shape of the whole: a collection spends 375 verses connecting character to outcome and then says, three times, that no one can audit his own character. That is a gap the collection opens and does not close.

Two texts close it, and they answer different halves of the gap.

1 Corinthians 1:30 — “he became to us wisdom from God, and righteousness” [T], SBLGNT. Both halves of the collection’s problem in one clause: the wisdom it has been teaching, and the standing it has just told you that you cannot verify.

1 John 1:7 — τὸ αἷμα Ἰησοῦ … καθαρίζει ἡμᾶς ἀπὸ πάσης ἁμαρτίας (“the blood of Jesus … cleanses us from all sin”) [T], SBLGNT. This is the better landing for 20:9 specifically, because 20:9 asks its question with the verb cleanse and the New Testament answers with the same word. The question is: who can say “I have cleansed my heart”? The answer is not that someone can, but that someone else has done the cleansing.

Say it in the third movement, not the first. If Christ arrives before the auditor has been disqualified, he answers a question the congregation has not yet felt. The whole design is to make 20:9 land before either text is offered — ask it, leave a silence, then answer it from outside the book.

Keep honest

1 · Do not quote a whole-section antithesis average. Version 1 said this and was right; here are the reproducible figures. Opposition runs 33.2% in the first half and 5.2% in the second, with chapters 16, 19 and 20 at zero [T]. A single averaged figure is arithmetically true and homiletically false — the two halves are formally opposite, and the sermon exists to show the change.

2 · Do not make 15:33/16:1 the hinge — but do not pretend nothing happens there either. No marker falls there; the transmitted paragraph runs 14:1 → 19:9; the longest divine-name run in the book straddles it; and 16:25 reaches back across it [T]. Chapter 16 is a crest, not a boundary. And the honest addition: the method really does change, so a preacher who has felt a shift there is not wrong — he is wrong only if he makes it a wall.

3 · Do not preach the two columns and stop. Eleven chapters of sorting, then 16:2, 20:9 and 21:2. A sermon that ends at the sorting has ended one movement early, and will send people home grading themselves — which is the opposite of the purpose.

4 · Do not address the congregation in the second person throughout. There is no vocative in 375 verses except 19:27, and no imperative governs either half [T]. The descriptive register is a decision, not an accident: the collection describes a world; it does not issue commands. A sermon that turns every saying into an instruction has changed the genre.

5 · Teach the genre from 22:6, because it is the verse that most needs it. “Train up a child in the way he should go.” It stands nine verses from the end of this unit, among sayings about lending, wealth and the poor, with nothing about child-rearing around it [T]. Two problems at once: a מָשָׁל is a generalisation, not a covenant undertaking [S: overview, trap 1]; and עַל־פִּי דַרְכּוֹ (“according to his way”) is genuinely ambiguous — NIV84 footnotes “Or Start” [T]. The pastoral cost of preaching it as a promise is measured in parents who conclude God broke it.

6 · Do not quote “God loves a cheerful giver” as Proverbs. It is 2 Cor 9:7, and its nearest Old Testament ancestor is a Greek plus at Prov 22:8 with no Hebrew behind it [T], Swete. The Hebrew of 22:8 is a harvest saying about sowing iniquity. If the sermon can carry it, say so in a sentence — it is a good moment to show a congregation how the apostles read. The same discipline applies to 15:27a on almsgiving cleansing sin, which is also Greek only. (Version 1’s figure of “nineteen verse-units” without a Hebrew counterpart is inherited and has not been re-verified; do not quote the number.)

7 · Do not preach the royal verses as leadership principles. 16:10–15 tells someone who lives near power how to read it, and the collection’s own theological statement about kingship is that the king’s heart is water in another hand (21:1) [T]. What those verses do for a congregation is relativise every authority they live under.

Anti-moralism gate

Test Result
“Be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? Passes. The design removes the hearer’s capacity to grade himself before anything is offered, so “be more righteous” is foreclosed by movement 3. And there is no X: 375 verses contain one proper noun, Solomon at 10:1, plus the divine name [T].
Is Christ the subject, or an example? Passes. 1 Cor 1:30 and 1 Jn 1:7 both make him the actor — he became to us wisdom and righteousness; his blood cleanses. The collection’s own actor in the second half is likewise God: seven sentences give him the second colon and the reader the first.
Grounded in Christ’s saving work? Passes. Righteousness received rather than assessed, and a cleansing performed rather than claimed — which is precisely the gap 20:9 opens.

Passes. The specific risk here is the reverse of moralism’s usual form: not “try harder” but “score yourself accurately”. Movement 3 exists to close that off.

Pulpit-text check

Pulpit text: NIV84. Study text NASB95. The plan’s load-bearing wording survives in the pulpit text, including the two places it most needed to.

What it carries NIV84
The weld (14:12 ‖ 16:25) Carried, and identically at both: “There is a way that seems right to a man, but in the end it leads to death.” The congregation will hear the repetition without being told. (It loses the internal repetition of דֶּרֶךְ — the Hebrew has “way” twice, “its end is the ways of death” — which NASB95 keeps. Minor, and only worth a word if the sermon traces it.)
The spine (3:7 → 12:15 → 16:2 ‖ 21:2) Carried across all four: “Do not be wise in your own eyes” (3:7); “the way of a fool seems right to him” (12:15); “All a man’s ways seem innocent to him” (16:2); “All a man’s ways seem right to him” (21:2). NIV84 supplies “seem”, which the Hebrew does not have — it says the ways are clean in his own eyes. NASB95 is closer, and the difference is worth one sentence because the flatness of the Hebrew is the point
The question (20:9) Carried as a question: “Who can say, ‘I have kept my heart pure; I am clean and without sin’?” The movement-3 landing is safe
16:2 ‖ 21:2 as a pair Recognisable, with one loss. Both versions render רוּחוֹת (“spirits”) at 16:2 as “motives”, which hides the spirits / hearts pairing with 21:2. Say the Hebrew has “spirits” at the first and “hearts” at the second
The counter-case (13:23) Softened. NIV84: “A poor man’s field may produce abundant food, but injustice sweeps it away.” The Hebrew has no “may” — NASB95’s “Abundant food is in the fallow ground of the poor” is flatter and harder. Since this verse is doing the genre-teaching in movement 1, read it from NASB95 or say what the Hebrew has
The corporate note (11:10–11) Carried well: “When the righteous prosper, the city rejoices… Through the blessing of the upright a city is exalted”
22:6 Footnoted — “Or Start”. The ambiguity the sermon needs is on the page, which is more than most versions give

The one substantive loss is at the six תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה verses of the first half, where NIV84 renders a verbless classification (“a false balance is an abomination to the LORD”) as a statement about God’s feelings (“the LORD detests dishonest scales”) [T]. If movement 1 uses the abomination sayings, one sentence restores it: the Hebrew says the thing is an abomination — the same category Deuteronomy puts idols in — not that God feels one.

Provenance and hand-off

Every element is drawn from the three runs of 18 September 2026: the FCF from the two halves’ candidate FCFs converging; the TP from the umbrella’s thesis and both halves’ Headline Findings; the PS from both So What sections; the structure from the transmitted paragraphs recorded in the first half’s Tool 3 and confirmed in the second; the keep-honest items from the three reports’ Preaching Pitfalls, with item 1 restated on reproducible figures and items 6 and 7 new. The Christ-landing’s 1 Jn 1:7 is the second half’s [T]; 1 Cor 1:30 is carried over from version 1 [S].

Received from sermon 4 (chapters 1–9): a reader who has been shown that the two invitations sound identical and given no way to tell them apart. This collection is the instrument he was promised — and the sermon’s whole movement is that the instrument does not work on its owner.

Hands forward to sermon 6 (22:17–24:34): the boundary. 22:17 is not a superscription in either Hebrew or Greek — it reads “incline your ear and hear the words of the wise”, an imperative with the phrase as its object [T]. The collection ends where the form changes: the vocative and the imperative return. Say it in one line at the close, and sermon 6 begins with its text already opened.

Hands forward to sermon 7 (25:1–29:27): on the measure of whole-verse doublets, the Hezekiah collection shares four with this unit’s second half and none with its first [T]. Worth knowing when that sermon asks what kind of material it is handling.
