# Claim Audit: Proverbs — "Right in His Own Eyes"

**Passages audited:** Proverbs 12:15; 14:12; 16:2; 16:25; 21:1–3; 29:18 — against Judges 17:6; 21:25 and Deuteronomy 12:8, with a supporting survey of Proverbs' wider Deuteronomic borrowing.

**Date:** 3 September 2026

**Purpose:** Testing the proposal that Proverbs' uses of *yāšār bə‘ênāyw* ("right in his own eyes") allude to, or quote, the refrain of Judges, and that the author of Proverbs therefore has the pre-monarchic period in view. The audit extends into the resulting question of Proverbs' relationship to Deuteronomy.

**Mode:** Claim Audit (Dig Deeper Phase 0.5b). Text-first; secondary sources quarantined to the checking stage.

**Transliteration note:** Hebrew is given in transliteration throughout for font portability.

***

## Claim 1: Proverbs 12:15 and 21:2 allude to Judges 17:6 and 21:25

**Source:** User proposal — first-glance impression that the author of Proverbs may have the days of the judges in mind.

**Claim:** The occurrences of *yāšār bə‘ênāyw* in Proverbs 12:15 and 21:2 constitute a verbal allusion to, or quotation of, the refrain at Judges 17:6 and 21:25, importing the pre-monarchic setting into the proverbs.

### The data

| Text | Hebrew (transliterated) | Rendering |
|---|---|---|
| Deut 12:8 | *îš kol-hayyāšār bə‘ênāyw* | everyone doing whatever is right in his own eyes |
| Judg 17:6; 21:25 | *îš hayyāšār bə‘ênāyw ya‘ăśeh* | every man did what was right in his own eyes |
| Judg 14:3 | *kî-hî' yāšərāh bə‘ênāy* | she is right in my eyes |
| Prov 12:15 | *derek 'ĕwîl yāšār bə‘ênāyw* | the way of a fool is right in his own eyes |
| Prov 21:2 | *kol-derek-îš yāšār bə‘ênāyw* | every way of a man is right in his own eyes |

### Hays criteria

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Possible | Granted in principle. But direction of dependence is not demonstrable from a two-word idiom, and nothing in either text marks a citation. |
| Volume | **Weak** | The shared material is *yāšār* + *bə‘ênāyw*, plus *îš*. Judges has a finite verb (*ya‘ăśeh*) and a relative construction; Proverbs has a nominal clause whose grammatical subject is *derek* — the book's own signature noun, wholly absent from the Judges refrain. |
| Recurrence | **Weak** | Proverbs alludes to Judges nowhere else: no Judges names, episodes, vocabulary or narrative shapes anywhere in the book. Judges is a cold source in the clustering sense — no foothold to raise the prior. |
| Thematic coherence | **Weak** | Judges: absent central authority produces national anarchy. Proverbs: individual self-assessment corrected by counsel (12:15b) or by YHWH (21:2b). Corporate-political versus individual-epistemological. |
| Historical plausibility | Possible | Both Israelite, both share the idiom. But shared idiom is not evidence of allusion. |
| History of interpretation | Possible | Commentators routinely note the phrase parallel; I am not aware of any who argue literary dependence. *Uncertain — flagged for Logos verification.* |
| Satisfaction | **Weak** | Positing the allusion illuminates neither verse. Both read fully and naturally without it. |

**Verdict: Discard** (as a direct allusion). Reframe as shared idiom — see Claim 2.

### Reasoning

Three findings converge against the claim.

**(a) The idiom is ordinary Hebrew, not a distinctive marker.** *Yāšār bə‘ênê* + a person is standard evaluative construction across the canon: "the thing was right in the eyes of Absalom" (2 Sam 17:4); "wherever it seems right to you" (Jer 40:4–5); the whole regnal formula of Kings, "he did what was right in the eyes of the LORD." Tool 11's test — *would this phrase occur to anyone without the source background?* — is failed decisively. It would. It repeatedly does. `[T]`

**(b) Proverbs 21:2 is generated internally.** It is a variant of Proverbs 16:2, not an import from anywhere:

> 16:2 — *kol-darkê-îš zak bə‘ênāyw, wə-tōkēn rûḥôt YHWH*
> 21:2 — *kol-derek-îš yāšār bə‘ênāyw, wə-tōkēn libbôt YHWH*

One proverb, two substitutions: *zak* → *yāšār* in the A-line, *rûḥôt* → *libbôt* in the B-line. This is Move 4 (internal echo) returning a positive, and it removes the need for any external source for the phrase at 21:2. `[T]` *High confidence.*

**(c) The phrase belongs to a book-wide series.** Proverbs cycles a single formula — *X in his own eyes* — substituting the adjective at will: *wise* in his own eyes (3:7; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11); *pure* in his own eyes (30:12); *right* in his own eyes (12:15; 21:2). It also varies the preposition: "there is a way that seems right *before* a man" — *yāšār lipnê-îš* — at 14:12 and 16:25. A book that generates this many variations of its own formula does not need to have borrowed one of them. `[T]`

**Action required:** None to any existing document. Record the verdict so the claim is not re-raised.

## Claim 2: The shared phrase derives from Deuteronomy 12:8

**Source:** Audit-generated, in the course of testing Claim 1.

**Claim:** Both Judges and Proverbs draw the phrase from Deuteronomy 12:8, which is its canonical origin.

### Hays criteria (Judges ← Deuteronomy)

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | Deuteronomy is antecedent to the events Judges narrates and to its composition. |
| Volume | **Strong** | *îš* + *hayyāšār bə‘ênāyw* shared verbatim, with the verb *‘āśāh* in both (Deut: *lō' ta‘ăśûn*; Judg: *ya‘ăśeh*). |
| Recurrence | **Strong** | Judges is saturated with Deuteronomic covenant logic — the cycle of apostasy and deliverance (2:11–23) is Deuteronomy 28 in narrative form. |
| Thematic coherence | **Strong** | Deut 12 legislates the central sanctuary; Judges 17–21 narrates cultic chaos, private shrines, a stolen ephod and an itinerant Levite. The refrain lands exactly where Deuteronomy 12 has been broken. |
| Historical plausibility | Strong | — |
| History of interpretation | Strong | Widely recognised. |
| Satisfaction | **Strong** | The allusion is the interpretive key to Judges 17–21. |

**Verdict (Judges ← Deuteronomy): Confirmed.** *High confidence.* `[T]`

### Verdict (Proverbs ← Deuteronomy 12:8): Needs reframing

Proverbs shows none of Deuteronomy 12's freight: no *‘āśāh*, no cultic frame, no covenant register, no place-that-the-LORD-will-choose. It is using the common idiom evidenced at 2 Sam 17:4 and Jer 40:4–5, not activating a specific text. `[T]`

### Reasoning

This is the audit's central structural finding, and it reframes the whole question.

In Judges the phrase is **a legal citation being violated**. Deuteronomy 12:8 states the prohibition; Judges 17:6 and 21:25 report the prohibition in force as description. That is precisely why the refrain bites — the narrator is not observing disorder, he is naming a specific covenant breach in the words of the law that forbids it.

In Proverbs the phrase is **a neutral idiom of self-assessment**, carrying no prohibition and no covenant frame, and standing in a proverb whose corrective is counsel or divine weighing.

The two uses are therefore not related as source and allusion but as **collateral descendants**. Judges quotes Deuteronomy 12:8 directly and pointedly. Proverbs uses an idiom which Deuteronomy also happens to use. Same distant ancestor; no contact between the cousins. `[I]` *High confidence.*

**Action required:** Where the phrase is discussed, present it as a canonical triad (see Convergent Findings) rather than as a chain of dependence.

## Claim 3: Proverbs 21:1–3 supports the pre-monarchic reading

**Source:** Audit-generated — identified as the strongest surface datum available to the user's proposal.

**Claim:** The juxtaposition of a king saying (21:1) with *yāšār bə‘ênāyw* (21:2) reproduces the Judges pairing of "no king in Israel" with the refrain, and so supports the allusion.

**Verdict: Needs reframing.** The adjacency is real and worth noticing; examined, the evidence runs the other way. *Moderate confidence.* `[I]`

### Reasoning

The unit reads:

> 1 The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will.
> 2 Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the heart.
> 3 To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice.

*Lēb* ("heart") stitches v. 1 to v. 2: the king's heart is in YHWH's hand, and YHWH weighs hearts. The "man" of v. 2 therefore **includes the king** rather than standing over against him. `[T]`

Where Judges holds that the absence of a king produces the problem, Proverbs places the king inside the problem, under the same weighing as everyone else. Verse 3 completes the move by relativising sacrifice — that is, the sanctuary — against righteousness and justice.

So Proverbs 21:1–3 subordinates **both** remedies that Deuteronomy 12 and Judges 17–21 respectively propose: the place (v. 3) and the king (v. 1). Far from importing the Judges frame, it declines it.

**Action required:** Retain 21:1–3 as evidence — but as evidence *against* the proposal and *for* the canonical triad.

## Claim 4: Proverbs 29:18 is the better candidate for a "days of the judges" echo

**Source:** Audit-generated during independent candidate-finding (Tool 11, pre-Move 1).

**Claim:** "Where there is no vision the people cast off restraint; but blessed is he who keeps the law" (29:18) reproduces the *shape* of the Judges refrain — absence of a governing centre producing social dissolution — and is a stronger candidate than 12:15 or 21:2.

### Hays criteria (29:18 ← Judges)

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Possible | — |
| Volume | **Weak** | Not one shared word. *Ḥāzôn* is not *melek*; *yippāra‘* is not *yāšār bə‘ênāyw*. |
| Recurrence | Weak | As Claim 1 — Judges has no foothold in Proverbs. |
| Thematic coherence | Possible | The shape genuinely matches: absence of X, then dissolution. |
| Historical plausibility | Possible | — |
| History of interpretation | Weak | Not, to my knowledge, proposed. |
| Satisfaction | Possible | The shape-match is real but adds little. |

**Verdict (29:18 ← Judges): Discard.**

### Hays criteria (29:18 ← Exodus 32:25)

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | — |
| Volume | **Possible–Strong** | The verb *pāra‘* in this sense is rare. Exod 32:25 twice: "the people had broken loose (*pāru‘a*) … for Aaron had let them break loose." Prov 29:18: *yippāra‘ ‘ām* — the same verb with *‘ām* as subject. |
| Recurrence | Possible | Proverbs' Torah engagement is demonstrably live (Claim 6), though concentrated in Deuteronomy rather than Exodus. |
| Thematic coherence | **Strong** | Exod 32: the leader is absent on the mountain, revelation is in transit, and the people break loose. Prov 29:18: revelation is absent, and the people break loose. The logic matches, not merely the shape. |
| Historical plausibility | Strong | — |
| History of interpretation | Uncertain | *Flagged for Logos verification.* |
| Satisfaction | **Strong** | It explains why the remedy in the B-line is *tôrāh* — the very thing Moses was receiving while the people broke loose. |

**Verdict (29:18 ← Exodus 32:25): Confirmed with nuance.** *Moderate confidence* — the verbal link is distinctive but single-word, and the B-line coherence is doing much of the work. `[I]`

### Reasoning

The user's instinct that Proverbs somewhere reflects on ungoverned Israel is sound; it was aimed at the wrong verse and the wrong source. 29:18 sits in a chapter dense with king sayings (29:2, 4, 14) — and conspicuously does **not** name the king as the remedy. Its answer is *ḥāzôn* and *tôrāh*: prophecy and law, that is, revelation.

**Action required:** Add to the intertextual map at moderate confidence, flagged for verification.

## Claim 5: Proverbs' literary habit excludes unmarked historical allusion

**Source:** Audit-generated, applying the user's established control (the Lukan naming-habit test).

**Claim:** Proverbs' demonstrated practice is to name historical particulars only in superscriptions and never to narrate or allude to Israel's national story; an unmarked allusion to a specific historical era would therefore be anomalous for this author.

Hays criteria are not the right instrument here — this is a claim about authorial habit, not about a specific echo. The evidence table below substitutes.

| Evidence | Warrant | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Superscriptions | `[T]` | 1:1; 10:1; 25:1 ("the men of Hezekiah king of Judah"); 30:1; 31:1. Every historical particular in the book sits here. |
| National narrative | `[I]` | No exodus, no Sinai, no wilderness, no conquest, no judges, no David-as-narrative, no exile anywhere in the book. |
| Named Israelites outside superscriptions | `[I]` | None. |
| Creation material | `[T]` | 3:19–20; 8:22–31; 30:4 — the book *does* reach into Genesis 1, but for cosmology, not for story. |

**Verdict: Confirmed.** *High confidence.*

### Reasoning

This satisfies the admissibility gate for a pattern-break absence argument: the pattern can be stated and its instances cited. Proverbs confines historical particularity to its editorial headings. An allusion to the pre-monarchic period would have to operate entirely unmarked, in a book that never operates that way.

This is the same control that demoted the Archelaus allusion in the Luke 19 report: where the author's own demonstrated practice weighs against an inference, the inference is demoted regardless of its plausibility from outside the text.

**Action required:** Retain as a standing control on any future proposal of narrative allusion in Proverbs.

## Claim 6: Deuteronomy is a live source for Proverbs

**Source:** Audit-generated. Raised because the clustering principle requires the book's live sources to be established before any candidate is weighed.

**Claim:** Deuteronomy is demonstrably a live source for Proverbs — at least two clear allusions elsewhere in the book — which raises the prior for Deuteronomic material and correspondingly isolates Judges as cold.

### The evidence

| Proverbs | Deuteronomy | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| 6:20–22 — bind them on your heart; when you walk / lie down / awake | 6:6–9 — when you walk by the way / lie down / rise; bind as a sign | **High** — same triad, same order, plus the binding image |
| 22:28; 23:10 — do not move the ancient landmark your fathers set | 19:14 — do not move your neighbour's landmark, set by the men of old | **High** — near-verbatim; cf. the curse at 27:17 |
| 11:1; 16:11; 20:10, 23 — a false balance is an abomination to the LORD | 25:13–16 — two kinds of weights … an abomination to the LORD your God | **High** — shared formula, identical offence |
| 17:15 — justifying the wicked and condemning the righteous | 25:1 — justifying the righteous and condemning the wicked | **High** — verbatim pair, inverted |
| 30:5–6 — do not add to his words, lest he rebuke you | 4:2; 12:32 — you shall not add to the word I command you | **High** — the canon formula (30:5a fuses Ps 18:30) |
| 3:11–12 — do not despise the LORD's discipline; as a father the son | 8:5 — as a man disciplines his son, so the LORD disciplines you | Moderate–high — shared root *ysr* |
| 3:1–3 — keep my commandments; bind on your neck; write on the heart | 6:6–8; 11:18 | Moderate–high |
| 2:21–22 — the upright dwell in the land; the wicked cut off from it | 4:26; 11:8–9; 28:63; 30:18 — land-tenure formula | Moderate (Ps 37:9, 22, 29 competes) |
| 3:9 — honour the LORD with the firstfruits of your produce | 18:4; 26:1–11 | Moderate — rare cultic vocabulary for Proverbs |
| 20:22; 24:29 — do not say "I will repay evil" | 32:35 — vengeance is mine, I will repay | Moderate |

**Verdict: Confirmed.** *High confidence* on the cluster as a whole; individual rows weighted as shown. `[T]` for the verbal correspondences; `[I]` for the dependence claim.

### Reasoning

Note the concentration: **Proverbs 11:1 and 17:15 draw on two adjacent laws within a single short stretch of Deuteronomy 25**, and both carry the *tô‘ēbat YHWH* formula. That is Move 2 behaviour — sustained conversation with one source, following its own local sequence — not scattered coincidence. The *abomination to the LORD* formula itself recurs roughly a dozen times across Proverbs and is heavily Deuteronomic in flavour.

### Note on direction of dependence

Weinfeld's influential contention runs the other way: that Deuteronomy is a seventh-century product of scribal-wisdom circles, and that its motive clauses, didactic address and humanitarian reasoning are borrowed *from* the wisdom tradition.

Two things travel together in that work and should be separated.

**The thesis is declined here.** It is a reconstruction standing against what the book claims for itself: Moses wrote this law and delivered it to the priests (Deut 31:9, 24), the whole framed as his address beyond the Jordan (1:1, 5). The editorial work the text itself evidences is of a different and modest order — the death notice (34:1–12), the "unto this day" glosses (2:22; 3:14; 34:6), the parenthesis at 2:10–12 — consistent with light preparation for later readers, not with wholesale scribal authorship. `[T]` for the self-attestation; `[I]` for the scope of editorial work.

**The affinity data survives** — the motive clauses, the "that it may go well with you" rationale, the second-person didactic address. These are observable features, gathered to serve a thesis but not created by it.

What changes is the explanation. Under Mosaic authorship the affinity has a better account than Weinfeld's: **Deuteronomy is sermonic catechesis by design** — Moses preaching law to a congregation, not promulgating a code. Preached law and instructed wisdom share a register because they share a function. The affinity is genre-level, not origin-level.

This settles the direction question. With Deuteronomy Mosaic, and the Proverbs collections Solomonic with Hezekian editing (10:1; 25:1), priority is not in doubt: Deuteronomy is the source, Proverbs the borrower.

### Competing third parties (recorded, not resolved)

- The boundary-stone saying has a close analogue in the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*, and sits precisely within 22:17–24:22 — the section with the acknowledged Egyptian relationship. That row has a double genealogy.
- Honest weights also appear at Lev 19:35–36.
- Psalm 37 competes for the land formula at 2:21–22.

**Action required:** Add Deuteronomy to the intertextual map as a confirmed live source; record Judges as tested and cold.

## Claim 7: Proverbs' Deuteronomic borrowing is de-indexed

**Source:** Audit-generated. This is the mechanism that explains why Claim 6 does not rescue Claim 1.

**Claim:** Proverbs systematically strips the deictic and land-specific elements from the Deuteronomic material it takes, retaining only the portable core. Deuteronomy 12:8 cannot survive that operation, because its content *is* its indexing.

### The evidence — the portability test

| Deuteronomy retains | Proverbs keeps | Proverbs drops |
|---|---|---|
| 19:14 — landmark; the men of old; *in the inheritance you will hold in the land the LORD your God is giving you to possess* | landmark; the fathers who set it (22:28) | the entire land-grant clause |
| 25:15 — honest weights; *that your days may be long in the land the LORD your God is giving you* | the formula: false balance an abomination, just weight his delight (11:1) | the land-tenure motive clause |
| 6:7–9 — sit / walk / lie down / rise; doorposts; gates; *these words I command you this day* | walk, lie down, awake — three of four, in order (6:22) | doorposts, gates, "this day" |

**Verdict: Confirmed.** *High confidence.* `[T]` for the textual comparison; `[I]` for the generalisation.

### Reasoning

The operation is consistent across every high-weight row in Claim 6: strip the deictics — *this day*, *the land the LORD your God is giving you*, *when you cross over* — and keep the transferable core.

Now apply the same test to Deuteronomy 12:8. What is *îš kol-hayyāšār bə‘ênāyw* doing in its own sentence? **It is not the law.** The law is "you shall not do according to all that we are doing here today." The phrase is the *descriptive clause characterising the present disordered moment*, and the following verse says why the moment is disordered: "for you have not as yet come to the rest and to the inheritance that the LORD your God is giving you" (12:9).

The whole content of the phrase is its indexing. It names a transitional condition — on this side of the Jordan, before the place the LORD will choose. De-index it and nothing remains to import.

Which yields the sharpest available statement of the Claim 1 verdict:

> An allusion detectable only after every distinguishing feature has been stripped is not detectable. Once "everyone doing what is right in his own eyes" has been de-indexed into "every man's way is right in his own eyes," it has become the ordinary idiom of 2 Sam 17:4 and Jer 40:4 — and there is no longer any evidence of a channel, because the operation that would have opened it has erased the evidence.

### The constructive payoff

The de-indexing habit is not a defect. It is obedience.

Deuteronomy 6:6 commands that these words be **on your heart**, and then taught to the son, in the house and on the road. Internalised law is by definition portable law: it goes where the son goes. Proverbs 6:20–22 does not merely echo Deuteronomy 6:6–9 — it exhibits the **result**: the commandment of a father and the *tôrāh* of a mother, bound on the heart, now leading, keeping and speaking as the son walks, lies down and wakes.

Proverbs is Deuteronomy 6 performed. That is why it takes Deuteronomy's law and catechesis and leaves its history: it stands at the point in Deuteronomy 6:7 where the son is being taught at home, and it supplies the curriculum. `[I]` *Moderate–high confidence.*

**Action required:** Promote to a standing principle — *Proverbs' relationship to the Torah is legal and catechetical, never historical.* This bears on any future proposal of narrative allusion in Proverbs, well beyond the present question.

## Convergent Findings

**1. Wherever Proverbs approaches the theme of ungoverned life, the remedy is never a king.**

| Text | Diagnosis | Remedy offered |
|---|---|---|
| 12:15 | the fool's way is right in his own eyes | listening to counsel |
| 21:1–3 | every man's way is right in his own eyes | the LORD who weighs the heart — with the king himself under the weighing |
| 29:18 | the people cast off restraint | *ḥāzôn* and *tôrāh* — revelation |

Three passages, three tools (Vocabulary, Structure, Quotation/Allusion), one convergent result. This is the opposite of the Judges refrain's implied politics, and it is the strongest single piece of evidence against the proposal — as well as being independently preachable.

**2. The canonical triad.** The user's instinct that these texts belong together was right; the mechanism was wrong. One diagnosis, three answers:

- **Deuteronomy 12:8** — every man right in his own eyes, *because there is not yet a place*. The remedy is **the place the LORD will choose**.
- **Judges 17:6; 21:25** — every man right in his own eyes, *because there is no king*. The remedy is **a king**.
- **Proverbs 12:15; 21:2** — every man's way right in his own eyes, with no shortage of either place or king. The remedy is **counsel, and the LORD who weighs the heart**.

Read this way, Proverbs 21:1–3 ceases to be awkward for the sequence and becomes its resolution: the king relativised in v. 1, the place relativised in v. 3, the diagnosis standing between them. It is the answer that outlives the loss of both temple and throne.

This is not an allusion. It is a canonical conversation — and it is richer material than the allusion would have been.

**3. Proverbs' Torah relationship is legal and catechetical, never historical.** Claims 5, 6 and 7 converge here from three directions: the absence of national narrative, the presence of a dense legal-catechetical borrowing cluster, and the de-indexing mechanism that explains both.
