Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

  1. Every block boundary the Greek rearrangement creates falls at a point where the received Hebrew marks a paragraph — five seams, five markers. [T] on both sides. Surfaced by: Structure, Translations, Textual Variants, and four prior runs’ marker data. ✅ Verified 20 August against Rahlfs, Septuaginta (see §Textual Variants). The Greek partitions Proverbs into seven blocks — 1:1–24:22 · 30:1–14 · 24:23–34 · 30:15–33 + 31:1–9 · 25:1–29:27 · 31:10–31 — and the MT carries a marker at every boundary: a setumah at 24:22 and petuchot at 30:14, 24:34, 31:9 and 29:27. (A sixth marker, at 30:33, falls at the internal joint of the moved block, where both traditions agree anyway.) The two traditions inherited the same inventory of division points and arranged the blocks differently. High confidence — the Greek order was predicted before the text was seen and matches exactly, block for block.

  2. The paragraphing-density gradient closes here, at its steepest point — and the sixth reading confirms that the markers track genre. [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Genre, Repetition. Nine markers in forty-two verses is one per 4.7, against one per 184 in Solomon 1A — a thirty-nine-fold spread across six sections, monotonic, and correlating exactly with how discrete the material’s units are. High confidence.

  3. The received paragraphing runs 30:1–6 unbroken and breaks at 30:6/30:7 — which is textual evidence against the standard two-source reading of 30:1–9. [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Author’s Purpose, checking stage. The reading that makes 30:1–4 a sceptic’s manifesto and 30:5–6 an orthodox answer requires a seam at 30:4/30:5. The transmitted text puts none there; it groups the unanswerable questions with the answer and separates the prayer. [S] Ansberry, Franklyn, Moore, Gunneweg and Waltke argue the unity on other grounds; this is a datum none of them uses. High confidence on the marker; moderate–high on its evidential weight.

  4. The section’s subject is appetite, and it is carried by three interlocking Hebrew roots the English cannot show as one. [T] Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Original Languages, Structure. √ʾKL “eat/devour” runs 30:14 → 30:17 → 30:20 (devouring the poor, being devoured by ravens, eating and denying), one occurrence per paragraph in three consecutive paragraphs; √ŚBʿ “be satisfied” runs 30:9, 15, 16, 22; and the whole thing terminates in Lemuel’s four verses on drinking (31:4–7). High confidence.

  5. The Greek makes Agur affirm what the Hebrew has him deny — and the negation is the whole point. [T] Surfaced by: Move 4, Vocabulary, Translations. MT 30:3: “I have not learned wisdom, nor have I knowledge of the Holy One.” LXX 30:3: θεὸς δεδίδαχέν με σοφίαν, καὶ γνῶσιν ἁγίων ἔγνωκα — “God has taught me wisdom, and I know the knowledge of the holy ones.” A straight polarity flip on both cola. In the Hebrew, Agur denies three of the four terms of 9:10; in the Greek he claims two of them. The book’s closing confession of ignorance exists only in the Hebrew. High confidence on the divergence; see Textual Variants for the triage.

  6. Agur ends with a hand on the mouth; Lemuel begins by being told twice to open his. The reversal sits exactly on a petuchah. [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Linking Words, Move 4. yād lə-peh (30:32) — פ — pətaḥ pîkā … pətaḥ pîkā (31:8, 9). The book’s whole speech-ethic, overwhelmingly restraint across chapters 10–29, turns over at a marked seam, and the thing that unlocks the mouth is somebody else’s cause. High confidence.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level. Proverbs exists to form a pethî into someone fit to be trusted — with property, with speech, with a household, and finally with rule — under the fear of the LORD (1:2–7). The book-overview’s microscript stands.

Passage level. These forty-two verses do the one thing the preceding twenty-nine chapters could not do for themselves: they say what must be said to a reader who has finished the course. The book has manufactured competence; 30:1–31:9 supplies the two things competence cannot supply — a true estimate of oneself, and a commission that points the competence outward. [I] High confidence; it is the reading the two superscriptions, the placement and the Positional Necessity Check converge on.

Three formal facts carry it, and they are the first things to notice about the section as a whole:

  • Two superscriptions in forty-two verses (30:1; 31:1), both attributing the material to someone who is not Solomon, both using maśśāʾ, both opening dibrê. [T] After 22:17’s “words of the wise” and 24:23’s “these also”, this is the book’s third and fourth change of voice — and its most complete, since these two speakers are named individuals with no stated connection to Israel’s court.
  • The first-person singular dominates the opening, and nowhere else in Proverbs does a named sage talk about himself for three verses. [T]
  • The section ends in the second-person imperative addressed to a king. [T] The book’s last instruction is not general.

Book-overview note: the overview reads the ending as the book’s “hermeneutical envelope” closing. This run confirms that from the paragraphing and the vocabulary rather than from the shape of the argument, which is a different kind of evidence and worth having.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 28:1–29:27 has subordinated the ruler to tôrāh — the run on that section found the absolute tôrāh five times in fifty-five verses against none in 25–27 — and closed at 29:27 on a petuchah. Behind it stand roughly nine hundred sayings and four sub-collections, plus the Words of the Wise, whose own last unit (24:30–34) shows the teacher himself still learning: “I saw and considered it; I looked and received instruction” (ʾāšît libbî, 24:32). The reader has been trained, and has been shown a trainer who is still a pupil. [T]

Necessity answer — and the text supplies a sharper one than “humility before power”. Read the two blocks in sequence and the join is specific:

30:21–23 “Under three things the earth trembles… a slave when he becomes king” — the first item on the list of what the earth cannot bear is an unformed person receiving rule.
30:32 “If you have been foolish, exalting yourself… put your hand on your mouth.” — the last thing Agur says.
פ The paragraph closes.
31:1–9 A king is instructed. He is told twice to open his mouth.

This passage exists here because the next thing that happens to the reader is a coronation. [T] on the sequence and the markers; [I] on the reading. Proverbs does not hand over power until it has named the promotion of the unformed as the thing the earth cannot bear, and told the reader to shut his mouth about himself. Only then is the mouth opened, and for someone else. The order is not decorative: 31:8 would be intolerable if 30:32 had not come first. High confidence on the sequence; moderate–high on the argument.

Implication for this passage. A sermon on 31:8–9 — “speak up for those who have no voice” — preached without 30:32 preaches the second half of a two-part instruction. And a sermon on 30:2–3 preached without 31:8–9 leaves the reader in a humility that never becomes useful to anybody. The two are one movement across one marker.

The general contextual notes

Immediately before: 29:27, the last line of the last Solomonic collection — “An unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, but one whose way is straight is an abomination to the wicked” — followed by a petuchah. [T]

Immediately after: 31:10, the acrostic. The received text closes 31:9 with a petuchah, so the paragraphing marks 31:1–9 as a discrete unit, which is exactly the scope this run was given. [T] It does not follow that 31:1–31 is not also a unit at a higher level (see the checking stage on Ansberry’s diptych); a section may contain paragraphs.

Section within book: the two closing compositions, standing outside the four Solomonic sentence collections and outside the two “words of the wise” appendices. Together with 1:1–9:18 they are the only extended first-person material in the book.

Commonly read out of context: 30:8–9 (“give me neither poverty nor riches”) is read as a general commendation of the middle-class mean. In context it is a prayer, with two theological reasons attached, offered by a man who has just said he knows nothing; the moderation is instrumental, not an ideal.

This is the first section in the project where the received paragraphing and the modern content-division coincide throughout. [T] In 10:1–15:33 a single marker covered 184 verses; in 16:1–22:16 a Masoretic paragraph ran 14:1 → 19:9, ninety-one verses, straight across the seam every modern scholar draws at 15:33/16:1. Here every marker falls where a reader would put one anyway.

Three of the divisions are non-obvious and repay attention, because in each case the marker binds material that commentators routinely separate:

  • 30:10 belongs with 30:11–14, and the link is lexical. 30:10 ends pen-yəqalleləkā — “lest he curse you”; 30:11 opens dôr ʾābîw yəqallēl — “a generation that curses its father.” √QLL, twice, across the verse join, and nowhere else in the section. [T] The stray-verse problem at 30:10 is a problem the transmitted text does not have.
  • 30:17 belongs with 30:15–16, and 30:20 with 30:18–19. In both cases a single appended verse follows a three-and-four saying, and in both the appendix is a sting in the tail: the insatiable devourers (15–16) are followed by the mocker who is devoured by ravens (17); the four unfathomable ways (18–19) are followed by the way of the adulteress (20). The Masoretes read them as attached; the kēn opening 30:20 (“so is the way of an adulteress”) proves it for the second. [T] High.
  • 30:32–33 belongs with 30:29–31. The stately four culminate in “a king whose army is with him” (31), and are answered immediately by “if you have been foolish, exalting yourself” (32). The paragraph is about bearing, and its last word is about how bearing goes wrong. [T]

The device

Not a chiasm and not a linear argument — a two-panel structure with an internal turn. [T]/[I]

30:1–9 First person throughout. Confession → questions → the given word → prayer. The sage empties himself and receives.
30:10–33 Second and third person. Observation, counting, the world’s order. The emptied sage can teach again.
31:1–9 Second person, imperative, to a named king. The teaching is handed to power.

The middle panel is built on a single form (the numerical saying) used seven times — twice with “two”, once with an unnumbered four, four times with “three… four”. The count is worth stating precisely because the form’s variation is where the meaning sits (see Tool 5).

4. Linking Words

Proverbs 30–31 is not argument-shaped, but it has one connector doing real structural work.

פֶּן pen, “lest” — five occurrences, and they are the section’s spine. [T]

Verse Prohibition or petition Pen-clause
30:6 Do not add to his words lest he rebuke you and you be found a liar
30:9a (from the petition of v.8) lest I be full and deny you
30:9b or lest I be poor and steal
30:10 Do not slander a servant lest he curse you
31:5 (from the prohibition of v.4) lest they drink and forget what has been decreed

Every imperative in the section that carries a motive carries it as a pen-clause, and every pen-clause names a consequence that falls on the speaker’s own relation to God or to another person — being found a liar, denying God, profaning the name, incurring a curse, perverting judgement. [T] There is no pen-clause about loss of money, health or reputation. Moderate–high; the pattern is complete within the section.

Two other connectors matter:

  • כִּי kî at 30:33 — the section’s only causal “for” at the head of a saying: “For pressing milk produces curds…” It grounds 30:32’s “hand on your mouth” in a physical law. [T] The argument is: shut your mouth because pressure has products, and one of them is blood.
  • וְ + jussive at 31:7 — “let them drink and forget their poverty” is jussive, not imperative; the mother is not commanding the king to distribute liquor but distinguishing the ruler who must remember (31:5) from the sufferer who may forget. [T] This is the interpretive key to 31:6–7 and it is easy to lose in English.

Two pattern-breaks, and both are meaningful [T]:

✅ Corroborated by the Septuagint, 20 August. LXX 30:15 gives the leech τρεῖς θυγατέρες, three daughters, then “these three did not satisfy her, and the fourth…” [T] The Greek harmonised the two into three to match the following saying — so the MT’s “two” was awkward enough, that early, to provoke a fix. The pattern-break below is real, and an ancient reader felt it.

First, 30:11–14 is a four-member list that never states its number — and it stands immediately before the first explicit “three… four”. The chapter performs the form before it names it. [I] Moderate–high.

Second, 30:24–28 alone drops the “three”. It is also the only one of the five lists that is commendatory: the insatiable, the unfathomable, the unbearable and the stately are matters of wonder, dread or ceremony; the ants, badgers, locusts and lizard are held up for imitation. The form changes exactly where the evaluation changes. [T] on the form; [I] on the correlation. Moderate–high.

And the four commended creatures share one syntax the other lists do not have — concessive: “a people not strong, yet they provide”; “not mighty, yet they make”; “no king, yet all march”; “you can take in your hands, yet it is in kings’ palaces.” [T] Four negations, four waw-consecutives. The praise is entirely in the “yet”.

Three bicola where the parallel decides the meaning

  • 30:2 — “too stupid to be a man” ∥ “I have not the understanding of a man”. Synonymous; the second half tells you the first is about knowledge, not moral worth. [T] The ESV’s “stupid” invites the wrong reading; see Tool 7 on baʿar.
  • 30:12 — “clean in their own eyes” ∥ “not washed of their filth”. Antithetical, and the only clearly antithetical bicolon in the whole section. [T] Its rarity is the point (see the closing note under Genre).
  • 30:19d / 30:20 — “the way of a man with a young woman” ∥ “so is the way of an adulteress”. The kēn forces the reading: the fourth wonder is sexual union, and v.20 is its perversion. [T] High confidence — and it settles a real interpretive fork; see Preaching Pitfalls. And the Septuagint settles it further from the other direction: the Greek de-sexualises v.19d to “the ways of a man in youth” (ἐν νεότητι) but keeps v.20’s connective, which now hangs on nothing. [T] A smoothing that leaves a dangling link is evidence that the link is original.

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A — wisdom instruction and numerical saying, not narrative. There is no narrator standing outside an action to interpret it. The nearest analogue is the first-person evaluation in 30:2–3 and 30:18, and that is the speaker assessing himself, which the Tool does not cover.

7. Vocabulary

The proper-noun inventory

Agur, Jakeh, Ithiel, Ucal, Massa, Lemuel — six, and unusually for this project not one of them can be triaged as routine scenery, because four are disputed as to whether they are names at all. [T]

Name Status Freight
Agur son of Jakeh (30:1) Almost certainly a personal name. [S] Waltke notes that the construction proper noun + “son of” + proper noun occurs over a thousand times in the OT as a nominal designation. Otherwise unattested. The name itself is the point: it is nobody’s.
Ithiel, Ucal (30:1) Disputed — see Tool 8. [S] Waltke’s objection is decisive against reading them as names: the repetition of ləʾîtîʾēl is abnormal and ʾukāl is never used as a proper name in any Semitic language. If not names, no freight.
Massa (30:1; 31:1) Disputed — “the oracle” or “of Massa”. Massa is named at Gen 25:14 and 1 Chr 1:30 as a son of Ishmael. If a place: both closing teachers are Ishmaelite Arabs.
Lemuel (31:1, 4) A personal name; otherwise unattested. Means something like “belonging to God” / “to God”. [T]/[S] Moderate on the etymology. Unknown outside Proverbs.

The Massa question, worked honestly — and the two decisions pull opposite ways. [T] on the grammar; [I] on the weighing.

  • At 30:1 the noun carries the article: ha-maśśāʾ. A gentilic would want ha-maśśāʾî, “the Massaite” — which is exactly why several scholars emend to get it. [S] (Sauer, McKane, Meinhold, Murphy, Clifford, Perdue all propose the emendation; Ansberry judges it unnecessary.) The article favours “the oracle”.
  • At 31:1 the words run dibrê ləmûʾēl melek maśśāʾ, which reads most naturally as a construct chain — “the words of Lemuel king of Massa”. The ESV has to insert a full stop and start a new sentence to get “The words of King Lemuel. An oracle.”
  • So the smoothest reading of 30:1 and the smoothest reading of 31:1 are not the same reading. Any translation must take a hit somewhere, and the ESV takes it at 31:1.

The syntactic argument for “oracle” is the strongest thing on the table, and it is not the article. [S: Ansberry] ha-maśśāʾ stands in apposition to both dibrê and nəʾum, and both of those are standard designations for prophetic speech. A noun sandwiched between two prophetic-speech terms is most naturally a third one. Moderate–high. Verdict: “the oracle” is the better-supported reading, and the Massa reading should be named in the pulpit as a live minority rather than adopted or dismissed.

The formula that is the section’s sharpest lexical finding

נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר nəʾum haggeber, “the man declares” (30:1). [T] on the occurrences; [S: Ansberry] on the significance, independently confirmed.

Outside Proverbs 30:1 the phrase occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible: Numbers 24:3, 24:15 — Balaam. 2 Samuel 23:1 — the last words of David. High confidence.

That is the whole company. And both of the other speakers explicitly ground their utterance in God: Balaam receives ʾimrê ʾēl, “the words of God” (Num 24:4); David speaks by the rûaḥ YHWH (2 Sam 23:2). Agur opens by claiming the diction of a non-Israelite seer through whom God nevertheless speaks, and of Israel’s king at the end of his life — and two verses later he quotes ʾimrat ʾĕlôah, “the word of God” (30:5), which is Balaam’s own category. [I] Moderate–high, and it is the strongest single argument that 30:1’s maśśāʾ* means “oracle”.*

8. Translations

30:1b — the most consequential rendering decision in the book, and the ESV has taken a side without saying which

The consonants are: לְאִיתִיאֵל לְאִיתִיאֵל וְאֻכָל. The ESV prints “I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and worn out” and footnotes the Hebrew as “to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal”. Three readings are live:

Reading Basis Sense
(a) Two proper names The MT vocalisation as it stands; KJV “unto Ithiel, even unto Ithiel and Ucal” Agur addresses two disciples
(b) Aramaic negation lêt ʾîtay ʾēl — “there is no God” “There is no God, there is no God, and I am powerless” — an atheistic confession
(c) Revocalisation lāʾîtî ʾēl — “I am weary, O God” The ESV’s text

Where the evidence sits. [S], from the checking stage:

  • Reading (a) has a specific defect: the repetition of ləʾîtîʾēl is abnormal, and ʾukāl is never used as a proper name in any Semitic language (Waltke). It is the reading the consonants give and the one the versions took, but it is not therefore the likeliest.
  • Reading (b) is Torrey’s, followed by Scott, McKane, Crenshaw and Murphy — and Ansberry’s objection is that it is “incompatible with the moral vision of the sapiential material”, which is a coherence argument rather than a philological one. It should be named, not dismissed.
  • Reading (c) is preferred by Plöger, Murphy, Waltke, BHQ (de Waard) and Barthélemy. [S] That BHQ takes it matters: the ESV is not being eccentric, it is following the current critical edition.

And now the point the ESV reader cannot see. Within reading (c) there is a second fork, on the last word וְאֻכָל:

Derivation Rendering Who
√כלה, “be used up, spent” “and worn out” — the ESV Franklyn, Garrett, Clifford, Fox
√יכל, “be able, prevail” “but I can prevail” Plöger, Murphy, Waltke, BHQ, Barthélemy — and Ansberry, who argues the defective form of √יכל occurs at Josh 7:12, Ps 18:39 and Jer 20:11

These are opposite endings. The ESV’s Agur is exhausted all the way down. BHQ’s Agur is exhausted and prevails anyway. [T] on the divergence; [S] on the parties.

⚠ Revised 20 August, on the Septuagint. LXX 30:1b reads καὶ παύομαι, “and I cease” — which is √כלה, the ESV’s derivation, not √יכל. [T] The ESV’s “worn out” therefore has the oldest version behind it, and this report’s first draft, which called it “the minority derivation”, was too hard on it. The two candidates are: “and worn out” (LXX, Fox, Garrett, Clifford) against “but I can prevail” (BHQ, Waltke, Barthélemy, Ansberry). Genuinely balanced, and pointing opposite ways.

The same verse shows that the Greek translator did not read Ithiel and Ucal as names — he rendered them (τοῖς πιστεύουσιν θεῷ, “to those who believe in God”) — which supports Waltke against reading (a). But it does not support the ESV’s “I am weary, O God” either: that revocalisation has no ancient witness. [T]

This is the section’s single most important translation note, and the honest pulpit line is that the verse is disputed at two levels and that nothing after v.1 depends on it.

30:19 — “virgin”

The Hebrew is עַלְמָה ʿalmâ: a young woman of marriageable age. The word for a virgin specifically is bətûlâ. NASB has “maid”, KJV “maid”; the ESV’s “virgin” imports a determination the Hebrew does not make and that the verse does not need. [T] High. Nothing doctrinal turns on it here — but a hearer who knows the Isaiah 7:14 debate will notice, and the honest note is that ʿalmâ means “young woman” and the context (v.20) is about sexual union rather than about virginity.

30:31 — two cruxes in one verse, and the ESV footnotes both

zarzîr motnayim is literally “girt of loins”, and the candidates are a strutting cock, a magpie, a greyhound and a war-horse. ʾalqûm ʿimmô is either “whose army is with him” or “against whom there is no rising up”. [T] on the ambiguity. No sermon should rest on either. Uncertain — the ESV’s own footnotes are the honest position.

9. Tone and Feel

Four registers in forty-two verses, and the transitions are marked by petuchot. [T]

Verses Register What carries it
30:1–4 Exhaustion turning to awe Four negations in two verses (lōʾ… wə-lōʾ… wə-lōʾ), then four mî-questions in a single enormous verse
30:5–9 Settled, then urgent A quoted confession, a prohibition, then direct second-person address to God — the only sustained instance in the book
30:10–33 A naturalist’s delight, dryly funny Ants, badgers, locusts, a lizard in a palace, a strutting bird, and a proverb about squeezing a nose
31:1–9 Alarmed intimacy becoming public charge Three vocatives in one verse, an immediate self-repetition (“it is not for kings… it is not for kings”), then two matched imperatives

Two devices worth naming.

Verse 30:4 is a structural spike. In the parsed Hebrew it runs to 24 words; the next longest verse in the section is 30:19 at 14, and the median is 8. [T] The verse is nearly three times the section’s normal length. Whatever else Agur is doing, the questions are where he slows the poem almost to a stop.

30:33 ends the chapter on an untranslatable joke. mîṣ (“pressing”) three times, yôṣîʾ (“brings out”) three times — and ʾap means both nose and anger. So: pressing milk brings out curds; pressing the nose brings out blood; pressing anger brings out strife. [T] The middle line is the pivot and the pun is the argument. This is a category 1 translation-loss item (see the triage note under Textual Variants): no English can carry it, and the ESV’s “pressing… pressing… pressing” preserves as much as any version can.

Soundtrack: a man who has given up, then looks out of the window and starts laughing; then a woman’s voice, low and fast, in a corridor.

Book usage: the Psalter is a live source across Proverbs; this is the most nearly verbatim borrowing in the book.

What it adds: at the end of the son’s book of wisdom, an outsider speaks in the diction of the father’s last words, and quotes the father’s victory song for the proposition that God’s word is what can be trusted. The book’s closing theological statement is borrowed from David, not from Solomon. [I] Moderate–high.

Deuteronomy 4:2 / 12:32 [13:1 MT] → Proverbs 30:6 (high confidence)

Source context: Deut 4:2 stands at the head of Moses’ exhortation to keep the statutes, immediately before the Horeb retrospective; Deut 13:1 closes the section on false worship. Both read “You shall not add to the word… nor take away from it.” It is the Torah’s canon-formula, and its function is to fence revelation against improvement.

Book usage: Deuteronomy is the book’s most-used source. [S] from the sweep. This is its most self-conscious use, because what Proverbs quotes is not a commandment but a statement about the status of commandments — and it comes hard on the heels of 29:18’s tôrâ and ḥāzôn. Two consecutive sections subordinate wisdom to revelation.

The observation the checking stage did not supply, and it is a small one worth having: Agur quotes only half the formula. Deuteronomy forbids adding and taking away; Proverbs 30:6 forbids only adding. [T] And the reason is sitting in the previous verse: if every word of God is already ṣərûpâ — refined, with nothing left to remove — then subtraction is not a live temptation. The only way to damage a text that has already been through the furnace is to put something back into it. [I] Moderate; the argument is clean but the abbreviation may be nothing more than abbreviation.

OT-to-OT: Ps 18:31 and Deut 4:2 are not obviously in prior canonical conversation, but they are the two poles of a single claim — one from experience, one from command. Agur pairs a tested word with a closed word, and the pairing is his whole answer to the questions of v.4. [I] Moderate–high. [S] Ansberry describes the same pairing as “a composite quotation” used “to assert that divine revelation is the authentic source of wisdom” — reached independently here from the two source texts.

12. Genre

Two genres, both marked by their own superscription. [T]

30:1–33 — oracular wisdom monologue plus numerical saying (mišpaṭ mispārî). The numerical saying is a recognised ANE and biblical form (Amos 1–2’s “for three transgressions… and for four”; Prov 6:16; Sir 25:7–11), and its reading rule is that the list is not a taxonomy but a rhetorical crescendo — the last member usually carries the weight. [T]/[S] Moderate–high. So: the way of a man with a young woman; a maidservant displacing her mistress; the lizard in kings’ palaces; a king with his army. In each case the final item is where the saying is going.

31:1–9 — royal instruction, the form of Egyptian sebayit addressed to a prince, and of Deut 17:14–20’s law of the king. [T]/[S]

And a formal statement about this section’s units, given deliberately in place of a percentage

The four sentence collections have now been classified on one scheme, and the figures are 81.0% (10:1–15:33), 20.4% (16:1–22:16), 7.3% (25:1–27:27) and 60.0% (28:1–29:27) antithetical. No antithesis percentage is given for 30:1–31:9, for the same reason it was withheld from the Words of the Wise: the scheme classifies bicola, and this material’s unit is the multi-verse saying. A number would look comparable and would not be.

What can be measured instead, on the same parsed text, is how much of the section consists of free-standing bicola at all:

Verses in the section 42
Distinct units (by Masoretic paragraph and content) 17 — mean 2.5 verses each
Free-standing single-verse sayings 5 — 30:10, 17, 20, 32, 33
As a proportion of the section 11.9%
Comparison: 10:1–15:33 184 units in 184 verses — 100%

[T] That is the real measure of the difference between this material and the sentence collections, and it is the same measure the paragraphing was recording all along. A section that is 12% free-standing bicola will carry nine paragraph markers; a section that is 100% free-standing bicola will carry one.

(For the record, and so that nobody has to guess: 30:12 is the section’s only clearly antithetical bicolon. One in forty-two.) [T]

Mostly N/A — there are no narrative characters here. Two items nevertheless need labelling, because both are regularly treated as prescriptive:

14. Bible Timeline

Where the passage sits: at the end of the Writings’ wisdom instruction, in a book whose superscriptions locate it in the Solomonic tradition and whose two closing voices are almost certainly not Israelite kings.

Where the reader sits: after the cross, after the resurrection, with the canon closed — which makes 30:6 read very differently than it did. Agur says “do not add to his words.” He said it before the last third of the canon was written. The Christian who reads 30:6 has to hold two things at once: that the word is complete and not to be improved, and that God spoke again — finally, in a Son (Heb 1:1–2). The resolution is not that Hebrews violates Deuteronomy’s formula but that the Son is not an addendum to the word; he is the Speaker. [I] Moderate — offered as the shape of the answer, not a settled formulation.

What has happened in between that changes the reading: 30:4’s question — “Who has ascended to heaven and come down? … What is his name, and what is his son’s name?” — is answered in the canon, and the answer is a person. That is the Christological reading below, and it is where the timeline tool and the Christological extension converge.

One timeline error to avoid: treating 31:8–9 as a piece of ancient near-eastern royal ideology now obsolete. The command is taken up and intensified by the prophets (Isa 1:17), by Christ’s own programme (Luke 4:18), and by James (1:27). Nothing about it has been superseded.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks. That the reader who has completed the book should (a) stop estimating his own competence, (b) receive God’s word as tested, sufficient and closed, (c) pray for the conditions in which he can go on fearing God, (d) recognise his appetites as the thing most likely to unmake him, and (e) use whatever authority he has for people who have none. [I] from Tools 1, 2, 10 and 16.

Stage 2 — worldview before behaviour.

How it tells us to think. That wisdom is not an achievement but a posture toward a given word. Everything Agur knows he learned from a text he did not write and may not edit (30:5–6); everything he does not know he lists at length (30:2–4, 18–19). The section’s epistemology is: no ascent, a given word, and a prayer. [T]

What to stop. Adding to God’s words — which in a Reformed church is rarely done by inventing doctrine and usually done by attaching certainties to Scripture that Scripture does not carry (30:6). Speaking while exalting oneself (30:32). Estimating oneself by the standard of those below (30:22–23 read on oneself rather than on others).

What to start. Praying 30:7–9 with its reasons attached, which will be for most hearers the first time they have asked God for less. Opening the mouth for someone with no standing — and the text is specific about who: the mute, “the sons of passing away”, the poor and the needy (31:8–9).

The motive, and it is in the text. Not “because the vulnerable deserve it” but because — on the Jeremiah 22:16 reading — doing this is what knowing God consists of. [I] Application without motive is moralism; the motive here is that the fear of the LORD which begins the book (1:7) shows up at the end of it as a mouth opened in a courtroom.

The four audiences.

  • For me: the honest question is which half of 30:9 I am nearer to, and whether I have ever prayed against the one I want.
  • For a Christian friend: anyone who has just become competent — newly ordained, newly promoted, newly qualified. 30:2–3 is for them, and it will not be welcome.
  • For the church community: 31:8–9 is corporate before it is individual. A church that has a voice in its town and does not use it for the voiceless has the mouth of 30:32 and not the mouth of 31:8.
  • For an unbeliever: 30:4 is a genuine apologetic entry point, and it is honest — the passage asks the question and refuses to answer it. The Christian claim is that the question was answered later and elsewhere, by someone who came down.

Prayer. The passage supplies its own: “Two things I ask of you; deny them not to me before I die.” It is the only prayer in Proverbs and it should probably be prayed rather than described.

Grammatical and syntactical features

  • The Aramaisms of 31:1–9 cluster, and they are concentrated in three verses. [T] בַּר bar for “son” appears three times in 31:2 where Hebrew would have bēn; מְלָכִין məlākîn at 31:3 carries the Aramaic masculine plural -în for Hebrew -îm; and אַל ʾal at 31:4 is used where the sense requires the negative lōʾ. [T] High on the data. What this supports is an origin outside Judahite Hebrew or a deliberate colouring of the speech as foreign — it does not by itself establish a late date, since Aramaic influence is attested early in the north and east. [I] Moderate. It does, however, sit well with the Massa reading, and the two questions should be weighed together rather than separately.
  • The concessive waw-consecutive chain in 30:25–28. Each of the four creatures gets: nominal clause with a negation (ʿam lōʾ-ʿāz, “a people not strong”) followed by wayyiqtol (wayyākînû, wayyāśîmû, wayyēṣēʾ). [T] The narrative-sequence form is being used for gnomic statement, which is what gives the four sayings their “and yet, look” quality.
  • 30:4’s four mî clauses are asyndetic — no connectives at all between them — and then the verse turns on mah… û-mah, “what… and what”. [T] The shift from “who” to “what” is the shift from the deeds to the name, and it is where the questions become unanswerable rather than merely rhetorical.

Wordplay and sound-features

  • 30:33 — mîṣ / ʾap. Threefold mîṣ (“pressing”), threefold yôṣîʾ (“brings out”), and ʾap meaning both nose and anger. [T] High. Category 1 translation loss — and the Septuagint demonstrates it rather than merely illustrating it. The Greek renders the first two lines literally (ἄμελγε γάλα, press milk; ἐὰν ἐκπιέζῃς μυκτῆρας, press the nostrils) and then, unable to carry ʾap’s double sense, swaps in a different third term: “if you drag out words (λόγους), judgements and quarrels come out.” The translator hit the pun, could not render it, and paraphrased. [T]
  • 30:15 — hab hab. The leech’s daughters are named by the imperative “Give!” doubled. [T] Onomatopoeic and, in a section about appetite, the only direct speech given to a non-human.
  • The √נשׂא chain (30:13, 21, 32) — see Repetition. The same three consonants across three semantic domains (lifting eyelids / bearing weight / exalting oneself). Moderate–high; the corroboration is that the middle instance is what makes the outer two speak to each other, and that 30:32 is the section’s closing imperative.

Category 2 — and there are two, one small and one very large.

30:1 — the Ithiel/Ucal crux

  • The issue: whether ləʾîtîʾēl ləʾîtîʾēl wəʾukāl is two proper names (MT vocalisation), an Aramaic denial of God, or a revocalised first-person confession.
  • Evidence: the consonants are undisputed; the vocalisation is the question, so this is strictly a reading rather than a manuscript variant. The ancient versions treat the words as names. [S]
  • Position: the revocalisation is preferred by BHQ (de Waard), Barthélemy, Waltke, Murphy and Plöger; Waltke’s objection to the names-reading (ʾukāl is unattested as a name in any Semitic language) is the strongest single argument. [S] The ESV’s main text is defensible and current; its footnote is honest; and its final word (“worn out”) follows the minority derivation within the majority reading.
  • Preaching implication: say the verse is disputed. Do not build the sermon’s emotional arc on “worn out”. The confession of vv. 2–3 is secure whichever way v.1b goes.
  • Confidence: moderate.

The LXX’s arrangement of the whole book — verified 20 August

  • The issue: Greek Proverbs is not merely a translation of a differently-worded Hebrew; it is a differently arranged book, and this section is one of the two blocks it moves.

  • Status: ✅ VERIFIED. The order was predicted in this report from memory on 20 August and then checked against Rahlfs, Septuaginta (Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, 1979), supplied in full from Logos. The prediction matched exactly, block for block:

    1:1–24:22 (+ the Greek plus at 24:22a–e) → 30:1–14 → 24:23–34 → 30:15–33 → 31:1–9 → 25:1–29:27 → 31:10–31

    Three content checks confirm it independently. After MT 24:22e the Greek runs τίς ἀνέβη εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ κατέβη; (= 30:4). After the material corresponding to 30:14 it resumes Ταῦτα δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν τοῖς σοφοῖς (= 24:23). After MT 31:9 it goes to Αὗται αἱ παιδεῖαι Σαλωμῶντος αἱ ἀδιάκριτοι, ἃς ἐξεγράψαντο οἱ φίλοι Εζεκιου (= 25:1). [T]

The observation, now stated precisely. The Greek arrangement creates five block boundaries. Every one of them falls at a Masoretic paragraph marker:

1 after 24:22 (and its Greek plus) ס — setumah, the only one found anywhere in the book Words of the Wise run

[T] on both sides — the Greek from Rahlfs, the markers from BHS.

A sixth marker is involved but is not load-bearing. The petuchah at 30:33 falls at the joint between 30:15–33 and 31:1–9 — but those two run consecutively in the MT as well, so the Greek is not cutting there. It is included for completeness and excluded from the count. Five seams, five markers, and the earlier draft’s “six for six” is corrected to this.

Two details that strengthen rather than weaken the finding.

  • The Greek’s own expansion sits inside boundary 1. The five-verse plus at 24:22a–e has no Hebrew counterpart, and it is attached to 24:22 — so the Greek both expanded at and cut after the point the MT marks with its unique setumah. [T] Two traditions, two different operations, one joint.
  • Boundary 5 was the least likely to be right by accident. Nothing about the MT makes 29:27 a natural place to break to the acrostic, and the marker there was found in the 28–29 run for unrelated reasons.

What follows from it — and this is why the finding matters beyond the passage. The 28–29 run flagged a methodological worry: whether appealing to the Masoretic petuchot in a compositional argument is a category error, since they are transmission-level marks pointed centuries after the fact. It is not. The Greek tradition cuts the book at five places and the Hebrew tradition pauses at all five. The likeliest explanation is that both inherited a common inventory of division points and made different arrangement decisions at them [I] — which means the markers record joints already present in the text when the Greek Vorlage was arranged.

This retrospectively raises the evidential value of every paragraphing finding in this project, including the three that ran against the modern consensus: the paragraph running 27:23 → 28:4 across Fox’s “common division”; the ninety-one-verse paragraph 14:1 → 19:9; and the silence at 15:33/16:1. Those are no longer “the Masoretes disagree with the scholars” — they are “the transmitted division-points disagree with the scholars.”

Ansberry’s own position — with Washington, Tov and Clifford — is that the Greek arrangement is secondary. That is probably right, and it is not what the observation depends on: a secondary arranger who cuts only at inherited joints is still evidence that the joints were inherited.

Confidence: high. The one thing still worth having is a scholarly statement of the same observation, if one exists; Tov’s “Recensional Differences between the MT and LXX of Proverbs” is where to look. The finding no longer depends on it.

And the same text supplies seven further divergences, triaged

The LXX of 30:1–31:9 diverges from the MT far more than the modern translations do. Each is classified below; only category 2 is a variant.

Category 2 — substantive, and the first is the most important.

30:3 — a polarity flip on both cola. [T]

MT וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע — “I have not learned wisdom, nor do I know the knowledge of the Holy One”
LXX θεὸς δεδίδαχέν με σοφίαν, καὶ γνῶσιν ἁγίων ἔγνωκα — “God has taught me wisdom, and I know the knowledge of the holy ones”

Both negatives are gone and a divine subject has been supplied. This is not a translator’s slip; it is a reading of the passage. The Greek Agur is a man who has been taught; the Hebrew Agur is a man who has not. Which is prior: the MT is the harder reading by some distance — a confession of ignorance from the book’s last sage is the more difficult thing for a tradition to invent, and the easier thing for a tradition to soften. The MT is very probably original here and the Greek is a theological smoothing. Moderate–high. Preaching implication: the whole force of Headline 3 and the 9:10 echo lives in the Hebrew, and the preacher should know that an ancient reader already found it uncomfortable enough to reverse.

30:9 — the divine name disappears. MT: “and say, Who is the LORD?” (mî YHWH) — Pharaoh’s line at Exod 5:2. LXX: Τίς με ὁρᾷ; “Who sees me?” [T] The only occurrence of YHWH in these forty-two verses of Hebrew is absent from the Greek, and with it the Exodus echo. Whether the translator’s Vorlage differed or he shied from putting the name into a blasphemy cannot be settled here. Moderate; the MT is preferred as the harder reading.

30:15a — the leech has three daughters, not two. MT: “two daughters: Give and Give”, then “three… four”. LXX: τρεῖς θυγατέρες, then “these three did not satisfy her, and the fourth was not content to say ‘Enough’”. [T] The Greek has harmonised the number to the following three-and-four saying. This is corroboration of a finding rather than a threat to one: §5 identified 30:15a’s “two” as a pattern-break, and the Greek translator found it awkward enough to fix. Moderate–high. The MT’s awkwardness is original.

30:16 — the insatiable are six, not four, and rāḥam (“barren womb”) becomes ἔρως γυναικός, “a woman’s passion”, with τάρταρος added. [T] The Greek expands and moralises a list of which three MT members are not moral failings at all — which is exactly the Preaching Pitfall flagged below, committed by an ancient reader. Moderate.

30:19 — the fourth wonder is de-sexualised. MT: “the way of a man with a young woman (bəʿalmâ)”. LXX: ὁδοὺς ἀνδρὸς ἐν νεότητι, “the ways of a man in youth” — reading the consonants as bāʿălûmāyw. [T] But the Greek keeps 30:20’s connective (τοιαύτη ὁδὸς γυναικὸς μοιχαλίδος, “such is the way of an adulterous woman”), which now hangs on nothing. The Greek has smoothed the referent and left the link stranded — which is positive evidence that the MT’s sexual reading is original and that vv. 19–20 belong together. Moderate–high, and it settles the fork in §5 more firmly than the kēn* alone did.*

31:8 — the mute become the word of God. MT: pətaḥ pîkā lə-ʾillēm, “open your mouth for the mute”. LXX: ἄνοιγε σὸν στόμα λόγῳ θεοῦ, “open your mouth with the word of God”. [T] Moderate. The doubled imperative survives intact in both traditions, so Headline 6 is unaffected; but the object of the opened mouth differs, and the MT’s is the one the parallel with 31:9 supports.

Category 1 — translation loss, and the Greek proves the point.

30:33’s pun is gone, and the Greek shows exactly how it went. MT has mîṣ three times and turns on ʾap meaning both nose and anger. The Greek keeps the first two lines literally — ἄμελγε γάλα (press milk), ἐὰν ἐκπιέζῃς μυκτῆρας (press the nostrils) — and then, unable to carry the double sense, substitutes a different third term: ἐὰν δὲ ἐξέλκῃς λόγους, “if you drag out words, judgements and quarrels come out.” [T] This is a category 1 item demonstrated rather than asserted: the oldest translator hit the pun, could not render it, and paraphrased. High.

30:31 — the Greek takes a side on the crux, and it is the ESV’s. zarzîr motnayim is rendered ἀλέκτωρ ἐμπεριπατῶν θηλείαις, “a cock strutting among the hens” — supporting “strutting rooster” against magpie, greyhound and war-horse. [T] Moderate–high, and worth having, since the ESV footnotes three options without indicating that the oldest version backs its main text. For ʾalqûm ʿimmô the Greek has βασιλεὺς δημηγορῶν ἐν ἔθνει, “a king making a public address among his people” — neither of the ESV’s two options, which is a fair indication of how obscure the phrase is.

And two entries that revise this report’s own earlier verdicts.

31:1 — the Greek renders maśśāʾ as “oracle”, not as a place. βασιλέως χρηματισμός, “a king’s divine oracle”. [T] This is the strongest piece of evidence yet on the Massa question, and it was not available when §7 was written. The oldest translator, working before the vocalisation was fixed, took the word as a prophetic-speech term at the very verse where the construct chain melek maśśāʾ would most naturally have produced “king of Massa”. Verdict upgraded: “the oracle” is now well supported, and the Massa reading, while still worth naming, is the weaker of the two. Moderate–high, up from moderate.

30:1 — and here the Greek complicates the picture in the ESV’s favour on one point and against it on another. LXX 30:1b reads τάδε λέγει ὁ ἀνὴρ τοῖς πιστεύουσιν θεῷ, καὶ παύομαι — “thus says the man to those who believe in God, and I cease.” Two things follow [T]:

  1. The translator did not read Ithiel and Ucal as proper names. He rendered them. That is independent support for Waltke’s objection and against reading (a) — the names-reading is not the reading of the oldest witness, whatever the MT vocalisation later did with it.
  2. He derived wəʾukāl from √כלה, “come to an end” — παύομαι, “I cease”. This is the derivation behind the ESV’s “and worn out”, not the √יכל “prevail” that Ansberry, Waltke and BHQ prefer. [T]

So the earlier verdict is revised. This report said the ESV’s “worn out” follows “the minority derivation within the majority reading” and warned against building a sermon’s emotional arc on it. That warning is now too strong. The ESV’s ending has the Septuagint behind it, which is not a minority position in any ordinary sense — it is simply a different one from the current critical edition’s. The honest statement: the line is disputed, the two candidate endings are “worn out” (LXX, Fox, Garrett, Clifford) and “but I can prevail” (BHQ, Waltke, Barthélemy), and they point opposite ways. The preacher should say so rather than choose silently. See the revised Pitfall below.

What the Greek does not support is the ESV’s first half. τοῖς πιστεύουσιν θεῷ, “to those who believe in God”, is not “I am weary, O God”; the translator read ləʾîtîʾēl as something like “to the there-is-God people”. So the revocalisation behind the ESV’s opening remains a modern reconstruction with no ancient witness. High confidence on the datum.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period: unrecoverable for the material itself; the collections’ compilation is placed by the superscriptions in the Solomonic and Hezekian tradition (1:1; 10:1; 25:1), and these two closing compositions carry no such attachment at all. [T] on the superscriptions’ silence; [I] on the implication.
  • Location: if maśśāʾ is a place, the north-west Arabian desert — Massa is listed at Gen 25:14 among the twelve sons of Ishmael, alongside Dumah, Tema and Kedar, a group associated with the Arabian trade routes. [T] on the genealogy; [S] on the geography, moderate.
  • Political context: a monarchy is assumed and addressed (31:1–9), and the machinery named is judicial rather than military — “what has been decreed” (məḥuqqāq), the dîn of the afflicted, “judge righteously”. [T]

What the original audience knew

  • That maśśāʾ was a prophetic term. It heads Nahum 1:1, Habakkuk 1:1, Malachi 1:1 and a whole series of oracles in Isaiah 13–23. [T] A reader meeting ha-maśśāʾ in a wisdom book would have registered the intrusion of a different kind of speech, and that is likely the intended effect. Moderate–high.
  • That the nəʾum haggeber formula had exactly two previous owners, and that one of them was a foreign diviner. [T] This is a piece of literary competence a modern reader has to be told about and an ancient reader had by heart.
  • That a king who drank was a live political danger, not a moral abstraction. Elah was assassinated “drinking himself drunk” (1 Kgs 16:9); Ben-hadad was defeated while “drinking himself drunk in the booths” with thirty-two kings (1 Kgs 20:16); Belshazzar’s feast is the paradigm. [T] on the narratives; [S] on the pattern being proverbial. 31:4–5 is a comment on known events. Moderate–high.
  • What the śənûʾâ of 30:23 was. Deuteronomy 21:15–17 legislates specifically for the household with a loved wife and a hated one, and protects the hated wife’s firstborn. The audience heard a legal category. [T]
  • What a rock badger and a lizard in a palace were. The šāpān (hyrax) is a small, defenceless cliff-dwelling mammal — listed among the unclean at Lev 11:5 — and the śəmāmît is a house gecko, which gets into buildings because it can climb walls. [T]/[S] Moderate–high. The commended creature that reaches the king’s palace is a lizard that walks up the wall, which is a joke about access, and an ancient reader in a mud-brick house got it instantly.
  • That gathering the wind in the fists and binding the waters in a garment (30:4) were creation-language. Both images belong to the standard repertoire of ANE and biblical creation poetry (Job 26:8; 38:8–11; Ps 104:3–6). [T]

What this changes about how we read

The section is culturally marked as coming from outside. A prophetic genre-word, a Balaam formula, Job’s divine name, Aramaic morphology, and possibly two Ishmaelite authors. [T] on each datum; [I] on the cumulative reading. Whether or not Agur and Lemuel were Arabs, the material is presented as though wisdom’s last word came from beyond Israel’s borders — and that is a canonical statement, not an accident of compilation.

And 31:4–5’s reason for royal sobriety is not personal holiness. It is that a drunk king “forgets what has been decreed and perverts the dîn of all the afflicted.” [T] The concern is judicial competence. A modern sermon on this text that lands on personal temperance has substituted a different argument for the one the mother makes.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: Writings. The canonical reader arrives having read the Torah and the full Prophets, ending at Malachi — post-exilic, temple restored, covenant faithfulness still lacking, no Davidic king on the throne.
  • What the canonical reader knows: Solomon’s whole story, including 1 Kgs 11 and the tearing of the kingdom. Josiah and Jehoiakim (Jer 22). Balaam. David’s last words. Job’s failure to get an answer. And the fact that Israel’s kings have already been weighed and found wanting.
  • Canonical register: Job, Psalms and Proverbs are neighbours. Agur’s ʾĕlôah and his Job-38-shaped questions are read within that neighbourhood, not across a gap.

The first hearers

  • Who they were: in the canonical frame, a post-exilic community that has the wisdom tradition and no king to apply it to. [I] Moderate.
  • Their situation: they hold a book that ends by instructing a monarch, and there is no monarch.
  • What they brought: the memory that Israel’s kings had done the opposite of 31:8–9, and a prophetic literature that said so at length.

Where the passage fits their world

31:1–9 addressed to a community with no throne is not a dead letter but an unfulfilled charter. [I] Moderate–high. The book’s last instruction describes a king nobody has yet seen — sober, incorruptible, and speaking for the mute — which is the same gap the prophets leave open at Isa 11:3–4 and the same gap Ps 72 prays into.

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: the book’s last named sage says he has not learned wisdom (30:3), and a foreigner claims Balaam’s and David’s formula (30:1). Also 30:17’s image — the ravens picking out the eye of a child who mocked his parents — which is deliberately unbearable and is meant to be.
  • Surprising: the prayer asks God for less (30:8). In a book that has repeatedly promised riches and honour to the wise (3:16; 8:18; 22:4), that is a reversal, and the book puts it near the end rather than harmonising it away.
  • Comforting: “Every word of God proves true; he is a shield to those who take refuge in him” (30:5). For a reader who has just been told that nobody has ascended to heaven, the word given is the whole consolation.
  • Disturbing: 30:9’s “lest I be full and deny you” — the assumption that prosperity is a spiritual danger of the same order as theft. Most hearers will have prayed for one of these two conditions and never against the other.

What we bring that they didn’t

  • A therapeutic reading of humility. 30:2–3 is not low self-esteem; it is an epistemological claim about the reach of human enquiry, and it is followed immediately by a confident assertion about the text (30:5).
  • Individualism. 31:8–9 is a public-office command, and it will be domesticated into private kindness unless the preacher resists.
  • A modern reflex about class and gender which will hear 30:22–23 as contempt. See Difficult Verses.

Implication for the sermon

The section’s emotional architecture is: emptied → given → asking → looking → handed over. A sermon that starts at 31:8 and works backwards for context will preach the commission without the emptying, which is precisely the order the petuchah at 30:33 forbids.

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. The Word of God

  • Where this passage sits: at the point where the wisdom tradition explicitly subordinates itself to revelation. [T]
  • What comes before: creation by word (Gen 1); the words written at Sinai and fenced by the canon-formula (Deut 4:2); the word tested in David’s deliverance (Ps 18:31); wisdom personified as present at creation (Prov 8:22–31).
  • What comes after: “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1); “in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son” (Heb 1:1–2); “his name is called The Word of God” (Rev 19:13).
  • What it contributes: Proverbs 8 put Wisdom beside God at the creation; Proverbs 30 puts the enquirer outside the heavens and hands him a book. The book contains both, and the order is deliberate. [I] Moderate–high.

2. Wisdom

  • Where this passage sits: at the wisdom tradition’s own limit — the point where the search stops and receives. [T]
  • What comes before: Eden’s contest over the knowledge of good and evil; Solomon’s request (1 Kgs 3) and its ruin (1 Kgs 11); Job 28’s “where shall wisdom be found?”; Prov 1:7 and 9:10.
  • What comes after: “a greater than Solomon is here” (Matt 12:42); “Christ the wisdom of God” (1 Cor 1:24, 30); and specifically 1 Cor 1:20 — “Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?” — which is Agur’s own position stated the other way round.
  • What it contributes: Proverbs does not end in mastery. [T] A book that opens “to know wisdom” closes with a sage saying “I have not learned wisdom” and a mother telling a king to speak for the mute. Wisdom’s terminus in this book is a confession and a duty.

3. Kingship

  • Where this passage sits: the last royal instruction in the OT wisdom corpus. [T]
  • What comes before: Deut 17:14–20’s law of the king; Solomon’s failure; Ps 72’s ideal; the prophets’ indictments (Jer 22); Prov 8:15; 16:10–15; 25:2–7; 29:4, 14.
  • What comes after: Isa 11:3–4’s shoot of Jesse who “with righteousness shall judge the poor”; Luke 4:18; and the King who is enthroned by descending.
  • What it contributes: the royal thread in Proverbs lands, finally, not on how to please a king or how to be a successful one, but on what a king is for. [T] And the passage supplies both the failure-mode (30:22, the unformed man who is promoted; 31:4–5, the ruler whose appetite corrupts his judgement) and the office (31:8–9).

Confluence — and it is climactic, not parallel

The three themes converge on a single reality the passage names. The word that is refined and closed (theme 1) is what the enquirer receives in place of the ascent he cannot make (theme 2), and the person who receives it is then commissioned to speak for those who cannot speak for themselves (theme 3). [I] The chain runs: no ascent → a given word → an opened mouth. That is one movement, not three, and its hinge is the petuchah at 30:33. Moderate–high.

Christological Reading

Type of Christological connection

Trajectory and contrast, with one point of near-direct fulfilment. No typology claim in this section passes all four tests, and the section is better served by trajectory readings honestly labelled than by a strained type. [I]

How the passage points to Christ

30:4 — the question and its answer. “Who has ascended to heaven and come down? … What is his name, and what is his son’s name?”

  • Test 1, theological category: passes — the category is the knowledge of God and access to him, not an incidental detail.
  • Test 2, NT precedent: John 3:13 — “No one has ascended into heaven except he who descended from heaven, the Son of Man.” The ESV cross-references it here. The verbal correspondence (ascend / descend) is exact, and John’s statement is formally an answer to a question of exactly this shape. [T] on the correspondence. Moderate–high that John is engaging this tradition; the Deut 30:12 form is also live and Paul uses that one at Rom 10:6, so the specific dependence on Prov 30:4 cannot be established.
  • Test 3, escalation: passes — the answer exceeds the question. Agur asks who has ascended; the gospel answers with one who descended first.
  • Test 4, authorial pattern: partial. The text raises an expectation and leaves it unresolved, which is the pattern’s signature; but it does not signal that a future figure will resolve it.

Verdict: trajectory, strongly warranted, not direct prophecy. [I] Moderate–high.

On “what is his son’s name?” — handle with care. A strand of the tradition reads this as a veiled reference to a divine Son. [S: Rydelnik, Leventhal, Fruchtenbaum] The alternative — that “son” is part of the rhetorical impossibility (“name his dynasty, if you can”) — is at least as natural and is the majority reading. The honest homiletical move is to let the question stand as a question, and then to say that the canon answers it. That is stronger than claiming Agur foresaw it, and it cannot be embarrassed. Uncertain on the messianic reading as authorial intent; high on the canonical answer.

30:8–9 — the prayer and the wilderness. Agur asks to be spared both conditions in which he might fail. Christ is put into one of them: forty days without bread, and the specific temptation “command these stones to become loaves” (Matt 4:3), answered from Deuteronomy. [I] Agur prays not to be tested at the point of appetite; Christ is tested there and holds. Moderate — offered as contrast, and it is the cleaner reading than making Agur a type. The ləḥem ḥuqqî / epiousios link at Matt 6:11 is a further resonance, flagged moderate because the Greek word’s sense is itself disputed.

30:5–6 — the closed word and the final Word. See Bible Timeline. Moderate.

31:8–9 — the king who opens his mouth. The command describes a monarch who never appeared in Israel’s history. The prophetic literature turns the same portrait into an expectation (Isa 11:3–4; Ps 72:12–14), and the gospel opens Christ’s public ministry with him reading Isa 61 in a synagogue: “he has sent me to proclaim liberty to the captives” (Luke 4:18). [I] The last command in Proverbs’ instruction is the first thing Jesus says he was sent to do. Moderate–high, and this is where the section’s Christological weight actually sits.

Trajectory

  • What the passage anticipates: an answer to 30:4 (a name, and a Son); a king who does 31:8–9.
  • What Christ fulfils: he is the descent that answers the ascent-question; he is the King who judges for the poor; he is the Word that is not an addition.
  • What still remains: the judgement of 31:9 is not yet executed. The mute are still mute.

Moralism check

  • The “be like X” temptation: be humble like Agur / speak up like Lemuel’s mother. Both are available, both are half-true, and both leave the hearer with a duty and no power.
  • The gospel grounding: the passage itself refuses moralism at the decisive point. Agur does not resolve his ignorance by trying harder; he receives a word (30:5) and prays (30:7). [T] The structure of the passage is the anti-moralism argument. A sermon that reproduces the passage’s own order — emptied, given, asking, then sent — will not be moralistic, because the giving comes before the sending.
  • Christ as hero, not example: at 30:4 the hero is emphatically not the reader. The verse exists to establish that.

Confidence

Moderate–high on the 30:4 → John 3:13 trajectory and the 31:8–9 → Isa 11 / Luke 4 trajectory. Uncertain on any messianic reading of “his son’s name” as authorial intent.

Difficult / Contested Verses

30:1b — the ESV’s main text is not the Hebrew

  • Category: apologetic / textual.
  • The difficulty: a hearer comparing the ESV with the KJV, or reading the ESV’s own footnote, will find that “I am weary, O God” is not what the Hebrew says.
  • Rhetorical function in context: whatever v.1b is, it introduces the confession of vv. 2–3, and that confession is secure on any reading.
  • The options and where the evidence points: see Tool 8. BHQ takes the emendation; Waltke’s argument against the names-reading is strong; and the ESV’s “worn out” follows the minority derivation within the majority reading.
  • Suggested handling: acknowledge briefly. One sentence: “the Hebrew here is genuinely difficult and the ESV has taken a reconstruction — it’s footnoted, and nothing in what follows depends on it.”

30:17 — the ravens and the eye

  • Category: ethical / pastoral.
  • The difficulty: the image of carrion birds eating the eye of a child who mocked his parents is grotesque, and it will land badly on anyone in the room who has an estranged child or an abusive parent.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the appended sting to the numerical saying about the insatiable (30:15–16), and the paragraph makes that attachment explicit. It is a wisdom saying about consequence, in the extreme register the genre permits, not a sentence handed down.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the verse’s function is completed by 30:14 — the generation whose teeth are swords devours the poor, and the mocker is devoured. [T] The point is reciprocity, and the horror is the argument. It does not say all who dishonour parents die unburied; it says contempt has a direction of travel and it comes back.
  • Suggested handling: address briefly, with care about who is listening. Name that Proverbs 30 has just described a generation that eats people, and that v.17 is what happens to eaters. Do not use it on the young.

30:22–23 — “a slave when he becomes king… a maidservant when she displaces her mistress”

  • Category: ethical / pastoral, and this is the passage’s biggest landmine in a modern congregation.
  • The difficulty: it reads as contempt for the socially mobile, and specifically for women.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the third of five numerical sayings, in the “unbearable” register, and it sits immediately before the commendatory list.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces — three things, and together they change the verse:
    1. śənûʾâ (30:23) is the legal term of Deut 21:15, the unloved wife in a bigamous household. [T] The saying is not sneering at an unattractive woman; it is describing a household in which a legally disadvantaged wife suddenly has standing, and the resentment that follows.
    2. The chapter refutes the classist reading itself. melek appears at 30:22 (a slave becomes king — unbearable) and at 30:28 (a lizard is in kings’ palaces — admirable). [T] Six verses apart, the same location, opposite verdicts. So the objection cannot be to the ascent as such. The difference is stated in the text: the lizard is “small and exceedingly wise”; the slave in v.22 is paired with a fool. The list is about the unformed receiving what they have not been formed for — which is exactly the warning the reader needs before 31:1.
    3. The reader is the target, not the servant. By position (see the Positional Necessity Check), 30:21–23 is the last warning before the reader is handed a crown.
  • Suggested handling: address it directly. If it is passed over, a thoughtful hearer will conclude the preacher did not notice. The corrective at 30:28 is in the text and is more persuasive than any apology.

30:19–20 — “the way of a man with a virgin”

  • Category: interpretive, with pastoral consequences.
  • The difficulty: the fourth wonder is read either as innocent courtship or as sexual union, and 30:20 is either attached or independent.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: kēn, “so”, opens 30:20, and the received paragraphing binds 18–20 as one unit. [T] Both point the same way: the fourth wonder is sexual union, and v.20 is its perversion. The adulteress “eats and wipes her mouth” — a euphemism the Hebrew makes no attempt to soften.
  • Suggested handling: take the sexual reading and say why. The saying is about the mystery of union and the flippancy that destroys it; sanitising v.19 into courtship costs the connection to v.20 and leaves v.20 stranded.

31:6–7 — “give strong drink to the one who is perishing”

  • Category: ethical / weaponised.
  • The difficulty: read alone it commends anaesthetising the poor.
  • Rhetorical function in context: it is the contrast half of vv. 4–7, and the verb in v.7 is a jussive, not a command to the king. [T]
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the argument is “the ruler must remember; the sufferer may forget” — the mother is not writing drinks policy but making the point that the king’s sobriety is owed to his office. And the very next verses (8–9) command him to change the sufferer’s conditions rather than dull them. [T]
  • Note for the preacher: wine offered to a dying man reappears at Mark 15:23, where it is refused.
  • Suggested handling: address briefly, and use the jussive.

30:10 — “do not slander a servant to his master”

  • Category: ethical.
  • The difficulty: it sounds like advice to protect oneself from the consequences of interfering, and so like a counsel of self-interested silence — awkward beside 31:8–9’s command to speak.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the prohibition is against lāšan — informing, denouncing, carrying a report to someone’s employer. [T] It forbids speech that harms a person with less power to someone with more; 31:8 commands speech that helps a person with less power in front of someone with more. They are not in tension; they are the two halves of one ethic of the mouth. [I] Moderate–high, and it is a good sermon point rather than a difficulty once it is seen.
  • Suggested handling: make it the bridge. It is the only speech prohibition in Agur’s material and it points straight at 31:8.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: preaching 31:8–9 as a standalone social-justice text

  • What it looks like: “Proverbs 31:8 — speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it takes the second half of a two-part instruction whose first half is “put your hand on your mouth” (30:32), and it detaches an imperative addressed to a monarch from the humbling that the received text marks as its precondition.
  • The corrective: preach across the petuchah. The mouth that is opened in 31:8 is the mouth that was shut in 30:32, and the sequence is the argument.

Pitfall: “give me neither poverty nor riches” preached as an endorsement of the comfortable middle

  • What it looks like: a sermon commending moderation, contentment, and not wanting too much — which lands as flattery in a comfortable congregation.
  • Why it’s wrong: the verse is a prayer, not a proverb, and both of its reasons are about God: lest I deny you, lest I profane your name. [T] Agur is not describing an ideal income bracket; he is naming two spiritual failure-modes and asking to be kept from both.
  • The corrective: preach the lest-clauses. The question the passage asks is not “how much do you have?” but “which of these two failures is nearer to you, and have you ever asked God to spare you the one you want?”

Pitfall: “I am weary, O God” as the sermon’s emotional foundation

  • What it looks like: an opening that builds on Agur’s exhaustion — a relatable, burnt-out sage.
  • Why it’s wrong: the ESV’s own footnote says the Hebrew reads otherwise, and the opening words have no ancient witness at all — the Septuagint read ləʾîtîʾēl as “to those who believe in God”, not as an address to God. [T] A sermon’s affective arc should not rest on a modern reconstruction of a disputed clause.
  • A correction to this report’s first draft, in the ESV’s favour: the ending is a different matter. LXX has καὶ παύομαι, “and I cease” — √כלה, which is the ESV’s “worn out.” The two candidate endings are genuinely balanced: “and worn out” (LXX, Fox, Garrett, Clifford) against “but I can prevail” (BHQ, Waltke, Barthélemy). They point opposite ways and the preacher should say so rather than pick one silently.
  • The corrective: build on vv. 2–3, which are secure in the Hebrew and say something sharper. Agur’s problem is not fatigue; it is that he cannot get to God by enquiry.

Pitfall: preaching Agur’s humility without knowing an ancient tradition deleted it

  • What it looks like: the sermon leans hard on “I have not learned wisdom, nor have I knowledge of the Holy One” as the book’s closing note of humility — which is right, and is this report’s Headline 3.
  • Why it needs care: the Septuagint reverses both negatives, making Agur say “God has taught me wisdom, and I know the knowledge of the holy ones.” [T] The confession exists in the Hebrew and not in the Greek.
  • The corrective: this is not a reason to soften the point — it is the strongest possible evidence that the point is real. A translator does not reverse a sentence he has not noticed. The Greek’s discomfort tells you the Hebrew’s denial was felt to be pointed within a few centuries of its writing. Preach the Hebrew, and if you mention the Greek at all, mention it as corroboration.

Pitfall: the numerical sayings preached as lists of vices

  • What it looks like: “four things that will never satisfy you — and don’t we know it.”
  • Why it’s wrong: three of the four insatiables (Sheol, the barren womb, parched land) are not moral failings at all, and the barren womb is a grief, not a sin. [T] Reading the list moralistically requires ignoring three-quarters of it.
  • The corrective: the saying is about the structure of appetite — that some things in the created order are constitutionally unfillable — and the reason it is here is 30:9. It is an observation offered to a man who has just prayed not to be full.

Pitfall: treating 30:22–23 as an embarrassment to be skipped

  • What it looks like: the reading stops at v.21 or the sermon moves briskly to the ants.
  • Why it’s wrong: the saying is doing positional work — it names the danger the reader is about to walk into at 31:1 — and skipping it leaves the class-and-gender objection unanswered in the minds of anyone who read ahead.
  • The corrective: śənûʾâ is Deuteronomy’s legal category, and the chapter answers itself at 30:28 with a lizard in a palace. See Difficult Verses. Both correctives are in the text.

Pitfall: reading 30:4 as an invitation to spiritual ascent

  • What it looks like: “have you gathered the wind? no — but God can, so trust him with your storms.”
  • Why it’s wrong: it converts a question whose answer is nobody into an encouragement, and it skips 30:5–6, which is Agur’s own answer: you do not ascend, you receive a word, and you do not add to it.
  • The corrective: let the question stay unanswered until John 3:13 answers it. The gospel’s answer to “who has ascended?” is “no one — but someone came down,” and that answer is worth waiting for.
