Point & Purpose: Proverbs 31:10–31

Sermon 9 of nine — the last — The closing poem: wisdom with a face Exegesis consumed: dig-deeper-proverbs-31-10to31 (27 September 2026) — fresh solo dig, [T] 212 · [I] 13 · [S] 31, the acrostic verified mechanically and five Wisdom chains lemma-verified against the whole book Also consumed: book-overview-proverbs v2.0 (Christological trajectory, preaching traps); point-purpose-proverbs-series-introduction v3 (the series Big Question and the nine answers to it) Study text: NASB95 · Pulpit text: NIV84 (1984 edition), declared for the first engagement Version: v2 — supersedes the August plan, which assumed the ESV and is archived Date: 27 September 2026

Phase 0 — Scope Decision

Single sermon, and the unit is fixed by its form rather than chosen. Twenty-two verses, one per letter of the alphabet, א to ת — verified mechanically this run: every verse’s first consonant matches its expected letter, with no gap, no doubling and no transposition [T], WLC. Unlike Psalm 145, which is missing its nun, and unlike the irregular acrostics of Lamentations, this one is complete.

Which settles the scope question before it is asked. You cannot preach a selection from an acrostic without contradicting the thing the form is saying. And the Leningrad layout agrees: there is no paragraph marker anywhere in the twenty-two verses — against nine in the forty-two verses of sermon 8’s unit, the densest stretch in the book [T], WLC. The poem is the longest undivided run in Proverbs.

How the twenty-two verses are handled. Four movements. Movements 1, 3 and 4 take eight verses between them and carry the sermon’s weight; the middle fifteen (31:13–27) are one movement, and most of them are read rather than expounded. That is the dig’s own reading of them: fifteen verses of wool, flax, bread, a field, a lamp and a spindle, and not one line in the poem says any of it is remarkable — the evaluation is held back to 31:29–30 and then given twice [T]. Read them at pace and let the ordinariness do its work. Stop twice: at 31:20 and at 31:26.

This sermon has a second job, and it should not be smuggled in at the end. It closes a nine-week series. The series Big Question has been asked in the same words for eight weeks, and this is the week it gets its answer [S: series introduction v3]. Budget time for that: the last three minutes are not a summary of the poem but the answer to the question.

1. Fallen Condition Focus

We want a verdict on our own life — and it is the one verdict we are structurally unable to pronounce. So we settle for what can be seen in a minute, and we stay silent about what we can see in each other.

Anchored, and the anchor is grammatical. There is exactly one imperative in twenty-two verses, it is the last line of the book, and it is not “be like her” [T]. Every verb from 31:11 to 31:30 is indicative or participial — she sought, made, rose, considered, planted, girded, stretched, opened, watches. 31:31 alone commands, and what it commands is that the audience speak about somebody else and pay her: תְּנוּ־לָהּ (“give to her”), plural [T].

And the book has been building to this for thirty chapters. 3:7 forbade self-certification; 20:9 asked who could say I have cleansed my heart and never answered it; 26:12 exposed the man wise in his own eyes; 30:12 showed a whole generation that passed its own examination; and 27:2 gave the rule outright — יְהַלֶּלְךָ זָר וְלֹא־פִיךָ (“let another praise you, and not your own mouth”) [T]. The poem is where the rule is finally kept: הלל (“to praise”) stands in nine verses of Proverbs and three of them are 31:28, 30 and 31, every one of somebody else speaking [T].

That is not a period problem. It is the position of anyone who has ever wanted to know whether their life has come to anything, and has no standing to say.

Secondary candidate, if the preacher takes the measuring problem head-on: we turn a portrait into a standard, and then use it on each other.

2. Teaching Point

The book that promised to make the simple wise does not end with a graduate — it ends with a life, described in the very words it used of Wisdom herself, and its one command is that you recognise it and say so out loud; because the verdict that makes a life count is never one you can pass on yourself, and the verdict God passes on his people was earned by his Son and has already been spoken.

Subject: how does a book about acquiring wisdom finish?

Complement: with wisdom shown as a life rather than a lecture, and a command — to the reader, not to her — to see it, say it and pay for it; grounded in a verdict God has already given in Christ.

Where it comes from. Three of the dig’s five headlines converge, and the third is new this run:

  • The poem is written in personified Wisdom’s vocabulary, and the overlap is countable. Five chains, each lemma-verified against the whole book [T], WLC: מִפְּנִינִים (“than jewels”) — 3:15, 8:11, 31:10, four verses in Proverbs; שַׁעַר (“gate”) — 1:21, 8:3 / 31:23, 31; שׂחק (“to laugh”) — 1:26, 8:30–31 / 31:25; אשׁר piel (“to call blessed”) and תמך (“to grasp”), both in 3:18 of Wisdom / 31:28 and 31:19; מצא (“to find”) — 3:13, 8:17, 8:35 / 31:10’s מִי יִמְצָא. The book does not end by describing a housewife. It ends by describing, in the words it has used of Wisdom herself, what wisdom looks like when it is a life rather than a lecture.
  • The poem performs 27:2 (above) — and it is the one place in the book where the rule is kept [T].
  • The book’s motto closes the book, and in a new grammatical form. 1:7 → 9:10 → 31:30, and the last instance uses the adjective יָרֵא predicated of a person, not the noun יִרְאָה naming a thing — which stands in fourteen verses and last appears at 23:17 [T]. The motto ends attached to somebody.

Why it is not moralism, and the gate is worth stating explicitly here because this is the passage in Proverbs most often preached as one. The sermon’s payoff is not imitation of a human figure. The poem issues her no command and the reader one, so a sermon that ends “be like her” has inverted the only grammar in the text [T]. What the hearer is asked to do — credit somebody else’s life — is not a way of earning anything; it is what a person is free to do once his own verdict is no longer in question. The gospel ground is precise: the praise in 31:31 is earned, and the verdict a Christian waits for is not — ὁ δὲ μὴ ἐργαζόμενος, πιστεύων δὲ ἐπὶ τὸν δικαιοῦντα τὸν ἀσεβῆ (“to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly”, Rom 4:5) [T], SBLGNT. And the shape of 31:28–31 — work assessed, in public, by somebody with the right to speak — is the shape of εὖ, δοῦλε ἀγαθὲ καὶ πιστέ (“well done, good and faithful servant”, Matt 25:21) [T], SBLGNT.

One more canonical note the Teaching Point rests on but the sermon need not labour. The trajectory the book has run — Wisdom personified as a woman (chs 1–9), then shown as a life (ch. 31) — is completed in the New Testament by wisdom identified as a person: ὃς ἐγενήθη σοφία ἡμῖν ἀπὸ θεοῦ (“who became for us wisdom from God”, 1 Cor 1:30) [T], SBLGNT. Moderate-to-high as a trajectory; it is a reading, not a citation, and should be offered as such.

3. Purpose Statement

To take the pen out of the congregation’s hand. To end nine weeks about becoming wise not with a self-assessment but with a command about somebody else — releasing those who have spent the series wondering whether they would pass, turning the capable outward, teaching the whole room what 31:30 says is worth crediting and what is not, and sending them out to say aloud, and to pay for, work they have been receiving in silence.

Differentiated.

Audience The change sought
The anxious believer To stop waiting for a verdict they were never able to issue. The book’s last word takes the pen out of their hand and gives it to someone else — and in Christ that verdict is already spoken.
The capable believer To discover that eight weeks have been training an eye, not building a CV. If you cannot see wisdom in the room, the book has not worked on you.
Husbands, parents, employers, elders To obey 31:31 this week. It is a command about public speech and money, and it can be done on Monday.
The unbeliever To be shown a life that is plainly worth something, and told what made it so — and that it is not what the culture prices (31:30).

The motive, and the poem supplies it. Not consequence but the fear of the LORD — the book’s motto, in its last appearance, at 31:30 [T]. For a Christian congregation the motive under 31:31 is that a hearer who knows his own standing is not competing: crediting someone else’s work costs nothing when your own verdict is not at stake.

4. Candidate Big Question

The series question, asked in the same words for the ninth time — and this is the week it is answered:

“How would you know if you were actually wise?”

This week’s answer, from this week’s text alone [S: series introduction v3]:

You wouldn’t. Somebody else would say it of you — and the book’s one closing command is that you be the somebody else.

Two alternative framings, if the preacher wants a question of this sermon’s own:

  • (Closer to 31:10, and the better opening if the sermon starts at the question) “Who can find a life like this — and would you know one if you were living next door to it?”
  • (Blunter, for the last week of a series) “Nine weeks on: who gets to say whether you have become wise?”

The series question is recommended, kept in its established wording. Ask it in the first two minutes as usual; do not answer it until movement 3.

5. Proposed Structure

Four movements. The arc runs FCF → TP → PS, and the sermon’s discipline is not to evaluate her until the poem does — which is at 31:29, seventeen verses in.

Movement 1 — The question the book has been asking all along (31:10–12)

Function: establish that this is not a poem about marriage but the book’s own question, asked one last time.

Open with מִי יִמְצָא (“who can find?”). Then say where the book has asked it before: 20:6, of a man who proclaims his own חֶסֶד — “but a faithful man who can find?” — and the two verses are the only places in Proverbs the question occurs [T]. A book that could not find one honest man now asks whether it can find one whole life.

Then the second colon, and this is where the congregation should sit up: וְרָחֹק מִפְּנִינִים מִכְרָהּ (“her worth is far above jewels”). They have heard that sentence twice already — at 3:15 and 8:11, both of Wisdom [T], and both versions in the pew carry it plainly (“more precious than rubies”). Say it explicitly: the book is describing this woman in the words it used of Wisdom herself. That single observation governs the whole sermon and it costs thirty seconds.

Close the movement on 31:11–12: her husband’s heart trusts — בטח, and this is the book’s last of eight [T] — and she does him good all the days of her life, which is the unit of measurement the poem works in.

Ask the series question here.

Movement 2 — The answer is a life, not a list (31:13–27)

Function: let fifteen verses of the ordinary accumulate, without grading anyone.

Read most of it. Wool and flax; merchant ships; rising while it is dark; a field considered and bought; a vineyard planted out of her own earnings; a lamp that does not go out; scarlet, fine linen and purple; garments made and sold. Do not stop to admire and do not stop to apply. The poem’s own restraint is the point — nowhere in these fifteen verses does it say any of this is impressive.

Stop twice.

At 31:19–20, and this is the movement’s hinge. The two verses are built on one skeleton and differ only in where the hands go: יָדֶיהָ שִׁלְּחָה … וְכַפֶּיהָ (31:19) ‖ כַּפָּהּ … וְיָדֶיהָ שִׁלְּחָה (31:20) — the same two nouns for hand and the same verb, turned first to the spindle and then to the poor [T]. 31:20 is the only verse in the poem where anything leaves the house without being sold. And the two words it turns toward, עָנִי (“afflicted”) and אֶבְיוֹן (“needy”), occur together three times in the book’s last two chapters — 30:14, 31:9, 31:20 — and nowhere at all in the 138 verses of 25:1–29:27 [T]. Last week’s unit ended by commanding a king to defend them; this week a household does it. See the pulpit-text note before you preach this: the NIV84 cannot show the mirror.

At 31:26. פִּיהָ פָּתְחָה בְחָכְמָה (“she opens her mouth in wisdom”) — her only sentence in twenty-two verses, and the last of the book’s four פתח verses: 24:7, where a fool does not open his mouth in the gate; 31:8 and 31:9, where a king is twice commanded to; and here [T]. And what is on her tongue is תּוֹרַת־חֶסֶד (“the teaching of covenant-kindness”) — a phrase found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, carrying the book’s last תּוֹרָה and one of its ten חֶסֶד verses [T]. Note what she does not say: she never speaks about herself.

Movement 3 — Somebody else says it (31:28–29)

Function: answer the series question, and do it from the text.

The children rise and call her blessed — אשׁר in the piel, the verb of 3:18, וְתֹמְכֶיהָ מְאֻשָּׁר, of Wisdom [T]. Then the husband, and this is the poem’s only direct speech and its only second-person address: וְאַתְּ עָלִית עַל־כֻּלָּנָה (“but you have gone up above them all”) [T].

Say the first half of 31:29 out loud before the second. רַבּוֹת בָּנוֹת עָשׂוּ חָיִל — “many daughters have done worthily” [T]. The verse is a superlative built on a concession: it grants the achievement of many before naming one. That half-line is the poem’s own guard against being used to shame anybody, and it is routinely skipped.

Then answer the question. Seventeen verses have described her and she has not evaluated herself once. The evaluation, when it comes, is in somebody else’s mouth — which is precisely what 27:2 required and what the book has spent thirty chapters saying no man can do for himself. You would never know. Somebody else would say it of you.

Movement 4 — What counts, and the one command (31:30–31)

Function: land the Purpose Statement, and close the book.

31:30 first, and handle it with care. שֶׁקֶר הַחֵן וְהֶבֶל הַיֹּפִי — and the two words are about reliability and duration, not about sin. הֶבֶל is a breath, Ecclesiastes’ word [T]. The verse does not condemn charm and beauty; it discounts them as evidence. Then the contrast, with no conjunction in the Hebrew at all: אִשָּׁה יִרְאַת־יְהוָה הִיא תִתְהַלָּל. The book’s motto, in its last appearance, predicated of a person [T]. This is the verse that must not be detached and must not be softened.

Then 31:31, and it is the book’s last line. תְּנוּ־לָהּ מִפְּרִי יָדֶיהָ (“give her of the fruit of her hands”) — the only imperative in the poem, plural, and addressed to us [T]. It is remuneration language, not applause: the same words that in 31:16 bought a field — מִפְּרִי כַפֶּיהָ, “from the fruit of her palms” — come back to be paid [T]. And the venue is בַשְּׁעָרִים, in the gates — where Wisdom cried in 1:21 and 8:3 and got no recorded answer, and where 24:7’s fool could not open his mouth [T]. The book’s first scene and its last are the same place, and this time somebody speaks.

Close on the gospel ground, briefly and without decoration. The praise here is earned, and it is given by other people. The verdict a Christian most needs is neither — it is spoken by the one with the right to speak it, over people who did not earn it (Rom 4:5), and it is the same shape as this scene: well done (Matt 25:21), said about work, out loud, by somebody else. That is why a Christian is free to obey 31:31: when your own verdict is settled, crediting someone else’s life costs you nothing.

And then stop. Do not add a summary of the series. The book’s own last word is a command to go and say something about somebody; end with it.

6. Keep Honest

1. Do not turn the portrait into a standard — and have the three defences ready, because somebody in the room will assume you are about to. This is the book-level trap [S: overview], and the dig gives it three textual answers rather than a pastoral apology. Genre: an acrostic is a completeness-claim about its subject, not a checklist for its reader. Grammar: no command to her, one to the audience [T]. Vocabulary: the words are Wisdom’s, so the subject is wisdom-embodied, not wifely performance [T]. A useful sentence from the front, early: “the only person commanded to do anything in this poem is you.”

2. Do not preach 31:30 as a rebuke to women who care how they look. שֶׁקֶר and הֶבֶל are claims about unreliability and transience [T]. The verse discounts charm and beauty as evidence; it does not condemn them as sins, and it cuts hardest against the person doing the assessing. The New Testament’s two echoes of this verse — 1 Pet 3:3–4 and 1 Tim 2:9–10, both in Greek that touches the Greek of Prov 31 [T] — make the same argument about what lasts, not about what is forbidden. If you use them, say so.

3. Do not let “many daughters have done worthily” disappear. 31:29’s first colon concedes the achievement of many [T]. A sermon that uses her to shame the room has cut the verse in half.

4. Do not allegorise her — and do not end the sermon where the Greek ended the book. The text marks nothing as figurative; the spindle is a spindle. And note what the ancient tradition did with the ending: Swete’s last line of Proverbs reads “give her of the fruit of her lips, and let her husband be praised in the gates” [T], Swete. The impulse to redirect the praise is two thousand years old, and the Hebrew resists it. If the sermon lands on the husband, it has quietly taken the Greek’s ending.

5. Do not preach it as the book’s nice afterthought. Seven of the book’s chains make their last appearance here — בטח, תּוֹרָה, חֶסֶד, הלל, מַעֲשֶׂה, הֶבֶל, יָרֵא [T]; the motto lands here; 27:2 is performed here; 31:10’s question is the book’s own from 20:6. This is the designed ending. Preach it as the last verse of a thirty-one-chapter argument, and say what the argument was.

The dig lists seven pitfalls; the two not carried here — the Mother’s Day framing and the NIV84-specific one — are covered under the Purpose Statement and §7 respectively.

7. Pulpit-text Note (NIV84)

Seven divergences bear on the plan. Two will cost a movement a finding; three run the other way and the pulpit text is the better witness.

Problem — 31:19–20, and it is movement 2’s hinge. The Hebrew repeats two nouns and one verb across the two verses; NASB95 keeps it (“stretches out her hands” at 31:19 and again at 31:20); NIV84 uses four different verbs — “In her hand she holds the distaff and grasps the spindle with her fingers” / “She opens her arms to the poor and extends her hands to the needy” [T]. What the congregation will hear: two unrelated verses about industry and charity. The mirror is invisible in the NIV84. Read 31:19–20 from the NASB95, or say plainly that the Hebrew uses the same two words twice.

Problem — 31:26, and it closes the mouth-chain that began in sermon 8. The Hebrew is פִּיהָ פָּתְחָה בְחָכְמָה, the last of the book’s four פתח verses. NASB95 keeps the mouth at all four: 24:7 “he does not open his mouth in the gate”; 31:8 “Open your mouth for the mute”; 31:9 “Open your mouth, judge righteously”; 31:26 “She opens her mouth in wisdom”. NIV84 keeps it at none: “he has nothing to say” / “Speak up” / “Speak up” / “She speaks with wisdom” [T]. Four for four. If movement 2 wants the chain — and it should, since it is what joins last week’s sermon to this one — read 31:26 from the NASB95 and name what it completes. On the verse’s second colon the NIV84 is fairer than it first looks: “faithful instruction is on her tongue” does render תּוֹרַת־חֶסֶד; what it loses is weight, not content — the book’s last תּוֹרָה and a phrase unique in the Hebrew Bible, flattened to an adjective and a general noun.

Problem, minor — 31:31, movement 4’s hinge. NIV84: “Give her the reward she has earned” [T], against NASB95’s “Give her the product of her hands”. The imperative survives; the fruit does not — and with it the link back to 31:16’s מִפְּרִי כַפֶּיהָ, the poem’s other פְּרִי verse. One sentence fixes it: the Hebrew says “give her of the fruit of her hands”, and it is the same phrase she bought her field with.

Gift — 31:30, and it is the best news in this note. NASB95: “Charm is deceitful and beauty is vain.” NIV84: “Charm is deceptive, and beauty is fleeting.” [T] Fleeting is what הֶבֶל means, and vain in current English carries a moral charge the Hebrew does not. The pulpit text has done movement 4’s exegesis for you — the corrective in Keep honest §2 is half-made before you open your mouth.

Gift — 31:25. NIV84: “she can laugh at the days to come”; NASB95: “she smiles at the future” [T]. שׂחק is laugh, and it is the verb of 1:26 and 8:30–31. Here the study text has softened it and the pulpit text has not.

Gift — 31:28. NIV84 shows the Wisdom chain and the NASB95 breaks it: NIV84 has 3:18 “those who lay hold of her will be blessed” / 31:28 “Her children arise and call her blessed”; NASB95 has “happy are all who hold her fast” / “rise up and bless her” [T]. So two of the five Wisdom chains — שׂחק and אשׁר — are visible in the pulpit Bible and hidden in the study Bible. Movements 1 and 3 should read those two from the NIV84.

Worth a sentence, not a paragraph — 31:10. NIV84: “A wife of noble character”; NASB95: “An excellent wife” [T]. חַיִל is a strength-word — the word used of an army, and of Boaz at Ruth 2:1, אִישׁ גִּבּוֹר חַיִל. Every English version softens it. Both versions do carry the מִי יִמְצָא link to 20:6 in plain words (“who can find”), and both carry the rubies/jewels link to 3:15 and 8:11 — so movement 1’s two key observations are readable in the pew Bible.

And one thing the congregation’s own Bible will hand you. NIV84 prints an explicit headnote at 31:10: the verses are an acrostic, each beginning with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet [T]. Use it. It is the fastest way into the genre argument, and it is printed in front of them.

8. Hand-off

The Big Question is the series question in its established wording; the two alternatives above are candidates for the preacher or a one-sentence-sermon step. The four movements and their verse-ranges are ready for the manuscript.

Three notes for the manuscript.

Order. This document takes the study order — original script first, English gloss in brackets. A manuscript inverts it: the English leads and the Hebrew follows, because those pages are read aloud.

Which words to say out loud. This sermon turns on four: מִי יִמְצָא (“who can find?”), מִפְּנִינִים (“than jewels”), הֶבֶל (“a breath”) and תְּנוּ־לָהּ (“give to her”). My recommendation: speak two. מִפְּנִינִים in movement 1, because the whole sermon depends on the congregation hearing that 31:10 says what 3:15 and 8:11 said — and here the English can almost do it for you, since both versions have rubies or jewels in all three places, so the Hebrew is confirmation rather than rescue. And הֶבֶל in movement 4, because a congregation that hears it is Ecclesiastes’ breath-word will not hear 31:30 as a rebuke.

Which version to read from, movement by movement. 1 — either, and the NIV84 for 3:18 if you use it. 2 — NASB95 for 31:19–20 and 31:26. 3 — NIV84 for 31:28. 4 — NIV84 for 31:30, NASB95 for 31:31. Say once, early, that you will occasionally read from a different version and why; then do it without further comment.
