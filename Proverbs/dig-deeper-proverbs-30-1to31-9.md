**Series context:** Sermon 8 of nine. HIGH weight — the overview flags this unit as carrying the series' Christological weight at 30:2–4. Preceded by 25:1–29:27 (sermon 7, dug fresh 27 September); followed by 31:10–31.

**A note on the brief.** As with chapter 2, 22:17–24:34 and 25:1–29:27, the instruction was that the run should rest on "what is considered to be the majority text". The correction stands and matters more here than in any previous unit: **the Hebrew Bible has no majority text in the sense the term carries in New Testament criticism.** There is no competing family of Hebrew manuscripts against which a numerical majority could be counted. What exists is the *Masoretic tradition*, whose standard complete witness is Codex Leningradensis (B19a, 1008/9), which the Westminster Leningrad Codex transcribes and which *BHS* prints diplomatically. That manuscript is the substrate throughout.

It matters more here because **this is the unit where the Greek tradition and the Masoretic tradition diverge most sharply in the whole book.** The Greek does not have these forty-two verses in this place, does not have the names Agur or Lemuel at all, and reverses the sense of the confession at 30:3. The report says so at every point, classifies each divergence under the Three-Way Triage, and resolves none of them by a preference for either text.

### 30:1–33 — the words of Agur

**1** דִּבְרֵ֤י ׀ אָג֥וּר בִּן־יָקֶ֗ה הַמַּ֫שָּׂ֥א נְאֻ֣ם הַ֭גֶּבֶר לְאִֽיתִיאֵ֑ל לְאִ֖יתִיאֵ֣ל וְאֻכָֽל׃  
The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, the oracle. The man declares to Ithiel, to Ithiel and Ucal:

**2** כִּ֤י בַ֣עַר אָנֹכִ֣י מֵאִ֑ישׁ וְלֹֽא־בִינַ֖ת אָדָ֣ם לִֽי׃  
Surely I am more stupid than any man, And I do not have the understanding of a man.

**3** וְלֹֽא־לָמַ֥דְתִּי חָכְמָ֑ה וְדַ֖עַת קְדֹשִׁ֣ים אֵדָֽע׃  
Neither have I learned wisdom, Nor do I have the knowledge of the Holy One.

**4** מִ֤י עָלָֽה־שָׁמַ֨יִם ׀ וַיֵּרַ֡ד מִ֤י אָֽסַף־ר֨וּחַ ׀ בְּחָפְנָ֡יו מִ֤י צָֽרַר־מַ֨יִם ׀ בַּשִּׂמְלָ֗ה מִ֭י הֵקִ֣ים כָּל־אַפְסֵי־אָ֑רֶץ מַה־שְּׁמ֥וֹ וּמַֽה־שֶּׁם־בְּ֝נ֗וֹ כִּ֣י תֵדָֽע׃  
Who has ascended into heaven and descended? Who has gathered the wind in His fists? Who has wrapped the waters in His garment? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is His name or His son’s name? Surely you know!

**5** כָּל־אִמְרַ֣ת אֱל֣וֹהַּ צְרוּפָ֑ה מָגֵ֥ן ה֝֗וּא לַֽחֹסִ֥ים בּֽוֹ׃  
Every word of God is tested; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him.

**6** אַל־תּ֥וֹסְףְּ עַל־דְּבָרָ֑יו פֶּן־יוֹכִ֖יחַ בְּךָ֣ וְנִכְזָֽבְתָּ׃ **׃פ**  
Do not add to His words Or He will reprove you, and you will be proved a liar.

**7** שְׁ֭תַּיִם שָׁאַ֣לְתִּי מֵאִתָּ֑ךְ אַל־תִּמְנַ֥ע מִ֝מֶּ֗נִּי בְּטֶ֣רֶם אָמֽוּת׃  
Two things I asked of You, Do not refuse me before I die:

**8** שָׁ֤וְא ׀ וּֽדְבַר־כָּזָ֡ב הַרְחֵ֬ק מִמֶּ֗נִּי רֵ֣אשׁ וָ֭עֹשֶׁר אַל־תִּֽתֶּן־לִ֑י הַ֝טְרִיפֵ֗נִי לֶ֣חֶם חֻקִּֽי׃  
Keep deception and lies far from me, Give me neither poverty nor riches; Feed me with the food that is my portion,

**9** פֶּ֥ן אֶשְׂבַּ֨ע ׀ וְכִחַשְׁתִּי֮ וְאָמַ֗רְתִּי מִ֥י יְה֫וָ֥ה וּפֶֽן־אִוָּרֵ֥שׁ וְגָנַ֑בְתִּי וְ֝תָפַ֗שְׂתִּי שֵׁ֣ם אֱלֹהָֽי׃ **׃פ**  
That I not be full and deny You and say, “Who is the LORD?” Or that I not be in want and steal, And profane the name of my God.

**10** אַל־תַּלְשֵׁ֣ן עֶ֭בֶד אֶל־אדנו פֶּֽן־יְקַלֶּלְךָ֥ וְאָשָֽׁמְתָּ׃  
Do not slander a slave to his master, Or he will curse you and you will be found guilty.

**11** דּ֭וֹר אָבִ֣יו יְקַלֵּ֑ל וְאֶת־אִ֝מּ֗וֹ לֹ֣א יְבָרֵֽךְ׃  
There is a kind of man who curses his father And does not bless his mother.

**12** דּ֭וֹר טָה֣וֹר בְּעֵינָ֑יו וּ֝מִצֹּאָת֗וֹ לֹ֣א רֻחָֽץ׃  
There is a kind who is pure in his own eyes, Yet is not washed from his filthiness.

**13** דּ֭וֹר מָה־רָמ֣וּ עֵינָ֑יו וְ֝עַפְעַפָּ֗יו יִנָּשֵֽׂאוּ׃  
There is a kind—oh how lofty are his eyes! And his eyelids are raised in arrogance.

**14** דּ֤וֹר ׀ חֲרָב֣וֹת שִׁנָּיו֮ וּֽמַאֲכָל֪וֹת מְֽתַלְּעֹ֫תָ֥יו לֶאֱכֹ֣ל עֲנִיִּ֣ים מֵאֶ֑רֶץ וְ֝אֶבְיוֹנִ֗ים מֵאָדָֽם׃ **׃פ**  
There is a kind of man whose teeth are like swords And his jaw teeth like knives, To devour the afflicted from the earth And the needy from among men.

**15** לַֽעֲלוּקָ֨ה ׀ שְׁתֵּ֥י בָנוֹת֮ הַ֤ב ׀ הַ֥ב שָׁל֣וֹשׁ הֵ֭נָּה לֹ֣א תִשְׂבַּ֑עְנָה אַ֝רְבַּ֗ע לֹא־אָ֥מְרוּ הֽוֹן׃  
The leech has two daughters, “Give,” “Give.” There are three things that will not be satisfied, Four that will not say, “Enough”:

**16** שְׁאוֹל֮ וְעֹ֪צֶ֫ר רָ֥חַם אֶ֭רֶץ לֹא־שָׂ֣בְעָה מַּ֑יִם וְ֝אֵ֗שׁ לֹא־אָ֥מְרָה הֽוֹן׃  
Sheol, and the barren womb, Earth that is never satisfied with water, And fire that never says, “Enough.”

**17** עַ֤יִן ׀ תִּֽלְעַ֣ג לְאָב֮ וְתָב֪וּז לִֽיקֲּהַ֫ת־אֵ֥ם יִקְּר֥וּהָ עֹרְבֵי־נַ֑חַל וְֽיֹאכְל֥וּהָ בְנֵי־נָֽשֶׁר׃ **׃פ**  
The eye that mocks a father And scorns a mother, The ravens of the valley will pick it out, And the young eagles will eat it.

**18** שְׁלֹשָׁ֣ה הֵ֭מָּה נִפְלְא֣וּ מִמֶּ֑נִּי וארבע לֹ֣א יְדַעְתִּֽים׃  
There are three things which are too wonderful for me, Four which I do not understand:

**19** דֶּ֤רֶךְ הַנֶּ֨שֶׁר ׀ בַּשָּׁמַיִם֮ דֶּ֥רֶךְ נָחָ֗שׁ עֲלֵ֫י צ֥וּר דֶּֽרֶךְ־אֳנִיָּ֥ה בְלֶב־יָ֑ם וְדֶ֖רֶךְ גֶּ֣בֶר בְּעַלְמָֽה׃  
The way of an eagle in the sky, The way of a serpent on a rock, The way of a ship in the middle of the sea, And the way of a man with a maid.

**20** כֵּ֤ן ׀ דֶּ֥רֶךְ אִשָּׁ֗ה מְנָ֫אָ֥פֶת אָ֭כְלָה וּמָ֣חֲתָה פִ֑יהָ וְ֝אָמְרָ֗ה לֹֽא־פָעַ֥לְתִּי אָֽוֶן׃ **׃פ**  
This is the way of an adulterous woman: She eats and wipes her mouth, And says, “I have done no wrong.”

**21** תַּ֣חַת שָׁ֭לוֹשׁ רָ֣גְזָה אֶ֑רֶץ וְתַ֥חַת אַ֝רְבַּ֗ע לֹא־תוּכַ֥ל שְׂאֵֽת׃  
Under three things the earth quakes, And under four, it cannot bear up:

**22** תַּֽחַת־עֶ֭בֶד כִּ֣י יִמְל֑וֹךְ וְ֝נָבָ֗ל כִּ֣י יִֽשְׂבַּֽע־לָֽחֶם׃  
Under a slave when he becomes king, And a fool when he is satisfied with food,

**23** תַּ֣חַת שְׂ֭נוּאָה כִּ֣י תִבָּעֵ֑ל וְ֝שִׁפְחָ֗ה כִּֽי־תִירַ֥שׁ גְּבִרְתָּֽהּ׃ **׃פ**  
Under an unloved woman when she gets a husband, And a maidservant when she supplants her mistress.

**24** אַרְבָּ֣עָה הֵ֭ם קְטַנֵּי־אָ֑רֶץ וְ֝הֵ֗מָּה חֲכָמִ֥ים מְחֻכָּמִֽים׃  
Four things are small on the earth, But they are exceedingly wise:

**25** הַ֭נְּמָלִים עַ֣ם לֹא־עָ֑ז וַיָּכִ֖ינוּ בַקַּ֣יִץ לַחְמָֽם׃  
The ants are not a strong people, But they prepare their food in the summer;

**26** שְׁ֭פַנִּים עַ֣ם לֹא־עָצ֑וּם וַיָּשִׂ֖ימוּ בַסֶּ֣לַע בֵּיתָֽם׃  
The shephanim are not mighty people, Yet they make their houses in the rocks;

**27** מֶ֭לֶךְ אֵ֣ין לָאַרְבֶּ֑ה וַיֵּצֵ֖א חֹצֵ֣ץ כֻּלּֽוֹ׃  
The locusts have no king, Yet all of them go out in ranks;

**28** שְׂ֭מָמִית בְּיָדַ֣יִם תְּתַפֵּ֑שׂ וְ֝הִ֗יא בְּהֵ֣יכְלֵי מֶֽלֶךְ׃ **׃פ**  
The lizard you may grasp with the hands, Yet it is in kings’ palaces.

**29** שְׁלֹשָׁ֣ה הֵ֭מָּה מֵיטִ֣יבֵי צָ֑עַד וְ֝אַרְבָּעָ֗ה מֵיטִ֥בֵי לָֽכֶת׃  
There are three things which are stately in their march, Even four which are stately when they walk:

**30** לַ֭יִשׁ גִּבּ֣וֹר בַּבְּהֵמָ֑ה וְלֹא־יָ֝שׁ֗וּב מִפְּנֵי־כֹֽל׃  
The lion which is mighty among beasts And does not retreat before any,

**31** זַרְזִ֣יר מָתְנַ֣יִם אוֹ־תָ֑יִשׁ וּ֝מֶ֗לֶךְ אַלְק֥וּם עִמּֽוֹ׃  
The strutting rooster, the male goat also, And a king when his army is with him.

**32** אִם־נָבַ֥לְתָּ בְהִתְנַשֵּׂ֑א וְאִם־זַ֝מּ֗וֹתָ יָ֣ד לְפֶֽה׃  
If you have been foolish in exalting yourself Or if you have plotted evil, put your hand on your mouth.

**33** כִּ֤י מִ֪יץ חָלָ֡ב י֘וֹצִ֤יא חֶמְאָ֗ה וּֽמִיץ־אַ֭ף י֣וֹצִיא דָ֑ם וּמִ֥יץ אַ֝פַּ֗יִם י֣וֹצִיא רִֽיב׃ **׃פ**  
For the churning of milk produces butter, And pressing the nose brings forth blood; So the churning of anger produces strife.

### 31:1–9 — the words of Lemuel's mother

**1** דִּ֭בְרֵי לְמוּאֵ֣ל מֶ֑לֶךְ מַ֝שָּׂ֗א אֲֽשֶׁר־יִסְּרַ֥תּוּ אִמּֽוֹ׃  
The words of King Lemuel, the oracle which his mother taught him:

**2** מַה־בְּ֭רִי וּמַֽה־בַּר־בִּטְנִ֑י וּ֝מֶה בַּר־נְדָרָֽי׃  
What, O my son? And what, O son of my womb? And what, O son of my vows?

**3** אַל־תִּתֵּ֣ן לַנָּשִׁ֣ים חֵילֶ֑ךָ וּ֝דְרָכֶ֗יךָ לַֽמְח֥וֹת מְלָכִֽין׃  
Do not give your strength to women, Or your ways to that which destroys kings.

**4** אַ֤ל לַֽמְלָכִ֨ים ׀ לְֽמוֹאֵ֗ל אַ֣ל לַֽמְלָכִ֣ים שְׁתוֹ־יָ֑יִן וּ֝לְרוֹזְנִ֗ים או שֵׁכָֽר׃  
It is not for kings, O Lemuel, It is not for kings to drink wine, Or for rulers to desire strong drink,

**5** פֶּן־יִ֭שְׁתֶּה וְיִשְׁכַּ֣ח מְחֻקָּ֑ק וִֽ֝ישַׁנֶּה דִּ֣ין כָּל־בְּנֵי־עֹֽנִי׃  
For they will drink and forget what is decreed, And pervert the rights of all the afflicted.

**6** תְּנוּ־שֵׁכָ֣ר לְאוֹבֵ֑ד וְ֝יַיִן לְמָ֣רֵי נָֽפֶשׁ׃  
Give strong drink to him who is perishing, And wine to him whose life is bitter.

**7** יִ֭שְׁתֶּה וְיִשְׁכַּ֣ח רִישׁ֑וֹ וַ֝עֲמָל֗וֹ לֹ֣א יִזְכָּר־עֽוֹד׃  
Let him drink and forget his poverty And remember his trouble no more.

**8** פְּתַח־פִּ֥יךָ לְאִלֵּ֑ם אֶל־דִּ֝֗ין כָּל־בְּנֵ֥י חֲלֽוֹף׃  
Open your mouth for the mute, For the rights of all the unfortunate.

**9** פְּתַח־פִּ֥יךָ שְׁפָט־צֶ֑דֶק וְ֝דִ֗ין עָנִ֥י וְאֶבְיֽוֹן׃ **׃פ**  
Open your mouth, judge righteously, And defend the rights of the afflicted and needy.

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

1. **Agur denies, in the book's own exact words, what the prologue held out — and the phrase he denies occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Repetition, Quotation/Allusion (Move 4), Structure, Christological Reading. דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים ("the knowledge of the Holy One") stands in exactly **two verses of the entire Hebrew Bible**: 9:10, תְּחִלַּת חָכְמָה יִרְאַת יְהוָה וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים בִּינָה ("the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the LORD, and the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding"), and 30:3, וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע ("neither have I learned wisdom, nor do I know the knowledge of the Holy One") `[T]`, WLC, whole-corpus search with a passed completeness control. And **all three of the book's great wisdom nouns occur in this unit exactly once each — חָכְמָה at 30:3, בִּינָה at 30:2, דַּעַת at 30:3 — and every one of the three is negated** `[T]`, against 38, 14 and 39 verses respectively across the book. 30:2–3 is not a modest disclaimer. It is the prologue's climactic sentence taken apart term by term.

2. **Both of this unit's superscriptions carry vocabulary no other heading in Proverbs uses, and one of them is the prophets' own word.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Structure, Quotation/Allusion, Genre. מַשָּׂא stands in exactly two verses of Proverbs — 30:1 and 31:1, the two headings of this unit `[T]`, WLC. נְאֻם ("the utterance of") stands in exactly one — 30:1 `[T]`. And **נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר ("the utterance of the man") occurs four times in the Hebrew Bible: Num 24:3 and 24:15 (Balaam), 2 Sam 23:1 (David's last words), and Prov 30:1** `[T]`, verified on the complete 23,213-verse corpus. All four introduce a final, oracular utterance by a man who stands outside the ordinary prophetic line. **The honest qualification, and it is *BHS*'s own:** מַשָּׂא may be the place-name Massa (cf. Gen 25:14), and *BHS* directs reading מֶלֶךְ מַשָּׂא ("king of Massa") at 31:1 and proposes a Massaite reading at 30:1 `[S: BHS]`. Either way the word marks this unit off from every heading before it; which sense it carries is genuinely open.

3. **The Greek reverses Agur's confession and has neither Agur nor Lemuel in it at all.** Surfaced by: Translations, Textual Variants, Bible Timeline, Vocabulary. Swete's Prov 24:26 — the verse that answers to MT 30:3 — reads **θεὸς δεδίδαχέν με σοφίαν, καὶ γνῶσιν ἁγίων ἔγνωκα** ("God has taught me wisdom, and I have known the knowledge of the holy ones") `[T]`, Swete; *BHS* records it and reconstructs a Hebrew behind it, (וְ)אֵל לִמַּד אֹתִי (apparatus 30:3^{a–a}) `[S: BHS]`. And **Ἀγούρ, Λεμουήλ, Ἰακή and Ἰθιήλ do not occur anywhere in Swete's Proverbs** — verified by a normalised whole-book search with the known Greek proper names (Σαλωμῶν, Δαυείδ, Ἰσραήλ, Ἑζεκίας, Ἰουδαία) passing as controls `[T]`, Swete. **The two outsiders on whom this sermon turns exist in the Hebrew tradition and not in the Greek one.** Category 2, and undecided.

4. **30:4's question is the one John 3:13 answers, and Proverbs is the only candidate in its field whose ascender is also its descender.** Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion (full Triad), Translations, Christological Reading, Biblical Theology. Swete's Prov 24:27 reads **τίς ἀνέβη εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ κατέβη;** ("who has gone up into heaven and come down?") `[T]`, Swete. John 3:13: **οὐδεὶς ἀναβέβηκεν εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εἰ μὴ ὁ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβάς, ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** `[T]`, SBLGNT. The two nearest rivals do not have the shape: Deut 30:12 LXX has an ascent only (τίς ἀναβήσεται ἡμῖν εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν), and Bar 3:29 has someone ascend and bring *wisdom* down (κατεβίβασεν αὐτήν) `[T]`, Swete. **Only Proverbs asks for one person who went up and came down — which is exactly the person John supplies.** *High confidence on the verbal and formal contact; moderate-to-high that John has this verse specifically in view rather than the family.* **And the qualification a sermon must carry:** 30:4's מַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ ("what is his son's name?") is **singular in the Masoretic text and plural in the Greek** — τοῖς τέκνοις αὐτοῦ, which *BHS* records as reflecting בָּנָו (apparatus 30:4ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`. The Christological reading of "his son" rests on the Hebrew.

5. **The unit frames itself on the two words for the destitute that the previous collection had withdrawn entirely.** Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Structure, Repetition, So What?, Biblical Theology. עָנִי ("afflicted") and אֶבְיוֹן ("needy") stand in two verses each here — **30:14 and 31:9** — and in none of the 138 verses of 25:1–29:27 `[T]`, WLC, both counts positively controlled. 30:14 is a generation whose teeth are swords לֶאֱכֹל עֲנִיִּים מֵאֶרֶץ וְאֶבְיוֹנִים מֵאָדָם ("to devour the afflicted from the earth and the needy from among men"); 31:9 is the unit's last line, a king commanded וְדִין עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן ("and plead the cause of the afflicted and needy") `[T]`. **Predators at one end and an advocate at the other, and the book's closing instruction to a king is that he should be the second.**

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview's microscript governs and this unit completes it: wisdom in Proverbs is something a man acquires, and the one thing he cannot do is certify that he has acquired it `[S: overview]`. The previous seven units built the case. **This one produces the first man in the book who gets his own self-assessment right — and he does it by reporting that he has none.**

**Unit level, and the text states more here than anywhere else in the book.** The two superscriptions do three things no other heading does:

- **They name outsiders.** אָגוּר בִּן־יָקֶה and לְמוּאֵל מֶלֶךְ are the only named human speakers in Proverbs besides Solomon, David and Hezekiah, and unlike those three they are attached to no Israelite institution `[T]`. What tribe, nation or period either belongs to the text does not say; that they are non-Israelite is **reconstruction** `[S]`, however widely held, and rests chiefly on the מַשָּׂא question (headline 2).
- **They use the language of oracle.** מַשָּׂא twice, נְאֻם once, and both words are otherwise absent from the book `[T]`. Whatever else these two speakers are, the headings present their material as *given* rather than as *gathered* — which is the opposite of what 25:1 said about the collection immediately before `[T]`.
- **They credit a woman with the teaching.** 31:1's relative clause is אֲשֶׁר־יִסְּרַתּוּ אִמּוֹ ("with which his mother instructed him"), and the verb is **יסר, the root behind מוּסָר** — the second noun of the book's purpose statement (1:2) `[T]`. The noun מוּסָר occurs in thirty verses of Proverbs and in none of these forty-two; the verb occurs in five, and **31:1 is its last appearance in the book** `[T]`, WLC.

**What the unit is doing.** Two purposes, one per speaker, and they are not the same.

**Agur's** is *epistemic*. He states the limit (30:2–3), asks the four questions that mark it (30:4), names the one thing that is reliable inside the limit (30:5–6, the word of God), prays for the economic conditions under which a man at the limit can survive (30:7–9), and then — for twenty-four verses — does nothing but *list*. Six numerical sayings, each of the form "three… and four", each a bare observation with no verdict attached `[T]`. **A man who has said he does not know proceeds to number what he has seen.** That is not a contradiction; it is the only kind of wisdom his confession leaves available.

**Lemuel's mother's** is *governmental*. Her nine verses are all imperative or prohibitive, all addressed to a king, and all about the mouth: what must not go into it (31:4–5, wine), what should (31:6–7, given to others), and what must come out of it (31:8–9, twice פְּתַח־פִּיךָ, "open your mouth") `[T]`. **She is the only voice in Proverbs that tells a king what to do rather than describing what kings are like.**

**The purpose of the whole, stated as an inference:** to close the book's instructional argument by putting two people outside the system in the position of teacher — one who says the system did not produce wisdom in him, and one who applies it to the single office the book has watched from a distance for twenty-nine chapters `[I]`, high confidence; every element is a counted or stated feature of the text.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** 25:1–29:27 removed the teacher, withdrew the book's wisdom vocabulary, reduced the divine name to six verses in 138, and left the reader with anonymous cases and no invigilator. Its self-assessment thread produced four men who judged themselves and were wrong (26:5, 12, 16; 28:11), and its last verse handed over two opposed verdicts without adjudicating (29:27) `[T]`. Behind that, 20:9's question — מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי ("who can say, 'I have cleansed my heart'?") — has stood unanswered since chapter 20.

**Necessity answer.** Three things, and the third is the one a sermon needs.

First, **somebody has to report a result.** The previous collection demonstrated that a reader *can* be left to judge; it never showed a reader who *had* judged and what he concluded. 30:1–3 is exactly that: a named man, at the end of the examination, reporting his mark. **He fails it, and in failing it he is the first man in the book to assess himself accurately.**

Second, **20:9's question finally gets an answer, and the answer is the wrong one.** 30:12 — דּוֹר טָהוֹר בְּעֵינָיו וּמִצֹּאָתוֹ לֹא רֻחָץ ("a generation pure in its own eyes, yet not washed from its filth") — is a whole generation saying what 20:9 asked whether anyone could say. The overview identifies this as the book's sharpest unadvertised echo `[S: overview]`, and this run confirms it independently: 20:9 uses the verb טהר and 30:12 the adjective טָהוֹר, with רחץ at 30:12 sealing the link `[T]`, WLC. **The question is answered by being answered falsely** — which is why the book cannot stop here and has one more chapter to go.

Third, **the material has to change kind before the book can end.** 31:10–31 displays a life. A display is only meaningful if the reader has been told he cannot generate it, and 30:2–4 is where he is told. Position is the argument: confession, then portrait.

**Implication.** The sermon on this unit is not "two eccentric appendices". It is the point at which Proverbs says out loud that its own programme does not produce what it promised, and then looks past itself for the name.

**Revisited after Tool 11.** The Move 4 work adds a fourth answer. 9:10 is the last verse of the prologue's argument and the summary of everything chapters 1–9 have claimed. Its distinctive phrase — דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים, found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible — reappears exactly once, at 30:3, negated `[T]`. **The book plants its thesis at the end of the prologue and revokes it in the mouth of the second-to-last speaker.** A structure that does that is not an anthology with appendices.

#### Immediate context

**Before.** 29:27, the close of the Hezekiah collection, with a paragraph marker after it. The two verses either side of the seam are as far apart in method as any two adjacent verses in the book: 29:27 is a symmetrical third-person aphorism with no speaker; 30:1 is a named man's first-person oracle `[T]`.

**After.** 31:10–31, the acrostic, which **carries no paragraph marker anywhere in its twenty-two verses** — the only stretch of that length in the book that the Leningrad layout leaves undivided `[T]`, WLC. Against the nine markers in these forty-two verses, the contrast is stark: this unit is the most heavily divided material in Proverbs and the one that follows is the least.

**Section and book.** Two of the book's seven headed sections, taken together as one preaching unit. That pairing is the series' decision, not the text's: 30:1 and 31:1 are separate headings, and 31:1–9 could stand alone `[I]`. What justifies the pairing is the shared מַשָּׂא, the shared outsider-status, and the עָנִי/אֶבְיוֹן frame that runs from 30:14 to 31:9 `[T]`.

### 3. Structure

**What the transmitted layout gives, and here it agrees with the vocabulary.** The Leningrad layout marks nine paragraph breaks in these forty-two verses — after 30:6, 9, 14, 17, 20, 23, 28, 33 and 31:9 `[T]`, WLC. That is **one break every 4.7 verses**, against one every 17 in 25:1–29:27 and one every 94 in 10:1–22:16 `[T]`. **This is by a wide margin the most heavily divided material in the book.**

And unlike in 25–29, the markers and the content agree. Every one of the numerical sayings is closed by a marker, and the two superscriptions open blocks that markers close:

| Block | Marker after | What the vocabulary says |
|---|---|---|
| 30:1–6 | 30:6 | Superscription, confession, four questions, the word of God |
| 30:7–9 | 30:9 | The two requests — שְׁתַּיִם ("two"), the only non-3/4 number |
| 30:10–14 | 30:14 | דּוֹר ("generation") four times (30:11, 12, 13, 14) |
| 30:15–17 | 30:17 | The leech: two / three / four |
| 30:18–20 | 30:20 | Three / four too wonderful, plus the adulteress |
| 30:21–23 | 30:23 | Three / four the earth cannot bear |
| 30:24–28 | 30:28 | Four small and wise |
| 30:29–33 | 30:33 | Three / four stately, plus the closing warning |
| 31:1–9 | 31:9 | Superscription, and the mother's instruction |

`[unchecked — apparatus spread]` on the markers themselves: *parashoth* are a feature of the manuscript's layout, not of the composition, and the corpus holds one manuscript tradition and prints them without variants. **But the agreement between layout and vocabulary is itself the argument** — the block boundaries can be derived from the numbers and the catchwords without the markers, and the markers then fall in the same places. Confidence: high on the vocabulary-derived structure; moderate, and capped, on any claim that rests on the markers alone.

**The numerical form, counted.** שָׁלוֹשׁ ("three") occurs in four verses of Proverbs and **all four are in chapter 30** (30:15, 18, 21, 29); אַרְבַּע ("four") in five, and **all five are in chapter 30** (30:15, 18, 21, 24, 29) `[T]`, WLC, lemma-verified. The only other x/x+1 saying in the book is 6:16, שֵׁשׁ־הֵנָּה שָׂנֵא יְהוָה וְשֶׁבַע ("six things the LORD hates, and seven"), which uses a different pair of numbers `[T]`. So **the 3/4 form is unique to this chapter, and the prologue planted the form once with different numbers.** The overview flags 6:16–19 as the seed `[S: overview]`; the corpus confirms the distribution and shows the numbers differ.

*A methodological note that belongs here.* A phrase search for this form, run as `שלוש` + `ארבע`, **returned a false nil on 30:18 and 30:29 and its positive control failed** — because those two verses spell the numeral defectively (שְׁלֹשָׁה, consonantally שלשה) while 30:15 and 30:21 spell it plene. The figures above are from the lemma index, which is immune. This is the plene/defective failure gate item (e3) names, caught by the control.

**The macro-shape.** Agur's material moves from the first person to the third and very nearly never comes back. **First-person verbs stand in exactly four of his thirty-three verses — 30:3, 7, 9 and 18** `[T]`, WLC (a fifth, 30:20's פָעַלְתִּי, belongs to the adulteress, not to Agur). Three of the four are in the opening confession and prayer; **the fourth, 30:18's נִפְלְאוּ מִמֶּנִּי … לֹא יְדַעְתִּים, is the last time he speaks of himself, and it is another admission of not knowing.** **The confession is front-loaded, the man then disappears behind his lists, and the one time he reappears he says the same thing again** — which is the formal counterpart of what he claimed about himself.

**The unit's frame.** 30:14 and 31:9 are the only verses in these forty-two, and in the whole of 25:1–31:9, that carry עָנִי and אֶבְיוֹן `[T]`. A unit that opens its non-confessional material on a generation devouring the poor and closes its last line on a king pleading for them is framed, and framed on the one subject the previous 138 verses had dropped.

### 4. Linking Words

Sentence collections run on juxtaposition; this unit does not, and the difference is a finding.

- **כִּי ("for, surely") at 30:2, 30:22, 30:23, 30:33** — and the first is the most important connector in the unit. 30:2 opens **כִּי בַעַר אָנֹכִי מֵאִישׁ**, which makes the confession the *ground* of something. Of what? The superscription has just ended; grammatically the כִּי hangs. **NASB95 renders it "Surely", NIV84 drops it entirely**, and both are reasonable — but the Hebrew presents 30:2–3 as an explanation, not an announcement, and the thing it explains is most naturally the questions that follow at 30:4 `[I]`, moderate confidence.
- **פֶּן ("lest") at 30:6, 30:9 (twice), 30:10, 31:5** — five occurrences, and each closes a block. 30:6 lest he reprove you; 30:9 lest I be full, and lest I be poor; 30:10 lest he curse you; 31:5 lest they drink and forget `[T]`. **The particle that governed the warnings of 25–26 here governs the requests and the instructions**, and 30:9's double פֶּן is the hinge of Agur's prayer: two dangers, one on each side of sufficiency.
- **מִי ("who") four times in 30:4**, and once more at 30:9 — מִי יְהוָה. **The interrogative that asks who made the world is the same one the full man uses to deny him** `[T]`. That is deliberate, and neither English version can show it, because 30:4's מִי is "who has…" and 30:9's is "Who is…".
- **אִם … וְאִם ("if … and if") at 30:32** — the unit's only conditional pair, closing Agur's material with a two-branch warning that resolves into one gesture: יָד לְפֶה ("hand to mouth").
- **אַל ("do not") at 30:6, 30:8, 30:10; 31:3, 31:4 (twice)** — six prohibitions, four of them in the two superscribed speeches' instructional cores `[T]`.

### 5. Parallels

**The dominant figure is the *list*, which is not parallelism at all**, and that is the observation. Six numerical sayings (30:15b–16, 18–19, 21–23, 24–28, 29–31, and the 2/3/4 of 30:15a) each state a number, raise it by one, and then enumerate — a form in which the members are *coordinate* rather than *paired* `[T]`. Proverbs has spent twenty-nine chapters on bicola; this chapter spends twenty-four verses on catalogues.

Three parallels do repay the preacher's attention.

- **30:2–3, a four-fold negation in two verses.** כִּי בַעַר אָנֹכִי מֵאִישׁ / וְלֹא־בִינַת אָדָם לִי // וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה / וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע. Three explicit לֹא and a fourth clause whose polarity is the crux of the passage (Tool 8). **The parallelism is cumulative, not synonymous: brutish → no human understanding → no learned wisdom → no knowledge of the Holy One.** The scale rises from the animal to the divine and the denial holds at every level `[T]`.
- **30:5–6, the only two-verse positive statement in Agur's material**, and it is built as a chiasm of sources: *every word of God* is tested / *he* is a shield // do not add to *his words* / *he* will reprove you `[T]`. The subject alternates word–person–word–person. **The one thing Agur affirms is not a proposition but a person with a word.**
- **31:8–9, an exact anaphora.** פְּתַח־פִּיךָ לְאִלֵּם / … // פְּתַח־פִּיךָ שְׁפָט־צֶדֶק / … `[T]`. The repeated imperative opens both verses, and the second supplies what the first left as a bare object. **This is the only anaphoric pair in the unit and it is the last thing the mother says.**

**A deliberate break.** 30:19 lists four "ways" — the eagle, the serpent, the ship, and דֶּרֶךְ גֶּבֶר בְּעַלְמָה ("the way of a man with a maiden"). 30:20 then opens כֵּן ("so") and supplies a fifth that is not in the count: כֵּן דֶּרֶךְ אִשָּׁה מְנָאָפֶת ("so is the way of an adulterous woman") `[T]`. **The verse breaks its own numbering to attach an application**, and it is the only place in the six sayings where that happens. Whether 30:20 is Agur's own comment or a later gloss is not recoverable from the text `[I]`, and both English versions print it as a separate saying.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

**N/A** — no narrative frame and no voice standing outside the material. The two superscriptions are editorial, and are treated under Tools 1, 2 and 7 where they belong. **One near-exception worth naming:** 30:1's נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר is a third-person description of a first-person speaker, so the heading and the speech are in different voices — but that is a feature of the oracle form (compare Num 24:3; 2 Sam 23:1), not an authorial intrusion `[T]`.

**Three absences carry the reading.** צַדִּיק and רָשָׁע — the antithesis on which 10:1–15:33 and 28–29 run, 143 verses between them — **do not occur once in these forty-two verses** `[T]`. Nor does כְּסִיל, the book's commonest character `[T]`. Nor does מָשָׁל, the book's own word for what it contains `[T]`. **The categories by which Proverbs has sorted people for twenty-nine chapters are simply not in use here.** *High confidence — five lemma searches, each returning substantial figures elsewhere in the book, which is the positive control.*

**And three presences make the point.** The three wisdom nouns the book was written to supply occur once each and all three are inside a denial (headline 1) `[T]`. This is not a unit that has run out of wisdom vocabulary; it is a unit that uses it once, precisely, to say it has not got it.

#### 7b. The words peculiar to this unit

- **מַשָּׂא** (30:1; 31:1) — two occurrences in the book, both here `[T]`. Either "oracle, burden" (the prophets' genre-word: Isa 13:1; Nah 1:1; Hab 1:1; Mal 1:1) or the place-name Massa (Gen 25:14; 1 Chr 1:30, an Ishmaelite tribe). *BHS* directs מֶלֶךְ מַשָּׂא ("king of Massa") at 31:1 and proposes a Massaite reading at 30:1 (apparatus 31:1^{b–b}; 30:1ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`; both NASB95 and NIV84 print "oracle" in the text, and **NIV84 footnotes the alternative at both verses** `[T]`. *Genuinely open; see Open Questions.*
- **נְאֻם** (30:1) — one occurrence in the book `[T]`. See headline 2.
- **אֱלוֹהַּ** (30:5) — **one occurrence in Proverbs**, against 49 verses in the Hebrew Bible, the great majority of them in Job `[T]`, whole-corpus search. And the verse Agur is reworking, Ps 18:31 ‖ 2 Sam 22:31, has **יְהוָה** in that slot `[T]`. **So at the one point where he quotes Scripture, Agur substitutes the divine name characteristic of the book that stands immediately before Proverbs in the Tanak.** *High confidence on the substitution; moderate on its deliberateness — a translator's or tradition's levelling is also possible.*
- **קְדֹשִׁים** (30:3) — two occurrences in Proverbs, 9:10 and 30:3, and the phrase דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים is found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible `[T]`.
- **בַּעַר** ("brutish, stupid", 30:2) — four occurrences in the Hebrew Bible as this adjective: Ps 73:22, Ps 92:7, Prov 12:1, Prov 30:2 `[T]`. **Ps 73:22 is the closest: וַאֲנִי־בַעַר וְלֹא אֵדָע ("I was brutish and did not know")** — the same adjective, the same first person, the same negated ידע, in the psalm of a man who envied the wicked until he entered the sanctuary. *Moderate-to-high confidence on the link; the words are common enough that the coincidence is short of proof, but the construction is identical and the subject-matter is the same.*
- **דּוֹר** ("generation") — five verses in Proverbs, and **four of them are 30:11, 12, 13 and 14** (the fifth, 27:24, is the idiom לְדוֹר דּוֹר) `[T]`. So **"a generation" as the subject of a sentence occurs only in this block in the whole book.**
- **Hapax legomena.** עֲלוּקָה ("leech", 30:15), שְׂמָמִית ("lizard", 30:28), קְטַנֵּי־אָרֶץ ("small ones of the earth", 30:24), בְּנֵי חֲלוֹף ("sons of passing away", 31:8), בַּר־בִּטְנִי ("son of my womb", 31:2), הַב הַב ("give, give", 30:15) — each found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible `[T]`, whole-corpus searches with passed controls. **A forty-two-verse unit with six absolute hapaxes is doing something lexically unlike the rest of the book.**

#### 7c. Proper nouns — the inventory

Four names in forty-two verses, all in the two superscriptions: **אָגוּר, יָקֶה, אִיתִיאֵל** (30:1) and **לְמוּאֵל** (31:1), plus the disputed אֻכָל `[T]`. No place, people or nation; no figure from Israel's history. Against the previous unit's three names in 138 verses, this is dense; against any narrative it is nothing.

**The name-hyperlink check returns almost nothing, and the nil is a finding.** None of the four names occurs anywhere else in the Hebrew Bible `[T]`, whole-corpus search. There is no canonical history to load, no outstanding word attached, and no wordplay the text marks. **Agur and Lemuel have no story.** That is what makes them usable: the book ends its instruction by handing the microphone to two people about whom nothing can be looked up.

**One name-wordplay candidate, reported and not pressed.** לְמוּאֵל can be parsed לְמוֹ־אֵל ("belonging to God"), and 31:4 spells it לְמוֹאֵל, which makes the division visible `[T]`. *Uncertain* — the text does not gloss it, and a name meaning "to God" in a chapter that never mentions God is either pointed or accidental.

**One textual crux that is a proper-noun question.** 30:1's לְאִיתִיאֵל לְאִיתִיאֵל וְאֻכָל may be two proper names or a re-divided sentence. *BHS* lists six conjectural re-divisions (apparatus 30:1^{d–d}), among them לָאִיתִי אֵל ("I am weary, O God") and אִתִּי אֵל ("God is with me"), and settles none `[S: BHS]`. NASB95 transliterates; **NIV84 prints the names and footnotes "I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and faint"** `[T]`. See Preaching Pitfalls.

#### 7d. Two words that carry more than they look

- **לֶחֶם חֻקִּי** (30:8) — "the bread of my allotted portion". The phrase is a hapax `[T]`. חֹק is a *decreed measure*; the request is neither for plenty nor for subsistence but for a *ration*. **NASB95 keeps it ("the food that is my portion"); NIV84 renders it "my daily bread"**, which is not wrong and is not neutral (Tool 8).
- **מְחֻקָּק** (31:5) — pointed as a passive participle ("what is decreed"), and **the Cairo Codex points the same consonants as מְחֹקֵק** (*BHS* apparatus 31:5ᵇ) `[S: BHS]` — the word that stands at **Gen 49:10**, לֹא־יָסוּר שֵׁבֶט מִיהוּדָה וּמְחֹקֵק מִבֵּין רַגְלָיו, and at Isa 33:22, יְהוָה מְחֹקְקֵנוּ `[T]`. On the Cairo pointing, a drunken king forgets *the ruler's staff* rather than *the statute*. **Both English versions take the Masoretic pointing and neither hints at the alternative.** *Moderate confidence that the ambiguity is real; the consonants are identical and the vocalisation is a reading tradition, not the text — see gate item (e4).*

### 8. Translations

**Pulpit divergence note (NIV84).** Five divergences bear on findings; two of them will change what the congregation concludes.

1. **30:7 — NIV84 supplies a vocative the Hebrew does not have.** Hebrew: שְׁתַּיִם שָׁאַלְתִּי מֵאִתָּךְ ("two things I have asked of you"). NASB95: "Two things I asked of You, Do not refuse me before I die." **NIV84: "Two things I ask of you, *O LORD*."** `[T]` The Hebrew names nobody; the addressee is inferred from 30:5–6 and confirmed only at 30:9's מִי יְהוָה. *What the congregation will hear:* an explicitly addressed prayer, with the ambiguity already closed. Defensible, and it costs the preacher the observation that **Agur prays for four verses before he names the one he is praying to.**
2. **30:8 — NIV84 imports the Lord's Prayer.** Hebrew: הַטְרִיפֵנִי לֶחֶם חֻקִּי. NASB95: "Feed me with the food that is my portion." **NIV84: "give me only my daily bread."** `[T]` *What the congregation will hear:* Matthew 6:11, whether the preacher intends it or not. The conceptual link is real (Tool 11) but it is not a verbal one in either Hebrew or Greek, and NIV84 has made the decision in advance.
3. **31:8 — NIV84 paraphrases away the word that carries the allusion.** Hebrew: פְּתַח־פִּיךָ לְאִלֵּם ("open your mouth for the mute"). NASB95 keeps both. **NIV84: "Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves."** `[T]` The paraphrase is accurate and loses אִלֵּם, and with it the link to **Exod 4:11**, מִי־יָשׂוּם אִלֵּם ("who makes a man mute?") — God's answer to Moses' protest that he is not a man of words `[T]`. It also flattens the repeated imperative into a repeated idiom: NIV84 gives "Speak up" at both 31:8 and 31:9, so **the anaphora survives and the mouth does not.**
4. **30:1 and 31:1 — both versions print "oracle" and both footnote "Massa".** NIV84's footnotes are the fuller: "Or Jakeh of Massa" at 30:1 and "Or of Lemuel king of Massa" at 31:1 `[T]`. *What the congregation will hear:* a prophetic word. **What the preacher should know is that *BHS* prefers the place-name at 31:1** (Tool 7b).
5. **30:3 — the crux, and both versions agree against the Greek.** NASB95 "Nor do I have the knowledge of the Holy One"; NIV84 "nor have I knowledge of the Holy One" `[T]`. Both supply the negative from the first colon, which is the natural reading of וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע, but **the Hebrew's second colon has no negative of its own** and the Greek read it positively (below). No English version the congregation is likely to hold shows this. *The sermon can and should say it.*

**Ancient versions check — and the Three-Way Triage.** This unit produces more Hebrew/Greek divergence than any other in Proverbs. Each is classified; none is resolved by a preference.

*Category 1 — translation loss.*
- The four-fold negation of 30:2–3 survives, but **the exact repetition of 9:10's phrase does not**: Swete has γνῶσιν ἁγίων at 24:26 and γνῶσιν ἁγίων at 9:10 — so in this instance the Greek *does* carry it `[T]`, Swete. Noted because the expected loss did not occur.
- The מִי of 30:4 and the מִי of 30:9 are one word in Hebrew and two constructions in Greek (τίς … / Τίς με ὁρᾷ) `[T]`.
- The מְחֻקָּק / מְחֹקֵק ambiguity at 31:5 cannot survive into any translation `[T]`.

*Category 2 — substantive variants (weighed; no default for either text).* **All of these are in the Greek's own arrangement, where this unit stands inside chapter 24** (Tool 14).
- **MT 30:3 ‖ Swete 24:26 — the confession reversed.** MT: וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי חָכְמָה וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע. Swete: **θεὸς δεδίδαχέν με σοφίαν, καὶ γνῶσιν ἁγίων ἔγνωκα.** *BHS* reconstructs (וְ)אֵל לִמַּד אֹתִי (apparatus 30:3^{a–a}) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* the Greek's reading is internally coherent — "I am the most foolish of men and human understanding is not in me; **God** has taught me wisdom" — and is arguably the easier sense, which counts *against* it as the original. The MT's אֵדָע has no negative particle of its own and depends on the לֹא of the first colon doing double duty, which is common Hebrew but is the harder reading. **I incline to the MT and say so with a flag: this is the single most consequential undecided variant in the book, and it sits on the verse the series' Christological weight rests on.** *Moderate confidence for the MT.*
- **MT 30:9 ‖ Swete 24:32 — the divine name removed.** MT: וְאָמַרְתִּי **מִי יְהוָה** ("and say, 'Who is the LORD?'"). Swete: καὶ εἴπω **Τίς με ὁρᾷ;** ("and say, 'Who sees me?'"); *BHS* reconstructs יֶחֱזֶה (apparatus 30:9ᵇ) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* the MT's reading is the sharper and the one that carries the Exod 5:2 link (Tool 11); the Greek turns a theological denial into a thief's calculation. **The MT is the harder and the better attested by the rest of the verse, whose second half is about stealing and swearing falsely by the *name* of God.** *Moderate-to-high confidence for the MT.*
- **MT 30:4 ‖ Swete 24:27 — "his son" singular or plural.** MT: מַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ. Swete: τί ὄνομα **τοῖς τέκνοις αὐτοῦ** ("what is the name of his children?"); *BHS* records it as reflecting בָּנָו (apparatus 30:4ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* the plural is easily explained as a translator generalising a singular he found theologically odd; the singular is harder and is what the consonants give. **I take the MT, and the sermon must know that the Christological reading of this clause rests on it.** *Moderate confidence.* **And a second item in the same verse:** the final כִּי תֵדָע ("surely you know") is **absent from the best Greek witnesses**, and *BHS* suspects it entered from **Job 38:5** (apparatus 30:4^{d–d}) `[S: BHS]`. If so, the verse's sardonic last clause is a scribe's importation from the whirlwind speeches — which would be a very Job-shaped importation. *Undecided.*
- **MT 30:1 ‖ Swete 24:24 — the names gone.** Swete: Τοὺς ἐμοὺς λόγους, υἱέ, φοβήθητι, καὶ δεξάμενος αὐτοὺς μετανόει ("fear my words, my son, and having received them, repent") `[T]`, Swete; *BHS* reconstructs בְּנִי וְקָחֵם … דְּבָרַי גּוּר (apparatus 30:1^{b–b}) `[S: BHS]`. **The Greek has read every name in the verse as a common noun.** *Assessment:* the Hebrew consonants are capable of both readings, which is why *BHS* lists six re-divisions and settles none. **Unresolved, and it is the reason the unit's whole "two named outsiders" framing belongs to the Hebrew tradition.**
- **MT 31:1 ‖ Swete 24:69 — Lemuel gone, and the words made God's.** Swete: **Οἱ ἐμοὶ λόγοι εἴρηνται ὑπὸ θεοῦ· βασιλέως χρηματισμὸς ὃν ἐπαίδευσεν ἡ μήτηρ αὐτοῦ** ("my words have been spoken by God; a king's oracle, which his mother taught him") `[T]`, Swete; *BHS* records it (apparatus 31:1^{a–a}) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* χρηματισμός ("divine oracle") is a real rendering of מַשָּׂא as a genre-word, so the Greek here supports the "oracle" reading against the place-name — **which cuts against *BHS*'s own preference for מֶלֶךְ מַשָּׂא at the same verse.** Report both; resolve neither.
- **MT 31:8 ‖ Swete 24:76 — the mute gone.** MT: פְּתַח־פִּיךָ לְאִלֵּם. Swete: ἄνοιγε σὸν στόμα **λόγῳ θεοῦ** ("open your mouth with the word of God") `[T]`. *Assessment:* an inner-Greek or *Vorlage* confusion of אִלֵּם with something else; the MT is supported by the parallel colon (בְּנֵי חֲלוֹף, "the sons of passing away") and by 31:9's עָנִי וְאֶבְיוֹן, both of which concern the vulnerable rather than revelation. **MT.** *Moderate-to-high confidence.*
- **MT 30:15–16 ‖ Swete 24:50–51.** MT: the leech has **two** daughters and **three**/four are never satisfied, the list being שְׁאוֹל וְעֹצֶר רָחַם ("Sheol and the barren womb"). Swete: **three** daughters, and ᾅδης καὶ ἔρως γυναικὸς καὶ τάρταρος ("Hades and a woman's love and Tartarus") `[T]`. *BHS* marks 30:15a `frt crrp` (apparatus 30:15^{a–a}) `[S: BHS]`. *Assessment:* the MT's 2/3/4 sequence is odd and is the harder reading; the Greek's is tidier. **Undecided; do not preach the arithmetic.**

*Category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text (stands whichever is prior).*
- **Prov 30:4 → John 3:13** (headline 4; full Triad under Tool 11).
- **Prov 30:8 → 1 Tim 6:6–9.** Swete's 24:31 asks for **τὰ δέοντα καὶ τὰ αὐτάρκη** ("what is needful and sufficient") where the MT has לֶחֶם חֻקִּי; 1 Tim 6:6 has **αὐταρκείας** and 6:8 **ἀρκεσθησόμεθα**, and 6:9 warns οἱ βουλόμενοι πλουτεῖν ("those who wish to be rich") `[T]`, Swete + SBLGNT. **The Greek of Proverbs is where the New Testament's word for contentment sits in this argument.** Reported under Tool 11.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Two speakers, two registers, and the change between them is the unit's most audible feature.**

**Agur's register is confessional, then flat.** 30:2–3 is the most exposed first-person speech in Proverbs — four negations in two verses, and the first word of the confession, בַּעַר, is the word for an animal's lack of understanding, not a man's modesty `[T]`. 30:4's four questions build to a fifth that is not a question but a taunt: כִּי תֵדָע ("surely you know!"). **And then, for twenty-four verses, nothing. No verdict, no motive clause, no address.** The leech, the eagle, the ant, the lizard in the king's palace — the tone is that of a man reading out a notebook. *The flatness is the point:* a man who has said he does not know does not tell you what things mean.

**One flash of savagery.** 30:17 — עַיִן תִּלְעַג לְאָב וְתָבוּז לִיקֲּהַת־אֵם יִקְּרוּהָ עֹרְבֵי־נַחַל וְיֹאכְלוּהָ בְנֵי־נָשֶׁר ("the eye that mocks a father and scorns a mother — the ravens of the valley will pick it out, and the young eagles will eat it") `[T]`. It is the most violent image in Proverbs, it names no punisher, and it sits between two catalogues.

**Lemuel's mother's register is urgent and personal.** 31:2 is three broken vocatives with no verb — **מַה־בְּרִי וּמַה־בַּר־בִּטְנִי וּמֶה בַּר־נְדָרָי** ("what, my son? and what, son of my womb? and what, son of my vows?") — using the Aramaic בַּר three times where the book has said בְּנִי twenty-two times `[T]`. **The one woman given a speech in Proverbs addresses her son in a word the book has never used for "son".** Then six imperatives in seven verses.

**Soundtrack.** For Agur: an unaccompanied voice, then a long instrumental passage with no melody — a list of intervals. For 30:17: a single cymbal. For 31:2–9: a low, insistent, spoken line over a pulse that will not let up.

**Two threads worth the pulpit.**

- **The king, and the direction of travel.** 30:27 says the locusts have **no king** (מֶלֶךְ אֵין לָאַרְבֶּה) and march in rank anyway; 30:28 puts a lizard **in kings' palaces**; 30:31 has a king with his army; and then 31:1, 3, 4 address an actual king and tell him what not to drink `[T]`. **The unit moves from a creature that needs no king to a king who needs his mother.**
- **Satiety, carried over from the previous collection.** שׂבע at 30:9 (lest I be *full* and deny), 30:15 and 30:16 (three things never *satisfied*), 30:22 (a fool when he is *filled* with bread) `[T]`. The previous unit ran the same root through 25:16, 25:17, 27:20 and 28:19 `[T]`. **Two consecutive units, one root, and in this one it has become a thing to pray against.**

**Idea-level: the four questions and the four generations.** 30:4 asks four times מִי ("who"); 30:11–14 says four times דּוֹר ("a generation") `[T]`. The two blocks are ten verses apart and are the unit's two four-fold structures outside the numerical sayings. **One asks who made the world; the other describes the people in it. Neither is answered.**

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources.** The overview's headline finding — Proverbs contains no citation formula and no marked quotation `[S: overview]` — holds everywhere in the book except here. **30:5 is the closest thing to a quotation in Proverbs** `[S: overview]`, and this run confirms it from the corpus and identifies precisely what was changed.

#### Outward citations — the full Triad

**Ps 18:31 ‖ 2 Sam 22:31 → Prov 30:5** *(high confidence — verbal reproduction with two deliberate changes)*

*Source context.* Ps 18 (‖ 2 Sam 22) is the long royal thanksgiving for deliverance, placed in Samuel as David's own words near the end of his reign. v.31 stands at the hinge between the theophany (vv.8–20) and the king's equipping for battle (vv.33–46): הָאֵל תָּמִים דַּרְכּוֹ אִמְרַת־יְהוָה צְרוּפָה מָגֵן הוּא לְכֹל הַחֹסִים בּוֹ ("as for God, his way is blameless; the word of the LORD is tested; he is a shield to all who take refuge in him") `[T]`. **Its function in its own psalm is to ground a king's confidence in battle.** The three verses are the only ones in the Hebrew Bible with this wording `[T]`, whole-corpus search.

*Book usage.* Proverbs has not used the Psalter by quotation anywhere else; the overview lists Ps 111:10 and Ps 49:4 as vocabulary overlaps rather than citations `[S: overview]`. **This is the book's one act of quotation, and it is saved for the man who says he has no wisdom.**

*OT-to-OT.* Ps 18 and 2 Sam 22 are the same poem in two canonical locations, and **2 Sam 22 stands one chapter before 2 Sam 23:1, the third of the four נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר texts** `[T]`. So Agur's superscription and Agur's quotation draw on two adjacent chapters of Samuel. *Moderate confidence that this is deliberate; high that the adjacency is real.*

*What it adds, and the two changes are the finding.*
- **יְהוָה becomes אֱלוֹהַּ.** The source has the covenant name; Agur has the name that occurs once in Proverbs and forty-nine times in the Hebrew Bible, mostly in Job `[T]`. **A man who has just said he does not know the Holy One quotes a Davidic psalm and puts in it the divine name of the book about a man who could not find God either.** *Moderate-to-high confidence on the significance.*
- **כֹּל moves.** The source reads מָגֵן הוּא לְכֹל הַחֹסִים בּוֹ ("a shield to **all** who take refuge in him"); Agur reads **כָּל־**אִמְרַת אֱלוֹהַּ צְרוּפָה ... מָגֵן הוּא לַחֹסִים בּוֹ ("**every** word of God is tested … he is a shield to those who take refuge in him") `[T]`. **The universal quantifier is taken off the people and put on the word.** For a man whose problem is that he knows nothing, that is exactly the right move: he cannot vouch for the refugees, and he can say that every word is tested.

**Deut 4:2 / Deut 13:1 → Prov 30:6** *(high confidence on the formula; the half-quotation is the point)*

*Source context.* Deut 4:2 stands at the head of Moses' exhortation to keep the statutes in the land; 13:1 closes the section on false prophets. Both read לֹא תֹסִפוּ/תֹסֵף … **וְלֹא תִגְרְעוּ/תִגְרַע** ("you shall not add … and you shall not take away") `[T]`. **These two verses are the only places in the Hebrew Bible where the add/take-away pair occurs** `[T]`, whole-corpus search with a passed control.

*Book usage.* No earlier use of Deuteronomy's canon-formula in Proverbs; the overview's intertextual map lists the boundary law, the weights law and the Shema's bind-and-write as live Deuteronomy links, none of them this one `[S: overview]`.

*OT-to-OT.* Deut 4:2 and 13:1 are the same formula at two points in one book, and the second applies it specifically to a prophet who works a sign `[T]`. **Prov 30:6 applies it to *his words* — the אִמְרַת אֱלוֹהַּ of the verse before.**

*What it adds.* **Agur takes only half.** He says אַל־תּוֹסְףְּ עַל־דְּבָרָיו ("do not add to his words") and never says "do not take away" `[T]`. The overview notes the half-quotation `[S: overview]`; what the corpus adds is that the pair is otherwise unbroken in the canon — and that **Rev 22:18–19 restores it**, with ἐπιθῇ / ἀφέλῃ `[T]`, SBLGNT. *Why the half?* Because the danger Agur has in view is **supplementation, not subtraction**: a man who knows nothing is not tempted to cut God's word down; he is tempted to fill the silence. `[I]`, moderate-to-high confidence.

**Prov 30:4 → John 3:13** *(high confidence on the contact; moderate-to-high that this verse is specifically in view)*

*Source context.* 30:4 is the last of Agur's confession and its rhetorical turn. Four questions — ascent and descent; wind in the fists; waters in a garment; the ends of the earth — then two more: מַה־שְּׁמוֹ וּמַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ, and the taunt כִּי תֵדָע `[T]`. **The questions are not about creation in general but about a person: they ask for the identity of someone who has done these things.** אַפְסֵי־אָרֶץ ("the ends of the earth") stands in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, overwhelmingly royal and eschatological — 1 Sam 2:10; Ps 2:8; 72:8; Isa 45:22; 52:10; Mic 5:3; Zech 9:10 `[T]`, whole-corpus search. *Moderate confidence that the phrase carries that field here.*

*Book usage.* John cites Proverbs nowhere else `[T]`, SBLGNT — a cold source, which raises the bar. The bar is met on the wording.

*OT-to-OT.* **Three texts in this field, and only one has the shape John uses.** Deut 30:12 LXX: τίς ἀναβήσεται ἡμῖν εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν — an ascent only, to fetch the commandment, and it is the verse **Paul** takes at Rom 10:6–7. Bar 3:29: τίς ἀνέβη εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ ἔλαβεν αὐτήν, καὶ **κατεβίβασεν** αὐτήν — an ascent, and wisdom *brought down*. Swete's Prov 24:27 (= MT 30:4): **τίς ἀνέβη εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ κατέβη** `[T]`, Swete. **Only Proverbs has one person who both went up and came down.** That the three are already in conversation is likely — Baruch 3 is a meditation on where wisdom is found and reads like a fusion of Deut 30 and Prov 30 `[I]`, moderate.

*What it adds.* John 3:13 answers Agur's question by denying it and then making one exception: **οὐδεὶς ἀναβέβηκεν εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εἰ μὴ ὁ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβάς, ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** `[T]`, SBLGNT. And 30:4 asked two things: who did it, and **what is his son's name**. **John's sentence answers both in one clause.** Three qualifications the sermon must carry: John does not mark it as a citation; the ἀναβαίνω/καταβαίνω pair is common enough to occur independently (Eph 4:9–10 uses it of Ps 68:19); and **the singular "his son" is the Masoretic reading, pluralised in the Greek** (Tool 8). *The finding survives all three; it is not weakened by being stated with them.*

**Prov 30:8 → 1 Tim 6:6–9** *(moderate-to-high)* and **→ Matt 6:11** *(moderate)*

*Source context.* 30:7–9 is the only prayer in Proverbs `[T]`. Two requests before death: falsehood removed, and neither poverty nor riches. The motive clauses are symmetrical and both are theological — too much and I deny you; too little and I steal and profane your name `[T]`.

*Book usage.* No earlier prayer in the book to track `[T]`.

*OT-to-OT.* **Deut 8:11–17 is the pattern behind the first danger**: "lest you eat and be satisfied (וְשָׂבָעְתָּ) … and your heart be lifted up and you forget the LORD … and you say in your heart, 'My power…'" `[T]`. The sequence — satiety, forgetting, a saying in the heart — is Agur's exactly, and Deut 8:3 supplies the measured-ration idea (the manna) that לֶחֶם חֻקִּי restates. *Moderate-to-high confidence.*

*What it adds.* 1 Tim 6:6–9 works the same two dangers in the same order and uses **αὐτάρκεια**, which is the word Swete's Prov 24:31 supplies (τὰ αὐτάρκη) `[T]`. **So the New Testament's vocabulary for contentment in this argument comes through the Greek of this verse.** Matt 6:11's ἄρτον ἐπιούσιον is conceptually the same request and is **not** verbally linked in either Hebrew or Greek — NIV84's "daily bread" supplies the link the texts do not (Tool 8). *Moderate on Matthew; the idea is shared and the wording is not.*

**Prov 30:9 → Exod 5:2** *(high confidence — two occurrences in the canon)*

*Source context.* מִי יְהוָה as a question occurs in exactly **two verses of the Hebrew Bible**: Exod 5:2, where Pharaoh says מִי יְהוָה אֲשֶׁר אֶשְׁמַע בְּקֹלוֹ ("who is the LORD, that I should obey his voice?"), and Prov 30:9 `[T]`, whole-corpus search with a passed control. Pharaoh's question is the opening move of the exodus narrative and the thing the plagues exist to answer. **Agur's prayer is that prosperity should not turn him into Pharaoh.** *High confidence on the count; moderate-to-high that the allusion is intended — but two occurrences in 23,213 verses is a strong base.*

#### Move 4 — internal echo *(required)*

The opening unit (1:1–9:18) was held open throughout.

**`Internal:` 9:10 → 30:2–3.** *Answers §earlier; negated. High confidence.* The headline. דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים in two verses of the Hebrew Bible; all three wisdom nouns present once each and all negated `[T]`. *What it adds:* the book's own thesis is revoked by a speaker the book itself introduces, forty-two verses from the end.

**`Internal:` 20:9 → 30:12.** *Answers §earlier; answered falsely. High confidence.* מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי טָהַרְתִּי מֵחַטָּאתִי (20:9, verb טהר) against דּוֹר טָהוֹר בְּעֵינָיו וּמִצֹּאָתוֹ לֹא רֻחָץ (30:12, adjective טָהוֹר, with רחץ) `[T]`, WLC — root-level, and the overview identifies it as the book's sharpest unadvertised echo `[S: overview]`. *What it adds:* the only answer the book gives to its own hardest question is a generation that gets it wrong, and the 3:7 self-assessment thread ends here, at its ninth and last occurrence.

**`Internal:` 1:8 → 31:1.** *Answers §earlier. High confidence.* The book opens שְׁמַע בְּנִי מוּסַר אָבִיךָ וְאַל־תִּטֹּשׁ תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ ("hear, my son, your father's instruction, and do not forsake your mother's teaching") `[T]`. **31:1 is the only place in the book where a mother's teaching is actually given** — אֲשֶׁר־יִסְּרַתּוּ אִמּוֹ, using the verb of 1:8's מוּסָר `[T]`. *What it adds:* the book names two teachers at the start and supplies the father's voice for thirty chapters. **It supplies the mother's voice once, at the end, and gives her a king to teach.**

**`Internal:` 6:16–19 → the six numerical sayings.** *Answers §earlier; developed. High confidence on the form, moderate on the intent.* The x/x+1 saying occurs once in chs 1–9 (6:16, with six/seven) and six times here (with three/four and two/three/four) `[T]`. The overview calls 6:16–19 the seed `[S: overview]`; the corpus shows the numbers differ, so the *form* is planted and the *numbers* are not.

**`Internal:` 1:12 and 27:20 → 30:15–16.** *Answers §earlier. High confidence.* שְׁאוֹל ("Sheol") stands in nine verses of Proverbs, and the never-satisfied motif runs 1:12 (the gang's boast, נִבְלָעֵם כִּשְׁאוֹל חַיִּים), 27:20 (שְׁאוֹל וַאֲבַדֹּה לֹא תִשְׂבַּעְנָה), and 30:15–16 (שְׁאוֹל … לֹא תִשְׂבַּעְנָה) `[T]`, WLC. **30:16 is the last of the three and the only one that lists Sheol among four coordinate things rather than making it a simile.**

**`Internal:` 25:1 → 30:1 and 31:1.** *Contrast; planted for the reader. High confidence.* 25:1's heading says the material was **transcribed** (הֶעְתִּיקוּ); 30:1 and 31:1 say it is **מַשָּׂא** `[T]`. Gathered against given, three verses apart at the seam.

**`Internal:` the poor.** *Answers §earlier. High confidence.* עָנִי and אֶבְיוֹן occur nowhere in 25:1–29:27 and twice each here, at 30:14 and 31:9 `[T]`. *What it adds:* headline 5.

**Addressee differentiation.** Not applicable — the book opens addressing one party (בְּנִי). But **the addressee changes here, and that is the differentiated resolution**: 31:2's three vocatives use the Aramaic בַּר, not בֵּן, and the addressee is a king rather than a son-in-general `[T]`.

### 12. Genre

**Two genres, neither of them the sentence-collection the book has been running for twenty-one chapters.**

**30:1–33 is an oracle followed by a catalogue.** The heading claims oracular status (מַשָּׂא, נְאֻם), the opening is a first-person confession, and the bulk is *Zahlensprüche* — numerical sayings, a recognised wisdom form in which the number is the organising device and the members are coordinate `[T]` on the form's presence; `[S]` on the form's name and its occurrence outside Israel. **Reading rules:** the members of a numerical saying are not ranked, and the last is not automatically the point — though 30:15–16, 18–19 and 21–23 each place the humanly interesting member last, which is a pattern worth noting rather than a rule `[I]`, moderate.

**31:1–9 is maternal instruction to a monarch.** Nine verses, six imperatives, one addressee, one office `[T]`. It is closer in form to 22:17–24:34 (direct address) than to anything in 25–29, and closer in content to nothing else in the book.

**Genre notes specific to this unit.**

- **The numerical saying is not an argument and must not be preached as one.** "Four things are too wonderful" is a *list*, and the preacher who finds a thesis uniting the eagle, the serpent, the ship and the man with a maiden is supplying it. What the saying states is the fact of the limit — the things are נִפְלְאוּ מִמֶּנִּי, "too wonderful for me" — and the limit is Agur's subject `[T]`.
- **30:1–6 is the one stretch of Proverbs that behaves like prophecy**, and the reading rules change accordingly: an oracle is *received*, and what it asserts about the word of God (30:5–6) is asserted on that basis rather than observed in the world `[T]`.
- **The book-level trap that bites hardest here is trap 3** — reading a wisdom text as direct Christological prediction `[S: overview]`. 30:4 invites exactly that, and the Christological Reading extension sets out what the text does and does not license.

### 13. Copycat

**Largely N/A** — wisdom, not narrative, and no characters with histories. But this unit has more commanded material than any since 22:17–24:34, and the descriptive/prescriptive line runs somewhere unexpected.

- **Agur's prayer (30:7–9) is descriptive of Agur and prescriptive of nothing.** It is the only prayer in Proverbs and the book does not tell the reader to pray it `[T]`. **That said, it is a prayer, and a prayer recorded in Scripture is offered for use in a way that an observation is not** — which is why the church has always prayed it and has never been told to `[I]`, moderate confidence. The right note for the pulpit: *this is not a command; it is a man praying, and you are allowed to want what he wanted.*
- **Lemuel's mother's instruction (31:2–9) is prescriptive and addressed to an office.** Six imperatives, all to a king `[T]`. The congregation is not a king. **The application therefore runs through the office, not around it:** what she tells a king to do with his mouth, a Christian does wherever he holds the power to be heard — which is the ordinary route from royal ethics to Christian ethics, not a shortcut past the text `[I]`, high confidence.
- **31:6–7 is the unit's copycat landmine.** תְּנוּ־שֵׁכָר לְאוֹבֵד וְיַיִן לְמָרֵי נָפֶשׁ ("give strong drink to the one perishing, and wine to the bitter in soul") is an imperative, and it is an imperative to give alcohol `[T]`. See Difficult Verses.
- **The catalogues (30:10–33) prescribe nothing at all**, with one exception: 30:10 (do not slander a servant) and 30:32 (put your hand on your mouth) `[T]`. Everything between is observation.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** Nowhere the text will say. The two speakers are undatable and unplaceable; the unit contains no event, no institution and no figure from Israel's history `[T]`. Whatever is said about Massa, north Arabia or a post-exilic setting is reconstruction `[S]`.

**Canonical position (Phase 0.55).** Extracted from the overview `[S: overview]`, with two additions this run contributes.

- **Section.** Ketuvim, third in the BHS order — Psalms, Job, **Proverbs**, Ruth, Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
- **Reading implication.** Proverbs follows the two books that press hardest on whether the world is morally legible. **This unit is where Proverbs concedes the point** — and it concedes it in Job's vocabulary (below).
- **Presupposes.** Ps 18 ‖ 2 Sam 22 (quoted at 30:5); Deut 4:2 / 13:1 (the add/take-away formula, half-quoted at 30:6); Deut 8:11–17 (the satiety-forgetting sequence behind 30:9); Exod 5:2 (מִי יְהוָה). Each checked `[T]`.
- **Handoff.** Immediately to 31:10–31, the acrostic — which the Leningrad layout leaves entirely undivided `[T]`.
- **Neighbours.** Job before, Ruth after, at book level.

**Where the reader is, and what has happened since.** The cross, and three things it changes.

1. **The four questions have a name attached.** 30:4 asks who ascended and descended and what his son is called. John 3:13 supplies the one and the Gospel supplies the other. The question is not made rhetorical by the answer; it is made answerable.
2. **The confession is no longer the last word.** Agur says he has not learned wisdom. 1 Cor 1:30 says Christ ἐγενήθη σοφία ἡμῖν ἀπὸ θεοῦ ("became wisdom to us from God") `[T]`, SBLGNT. **The book's problem is not that its readers lack wisdom but that wisdom is not the kind of thing that can be acquired by the person who needs it — which is why it has to be given as a person.**
3. **The prayer has been prayed into.** 30:8's request for a measured ration stands behind, or at least beside, the petition of Matt 6:11, and 1 Tim 6:6–9 works Agur's two dangers in Agur's order using the Greek of Agur's verse (Tool 11).

### 15. Who Am I?

| Figure | Function | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Agur** | **The reader, at his best** | Not a type of Christ and not an example of virtue. He is what the book's programme produces in an honest man: a confession. **The identification the unit engineers is with him, and the invitation is to say what he says.** This is the only figure in Proverbs the reader is meant to *agree* with about himself. |
| **The four generations** (30:11–14) | The reader, at his worst — and a warning | Cursing the father, pure in its own eyes, lofty-eyed, devouring the poor. 30:12 is the one that stings, because it is the only description in the book of a group that has passed its own examination `[T]`. |
| **Lemuel** | Recipient, not model | A king being taught. The congregation is not a king; the application runs through the office (Tool 13). |
| **Lemuel's mother** | Teacher, and the book's answer to its own opening | 1:8 promised a mother's teaching; this is it `[T]`. Not a template for mothers — a demonstration that the book meant what it said at 1:8. |
| **The ant, the rock-badger, the locust, the lizard** (30:24–28) | Neither example nor warning — **evidence** | They are קְטַנֵּי־אָרֶץ ("small ones of the earth") who are חֲכָמִים מְחֻכָּמִים ("wise, made wise") `[T]`. **The Moses-is-me check.** There is no hero to insert oneself into, and the danger here is the reverse of the usual: **it is far too easy to identify with Agur's humility while not meaning it.** The text supplies its own test. A man who says "I am the most stupid of men" as a rhetorical opening and then tells you what four things are too wonderful for him has not conceded anything he wanted to keep.

**Where Christ stands.** At 30:4, and nowhere else in the unit by direct line. See the Christological Reading extension.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the unit seeks.** Two responses, one per speaker, and they are not the same response.

Agur's material asks for a **confession** — that the reader say about himself what Agur says, and then rest where Agur rests: on a word that is תָּרוּף ("tested") and on a God who is a shield to those who take refuge, without adding to what he has said `[T]`.

Lemuel's mother asks for an **act** — that whoever has a mouth and a hearing use it for people who have neither `[T]`.

**Stage 2 — worldview, then behaviour.**

*How it tells us to think.*
- That **the limit of what a man can know about God is real, and naming it is not scepticism but wisdom.** 30:2–3 is in Scripture, and it is in a book written to make men wise.
- That **the reliable thing inside the limit is a word, not a method.** 30:5–6 is Agur's entire positive content: every word of God is tested; he is a shield; do not add.
- That **wealth and poverty are both spiritually dangerous, and in that order.** 30:8–9 puts satiety first and gives it the worse consequence: poverty makes a man a thief, plenty makes him Pharaoh `[T]`.
- That **the measure of a government is what comes out of the ruler's mouth on behalf of people who have no voice.** 31:8–9 is the last instruction in the book before the portrait.

*What to stop.*
- Adding to God's words (30:6) — which for a preacher is an occupational hazard and not a metaphor.
- Slandering a servant to his master (30:10); cursing a father, congratulating yourself, looking down, living off the poor (30:11–14).
- Exalting yourself, and plotting (30:32) — and when you have, **put your hand over your mouth.**
- Drinking, if you hold an office where judgement is owed to someone (31:4–5).

*What to start.*
- Praying 30:7–9, or something like it, about your own income.
- Giving what comforts to those who are perishing and bitter in soul (31:6–7) — read with care (Difficult Verses).
- **Opening your mouth for the mute** (31:8–9). The unit's final instruction, repeated, and the only thing in these forty-two verses the reader is told twice to do.

*The motive.* Agur supplies his own and it is unusual for this book: not consequence but **reverence** — the danger he prays against is not ruin but blasphemy, וְתָפַשְׂתִּי שֵׁם אֱלֹהָי ("and I would profane the name of my God", 30:9) `[T]`. For a Christian congregation the motive under 31:8–9 is that **the one who came down from heaven opened his mouth for people who had no standing**, which is the same act the mother commands and is the reason it is not moralism to command it.

**Four audiences.**
- *For me:* have I ever said Agur's sentence and meant it? The test is whether I would let somebody else say it about me.
- *For a Christian friend:* 30:8–9 is the most useful prayer in Scripture for anyone whose circumstances have changed — in either direction.
- *For the church together:* 31:8–9. A church that cannot be found opening its mouth for the voiceless has not applied the last thing Proverbs says before it stops teaching.
- *For an unbeliever:* 30:4. It is a genuine question, it is not rhetorical, and there is an answer.

**Prayer in response.** Agur's own, verbatim — for truthfulness, for a measured portion, and against both of the ways a man loses God. Then thanks that the question of 30:4 has a name, and that the name is a son's.

## Extensions

### Original Language Observations

**The confession's four steps, and the scale it climbs.** 30:2–3 negates on a rising scale: בַּעַר ("brutish") — the animal level, and the word of Ps 73:22 `[T]`; בִינַת אָדָם ("the understanding of a man") — the human level; חָכְמָה ("wisdom") — the acquired level, which is what the book has been offering since 1:2; דַּעַת קְדֹשִׁים ("the knowledge of the Holy One") — the level 9:10 called בִּינָה `[T]`. **Four denials, each one level higher, ending exactly where the prologue ended.**

**The crux at 30:3b, stated plainly.** וְדַעַת קְדֹשִׁים אֵדָע. The verb אֵדָע is a plain qal imperfect 1cs with **no negative particle attached** `[T]`, WLC morphology. The negative must be carried over from וְלֹא־לָמַדְתִּי in the first colon — which is ordinary Hebrew (the *double-duty* negative) and is how every English version reads it. **But it is an inference, and the Greek did not make it** (Tool 8). *A sermon may state the negative with confidence and should say that the second colon does not carry its own "not".*

**מִי, five times, doing two jobs.** Four times at 30:4 as the interrogative of creation, once at 30:9 as the interrogative of denial `[T]`. **The same word asks who made the world and refuses to acknowledge him.** No translation can show it; both English versions render the first "Who has…" and the second "Who is…".

**The numerals, and why the phrase search failed.** שָׁלוֹשׁ and אַרְבַּע each occur in Proverbs only in chapter 30 — four and five verses respectively `[T]`, lemma-verified. A consonantal phrase search for the pair **returned a false nil on 30:18 and 30:29** because those verses spell the numeral defectively (שְׁלֹשָׁה) where 30:15 and 30:21 spell it plene (שָׁלוֹשׁ). The positive control caught it. *Recorded because this is the plene/defective failure gate item (e3) names, and the lemma index is the cure.*

**The ketiv forms, and a correction to the method.** Three unpointed forms stand in the unit: 30:10 **אדנו** (qere אֲדֹנָיו), 30:18 **וארבע** (qere וְאַרְבָּעָה), 31:4 **או** (qere אֵי) `[T]`, WLC; *BHS* documents each (apparatus 30:10ᵇ; 30:18ᵃ; 31:4ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`. **30:10's was invisible to the detector this project has been using**, because that detector splits on whitespace and אדנו is joined to אֶל־ by maqqef, so the token carries the pointing of its neighbour and fails the "no combining marks" test. **Re-run splitting on maqqef as well as whitespace, Proverbs has 69 ketiv-bearing words rather than 54 — the whitespace-only method was missing 22 %** `[T]`, WLC. See Open Questions; this is a toolkit defect, not a passage finding. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]` on the ketiv distribution itself.

**Sound, twice.**
- **30:15** — הַב הַב (*hav hav*), "Give! Give!", the leech's two daughters. A reduplicated monosyllable found nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible `[T]`. The sound *is* the characterisation.
- **31:2** — מַה־בְּרִי וּמַה־בַּר־בִּטְנִי וּמֶה בַּר־נְדָרָי (*mah-bĕrî ûmah-bar-biṭnî ûmeh bar-nĕdārāy*). Three *mah/meh* + three *bar*, with the internal rhyme of *biṭnî / nĕdārāy* `[T]`. **The verse has no finite verb at all**; it is pure address, and it is audibly a mother's voice. *High confidence — the pattern is on the page.*

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Thin, deliberately, and the thinness is contested.** The unit names no place, institution or event `[T]`. Everything a commentary supplies about Massa, north-Arabian wisdom, the Ishmaelite tribes of Gen 25:13–15 or a Qedarite court is reconstruction resting on the מַשָּׂא reading `[S]`, and that reading is the unit's principal open question (Tool 7b).

Four items are load-bearing and each is marked.

- **The leech (30:15).** עֲלוּקָה is a hapax `[T]`. Its two "daughters" crying *hav hav* are most naturally its suckers `[I]`, moderate confidence — the text does not say, and older readings took them as demons.
- **The four small creatures (30:24–28).** Ants storing in summer; rock-badgers (שְׁפַנִּים) housing in the crag; locusts marching without a king; a lizard in a palace `[T]`. **One of the four is named unclean in the food laws** — הַשָּׁפָן, the rock-badger, at Lev 11:5 and Deut 14:7 `[T]`, checked. *(The lizard-word here, שְׂמָמִית, is a hapax and is **not** among the five creeping things listed at Lev 11:29–30, which name different lizards; and locusts are explicitly permitted food at Lev 11:22. The tidier claim that Agur's exemplars are all unclean will not stand.)* **What does stand: the first of Agur's four wise creatures is one an Israelite may not eat, and the fourth lives in a king's palace without being invited** `[I]`, moderate confidence on the significance; the text draws no conclusion from either.
- **The measured ration (30:8).** לֶחֶם חֻקִּי is a hapax, and חֹק is the word for an allotted portion `[T]`. Whether it evokes a temple allowance, a soldier's rations or the manna is not stated; **Deut 8:3's manna is the nearest canonical analogue and the link is verbal only at the level of the idea** `[I]`, moderate.
- **Strong drink to the perishing (31:6–7).** שֵׁכָר is a fermented drink distinct from wine and is what the priest is forbidden at Lev 10:9 and the Nazirite at Num 6:3 `[T]`. **The mother commands for the dying exactly what the law forbids to the man on duty — and she has just forbidden it to the king on duty in the previous two verses** `[T]`. That symmetry is in the text and needs no background to see it.

### Original Audience Reception

**Who they were.** The book has constructed one addressee for twenty-nine chapters: בְּנִי, a young man at the point of leaving supervision `[S: overview]`, `[T]` on the vocative's twenty-two occurrences. **This unit changes the addressee twice** — first to לְאִיתִיאֵל (whoever that is), then to a king addressed as בַּר `[T]`.

**What they brought.** A purpose statement (1:2–6), a motto (1:7), a thesis (9:10), a prohibition against self-assessment (3:7), four men who ignored it, an unanswered question (20:9), and — immediately behind them — 138 verses in which God was named six times and nobody was told what to conclude.

**What would have surprised them.**
- **That the book's own thesis gets denied.** 9:10's phrase, negated, by a speaker the book has just introduced `[T]`. *This is the shock, and a sermon that does not produce it has not preached the passage.*
- **That somebody prays.** The only prayer in Proverbs, and it is a prayer about money `[T]`.
- **That the categories are gone.** No righteous, no wicked, no fool `[T]`.
- **That the mother finally speaks, and she is talking to a king** `[T]`.
- **That the last instruction in the book is about people who cannot speak** (31:8–9) `[T]`.

**What would have comforted them.** 30:5 — כָּל־אִמְרַת אֱלוֹהַּ צְרוּפָה מָגֵן הוּא לַחֹסִים בּוֹ. **In a unit built on what cannot be known, one sentence says what can be relied on, and it is a word and a person rather than a method** `[T]`.

**What we bring that they did not.** A name for 30:4; a son for 30:4b; the whole of Job read as Scripture rather than as the book next door; and 1 Tim 6:6–9 and Matt 6:11 standing behind 30:8.

**Candidate Fallen Condition Focus** *(feeds `/point-purpose`)*. Anchored to the text and shared: **we want a wisdom we can hold, and what we are finally offered is an admission that we have none and a word from outside that we did not produce.** Anchors: 30:2–3 against 9:10; 30:4's unanswered questions; 30:5–6's single positive; 20:9 and 30:12. A secondary candidate for a sermon that takes the second half: **we would rather be told what to think than be handed a list and a poor man's cause.**

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. The limit of wisdom, and where the canon puts it.** Job 28 asks where wisdom is found and answers that God alone knows the way to it (28:23); Ecclesiastes reports that the wise man cannot find out the work of God (8:17); **Prov 30:2–4 is the same confession inside the book that promised otherwise** `[T]` on the Proverbs verses, `[I]` on the canonical grouping. The trajectory runs to 1 Cor 1:18–2:16, where the wisdom of God is a crucified man and the Spirit ἐραυνᾷ ("searches") the depths of God (2:10) — **which is what Agur's four questions asked for and could not get** `[T]`, SBLGNT. *Moderate-to-high confidence.*

**2. The word as the one reliable thing.** 30:5–6 is a two-verse doctrine of Scripture: **tested** (צְרוּפָה, the metallurgical word), **protective** (מָגֵן), and **closed** (do not add) `[T]`. It quotes a psalm to say the first two and Deuteronomy to say the third. The canonical field runs Ps 12:7; Ps 119:140; Deut 4:2; 13:1; and forward to Rev 22:18–19, which restores the half Agur dropped `[T]`. **Proverbs' one explicit statement about Scripture is made by a man who has just said he knows nothing — which is the right speaker for it.**

**3. The mouth, and who gets one.** The unit runs a thread from 30:32 (יָד לְפֶה, "hand to mouth" — *shut it*) through 31:4–5 (what must not go in) and 31:6–7 (what may be given to others) to 31:8–9 (פְּתַח־פִּיךָ twice — *open it*) `[T]`. **Closed for yourself, opened for the voiceless.** The field: Exod 4:11 (who makes a man mute); Isa 35:6 (the tongue of the mute will sing); Isa 53:7 (he opened not his mouth); Luke 4:18. *Moderate confidence on the canonical field; high that the thread is in the unit.*

### Christological Reading

**This is the unit the series' Christological weight rests on, and the weight is real. It is also easy to overload, so the categories matter.**

**Category: contrast and trajectory, with one genuine New Testament answer to a question this text asks.** There is no prophecy here, no type and no Christophany.

**1. The question, and the answer — 30:4.** Four questions about a person, then two more: מַה־שְּׁמוֹ וּמַה־שֶּׁם־בְּנוֹ ("what is his name, and what is his son's name?") `[T]`. **John 3:13 answers the first pair and the last clause in one sentence** (Tool 11, full Triad): οὐδεὶς ἀναβέβηκεν εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εἰ μὴ ὁ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβάς, ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου `[T]`, SBLGNT. *High confidence on the contact, moderate-to-high on the intention.*

**What this licenses and what it does not.** It licenses saying: *Proverbs asks a question to which the New Testament gives a name, and the question includes a son.* **It does not license saying that Agur knew, or predicted, or was shown the Son.** 30:4's last clause is a taunt — כִּי תֵדָע, "surely you know!" — addressed to a reader who does not `[T]`. **The verse's force is the force of an unanswered question, and a sermon that answers it too early loses the very thing that makes the answer worth having.** Preach the question first, and at length.

**And the honesty the pulpit needs:** the singular "his son" is the Masoretic reading; Swete has the plural (Tool 8). A hearer who looks it up in a Septuagint will not find the singular. *Say so.*

**2. The contrast — 30:2–3 and the whole book behind it.** The book's own programme fails in the mouth of its own speaker. 20:9 asked who can say he is clean; 30:12 answers with a generation that says it and is not `[T]`. **Only one person in Scripture can answer 20:9 without lying and 30:4 without guessing, and the two answers are the same person.** That is the doorway, and it requires no allegory: the text supplies both questions.

**3. The trajectory — 30:5–6.** Every word of God is tested and he is a shield to those who take refuge. **The New Testament's claim is not that a better word arrived but that the word became a person** (John 1:1–14), and that the shield is the same one `[I]`, moderate confidence on the connection as a trajectory rather than a citation.

**4. The last instruction — 31:8–9.** *Open your mouth for the mute.* **The one who came down from heaven (30:4 ‖ John 3:13) is the one who opened his mouth for people with no standing, and who at his trial did not open it for himself** (Isa 53:7; Matt 27:12–14) `[T]`, SBLGNT. That pairing is a reading, not a citation `[I]`, moderate — but it is the natural bridge from the unit's last command to its own Christological centre, and it keeps the command from becoming a campaign.

**Moralism check.** Two traps, and they are opposite.
- The first is to preach **Agur as an example of humility**: *be humble like Agur*. That converts the one honest confession in the book into another achievement to be graded, which is 26:12 in a new costume.
- The second is to preach **31:8–9 as social ethics with the confession removed**: *speak up for the voiceless*. True, commanded, and — detached from 30:2–4 — indistinguishable from any decent secular programme.
**The unit holds them together and the sermon must: the man who has stopped claiming to know is the one free to open his mouth for somebody else, and the reason he can is that the one who came down from heaven did it first.**

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**30:1b — לְאִיתִיאֵל לְאִיתִיאֵל וְאֻכָל.** Two proper names, or a sentence. *BHS* lists six re-divisions and settles none (apparatus 30:1^{d–d}) `[S: BHS]`; NASB95 transliterates; **NIV84 prints the names and footnotes "I am weary, O God; I am weary, O God, and faint"** `[T]`. **A congregation reading NIV84 will see the footnote.** Say what it is: the consonants are certain, the word-division is not, and nothing in the sermon should depend on either reading.

**30:3b — the missing negative.** See Original Language Observations. The English versions are right and the Greek read it the other way. Not a difficulty for faith; a difficulty for the preacher who claims more certainty than the text gives.

**30:15–16 — "the leech has two daughters" and the 2/3/4 sequence.** *BHS* marks it possibly corrupt; the Greek has three daughters `[S: BHS]`. Do not build on the numbers.

**30:17 — the ravens and the eye.** The most violent image in Proverbs, and it names no agent `[T]`. It is a *māšāl*, not a threat of judicial punishment, and the genre rules apply: it states what such contempt deserves and comes to, not what a court should do.

**30:19 — דֶּרֶךְ גֶּבֶר בְּעַלְמָה.** עַלְמָה occurs in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Gen 24:43, Exod 2:8, **Isa 7:14**, Prov 30:19 `[T]`, whole-corpus search. **Somebody will ask.** The honest answer: the word means a young woman of marriageable age; the four contexts are unrelated; Prov 30:19 tells us nothing about Isa 7:14 and Isa 7:14 tells us nothing about Prov 30:19; and the Greek here read it as "youth" (ἐν νεότητι) `[T]`, Swete. *Do not let the sermon be captured by it.*

**31:4–7 — the king must not drink, and the dying must be given drink.** The two halves are usually preached apart and belong together. **She forbids to the man with judgement to give exactly what she commands for the man with nothing left** `[T]`. *Pastoral care needed:* the congregation will include people in recovery and people caring for the dying, and 31:6 read flatly is a text that can do harm. The corrective is the structure: **the verse is about who may be spared consciousness, and the answer is: not the man who owes somebody a verdict.**

**31:5's מְחֻקָּק.** The Cairo Codex points the same consonants as the מְחֹקֵק of Gen 49:10 `[S: BHS]`. Worth knowing; too thin to preach.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: answering 30:4 in the first five minutes

- **What it looks like:** "Who has ascended into heaven and descended? We know the answer, don't we — it's Jesus!" — delivered as the sermon's opening move, to knowing nods.
- **Why it's wrong:** it converts a question into a quiz and throws away the thing that makes the answer worth anything. 30:4's last clause is **כִּי תֵדָע** — *surely you know!* — a taunt aimed at a reader who does not `[T]`. The verse is designed to leave you without an answer, and it is preceded by three verses in which a wise man says he has none. **A congregation that gets the answer before it has felt the question has been robbed.**
- **The corrective:** preach 30:2–4 as one movement and stay inside it. Let the four questions stand unanswered for as long as the sermon can bear. John 3:13 is the last thing you say, not the first.

### Pitfall: preaching Agur as a model of humility

- **What it looks like:** "Agur shows us the humility that is the beginning of wisdom. Be like Agur."
- **Why it's wrong:** it turns the one honest confession in Proverbs into one more attainment to be graded — which is precisely the move 26:12 exists to expose, and the book has already shown four men who did it (26:5, 12, 16; 28:11) `[T]`. **A man who says "I am the most stupid of men" as a technique has said nothing.** And the book knows: 30:12 is a whole generation that passed its own examination.
- **The corrective:** preach the confession as a *report*, not a virtue. Agur is not being admirable; he is being accurate. The sermon's question is not "are you humble enough?" but "is what he said true of you?"

### Pitfall: the "two odd appendices" sermon

- **What it looks like:** treating 30–31:9 as miscellaneous material tacked onto the end — an eccentric sage, some nature notes, a queen mother's advice about drink.
- **Why it's wrong:** the unit is the most heavily structured stretch in the book (nine markers in forty-two verses, every numerical saying closed by one) `[T]`, it contains the book's only quotation, only prayer, only oracle-heading, only mother's teaching, and its only denial of its own thesis `[T]`. **Nothing in Proverbs is less miscellaneous.**
- **The corrective:** name the shape from the front. Two speakers from outside, both introduced as מַשָּׂא; one says the programme did not work in him; the other applies it to the one office the book has watched from a distance for twenty-nine chapters.

### Pitfall: 31:6 preached flat

- **What it looks like:** "Give beer to those who are perishing" — either quoted as a licence, or hurried past in embarrassment.
- **Why it's wrong:** it detaches the verse from the two before it. **The same speaker forbids to the king on duty exactly what she commands for the man with nothing left** (31:4–5 against 31:6–7) `[T]`, and שֵׁכָר is the drink forbidden to the priest on duty at Lev 10:9 and the Nazirite at Num 6:3 `[T]`. The subject is not alcohol; it is **who may be spared consciousness, and the answer is: not the man who owes somebody a verdict.**
- **The corrective:** preach 31:4–7 as one unit and name the symmetry. And know the room — a congregation contains people in recovery and people sitting with the dying, and both will hear this verse personally.

### Pitfall: making 30:19 about Isaiah 7:14

- **What it looks like:** a digression on עַלְמָה, virgin birth and the Septuagint, arriving from "the way of a man with a maiden".
- **Why it's wrong:** עַלְמָה stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible and the four contexts are unrelated `[T]`. Prov 30:19 contributes nothing to Isa 7:14 and Isa 7:14 contributes nothing here — and the Greek of Prov 30:19 does not even render it "maiden" (ἐν νεότητι, "in youth") `[T]`, Swete. **The digression also hands the sermon's Christological weight to the wrong verse**, eleven verses after the right one.
- **The corrective:** if asked, answer in two sentences (Difficult Verses) and go back to 30:4.

### Pitfall: claiming Agur knew about the Son

- **What it looks like:** "Agur is looking forward to Christ here" — or, worse, "Agur knew the Son's name."
- **Why it's wrong:** 30:4 is a question and its last clause is sarcastic `[T]`. And the singular "his son" is the **Masoretic** reading; the Greek has "his children", which *BHS* records (apparatus 30:4ᶜ) `[S: BHS]`. A hearer checking a Septuagint will not find the singular.
- **The corrective:** say what is true and say it plainly. *Proverbs asks a question that includes a son, and the New Testament answers it with one.* That is enough, and it is more honest than a claim about what Agur could see.

### Pitfall: 31:8–9 as a campaign with the confession removed

- **What it looks like:** a rousing close on speaking up for the voiceless, with 30:2–4 left behind in the first half of the sermon.
- **Why it's wrong:** detached from the confession, the command is indistinguishable from any decent secular programme, and it hands the congregation one more thing to be graded on. **The unit binds them: the man who has stopped claiming to know is the one free to open his mouth for somebody else.**
- **The corrective:** run the line explicitly — 30:2–4, the question and the name, then 31:8–9 as what the one who came down did with his mouth, and what he did not do with it at his own trial.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **Is מַשָּׂא the genre-word or the place-name Massa?** *BHS* directs מֶלֶךְ מַשָּׂא at 31:1 and proposes a Massaite reading at 30:1 (apparatus 31:1^{b–b}; 30:1ᶜ) `[S: BHS]` — but the Greek renders it χρηματισμός ("divine oracle") at the same verse, which supports the genre-word `[T]`, Swete. **Both English versions print "oracle" and footnote "Massa".** The question decides whether Agur and Lemuel are non-Israelites, which in turn decides how much of the unit's "outsider" framing is text and how much is reconstruction. **This is the run's principal open question and the best single target for a Logos pass** — a lexicon entry on מַשָּׂא and two or three commentaries on 30:1 would settle how the question is currently weighted.
2. **Is 30:3b's second colon negative?** The Hebrew has no negative particle of its own; the double-duty reading is ordinary and is what every English version takes; the Greek read it positively and *BHS* reconstructs a Hebrew behind that reading. **The most consequential open variant in the book.**
3. **Is 30:4's final כִּי תֵדָע original, or an importation from Job 38:5?** *BHS* suspects the latter (apparatus 30:4^{d–d}) `[S: BHS]`; it is absent from the best Greek witnesses. If secondary, the verse's sardonic edge is a scribe's.
4. **30:1b's word-division.** Six *BHS* proposals, none preferred. Nothing in the sermon should rest on either reading.
5. **The Swete/Rahlfs question on two entries.** 30:4's omission is marked > G^{BS} and 30:15's divergence is edition-sensitive; both would repay a Rahlfs-Hanhart check from Logos before entering a sermon. Swete is a diplomatic Vaticanus text and is the wrong witness to cite on a Vaticanus-specific omission.
6. **The manuscript spread of the nine paragraph markers** is unchecked. `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`; barred from the book overview until a library pass is run. **Note that the structural claim does not depend on them** — the block boundaries are derivable from the numerals and catchwords alone.
7. **Whether John 3:13 has Prov 30:4 specifically in view** or the Deut 30:12 / Bar 3:29 family. The ascender-is-also-the-descender argument is discriminating but not decisive; a commentary on John would settle how it is usually taken.

**Closed by this run.** Recorded because the record shows the gate working, and because two of the three are new failure-modes rather than repeats:

- **A whole-Bible search was run against a corpus that was missing a third of the Hebrew Bible.** The file pattern used to load the corpus picked up the Torah and Ketuvim folders but not the nested Neviim folders, loading 13,917 of 23,213 verses. **The positive control passed** — Prov 30:1 was returned — because the control tests the *pattern*, not the *corpus*. The consequence was a reported count of **three** for נְאֻם הַגֶּבֶר, missing **2 Sam 23:1**, David's last words, which is the single most interesting member of the set. Caught by reading a targeted verse that came back missing. **A corpus-completeness check is a distinct requirement from a positive control on the pattern, and this run now confirms that all 23,213 verses are loaded before any whole-Bible claim.**
- **A phrase search for the 3/4 numerical form returned a false nil** on 30:18 and 30:29, which spell the numeral defectively. Its control failed. The lemma index was immune and supplied the correct figures.
- **The ketiv detector was missing 22 % of its targets.** Splitting on whitespace alone, Proverbs yields 54 ketiv-bearing words; splitting on maqqef as well, 69 `[T]`. A ketiv joined by maqqef to a pointed neighbour carries the neighbour's combining marks and fails the "no combining marks" test — 30:10's אדנו is the instance here. **The previous unit's figure of ten for 25:1–29:27 is unaffected** (none of the fifteen missed forms falls in those chapters), but the method needs amending. See Book-Overview Tensions.
