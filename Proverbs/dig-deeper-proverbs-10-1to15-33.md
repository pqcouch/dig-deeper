## Headline Findings

1. **Both ends of this preaching unit are Masoretic, the Greek has neither of them, and inside them the scribe makes one break in 184 verses.** `[T]`, Swete and WLC. At **10:1** Swete has **no superscription at all** — no מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה ("the proverbs of Solomon"), no heading, no break: the Greek runs straight out of Folly's house at 9:18 into Υἱὸς σοφὸς εὐφραίνει πατέρα ("a wise son gladdens a father"). At the other end, **MT 15:33 stands at Swete 16:4**, behind material the MT puts at 16:6–8. **The collection the preacher is about to expound opens with a heading the Greek does not have and closes with a motto the Greek has moved past the seam.** And between those two ends the Hebrew is barely divided at all: chapters 1–9 carry **24** paragraph markers in 256 verses, chapters 10–15 carry **two** — one immediately after the superscription at 10:1 and one at **13:25** `[T]`, WLC printing the Leningrad layout. *That last is a reading tradition rather than an authorial signal, and its manuscript spread has not been checked* `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`. Surfaced by: Structure, Translations, Context, Genre. *High confidence on the Greek; moderate on the layout.*

2. **This is the book's contrast engine, and four independent measures say so.** `[T]`, WLC. Of the unit's 184 verses, **111 (60.3%) carry a righteous/wicked/wise/fool word**, against 44.5% in 16:1–22:16 and 27.0% in chapters 1–9. **צַדִּיק ("righteous") stands in 39 verses here and 10 in 16:1–22:16**; רָשָׁע ("wicked") 39 against 14. **The two words meet inside a single verse 41 times here and 8 times in 16:1–22:16** — a fivefold difference. And **86.9% of these verses join their second line with a waw**, against 50.8% in chapters 1–9. Surfaced by: Structure, Parallels, Repetition, Vocabulary, Genre. *High confidence on the counts.*

3. **The New Testament quotes this collection twice inside eleven verses of one chapter — and takes the Hebrew's sense once and the Greek's once.** `[T]`, SBLGNT + Swete + WLC. **1 Pet 4:8** ἀγάπη καλύπτει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν ("love covers a multitude of sins") answers **Prov 10:12**'s וְעַל כָּל־פְּשָׁעִים תְּכַסֶּה אַהֲבָה ("but love covers all transgressions") — **and not Swete, which at 10:12 has friendship covering the non-contentious and no sins in it at all.** Ten verses later **1 Pet 4:18** quotes **Swete's Prov 11:31 word for word** — εἰ ὁ δίκαιος μόλις σῴζεται, ὁ ἀσεβὴς καὶ ἁμαρτωλὸς ποῦ φανεῖται ("if the righteous is scarcely saved, where will the ungodly and the sinner appear?") — where the Hebrew says the righteous is **recompensed on the earth**. **Two citations, eleven verses apart, going opposite ways.** Surfaced by: Translations, Tool 11, Biblical Theology, Christological Reading. *High confidence on all three wordings, each checked in its own edition.*

4. **The divine name is rarer here than in the next collection, it is attached to two formulae, and it disappears entirely for a chapter.** `[T]`, WLC. יְהוָה stands in **20 of the unit's 184 verses (10.9%)** against **35 of 191 (18.3%)** in 16:1–22:16. **Twelve of the twenty are one of two formulae** — six תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה ("an abomination to the LORD", 11:1; 11:20; 12:22; 15:8; 15:9; 15:26) and six of יִרְאַת יְהוָה ("the fear of the LORD") or of fearing him (10:27; 14:2; 14:26; 14:27; 15:16; 15:33). **Chapter 13 has none at all**: from 12:22 to 14:2 the name is absent for **32 consecutive verses**. Then chapter 15 has nine, running into the densest concentration in the book at 15:25–16:11. Surfaced by: Repetition, Vocabulary, Structure, Biblical Theology. *High confidence on the counts.*

5. **The collection quotes itself, and one of its self-quotations swaps the subject of a sentence and leaves everything else standing.** `[T]`, WLC. **13:14** תּוֹרַת חָכָם מְקוֹר חַיִּים לָסוּר מִמֹּקְשֵׁי מָוֶת ("the teaching of the wise is a fountain of life, to turn aside from the snares of death") and **14:27** יִרְאַת יְהוָה מְקוֹר חַיִּים לָסוּר מִמֹּקְשֵׁי מָוֶת — **identical but for the subject.** Alongside it: **10:1a recurs word for word at 15:20a**; **10:2b at 11:4b**; **10:6b at 10:11b**, five verses apart; and **14:12 is repeated exactly at 16:25**, over the seam. Surfaced by: Repetition, Structure, Tool 11 Move 4, Parallels. *High confidence — all verified consonantally against the WLC.*

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**At book level** the overview's microscript holds and this unit is where it is executed: wisdom is acquired, and the thing the book will not let the son do is grade his own paper `[S: overview]`. The overview's own test of the movements says of this one that it "supplies the categories by which a man is graded" `[S: overview]` — and the counts in Headline 2 are what that claim looks like when it is measured.

**At passage level** the purpose is narrower and it is formal as much as topical: **to install a pair of categories by repetition until they feel like a description of the world rather than a claim about it.** The collection does not argue that the righteous and the wicked are two kinds of people. It says so 39 times each, sets the two inside one verse 41 times, and never once defends the division `[T]`. **The teaching method is saturation, and the absence of argument is the method working.**

Two supports for reading the purpose this way, both from the text. First, **the vocabulary of chapters 1–9 largely stops at 10:1 and the field's own vocabulary starts**: פְּתִי ("naive"), the frame's named addressee, stands in 8 verses of chapters 1–9 and **2** here; חָכְמָה ("wisdom") 16 there and **9** here; while צַדִּיק runs 4 → **39** and רָשָׁע 7 → **39** `[T]`, WLC. The frame argues for wanting wisdom in one dialect; the collection dispenses it in another. Second, **there is no addressee.** בְּנִי ("my son") occurs 15 times in chapters 1–9 and **not once** in chapters 10–15 `[T]`, WLC — the vocative does not return until 19:27. **A collection with no one addressed cannot exhort; it can only describe, and leave the reader to place himself.**

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Chapters 1–9 have done three things this collection needs. They have argued that wisdom is worth having and is *given* (2:6); they have named the reader as the simple rather than the sage (1:4, and the parenthesis at 1:5); and they have ended, at 9:18, with a man at a table who does not know where he is — the frame's own last word, and a verdict rather than an appeal `[S: chs 1–9 dig]`. **What chapters 1–9 have not done is give the reader a single case to judge.**

**Necessity answer.** The collection exists *here* because **a reader has just been told that the two invitations sound identical (9:4 ‖ 9:16) and has been given no way to tell them apart.** Chapters 10–15 supply the missing instrument: several hundred concrete cases in which the two kinds of life are set side by side and their outcomes stated. **The frame teaches the reader to want the categories; the collection hands him the categories.** And it hands them over in words the frame did not use — which is why this material cannot simply have been placed anywhere: **it is the answer to a question the preceding nine chapters have made urgent and left standing.**

**Implication.** A sermon on this collection is not a sermon on good advice. It is a sermon on **how a person learns to read a situation** — and the collection's own method, saturation without argument, is part of what is being preached. *The positional answer also sets the sermon's boundary: the material does not argue, so a sermon that argues from it is doing something the text declines to do.*

**Immediate context.** Before: 9:13–18, Folly's call and the narrator's verdict. After: 16:1, where the divine name spikes, the king arrives, and the recurring shape becomes *a man plans, YHWH disposes* `[S: overview]`. **The unit therefore sits between the two chapters that most obviously belong to somebody else** — the frame's closing tableau and the next collection's theology of providence.

**Section and book.** The second of the book's superscribed collections `[T]` (1:1; 10:1; 25:1 are the three occurrences of שְׁלֹמֹה in the book, and all three are superscriptions). Widely read out of context: **almost every verse in it is**, since the collection's ordinary use is the single verse on a calendar. The corrective the context supplies is the one Tool 12 states — these are generalisations in a book that supplies its own counter-cases.

#### Canonical position

Ketuvim, third in the BHS order, after Psalms and Job `[T]`; the overview's five-field block stands and is not re-derived here `[S: overview]`.

### 3. Structure

**The honest finding first: the text marks almost no structure at all, and that is itself the finding.**

**What the manuscript marks.** WLC, printing the Leningrad layout, gives the 184 verses **two** markers `[T]`: a **פ** at 10:1 *between the superscription and the first proverb*, and a **פ** at the end of **13:25**. For comparison, chapters 1–9 carry 24 markers in 256 verses, and chapters 30–31 carry nine in 64 `[T]`. **The scribe divided the instruction material heavily and this collection hardly at all.** *This is an apparatus feature, in one manuscript, and it is a reading tradition rather than an authorial signal; its spread across manuscripts has not been checked here* `[unchecked — apparatus spread]`. *Moderate confidence.*

**What the wording marks.** Three things, and they are at the edges rather than inside.

| Where | What | Warrant |
|---|---|---|
| **10:1** | מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה ("the proverbs of Solomon") — a superscription, and the second of the book's three | `[T]` |
| **10:1a ‖ 15:20a** | בֵּן חָכָם יְשַׂמַּח־אָב ("a wise son makes a father glad") — **word for word**, the collection's first line and a line thirteen verses from its end. The second cola differ: 10:1b וּבֵן כְּסִיל תּוּגַת אִמּוֹ ("but a foolish son is a grief to his mother"), 15:20b וּכְסִיל אָדָם בּוֹזֶה אִמּוֹ ("but a foolish man despises his mother") | `[T]`, WLC |
| **15:33** | יִרְאַת יְהוָה מוּסַר חָכְמָה ("the fear of the LORD is the instruction for wisdom") — the motto of 1:7 and 9:10 restated in the collection's last verse, and carrying **both** of the prologue's two nouns, חָכְמָה and מוּסָר | `[T]` |

**So the collection is bracketed, and the brackets are lexical rather than scribal.** A wise son at the top and the fear of YHWH at the bottom. *Whether 10:1 ‖ 15:20 is a designed inclusio or a repeated proverb that happens to fall there cannot be settled from the corpus — 15:20 is not the last verse, and the book repeats itself elsewhere without structural intent (10:6b ‖ 10:11b, five verses apart). Stated as an observation, not a design claim.* `[I]`, *moderate confidence.*

**The two counted gradients, reproduced independently of the overview.** Both turn at the same place.

| Section | Verses | Lexical opposition across the atnach | Colon B begins with waw |

| 1:1–9:18 | 256 | 4 (1.6%) | 50.8% |
| **10:1–15:33** | **184** | **61 (33.2%)** | **86.9%** |

`[T]`, WLC, both measures run on the lemma index with the atnach as the caesura. **The opposition measure counts only a fixed set of opposed lemma-pairs split across the caesura**, so it undercounts antithesis carried by syntax alone; it is reported because it is reproducible in one command. *(The overview's run of the same measure returned 58/184 = 31.5% on a slightly smaller pair-list, and 10/191 for 16:1–22:16 — identical. The independent reproduction is what makes the figure usable.)*

**And the gradient runs inside the unit as well as across the seam** `[T]`:

| | ch10 | ch11 | ch12 | ch13 | ch14 | ch15 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Opposition rate** | 53% | 23% | 43% | 32% | 26% | **24%** |

**Chapter 10 is the most sharply antithetical chapter in the collection and chapter 15 the least; the divine name runs the other way.** The collection opens at maximum contrast and ends with the fear of YHWH — which is what its two brackets say in a different register. *Moderate confidence on reading the gradient as design; high on the figures.*

**Device: a deposit, not an argument.** No chiasm has been sought and none is claimed. **A sermon that divides this collection into thematic sections is supplying a structure the manuscript, the vocabulary and the syntax all decline to supply** — which is the overview's trap 4, and it bites here more than anywhere else in the book `[S: overview]`.

### 4. Linking Words

Sparse in kind, overwhelming in quantity, and the finding is the ratio.

| Marker | Where | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **וְ** opening colon B | **159 of the 183 verses where an atnach can be found** `[T]` | Almost always adversative in this collection — "but" — though the Hebrew waw itself is neutral |
| **כִּי** ("for, because") | scarce | The collection grounds almost nothing; it states |
| **אִם** ("if") | scarce | Conditions belong to chapter 2, not here |
| **לָכֵן / עַל־כֵּן** | — | Absent as a governing connector |

**The whole logical weight of the collection rests on one letter.** A waw at the caesura, 159 times — and whether it means *and* or *but* is decided by the lexical opposition it carries, not by the conjunction. **That is why the antithesis measure matters**: in a collection where 86.9% of verses join their halves with a waw, the waw cannot be what makes the verse antithetical, and the lexical opposition is what the reader is actually reading.

**One consequence for the preacher.** English versions must choose *and* or *but* 159 times, and both choose *but* in the great majority. **The Hebrew does not decide it that often; the translators do** — correctly, in most cases, and invisibly. See Tool 8.

### 5. Parallels

Antithetical parallelism is the collection's default and this is the tool where that fact belongs. Four pairs that clarify meaning rather than merely illustrate the form.

**10:2 — and the second colon is worth more than the first.**
> לֹא־יוֹעִילוּ אוֹצְרוֹת רֶשַׁע וּצְדָקָה תַּצִּיל מִמָּוֶת
> "Ill-gotten gains do not profit, / But righteousness delivers from death."

The halves are not on the same scale: *treasure fails to profit* against *righteousness rescues from death*. **The antithesis is not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the point** — the second colon over-answers the first. *And the whole colon recurs at 11:4b with a different first line ("riches do not profit in the day of wrath")* `[T]`, *so the collection itself treats this half-verse as portable.*

**11:1 — a nominal clause, and a cultic category.**
> מֹאזְנֵי מִרְמָה תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה וְאֶבֶן שְׁלֵמָה רְצוֹנוֹ
> "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, / But a just weight is His delight."

Both halves are **verbless**: X *is* an abomination; Y *is* his delight. **No emotion is predicated and no action is described** — two objects are assigned to two categories. תּוֹעֵבָה and רָצוֹן are the sanctuary's vocabulary for what may and may not come in `[I]`, *moderate-to-high*. The pairing recurs at 11:20, 12:22 and 15:8 `[T]`, four of the twenty verses in the book where both words stand together.

**13:12 and 15:4 — the same predicate given to two different things.**
> תּוֹחֶלֶת מְמֻשָּׁכָה מַחֲלָה־לֵב וְעֵץ חַיִּים תַּאֲוָה בָאָה (13:12)
> "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, / But desire fulfilled is a tree of life."
> מַרְפֵּא לָשׁוֹן עֵץ חַיִּים וְסֶלֶף בָּהּ שֶׁבֶר בְּרוּחַ (15:4)
> "A soothing tongue is a tree of life, / But perversion in it crushes the spirit."

**עֵץ חַיִּים ("tree of life") stands four times in Proverbs — 3:18; 11:30; 13:12; 15:4 — and three of the four are in this collection** `[T]`, WLC. See Tool 11.

**14:12 — the one verse in the collection that breaks its own rule.**
> יֵשׁ דֶּרֶךְ יָשָׁר לִפְנֵי־אִישׁ וְאַחֲרִיתָהּ דַּרְכֵי־מָוֶת
> "There is a way which seems right to a man, / But its end is the way of death."

**Every other verse in the collection tells you which kind of person you are looking at. This one says you cannot tell** — and it does so in the collection's own antithetical form, with דֶּרֶךְ ("way") standing in both halves. **The form says *contrast*; the content says *the contrast is invisible from inside*.** *High confidence — and it is the verse the sermon will most likely be built on.*

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory, run first.** The whole 184 verses contain **one** proper noun: שְׁלֹמֹה ("Solomon") at 10:1, and יְהוָה in twenty verses `[T]`, WLC. No place, no people, no person, no number with canonical freight. **A collection with one name in 184 verses is making a claim about its own scope** — nothing here is tied to an occasion, and the canonical-history check has nothing to bite on. *Inventory complete; no name-hyperlink findings.*

The words that do the work are six.

| Term | Where | Range | Usage here | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **צַדִּיק** ("righteous") | 39 verses here; 66 in the book `[T]` | legally in the right · in covenant good standing | Not a moral grade but a **standing** — the word of a verdict. Its opposite is not "sinner" but רָשָׁע, the one against whom a case goes | *High* |
| **רָשָׁע** ("wicked") | 39 verses here; 77 in the book `[T]` | guilty · condemned in a suit | The forensic partner of צַדִּיק. **The pair is a courtroom, not a character sketch** — which is why the collection can predicate outcomes of them without describing their inner lives | *High* |
| **תּוֹעֵבָה** ("abomination") | 7 verses here; 20 in the book; **6 of the 7 in the phrase תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה** `[T]` | what is excluded from the sanctuary | **Levitical/Deuteronomic classification vocabulary** — the term for idols, forbidden foods, and (Deut 25:16) false weights. Its presence at 11:1 activates the weights law | *Moderate-to-high* on the taxonomy; *high* on the count |
| **רָצוֹן** ("favour, delight") | 14 verses in the book, 4 of them paired with תּוֹעֵבָה `[T]` | acceptance · what is received | The sanctuary's positive term: what may be *brought in*. The pair sorts the world into accepted and excluded | *Moderate-to-high* |
| **לֵב** ("heart") | 26 verses here; 91 in the book `[T]` | the whole inner person — mind, will, affections | Not the seat of feeling but **the place where the case is actually decided** (15:28; 14:10). Densest here of any collection after chs 1–9 | *High* |
| **מְקוֹר חַיִּים** ("a fountain of life") | 10:11; 13:14; 14:27 here, 16:22 beyond `[T]` | a spring, a source that does not run dry | **Five verses in the whole Hebrew Bible**: Ps 36:10 and these four `[T]`, WLC. Three of the five are in this collection | *High* on the count |

**The speech cluster.** פֶּה ("mouth") stands in 16 verses, שָׂפָה ("lip") in 15, לָשׁוֹן ("tongue") in 6, דָּבָר ("word") in 10 `[T]`, WLC. Taken together **38 of the 184 verses (20.7%) name an organ of speech** — against 13.7% in chapters 1–9 `[T]`. **The collection is more interested in what a person says than in anything else he does**, and the two chief formulae of the divine name are attached to speech as well: לֹשׁוֹן שֶׁקֶר at 12:22 is what YHWH abominates, and the prayer of the upright at 15:8 is what he receives.

**Paronomasia check.** Run on the words recurring three or more times; nothing sounds. The collection's repetitions are lexical and formulaic rather than phonetic, which is consistent with material shaped for memorisation by category rather than by sound. *No finding.*

### 8. Translations

Five divergences bear on findings; the first is the one to take into the pulpit.

| | NASB95 | NIV84 | What turns on it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The six תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה verses** (11:1; 11:20; 12:22; 15:8; 15:9; 15:26) | "…**is an abomination to the LORD**" — a **verbless clause**, six times identically, with "**His delight**" as the matching term | "**The LORD detests** / **abhors** X" — a **verb of emotion** with YHWH as subject, and "**delights in**" / "**pleases him**" / "**loves**" opposite | **The Hebrew assigns a category; NIV84 reports a feeling.** תּוֹעֵבָה is the sanctuary's word for what is excluded, and מֹאזְנֵי מִרְמָה תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה says a rigged scale *belongs to that class* — the same class as an idol. The congregation hearing "the LORD detests dishonest scales" will hear divine disapproval, which is true and is not the claim. NIV84 also varies the verb once ("abhors", 11:1) where the Hebrew does not vary at all |

| **13:14 ‖ 14:27** | Both rendered identically | Both rendered identically | **The substitution doublet survives in both versions.** Whatever else the pulpit text costs, this finding can be preached straight from the pew Bible |
| **10:1a ‖ 15:20a** | "A wise son makes a father glad" at both | "A wise son brings joy to his father" at both | **The bracket is audible in both.** So is 10:2b ‖ 11:4b ("but righteousness delivers from death", identical in NIV84) |

**Pulpit divergence note.** NIV84 serves this collection better than might be expected: **it preserves every one of the self-quotations the report rests on**, which is the material a sermon on *how the book teaches* actually needs. **Its one real cost is the תּוֹעֵבָה formula**, where it converts a repeated cultic classification into a repeated statement about God's feelings — and since six verses are involved, the loss compounds. *If the sermon makes anything of "abomination", say once that the Hebrew says a thing* **is** *an abomination rather than that God feels one.*

**Ancient versions check — and it returns the largest finding in the report.**

- **10:1 — the Greek has no superscription.** The Hebrew reads מִשְׁלֵי שְׁלֹמֹה followed by a paragraph marker; **Swete has nothing there at all**, running out of 9:18's pluses directly into Υἱὸς σοφὸς εὐφραίνει πατέρα ("a wise son gladdens a father") `[T]`, Swete. **Triage: category 2, a substantive divergence.** Whether the Greek's *Vorlage* lacked the heading or the translator dropped it is not decidable from a diplomatic text; what is decidable is that **the Greek book has no seam here**, and the MT's second collection is not marked off as a collection at all. *High confidence on the fact; the explanation is an open question.* Any citation should come from Rahlfs-Hanhart or Göttingen via Logos, not from this observation layer.
- **15:33 — the Greek has moved it.** MT's closing motto stands at **Swete 16:4**, φόβος θεοῦ παιδεία καὶ σοφία ("the fear of God is instruction and wisdom"), *after* the material MT places at 16:6–8 and after MT 15:30 and 15:32 (Swete 16:2, 16:3) `[T]`, Swete. BHS records the reordering at the apparatus to 16:6 `[S: BHS]`. **Triage: category 2.** **In the Greek the motto does not close anything.** *High confidence on the placement.*
- **14:12 ‖ 16:25 — the Greek breaks the doublet.** The Hebrew verses are consonantally identical; Swete renders the first ἔστιν ὁδὸς ἣ δοκεῖ… ἔρχεται εἰς πυθμένα ᾅδου (singular "way", "comes to the depth of Hades") and the second εἰσὶν ὁδοὶ δοκοῦσαι… βλέπει εἰς πυθμένα ᾅδου (plural "ways", a different verb) `[T]`, Swete. **Triage: category 1 for the loss of the repetition — a translator working verse by verse need not notice a doublet 68 verses away — with a category-2 element in both renderings' ᾅδης for מָוֶת.** *High confidence on the wording.*
- **10:12 — the Greek has no sins in it, and the New Testament does.** The Hebrew's וְעַל כָּל־פְּשָׁעִים תְּכַסֶּה אַהֲבָה ("but love covers all transgressions") appears in Swete as πάντας δὲ τοὺς μὴ φιλονεικοῦντας καλύπτει φιλία ("but friendship covers all who are not contentious") `[T]`, Swete — **a different noun for love and no word for sin at all.** **1 Pet 4:8 and Jas 5:20 have ἀγάπη … πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν** `[T]`, SBLGNT, **which follows the Hebrew's sense against this Greek witness.** **Triage: category 2 on the Greek rendering, and category 3 on the apostolic wording.** *A diplomatic Greek text cannot tell you what every Greek copy read; the finding is that the Old Greek the corpus holds is not what the apostles are quoting here.* Routed to Open Questions and to Logos. *High confidence on all three wordings.* See Tool 11.
- **11:1 — the Greek keeps the category.** Swete's standard rendering of תּוֹעֵבָה is **βδέλυγμα**, the Levitical word (so at 15:26, βδέλυγμα Κυρίῳ λογισμὸς ἄδικος) `[T]`, Swete. **The ancient version reads the term as classification, as the Hebrew does, and the modern dynamic version does not.** *That split between the ancient and the modern tradition is itself the finding, and it supports the pulpit note above.*

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Flat, level, and relentless — and the flatness is a deliberate instrument, not a stylistic failure.**

184 verses, almost all of them two lines, almost all of them joined by a waw, none of them addressed to anybody. **There is no crescendo, no vocative, no question, and no appeal.** Chapters 1–9 shout in the street; this material does not raise its voice once.

What carries the tone: **accumulation**. The devices are the absence of devices. No simile governs a verse until the collection is well under way; the imagery, where it comes, is domestic and agricultural — a negligent hand (10:4), a son asleep in harvest (10:5), a ring of gold in a pig's snout (11:22), a soothing tongue (15:4). **The register is a man watching how things usually turn out and saying so.**

**Two places where the tone breaks, and both are worth the preacher's attention.** At **14:10** לֵב יוֹדֵעַ מָרַּת נַפְשׁוֹ ("the heart knows its own bitterness") the collection turns inward for one verse and concedes that another person's grief is not available to you. At **14:13** גַּם־בִּשְׂחוֹק יִכְאַב־לֵב ("even in laughter the heart may be in pain") it concedes that the outside of a life does not report its inside. **In a collection whose method is to read people by their visible outcomes, two verses saying you cannot read a person are a crack in the wall** — and they sit either side of 14:12, the way that seems right. **14:12 and 14:13 share a word**: וְאַחֲרִיתָהּ ("and its end"), the same form in both `[T]`, WLC. *A road whose end is death, and a laugh whose end is grief, in consecutive verses.* *Moderate-to-high confidence that the cluster is deliberate; the corpus establishes the adjacency, not the intent.*

**Soundtrack:** a metronome. Not a violin and not a fanfare — something that does not vary and does not stop, until 14:10–13, where it misses a beat.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources for this book** `[S: overview]`: Genesis 1–3, Deuteronomy in four strands, Job 28, Psalms, the Solomon tradition, Amenemope. **Proverbs contains no citation formula anywhere** `[T]`, so everything below is allusion or shared vocabulary, and **Move 4 is where the work is** — which is the overview's own prediction for passage work in this book `[S: overview]`, and it holds.

**Genesis 2–3 → Prov 11:30; 13:12; 15:4** *(high confidence on the lexical fact; moderate on allusion)*

*Source context:* עֵץ הַחַיִּים ("the tree of life") stands in the garden at Gen 2:9, is guarded at 3:22 and 3:24, and is the thing the expulsion is *for* — lest the man eat and live for ever. In its own passage it is not a metaphor: it is an object with a location, and the story ends with access to it closed.

*Book usage:* Proverbs uses the phrase **four times — 3:18; 11:30; 13:12; 15:4** `[T]`, WLC, and **three of the four are in this collection**. At 3:18 wisdom *is* one; here it is **the fruit of the righteous** (11:30), **a desire fulfilled** (13:12), and **a soothing tongue** (15:4).

*OT-to-OT:* the phrase occurs in the Hebrew Bible only in Genesis 2–3 and Proverbs `[T]`, WLC — a closed pair.

*What it adds:* **Proverbs takes the one object the canon's third chapter closes off and hands it out as a predicate.** Not "wisdom will get you back to the garden", but: *the thing that was withdrawn is what a healed tongue is like.* **And the collection's own use is the boldest of the four** — 3:18 says wisdom is a tree of life, which is a claim about wisdom; 15:4 says a **soothing tongue** is one, which is a claim about Tuesday. *The link is carried by a two-word phrase and must not be over-pressed; but the phrase has exactly two homes in the canon.*

**Deuteronomy 25:13–16 → Prov 11:1** *(high confidence)*

*Source context:* Deut 25 closes the covenant's case law. vv.13–16 forbid אֶבֶן וָאֶבֶן ("differing weights") and end כִּי תוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ כָּל־עֹשֵׂה אֵלֶּה ("for everyone who does these things… is an abomination to the LORD your God"). **The law itself supplies both halves of Prov 11:1 — the false balance and the category it belongs to.**

*Book usage:* Proverbs returns to the weights four times — 11:1; 16:11; 20:10; 20:23 — and 20:10 carries Deuteronomy's doubled-noun idiom outright `[S: overview, confirmed]`. **11:1 is the first of the four and it opens its chapter.**

*OT-to-OT:* Lev 19:35–36 legislates the same balances without the תּוֹעֵבָה category; Deuteronomy supplies the category. **Proverbs has taken the Deuteronomic version, not the Levitical one.**

*What it adds:* the collection's second chapter opens by restating a covenant law as a proverb — **and by keeping the law's own classification word.** A sermon that reads 11:1 as commercial ethics has read half of it; the verse says a rigged scale is in the same class as an idol.

**Job 28:28 → Prov 14:16** *(moderate confidence — the phrase is short)*

*Source context:* Job 28 asks for twenty-seven verses where wisdom can be found and answers הֵן יִרְאַת אֲדֹנָי הִיא חָכְמָה וְסוּר מֵרָע בִּינָה ("behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom, and to depart from evil is understanding"). **It is the terminus of a failed search.**

*Book usage:* the pairing of fearing and turning from evil stands at 3:7, **14:16** and 16:6 `[T]`, WLC. 3:7 is a prohibition — אַל־תְּהִי חָכָם בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("do not be wise in your own eyes"), then יְרָא אֶת־יְהוָה וְסוּר מֵרָע. **14:16 turns the prohibition into a description**: חָכָם יָרֵא וְסָר מֵרָע ("a wise man fears and turns away from evil").

*What it adds:* Job states the equation as the end of an enquiry; Proverbs uses it as a rule of life, and **this collection is where the rule stops being addressed to anyone and becomes a report on what a wise man does.** *The direction of dependence is not recoverable.*

**Proverbs 10:12 and 11:31 → 1 Peter 4:8, 18** *(high confidence on the wordings; the direction of each is the finding)*

**This is the only place in the report where the traffic runs the other way** — a later book citing this collection — and the triad is run on it because it is the collection's one demonstrable New Testament footprint.

*Source context (Prov 10:12; 11:31):* both stand in the collection's opening two chapters, in its ordinary antithetical form and with no special marking. 10:12 שִׂנְאָה תְּעוֹרֵר מְדָנִים וְעַל כָּל־פְּשָׁעִים תְּכַסֶּה אַהֲבָה ("hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all transgressions") is a saying about what love does to a quarrel. 11:31 הֵן צַדִּיק בָּאָרֶץ יְשֻׁלָּם אַף כִּי־רָשָׁע וְחוֹטֵא ("if the righteous is recompensed on the earth, how much more the wicked and the sinner") is an *a fortiori* about recompense **in this life** — בָּאָרֶץ, "on the earth", is the word that carries it.

*Book usage:* Proverbs is quoted in the New Testament at 3:11–12 (Heb 12:5–6), 3:34 (Jas 4:6; 1 Pet 5:5), 25:21–22 (Rom 12:20) and 26:11 (2 Pet 2:22) `[S]` — **and 1 Peter reaches for it three times**, twice from this collection and once from the frame. *That a single short letter should draw on Proverbs three times is a fact about 1 Peter; that two of the three come from these six chapters is a fact about this collection's currency.*

*The two directions, and this is the point:*

| | Hebrew (WLC) | Swete | 1 Peter (SBLGNT) | Triage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **10:12** | אַהֲבָה ("love") covers כָּל־פְּשָׁעִים ("all transgressions") | φιλία ("friendship") covers πάντας τοὺς μὴ φιλονεικοῦντας ("all the non-contentious") — **no sins in it** | ἀγάπη καλύπτει **πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν** ("love covers a multitude of **sins**") | **The apostle's wording follows the Hebrew's sense against this Greek witness** |
| **11:31** | צַדִּיק בָּאָרֶץ יְשֻׁלָּם ("the righteous is **recompensed on the earth**") | εἰ ὁ δίκαιος μόλις σῴζεται ("if the righteous is **scarcely saved**") | εἰ ὁ δίκαιος μόλις σῴζεται — **verbatim** | **Category 3: the Greek is the New Testament's own text** |

*What it adds:* **the two verses land in the same chapter of 1 Peter, eleven verses apart, and they are being used for the same argument** — a church under pressure told to love each other earnestly (4:8) and then told that judgement begins at the house of God (4:17–18). **The proverb about a quarrel becomes a rule for a suffering congregation; the proverb about recompense in this life becomes a statement about being saved at all.** *And the wording at 4:18 is a category-3 case in its clearest form: the Greek's μόλις σῴζεται is what Peter argues from, and it stands whichever text is prior. Do not "correct" it from the Hebrew in the pulpit.* **What the 10:12 case shows is the converse — that the New Testament is not simply reading the Greek Proverbs the corpus holds.** *Whether another Greek recension read ἁμαρτιῶν at 10:12 is beyond a diplomatic witness; that question goes to Rahlfs-Hanhart or Göttingen via Logos, and it is routed to Open Questions.* Jas 5:20 carries the same phrase, καλύψει πλῆθος ἁμαρτιῶν `[T]`, SBLGNT.

**Internal echoes (Move 4)** — the collection's opening material was held open throughout, as the move requires.

- `Internal:` **9:1 → 14:1** — *answers §earlier*, and it is the clearest bridge from the frame into the field. Wisdom personified builds her house at 9:1 חָכְמוֹת בָּנְתָה בֵיתָהּ; at **14:1** חַכְמוֹת נָשִׁים בָּנְתָה בֵיתָהּ ("the wise woman builds her house"). **Same odd plural form חָכְמוֹת, same verb בנה, same noun בַּיִת** `[T]`, WLC — the form stands four times in Proverbs (1:20; 9:1; 14:1; 24:7) and once elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible (Ps 49:4). **The woman who was a figure in a tableau becomes a woman in a house.** *High confidence on the lexical link; moderate on the reading.*

- `Internal:` **3:7 → 12:15** — *answers §earlier*, and it is the series' own spine. The frame prohibits being חָכָם בְּעֵינֶיךָ ("wise in your own eyes", 3:7); **12:15** supplies the first case study: דֶּרֶךְ אֱוִיל יָשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו ("the way of a fool is right in his own eyes") `[T]`. **The prohibition is in the frame; the man who ignored it is in the field.** *High confidence.*
- `Internal:` **3:18 → 11:30; 13:12; 15:4** — *answers §earlier*. The tree of life, above. *High confidence on the chain.*
- `Internal:` **14:12 → 16:25** — *planted for later*, and it is the collection's one exact whole-verse self-quotation across the seam `[T]`. **The verse that says you cannot read a road is the verse the next collection repeats.** *High confidence.*
- `Internal:` **13:14 → 14:27** — *within the unit*. The substitution doublet, Headline 5. **What is said of a wise man's teaching is said, thirteen verses later, of the fear of YHWH** `[T]`. *High confidence.*
- `Internal:` **10:11 → 16:22** — *planted for later*. מְקוֹר חַיִּים passes from the righteous man's mouth (10:11) through the wise man's teaching (13:14) and the fear of YHWH (14:27) to שֵׂכֶל ("understanding", 16:22) `[T]`. **Four subjects, one predicate, across the seam.** *High confidence.*

### 12. Genre

**Wisdom — the sentence collection (מָשָׁל), in two-line antithetical form.** The reading rules are the ones that decide whether the sermon does harm.

1. **A מָשָׁל is a generalisation about how the world usually runs, not a covenant undertaking.** The book supplies its own counter-cases, and **the clearest of them is inside this collection**: 13:23 רָב־אֹכֶל נִיר רָאשִׁים וְיֵשׁ נִסְפֶּה בְּלֹא מִשְׁפָּט ("abundant food is in the fallow ground of the poor, but it is swept away by injustice") `[T]`. **A collection that says the righteous will not go hungry (10:3) also says a poor man's harvest is taken by injustice (13:23), eighty verses later, and does not reconcile them.**
2. **The unit of meaning is the verse.** There is no argument between verses, and the manuscript makes almost no divisions (Tool 3). **Reading consecutive proverbs as consecutive steps is a genre error**, and it is the commonest one here.
3. **The form itself teaches.** Two lines, a caesura, a waw, and two opposed words. **The reader is being trained to hold two possibilities in view at once and assign the case to one of them** — which is what the antithesis rate is measuring.

### 13. Copycat

**Largely N/A** — there are no narrative characters to imitate or avoid. **One observation earns the section.** The collection's "characters" are **types, not persons**: the righteous, the wicked, the wise, the fool, the sluggard, the diligent. `[T]` **A type cannot be copied and cannot be avoided; it can only be recognised.** The reader is not asked *be like the righteous man* — no verse asks him anything — but is handed a description and left to see which one fits. *That is a different operation from imitation, and a sermon that converts it into imitation has changed the genre.*

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits:** the Solomon tradition, in a book whose own superscriptions place material under Solomon (1:1; 10:1) and report a later transcription under Hezekiah (25:1) `[T]`. Who compiled this collection and when is not stated by the book and is a reconstruction `[S]`.

**Canonical position:** Ketuvim, after Psalms and Job `[T]`. **The reader who arrives at 10:1 has just read Job**, and Job is the canon's fullest statement that the outcomes this collection predicts do not always arrive. *The order does not let the confidence come first* `[S: overview]`.

**Where the reader sits:** this side of the cross. What has happened in between bears on the collection in one specific way. **The two words that carry it — צַדִּיק and רָשָׁע — are forensic**, and the New Testament's use of the first is the hinge: ὁ δὲ δίκαιος ἐκ πίστεως ζήσεται ("the righteous shall live by faith", Rom 1:17, quoting Hab 2:4) `[T]`, SBLGNT. **The collection sorts people into the two classes and never asks how anyone gets into the first.** That is not a defect in it; it is the question the book itself raises at 20:9 and does not answer `[S: overview]`.

**How that reframes the reading:** the categories stand, and the way into the right one has been supplied from outside the book. **Neither half of that sentence may be dropped.** Dropping the first produces a sermon that treats Proverbs as sub-Christian; dropping the second produces the moralism the overview's trap 5 names.

### 15. Who Am I?

**There is no one to be.** The collection has no characters, no addressee and no vocative `[T]`. Its "you" is absent and its types are third-person throughout.

**So the identification question changes shape, and this is the most useful thing the tool does here.** The reader is not invited to identify with anyone; he is invited to **find himself described** — and the collection has arranged matters so that he will find himself on both sides of the caesura in the course of 184 verses. **14:12 is the verse that makes the arrangement explicit**: there is a way that seems right *to a man*, and the man is not told which one he is on.

**A note for the preacher who is about to stand up.** The one role the collection does supply is the one it withholds: **there is no teacher in it.** The father of chapters 1–9 has stopped speaking; the sages of 22:17 have not yet arrived. *In this collection the preacher is not the voice; he is one of the cases.*

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response the author seeks.** Not obedience: there is no command here. Not decision: there is no appeal. **What the collection seeks is a trained eye** — a reader who has held the two categories side by side so many times that he sees a situation in their terms without being told to. The method is saturation and the sought response is **recognition**.

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**

| Domain | Application |
|---|---|
| **Worldview** | The world is morally legible **most of the time and not always** — 10:3 and 13:23 stand in the same collection. A reader who takes only the first becomes brittle; a reader who takes only the second stops looking. **The collection asks for both, and does not resolve them** |
| **Behaviour — start** | Watching speech, one's own first: 20.7% of these verses name a mouth, a lip or a tongue `[T]`. And reading cases rather than collecting maxims — the discipline of asking, of an actual situation, which of the two lines it belongs on |
| **Behaviour — stop** | Quoting single verses as guarantees. **The collection's own 13:23 forbids it**, and 14:12 forbids the more subtle version: concluding that because a road looks straight, it is |
| **Motivation** | 15:33 — the fear of the LORD is what wisdom's instruction *is*. **The collection ends by saying that the whole apparatus of categories rests on a relation**, which is what 1:7 said before the apparatus started |

**Four audiences.**

- **For me:** the collection describes me on both sides of the caesura, and 14:10's "the heart knows its own bitterness" is the verse that stops me diagnosing anyone else too quickly.
- **For a Christian friend:** for anyone who has been handed Proverbs as a set of promises and found they did not hold, **13:23 is the verse to show them** — it is in the same collection, and the book put it there.
- **For the church:** a congregation that has learned to read a מָשָׁל can read the rest of the book; one that has three favourite verses cannot. **This collection is where the reading is taught**, and it is taught by volume rather than by rule.
- **For an unbeliever:** the collection makes a claim anyone can test — that lives run in recognisable ways and that the ways have ends. It also, at 14:12, concedes the thing that makes the claim bearable: **that a man cannot certify his own road.**

**Prayer in response.** Thanks for a book that says how things usually go and does not pretend they always do. Sorry for the verses used as guarantees and for the diagnoses made of other people. Please give the trained eye this collection is trying to form, and the honesty of 14:12 about my own road.

**Grammatical and syntactical features.**

- **The verbless clause is the collection's signature sentence.** מֹאזְנֵי מִרְמָה תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה (11:1); יִרְאַת יְהוָה מוּסַר חָכְמָה (15:33); תּוֹרַת חָכָם מְקוֹר חַיִּים (13:14). **Hebrew can assert identity without a verb, and this material does so constantly.** English cannot, so every one of these becomes "X *is* Y" — which is right, and which quietly adds a copula the Hebrew did not need. *The theological effect is worth a sentence in the study: these are not statements about what God does but about what things* **are**.
- **The waw at the caesura, 159 times** `[T]`. See Tool 4. The Hebrew conjunction is not adversative in itself; **the opposition is lexical and the waw is neutral**, so the English "but" is an interpretation made 159 times over.
- **No imperative governs the collection.** Chapter 10 has none addressed to a reader; the instruction material either side of this deposit is imperative-dense (25 imperatives in ch. 4 alone) `[S: chs 1–9 dig]`. **The mood of this collection is the plain indicative.**

**Wordplay and sound.** The paronomasia check (Tool 7) returns nothing. **What the collection does instead of sound-play is *formulaic* play** — the same predicate re-used with a new subject (13:14 / 14:27; 10:11 / 16:22). *That is a compositional device rather than a phonetic one, and it is the device a preacher can actually demonstrate in English.*

**Recommended verification:** the semantic weight of תּוֹעֵבָה in wisdom as against legal contexts — whether the sanctuary force is fully live at 11:1 or has weakened into "detestable thing" — is a lexicon question (HALOT, NIDOTTE, or TDOT's article). The corpus establishes the distribution and cannot settle the sense.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Nothing in the 184 verses is dated, placed or occasioned `[T]` — one proper noun, no king in the frame of the collection itself (מֶלֶךְ stands in only **2** of these verses against 12 in 16:1–22:16) `[T]`, WLC, no temple, no festival, no enemy.

**What the original audience knew that we do not.**

- **A balance was a public instrument and a rigged one was a public crime.** 11:1's מֹאזְנֵי מִרְמָה assumes a market where weights were carried in a pouch and could be swapped; Deut 25:13 says as much. **The verse is about a thing in a man's bag, not a metaphor.**
- **Grain harvest has a narrow window.** 10:5's son asleep in harvest is not lazy in general; he has missed a fortnight that does not come again. **The proverb is about timing, and an agrarian hearer would feel the clock in it.**
- **Speech was the main instrument of public life** in a society with low literacy, where reputation, legal testimony and commerce all ran through what a man said in the gate. **That, and not a modern interest in "communication", is why a fifth of these verses name a mouth.**
- **תּוֹעֵבָה was a sanctuary word before it was an ethical one.** A hearer who knew Leviticus and Deuteronomy would hear 11:1 place a shopkeeper's scales in the same class as an idol `[I]`, *moderate-to-high*.

**What this changes.** It moves three of the collection's commonest topics — weights, harvest, speech — out of the register of personal virtue and into the register of **public, checkable behaviour**. *The collection is not describing an inner life. It is describing what other people can see.*

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Ketuvim `[T]`; the reader arrives after the Torah, the Prophets, the Psalter and Job `[I]`, high confidence. **He has just read Job**, which is the canon's fullest counter-witness to the outcomes this collection states.

**The first hearers.** A young man being handed the categories by which his community graded conduct, and a household or school for whom the categories were worth transmitting `[I]`.

**Where the passage fits their world.** They would have recognised the two-line form immediately, expected the second line to turn, and been at home with the assumption that character shows itself in outcomes. **They would not have expected the address to stop** — chapters 1–9 have called them "my son" fifteen times and the vocative does not come back.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Surprising:** the silence. Nobody is addressed for 184 verses.
- **Shocking:** 13:23 — a poor man's field yields plenty and it is swept away **בְּלֹא מִשְׁפָּט** ("for lack of justice"). In a collection that has just said the LORD will not let the righteous go hungry (10:3).
- **Comforting:** 15:29, that he hears the prayer of the righteous — and the collection's two closing verses, where instruction and humility have the last word rather than outcome.
- **Disturbing:** 14:12, and the fact that it is not withdrawn.

**What we bring that they didn't.** A habit of reading proverbs as promises, which a culture that had also memorised 13:23 would have found strange. An interest in motive that this material does not share. And an expectation that a religious text will address us — **which is exactly what this collection declines to do.**

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**(1) The two ways.** *Where this passage sits:* the fullest statement in the canon of the two-ways pattern, by volume `[T]`. *Before:* Deut 30:15–20's life and death set before the people; Ps 1's two ways, with the same pair צַדִּיק / רְשָׁעִים `[T]`. *After:* Jer 17:5–8 reworking Ps 1; and in the NT, the two ways at Matt 7:13–14 — **where the gate that leads to life is the narrow one, and the broad road is the one that looks passable.** *Confidence: high on the OT chain; the Matt 7 link is thematic, not verbal.*
*Contribution:* Proverbs supplies the pattern's working vocabulary, and 14:12 supplies its one honest qualification.

**(2) The tree of life.** *Where this passage sits:* three of the canon's four non-Genesis occurrences `[T]`. *Before:* Gen 2:9; 3:22, 24. *After:* Rev 2:7 and 22:2, 14, 19, where access is restored and the tree is in the city. *Confidence: high on the occurrences; the Revelation link is canonical rather than allusive.*
*Contribution:* **between the garden and the city, the phrase lives almost entirely in Proverbs, and in this collection it is attached to ordinary things** — fruit, a fulfilled longing, a kind word.

**(3) What may be brought in.** *Where this passage sits:* six תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה verses, four of them paired with רָצוֹן `[T]`. *Before:* Lev 19:35–36; Deut 25:13–16. *After:* the prophets' collision of sacrifice and justice (Isa 1:11–17; Amos 5:21–24), which says what 15:8 says in a single line. *Confidence: high on the vocabulary; moderate on the arc.*
*Contribution:* **the collection quietly relocates the sanctuary's admission criteria into the market and the mouth.** A scale and a lying tongue are sorted by the same word that sorts an offering.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection: trajectory and contrast. No typology is claimed** — there is no person, institution or event in 184 verses to serve as a type, and a typological claim would fail the category test at the first step.

**How the collection points to Christ.**

- **The unanswered question the categories raise.** The collection sorts everyone into צַדִּיק or רָשָׁע and **never once says how a man moves from the second class to the first** `[T]`. That is not an oversight; it is the shape of the book, which asks at 20:9 מִי־יֹאמַר זִכִּיתִי לִבִּי ("who can say, 'I have cleansed my heart'?") and does not answer `[S: overview]`. **The New Testament's answer uses the collection's own word**: δικαιοσύνη θεοῦ ("the righteousness of God") given to those who had none (Rom 3:21–26) `[T]`, SBLGNT. *High confidence on the trajectory; the link is by the term, not by citation.*
- **The tree of life, taken out of the garden and given back.** Proverbs hands the phrase to ordinary things; Revelation puts the tree in the city with its leaves for the healing of the nations (Rev 22:2) `[T]`, SBLGNT. **The trajectory runs from an object closed off, through a predicate, to an object restored.** *Moderate-to-high.*
- **The contrast, and it is 14:12.** "There is a way which seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death." **Nothing in this collection retrieves the man on that road**, and the collection does not pretend otherwise. *The most Christ-shaped feature of the material is the shape of the gap it leaves.*
- **One caution.** The fountain-of-life chain (10:11 → 13:14 → 14:27 → 16:22) is **not** a christological chain in itself; its four subjects are a righteous man's mouth, a wise man's teaching, the fear of YHWH and understanding. **Preach it as the collection's own convergence on where life comes from, and let the New Testament supply the name** — do not read Christ into the fourth slot as though the text had.

**Trajectory.** *Anticipates:* a righteousness that can be had, and a way whose end can be known before it arrives. *Christ fulfils:* the first directly, in the collection's own vocabulary; the second by being the way rather than by describing one. *Remains:* the categories still sort us, and 13:23 still happens.

**Moralism check.**

- **The "be like X" temptation:** here it is the strongest in the book, because the material is a catalogue of behaviours. **The live danger is a sermon of the form "be the righteous man, not the wicked one"** — which is available from almost any verse and which the collection never says, since it contains no imperative and no addressee `[T]`.
- **The gospel grounding:** the categories are forensic, and a verdict is something received, not achieved.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** there is no human figure here to be the hero, which removes one temptation and leaves another — **making the collection's implied competent reader the hero.** The corrective is 14:12: the reader cannot certify his own road.

**Confidence:** *high* on the trajectory; *no typological claim advanced*; the Rev 22 link is canonical reflection and is labelled as such.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

- **10:3 against 13:23.** "The LORD will not allow the righteous to hunger" and "abundant food is in the fallow ground of the poor, but it is swept away by injustice" `[T]`. **The collection makes both statements and reconciles neither.** Not a contradiction to be solved but a genre feature to be taught — and the pastoral cost of preaching only the first is high.
- **11:31 — recompense on earth, or scarcely saved?** The Hebrew says the righteous is **recompensed on the earth**; Swete says he is **scarcely saved**; and **1 Pet 4:18 quotes the Greek verbatim** `[T]`, SBLGNT + Swete. **Triage: category 3 — the Greek is the New Testament's own text here**, and it stands whichever is prior. **The difficulty is real and it is not a translation error to be tidied**: a preacher working from an English Old Testament and an English New Testament will find the same verse saying two different things and should say so rather than choose. See Tool 11.
- **13:24 — the rod.** "He who withholds his rod hates his son." In a congregation this is the book's sharpest pastoral landmine after 22:15 and 23:13–14 `[S: overview, trap 6]`. **Handle deliberately or leave it; do not let it arrive by accident in a survey sermon.**
- **14:12 — whether the man can ever know.** The verse states the problem and the collection does not solve it. *This is a real crux and a sermon is stronger for saying so than for supplying a solution the text withholds.*
- **No other pastoral landmines** of comparable weight in the 184 verses.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: Preaching the collection as a structured argument

- **What it looks like:** "Chapter 10 on the righteous, chapter 11 on integrity, chapter 12 on the tongue…" — or any sermon that moves through the unit in thematic sections.
- **Why it's wrong:** the manuscript makes **two** breaks in 184 verses `[T]`; there is no governing connector; there is no addressee; and the vocabulary is flat across all six chapters. **The structure is imposed, and it will be imposed differently by every preacher who tries** — which is the overview's trap 4, biting harder here than anywhere `[S: overview]`.
- **The corrective:** preach what the collection *is* before preaching what it *says*. Its shape is the two-line contrast repeated 184 times, and the sermon can demonstrate that in three minutes with the counts.

### Pitfall: Preaching the proverbs as promises

- **What it looks like:** 10:3 — "the LORD will not allow the righteous to hunger" — preached as an undertaking; 10:4 preached as a doctrine of prosperity.
- **Why it's wrong:** **the collection itself says otherwise eighty verses later.** 13:23 has a poor man's field yield plenty and the yield swept away בְּלֹא מִשְׁפָּט ("for lack of justice") `[T]`. A מָשָׁל states how things usually run.
- **The corrective:** **read 10:3 and 13:23 aloud together.** The congregation will hear the genre lesson without being taught it, and the preacher will have pre-empted the objection that otherwise arrives in the car park.

### Pitfall: "Be the righteous man, not the wicked one"

- **What it looks like:** the obvious sermon, available from almost any verse in the unit.
- **Why it's wrong:** **there is no imperative and no addressee in 184 verses** `[T]`. The words are forensic — צַדִּיק and רָשָׁע are the vocabulary of a verdict — and **the collection never says how anyone gets into the first class.** A sermon that supplies the missing exhortation has changed both the mood and the theology.
- **The corrective:** let the categories stand as description, and let the question they raise stand too. The book asks it at 20:9 and does not answer it; the answer comes from outside the book, in the collection's own word — *the righteousness of God* given to those who had none.

### Pitfall: Making 14:12 the reassuring verse

- **What it looks like:** "There is a way that seems right… but thank God we have his word to show us the right one."
- **Why it's wrong:** the verse does not say that, and **nothing in the collection retrieves the man on that road.** Its parallel at 14:13 makes the same move about joy, and 16:25 repeats the whole verse without softening it `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach it as the collection's own limit-statement. **It is the verse that stops the sermon becoming a technique**, and it is more use to a congregation unresolved than resolved.

### Pitfall: Translating "abomination" as a feeling

- **What it looks like:** "God hates dishonest scales" — warm, vivid, and the way the pulpit text puts it six times.
- **Why it's wrong:** תּוֹעֲבַת יְהוָה is a **verbless classification**, and the class is Deuteronomy's — the one that holds idols and false weights (Deut 25:16) `[T]`. The Hebrew says a rigged scale *belongs* there; it does not report divine emotion. **Swete keeps the category (βδέλυγμα); NIV84 does not.**
- **The corrective:** one sentence restores it — *the Hebrew says the scales are an abomination, not that God feels one* — and the verse gets sharper, not milder.

### Pitfall: Quoting 11:31 from the Old Testament and 1 Peter 4:18 from the New as though they said the same thing

- **What it looks like:** an unremarked slide between "the righteous is recompensed on the earth" and "if the righteous is scarcely saved".
- **Why it's wrong:** they are the same verse in two textual traditions, and **Peter is arguing from the Greek** `[T]`. The English Old Testament renders the Hebrew and the English New Testament renders Peter's Greek, so the discrepancy is real and visible to anyone with a concordance.
- **The corrective:** name it. It takes a sentence, it is honest, and it teaches the congregation something true about how the apostles read their Bible.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

- **Why does the Greek have no superscription at 10:1?** A *Vorlage* without it, or a translator who did not reproduce headings? A diplomatic text cannot decide. Rahlfs-Hanhart and Göttingen, via Logos. **This is the run's most consequential open question, because the sermon's text begins there.**
