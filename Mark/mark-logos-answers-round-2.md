## <span id="anchor"></span>Question 1: Ephphatha and the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 (Mark 7:34–35)

**The claim being tested.** In the Hebrew of Isa 35:5 the ears of the deaf are **opened**: וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה. The Greek has “will **hear**” (ἀκούσονται). Mark has Εφφαθα … Διανοίχθητι (“be opened”, 7:34) and “his ears were opened” (7:35).

If the link holds, it is a seventh place where Mark stands nearer the Hebrew than the Greek. The link to Isa 35:5–6 itself is already rated high, because μογιλάλος (“speaking with difficulty”) occurs only at Isa 35:6 in the Greek OT and at Mark 7:32 in the NT. What is new is the “opened” point.

1a. Scope: Mark commentaries, then OT-in-NT.

- **Ask:** Which commentators on Mark 7:31–37 connect “Ephphatha, that is, be opened” and “his ears were opened” (7:34–35) with Isaiah 35:5? Do any of them point out that the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 says the ears of the deaf will be “opened”, where the Septuagint says they “will hear”?

- **Why:** The audit found the Hebrew point. If no commentator has made it, it is a genuine finding of this work. If one has, it is a rediscovery and should be credited, as Watts was for Isa 42:16.

- Watch for:

  - an answer about μογιλάλος only, which is already settled;
  - an answer that quotes the English of Isa 35:5 (“unstopped”, “opened”) and treats that as the Hebrew. Check which language the commentator actually argues from.

Several commentators identify the connection between Mark’s healing narrative and [Isaiah 35:5](https://ref.ly/Is35.5), though none of the search results directly address the specific Hebrew-versus-Septuagint distinction you’re asking about.

English notes that the word describing the man’s speech difficulty appears only twice in the Greek Bible—here and in [Isaiah 35:6](https://ref.ly/Is35.6)—and observes that the crowd’s words expressing amazement (“He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak”) echo [Isaiah 35:5–6](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6), a passage where the prophet speaks of the coming Messiah.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BSTUS62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABSTUS62MK%2F2016-10-03T14%3A58%3A27Z%2F328180) Hurtado similarly identifies the speech impediment term as appearing only in Mark and [Isaiah 35:6](https://ref.ly/Is35.6), calling it “a deliberate allusion in Mark.”[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIBCNT62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIBCNT62MK%2F2012-08-03T21%3A42%3A08Z%2F280490)

Garland observes that when the people exclaim “he has done everything well” and “he even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak,” their words echo Scripture, with the second echo coming from Isaiah’s promise that “the ears of the deaf unstopped” and “the mute tongue shout for joy” ([Isa. 35:5–6a](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6)).[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC62MK%2F2018-02-23T16%3A40%3A32Z%2F848211) Cooper also notes that the words “He even makes the deaf hear and the mute speak” reflect [Isaiah 35:5–6](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6), which refers to the healing power of the coming Messiah.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:HNTC62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3AHNTC62MK%2F2014-10-16T00%3A21%3A00Z%2F264656)

Evans connects the healing to [Isaiah 35:5–6](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6), citing the phrase “the ears of the deaf will be unstopped.”[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BKBC61MT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABKBC61MT%2F2016-02-11T02%3A20%3A58Z%2F1133761)

However, **none of these commentaries examine the Hebrew text of **[**Isaiah 35:5**](https://ref.ly/Is35.5) **or compare it with the Septuagint rendering**, so they do not address your specific question about the difference between “opened” (Hebrew) and “will hear” (Septuagint).

Regarding the specific Hebrew versus Septuagint distinction you asked about, Watts does discuss the wording of [Isaiah 35:5](https://ref.ly/Is35.5). He observes that [Isaiah 35:5](https://ref.ly/Is35.5) portrays the age of restoration as a time when “the eyes of the blind will (literally?) be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped.”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F663287) However, this phrasing reflects the English translation rather than an explicit comparison between the Hebrew and Greek texts.

Watts does not directly address the difference between the Hebrew word for “opened” (פתח) and the Septuagint’s rendering emphasizing “hearing” (ἀκούω). His focus remains on the conceptual connection between Mark’s healing narrative and Isaiah’s eschatological vision. He emphasizes that in the deaf-mute healing, Jesus “effects a healing of a kind promised in the INE (i.e. [Isa 35:5f](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6))”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F670726), situating the passage within his broader argument about Mark’s presentation of Jesus inaugurating Isaiah’s New Exodus.

So while Watts clearly connects [Mark 7:31–37](https://ref.ly/Mk7.31-37) with [Isaiah 35:5–6](https://ref.ly/Is35.5-6), he does not engage in the textual-critical analysis comparing Hebrew and Septuagint wording that your question addresses.

**1c. No AI credits.** In France (NIGTC) and Williams (EGGNT), read the comment on Mark 7:34 for ἐφφαθα and διανοίχθητι. Note whether either mentions the Hebrew פָּתַח (“open”) of Isa 35:5.

Εφφαθα probably represents the Aramaic[^1] ethpeel imperative, *ʾetp*<sup>*e*</sup>*taḥ*, which has a reflexive sense: ‘Open up!’ If, as Mark’s translation indicates, the form is singular, it is formally addressed to the man (who ought not to be able to hear it!) rather than to his ears, but in effect it is the ears which are commanded to function again. The use of Aramaic in a probably non-Jewish context is not particularly significant, since the language was widely spoken outside Jewish circles, but Mark’s recording of the Aramaic form suggests a memory of an impressive command on the part of someone who was there, perhaps the patient himself, for whom this may have been the first word he had ever heard.[^2]

(Nothing on διανοίχθητι)

The actual healing takes place at the moment of Jesus’s authoritative word, which Mark reports as “ephphatha,” an Aram.[^3] word that means (on ὅ ἐστιν, see 3:17) “be opened” (διανοίχθητι, 2nd sg.[^4] aor.[^5] pass.[^6] impv.[^7] of διανοίγω, “open”; pronouncement impv.[^8] \[see 1:41\]; on the pass.[^9], see 10:40). Aram.[^10] words or phrases appear at various places within Mark’s Gospel: “Boanerges” in 3:17, “talitha koum” in 5:41, “ephphatha” in 7:34, “hosanna” in 11:9–10, “abba” in 14:36, “Golgotha” in 15:22, and “Eloi, Eloi lemá sabachtháni” in 15:34. Each time, they seem to depict an event, statement, or place as particularly memorable. Mark translates all these Aram.[^11] words or phrases into Gk.[^12] for the benefit of his audience, with the exception of “hosanna.” Although διανοίχθητι is impv.[^13] in form, it does not give a command to be obeyed but rather makes a pronouncement. The pass.[^14] impv.[^15] pronounces what should happen and by so doing causes the action to take place at that moment (Wallace[^16] 440, 492–93; cf.[^17] 1:41; 11:23).[^18]

France mentions Is 35:5 in his comments on Mk 7:35 -

Jesus’ journey outside Galilee now leads him apparently (see on v. 31 for the obscure geography) back to the Decapolis, the largely non-Jewish region to the east of the lake of Galilee, which was also the scene of the spectacular exorcism in 5:1–20. This time, however, it is a physical abnormality that is cured. The incident, recorded only by Mark, is notable not only for its non-Jewish location, but also for the use of saliva for healing (cf. also 8:23), for the recording of Jesus’ Aramaic word of healing (cf. the formula remembered in 5:41), and for the addition of a further complaint (deaf and dumb) to the list of those he has already healed. In the light of the vision of Is. 35:5–6, ‘Then the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped … and the tongue of the speechless sing for joy’, such a healing is loaded with eschatological significance. The actual method of healing is described in more detail than usual, and the crowd reaction in v. 37 suggests that this was a particularly impressive form of healing. In 9:17, 25 another case of deafness and dumbness (along with other more spectacular symptoms) is attributed to demon possession, but here there is no hint of the language of exorcism; the healing is effected by touch (which is not mentioned in any of Jesus’ exorcisms, except after the exorcism has been completed, 9:27) and by a word of command addressed not to a demon but to the patient himself.<sup>46</sup>[^19][^20]

## <span id="anchor"></span>Question 2: The strong man bound (Mark 3:27)

**The claim being tested.** Mark 3:27: “no one can enter the strong man’s house and plunder his goods unless he first binds the strong man”. Two Isaiah texts have been proposed:

- **Isa 49:24–25:** prey taken from the mighty man;
- **Isa 53:12:** the Servant “will divide the spoils of the strong”.

Claim audit 2 rated both **uncertain**, for three reasons:

- Mark shares no phrase with either text;
- Isaiah has no binding;
- Mark’s “goods” (σκεύη) is not Isa 53:12’s “spoils” (σκῦλα). Luke 11:22 has “spoils” and so is closer to Isa 53:12.

The binding itself is found in Tob 8:3, where Raphael binds the demon, and 1 Enoch 10:4 (“Bind Azazel”).

2a. Scope: Watts.

- **Ask:** How does Watts connect Jesus’ exorcisms, and the saying about binding the strong man in Mark 3:27, with Isaiah 49:24–25? What wording does he point to, and does he discuss Isaiah 53:12 or the binding traditions of Tobit and 1 Enoch?

- **Why:** Round 1 found that Watts ties the exorcisms to Isa 49, and this is the first of his three stages. It is the weakest stage on the audit’s evidence, so his own argument for it matters most.

- Watch for:

  - an argument from the *theme* of rescue from the mighty, offered as if it were shared *wording*;
  - “the stronger one” (1:7) and “the strong man” (3:27) used as evidence for Isaiah. ἰσχυρός (“strong”) is Mark’s own word in both places.

**\> How does Watts connect Jesus’ exorcisms, and the saying about binding the strong man in Mark 3:27, with Isaiah 49:24–25?**

Watts identifies [Mark 3:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.3.27)’s saying about the strong man as Jesus’ commentary on the significance of his exorcisms, understood through the lens of [Isaiah 49:24–25](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.49.24-23.49.25)’s depiction of Yahweh as a warrior.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1031751) The connection operates through a reinterpretation of captivity and liberation: whereas Isaiah’s captives were enslaved to Babylon and its idols, in Mark the oppressors are unclean spirits and demons—the powers understood to operate behind those idols.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1031950) From this perspective, Jesus’ exorcisms become the Markan equivalent of Yahweh’s promised release of the Isaian exile.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1032158)

Watts strengthens this connection by situating it within broader eschatological expectations. The imagery of binding a strong figure and plundering his possessions appears in contemporary Jewish traditions as associated with God’s eschatological deliverance of his people, where captives are portrayed as under Satan’s power and their deliverance is effected by a warrior figure, all described in terms of God’s reign and reminiscent of the Isaianic day of salvation.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F623453) This context suggests that Jesus’ characterization of his exorcisms reflects the influence of [Isaiah 49:24–25](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.49.24-23.49.25), and possibly the spoils-of-victory imagery of [Isaiah 53:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53.12).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F624754)

Notably, Watts observes that [Mark 3:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.3.27) exhibits a similar innovative hermeneutic to [Mark 10:45](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.10.45), with both statements occurring at climactic moments in their respective sections and both being crucial to interpreting central features of those sections—Jesus’ exorcisms and his passion.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F863217) The parallel demonstrates that Watts views Jesus’ exorcistic activity as fundamentally redemptive: just as the suffering servant accomplishes Israel’s salvation through his passion, the exorcisms accomplish liberation from demonic oppression through the power of one greater than Satan.

Watts emphasizes that while there is no direct verbal correspondence between [Mark 3:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.3.27) and the LXX [Isaiah 49:24f](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.49.24$2B)., “the similarity in meaning is so great that there is little doubt that Jesus had this passage in mind when he spoke these words; nor is there any comparable picture elsewhere in the Old Testament.”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F863964) The key wording Watts highlights is the “stronger man” (ὁ ἰσχυρότερος) saying itself—the claim that Satan is bound and this binding is Yahweh’s promised deliverance[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F627581).

However, Watts expresses caution because [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53) implies this plundering follows the servant’s suffering, whereas Mark makes no mention of Jesus’ suffering in this context; yet an appeal to [Isaiah 53:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53.12) could signify that the exorcisms foreshadow a greater victory to come—especially as Mark writes after the resurrection—and while such an allusion cannot be established with certainty, it would be congenial to the passage’s concerns[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F630711).

Watts focuses instead on the Isaianic warrior imagery and the broader eschatological framework of the New Exodus, without reference to those intertestamental texts. He observes that “Satan,” “Spirit,” and “strong man” terminology occur together only in [Mark 3:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.3.27) and the prologue, suggesting they are intended to be linked, and seeing 3:27 as an allusion to Yahweh’s warrior activity clarifies this connection, particularly if the descent of the Spirit on Jesus as “the stronger man” responds to [Isaiah 63:7–64:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.63.7-23.64.12)[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F628099).

- **Ask:** What background do commentators give for binding the strong man and plundering his house in Mark 3:27? Do they cite Isaiah 49:24–25, Isaiah 53:12, Tobit 8:3, 1 Enoch 10:4, or the Testament of Levi 18:12? Which do they think most likely?

**\> What background do commentators give for binding the strong man and plundering his house in Mark 3:27?**

Commentators identify the “strong man” passage as Jesus’s response to accusations that he exorcised demons through demonic power. The religious authorities never disputed Jesus’s ability to cast out demons—exorcism was common in that era—but claimed his power derived from an alliance with Satan, the chief demon, who allegedly enabled him to expel lesser demons[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NDSB62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANDSB62MK%2F2011-06-30T23%3A15%3A46Z%2F177522).

Jesus countered by asking how Satan could logically cast out Satan, since that would involve undoing his own work[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM61MT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM61MT%2F2009-06-10T20%3A49%3A37Z%2F1304329). Internal division weakens any kingdom and makes it vulnerable to collapse, a strategy Satan would never pursue[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM61MT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM61MT%2F2009-06-10T20%3A49%3A37Z%2F1304611). The binding imagery then shifts the argument’s framework entirely. The “strong man” represents Satan, and Jesus maintained that his miracles demonstrated that someone stronger than Satan had invaded Satan’s domain and was seizing his power and possessions[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM61MT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM61MT%2F2009-06-10T20%3A49%3A37Z%2F1305513).

Jesus implies he is the one stronger than the strong man—Satan himself—declaring that no one can plunder a strong man’s house without first binding him[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:14.0.22?pos=res%2FLLS%3A14.0.22%2F2021-07-07T22%3A00%3A31Z%2F1230001). Rather than showing Jesus was allied with Satan, the demons’ defeat proved Satan’s defenses had been breached and that a stronger power had arrived to begin Satan’s conquest[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NDSB62MK?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANDSB62MK%2F2011-06-30T23%3A15%3A46Z%2F178687). As the stronger one plundering Satan’s kingdom, Jesus begins liberating Israel by casting out demons, healing, and reclaiming the lost—activities manifesting God’s powerful presence[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:14.0.22?pos=res%2FLLS%3A14.0.22%2F2021-07-07T22%3A00%3A31Z%2F1230792).

The passage thus frames Jesus’s exorcisms not as demonic collaboration but as evidence of cosmic conflict, with his miraculous authority demonstrating his superiority over Satan and validating his messianic identity.

**\> Do any connect σχίζω (“tear”) in 1:10 with the tearing of the temple curtain in 15:38?**

Yes, commentators explicitly connect these two events through the verb σχίζω. Hooker and Hurtado note that the verb schizō (“to split, divide, separate, tear apart”) points to 1:10, the only other place where Mark uses the verb: as the heavens are split at Jesus’ baptism, revealing the unique, direct access that Jesus has to the Father as his Son, so now Jesus’ followers have direct access to the Father on account of Jesus’ death[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TNTC62MKSCHNABEL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATNTC62MKSCHNABEL%2F2017-07-21T01%3A13%3A14Z%2F1031510).

The verb σχίζω appears only twice in Mark, both times for acts of God—for the tearing of the heavens at Jesus’s baptism (1:10) and for the tearing of the temple’s curtain at Jesus’s death (15:38)[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781535905985?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781535905985%2F2021-07-08T18%3A14%3A16Z%2F900357). This deliberate repetition of the same rare verb signals Mark’s intentional theological connection between the two moments.

Bayer explicitly notes that Mark likely speaks of the inner “curtain” of the temple being “torn” (cf. [Mark 1:10](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.10))[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ESVEXPCOMMTLU?pos=res%2FLLS%3AESVEXPCOMMTLU%2F2021-09-07T17%3A56%3A17Z%2F2059627), making the verbal connection unmistakable. The theological significance is substantial: at Jesus’ baptism, the heavens tear open to reveal his unique sonship and access to the Father; at his death, the temple curtain tears to grant all believers that same access. It is possible that Mark intends both the “judgmental” and the “revelatory” interpretation[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TNTC62MKSCHNABEL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATNTC62MKSCHNABEL%2F2017-07-21T01%3A13%3A14Z%2F1031890)—the curtain’s tearing simultaneously signals God’s judgment on the temple system and the opening of a new way to God through Christ’s sacrifice.

203 Why does Mark, or whoever redacted the “miracle catenae” (see Fowler, Loaves, 24–31, for

a critique of Achtemeier), follow up the storm-stilling and deliverance of the demoniac with
the intercalated accounts of the raising of the dead girl and the healing of the flow of blood
(on the unity of 4:1–5:43, Tolbert, Sowing, 148f)? It has already been proposed that Mk
4:35–5:20 could be a reflection on Isa 63:7–65:7 where Jesus, already identified with Yahweh’s
intervention by the prologue’s allusion to the same passage (63:19; Mk 1:10), is presented as
the Divine-Warrior. In addition, in fn. 180 I suggested that the healing of the flow of blood
might be symbolic or ‘parabolic’ of Israel’s cleansing—Mark appears to use miracles
(particularly Isaianic ones) in this way, see fn. 132. Interestingly, this unusual imagery is found
in Isaiah 63:7–64:11 MT where it graphically describes the hopelessness of Israel’s a!empts at
righteousness (64:5). More striking, however, is Yahweh’s reply (Isa 65:1–25) which speaks of
the longed-for, new-creational restoration as a time when weeping will cease, ‘the infant will
not die’, and all will live out their allo!ed days (65:19f; cf. Jub. 23:27–30). In Mark, Jesus
queries the need for weeping (5:38f) and reverses the untimely death of a young girl by
restoring her to life (5:41f), and in each of the four miracles the response is one of fear or awe
(4:41; 5:15, 33, 42; cf. Isa 64:2 MT). Could it be that this whole section, 4:35–5:43, is redacted
around the final lament of the people in the book of Isaiah, as they await the NE, and Yahweh’s
response? If so, then in 4:35–5:20 Jesus is presented as the Divine-Warrior who commands the
sea and delivers the oppressed, even those who dwell among the tombs and who do not seek
him, while in 5:21–43 he symbolically demonstrates that he is the one who heals Israel of her
uncleanness and, by restoring the child to life, signals the inauguration of the promises of
Yahweh’s new creational restoration of Israel. In other words, these miracles bear all the
hallmarks of indicating that, in response to the lament and promise which concludes the book
of Isaiah, Yahweh has indeed ‘split the heavens’ (Mk 1:10; Isa 63:19), sent his Holy Spirit
among his people (Mk 1:10; 3:22–30; cf. Isa 63:10–14), and come down in Jesus, as the mighty
Warrior (Mk 3:27), to inaugurate the NE.
