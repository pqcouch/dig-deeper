## The Passage

**NA28** (sigla removed; the brackets are NA28's own):

> **1** Ἀρχὴ τοῦ εὐαγγελίου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ [υἱοῦ θεοῦ].
> **2** Καθὼς γέγραπται ἐν τῷ Ἠσαΐᾳ τῷ προφήτῃ· ἰδοὺ ἀποστέλλω τὸν ἄγγελόν μου πρὸ προσώπου σου, ὃς κατασκευάσει τὴν ὁδόν σου· **3** φωνὴ βοῶντος ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ· ἑτοιμάσατε τὴν ὁδὸν κυρίου, εὐθείας ποιεῖτε τὰς τρίβους αὐτοῦ,
> **4** ἐγένετο Ἰωάννης [ὁ] βαπτίζων ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ καὶ κηρύσσων βάπτισμα μετανοίας εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν. **5** καὶ ἐξεπορεύετο πρὸς αὐτὸν πᾶσα ἡ Ἰουδαία χώρα καὶ οἱ Ἱεροσολυμῖται πάντες, καὶ ἐβαπτίζοντο ὑπ᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐν τῷ Ἰορδάνῃ ποταμῷ ἐξομολογούμενοι τὰς ἁμαρτίας αὐτῶν. **6** καὶ ἦν ὁ Ἰωάννης ἐνδεδυμένος τρίχας καμήλου καὶ ζώνην δερματίνην περὶ τὴν ὀσφὺν αὐτοῦ καὶ ἐσθίων ἀκρίδας καὶ μέλι ἄγριον.
> **7** Καὶ ἐκήρυσσεν λέγων· ἔρχεται ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου ὀπίσω μου, οὗ οὐκ εἰμὶ ἱκανὸς κύψας λῦσαι τὸν ἱμάντα τῶν ὑποδημάτων αὐτοῦ. **8** ἐγὼ ἐβάπτισα ὑμᾶς ὕδατι, αὐτὸς δὲ βαπτίσει ὑμᾶς ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ.
> **9** Καὶ ἐγένετο ἐν ἐκείναις ταῖς ἡμέραις ἦλθεν Ἰησοῦς ἀπὸ Ναζαρὲτ τῆς Γαλιλαίας καὶ ἐβαπτίσθη εἰς τὸν Ἰορδάνην ὑπὸ Ἰωάννου. **10** καὶ εὐθὺς ἀναβαίνων ἐκ τοῦ ὕδατος εἶδεν σχιζομένους τοὺς οὐρανοὺς καὶ τὸ πνεῦμα ὡς περιστερὰν καταβαῖνον εἰς αὐτόν· **11** καὶ φωνὴ ἐγένετο ἐκ τῶν οὐρανῶν· σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός, ἐν σοὶ εὐδόκησα.
> **12** Καὶ εὐθὺς τὸ πνεῦμα αὐτὸν ἐκβάλλει εἰς τὴν ἔρημον. **13** καὶ ἦν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ τεσσεράκοντα ἡμέρας πειραζόμενος ὑπὸ τοῦ σατανᾶ, καὶ ἦν μετὰ τῶν θηρίων, καὶ οἱ ἄγγελοι διηκόνουν αὐτῷ.

**Where the SBLGNT differs** in these verses:

- **1:1** omits υἱοῦ θεοῦ ("Son of God").
- **1:4** reads ὁ βαπτίζων ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ κηρύσσων, without καί ("and").
- **1:6** reads ἔσθων ("eating"), a variant form with no difference in sense.

## Headline Findings

1. **The only divine speech in the prologue is addressed to Jesus in the second person, twice. The first time it comes through Scripture and the second time from heaven.** At 1:2 God says, "I send my messenger πρὸ προσώπου σου ('before your face') … who will prepare τὴν ὁδόν σου ('your way')." At 1:11 the Voice says, "σὺ εἶ ('you are') my beloved Son; ἐν σοί ('in you') I am well pleased." σύ ("you") stands six times in thirteen verses, at 1:2 (twice), 1:8 (twice) and 1:11 (twice) `[T]`. The sources have been re-pointed to produce this. Malachi's "a way before *me*" (לְפָנָי, "before me") becomes "*your* way", and Isaiah's "the paths of *our God*" becomes "*his* paths" (αὐτοῦ, "his"). So the κύριος ("Lord") whose way is prepared is the "you" whom God addresses, and at 1:9 that turns out to be Jesus `[T]` for the wording, `[I]` for the identification (high). *Surfaced by:* Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Tool 10 (σύ), Tool 8, Original Languages. *Why it matters:* the book's first claim about Jesus is made by God, in God's own words, before any human says anything.

2. **The Isaiah chapter Mark names supplies more than the verse he quotes. Its herald of good news and its "Lord coming with strength" become Mark's heading and John's announcement.** Isa 40:3 is quoted (1:3). Six verses later the same oracle has ὁ εὐαγγελιζόμενος ("the one who brings good news", 40:9, twice). Then it has ἰδοὺ κύριος … μετὰ ἰσχύος ἔρχεται ("behold, the Lord comes with strength", 40:10 Swete), and then the Lord shepherding his flock (40:11) `[T]`, verified in Swete. Mark's heading is Ἀρχὴ τοῦ εὐαγγελίου ("the beginning of the good news", 1:1), and John's message is ἔρχεται ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου ("the one stronger than I is coming", 1:7). Two verses later ἦλθεν Ἰησοῦς ("Jesus came", 1:9) `[T]`. *Surfaced by:* Tool 11 Move 1, Tool 7, Tool 10 (ἔρχομαι, "to come", at 1:7 and 1:9). Confidence: moderate–high. The chapter context is `[I]`, but each wording is `[T]`. *Why it matters:* the "gospel" of 1:1 is Isaiah's good news that God himself is coming, not a generic label.

3. **The Voice's sentence is built from three texts. Mark has shaped it himself, and two of its words come through the Hebrew, not the Septuagint.**
   - **Σὺ εἶ ὁ υἱός μου ("You are my Son").** It follows neither the LXX word order of Ps 2:7, Υἱός μου εἶ σύ ("my Son are you"), nor the Hebrew, בְּנִי אַתָּה ("my son [are] you"). It is Mark's own Σὺ εἶ ("you are") formula, which returns at 3:11, 8:29, 14:61 and 15:2 `[T]`.
   - **ὁ ἀγαπητός ("the beloved").** This is the LXX's rendering of יָחִיד ("only one") for Isaac, at Gen 22:2, 12 and 16 `[T]`.
   - **ἐν σοὶ εὐδόκησα ("in you I am well pleased").** This matches the Hebrew of Isa 42:1, רָצְתָה נַפְשִׁי ("my soul delights"), not the LXX's προσεδέξατο ("accepted"). Matthew renders that same verse with both of Mark's words: ὁ ἀγαπητός μου εἰς ὃν εὐδόκησεν ἡ ψυχή μου ("my beloved, in whom my soul is well pleased", Matt 12:18 SBLGNT) `[T]`.
   
   *Surfaced by:* Tool 11, Tool 8 (ancient versions and triage), Original Languages. *Why it matters:* king, only son and Servant are named in one sentence. The Isa 42:1 link rises from the overview's *moderate* to **moderate–high** on the evidence of Matt 12:18.

4. **Messengers and wilderness frame the prologue, and the Spirit who will baptise others drives the Son out to be tested by Satan.**
   - **The frame.** ἄγγελος ("messenger, angel") stands at 1:2 and 1:13 and nowhere else in Mark before 8:38 `[T]`, verified. ἔρημος ("wilderness") stands four times in the thirteen verses (1:3, 4, 12, 13) `[T]`, verified.
   - **The Spirit's sequence.** πνεῦμα ("Spirit") occurs three times, in a sequence: promised (1:8), descending (1:10), driving out (1:12). The next πνεῦμα in the book is an unclean one (1:23) `[T]`, verified.
   - **The verb.** ἐκβάλλει ("drives out", 1:12) is the book's exorcism verb (1:34, 39; 3:15, 22–23 and following) `[T]`.
   
   *Surfaced by:* Tool 3, Tool 10, Tool 7, Tool 11 Move 4. *Why it matters:* the wilderness scene is a sending, not a retreat. Mark gives it no temptation dialogue at all, and 3:27 will explain what the Stronger One was doing in Satan's territory.

5. **The prologue tells the reader what no character in the book knows.**
   - "Χριστός" ("Christ", 1:1) is not said again until Peter says it at 8:29 `[T]`, verified.
   - God calls Jesus Son at 1:11 and 9:7. No human says it until the centurion at 15:39 `[T]`.
   - The citation formula at 1:2 is the only γέγραπται ("it is written") in Mark spoken by the narrator. All six others are in Jesus' mouth (7:6; 9:12, 13; 11:17; 14:21, 27) `[T]`.
   - The Spirit-baptism promised at 1:8 is never narrated in 1:1–16:8 `[T]`.
   
   *Surfaced by:* Tool 2 (Positional Necessity), Tool 6, Tool 11 Move 4. *Why it matters:* the reader listens to the whole of Galilee knowing what the disciples do not. The prologue creates Mark's irony, and the sermon should let it.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level (from the overview, and not re-derived).** Mark writes for a Greek-reading, largely Gentile audience under pressure `[I]`. His aim is to bring them to the confession the centurion makes, and to call them onto the way behind a Son who must be rejected and who gives his life as a ransom `[I]`.

**Passage level.** The prologue exists to settle, before the story starts, *who Jesus is* and *on what authority we are told so*.

- **Who.** He is the Lord whose way Scripture said would be prepared (1:2–3), the Stronger One whom the prophet announced (1:7), and the beloved Son on whom the Spirit comes (1:10–11). He is sent at once to face Satan (1:12–13) `[T]`/`[I]`.
- **On what authority.** Scripture (1:2–3), a prophet dressed as Elijah (1:6), and the Voice from the torn heavens (1:11) `[T]`.

The purpose is to *inform the reader in advance*. The next eight chapters are shaped by the gap between what the reader knows and what the characters work out (see Tool 2 and Headline Finding 5).

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Nothing precedes the passage in the book except the heading. What precedes it is the canon. The unit's first clause after the heading is καθὼς γέγραπται ("as it is written"), and that places the whole book downstream of what Isaiah (and, in fact, Exodus and Malachi) had already said `[T]`. The canon has left several things open:

- a messenger promised before the Lord comes to his temple (Mal 3:1);
- Elijah promised before the day of the LORD (Mal 3:23 Heb);
- a voice commanded to prepare a way in the wilderness for God himself (Isa 40:3);
- a prayer that God would tear the heavens and come down (Isa 63:19 Heb).

**Necessity answer.** The prologue sits here because it is the answer to those open promises, and because Mark's method needs the reader to hold that answer *before* the narrative begins. It is also placed before 1:14–15, the first words of Jesus, "the time is fulfilled". Only a reader who has heard 1:2–3 knows *which* time, and whose kingdom `[I]`, high.

**Implication.** The prologue is not a warm-up. It is the frame that makes the rest of the book ironic: the reader knows, and the characters do not. Preach it as the book's key, not as biography.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 shows that every source opened here returns at a structurally decisive point:

| Source | Returns at |
|---|---|
| Malachi | 9:11–13 (Elijah); 11:11–17 (the Lord comes to his temple) |
| Isaiah | 7:6 (named again); 11:17 |
| Gen 22's ἀγαπητός ("beloved") | 9:7; 12:6 |
| Ps 2 (Σὺ εἶ, "you are") | 9:7; 14:61 |
| The torn heavens | 15:38 |

The prologue sets up in advance the scriptural frames the later book needs `[T]` for the returns, `[I]` for the design.

#### General context

**After.** 1:14–15 begins Jesus' public ministry "after John was handed over", with the εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ θεοῦ ("the gospel of God"). εὐαγγέλιον ("gospel") at 1:14–15 picks up 1:1, and κηρύσσω ("to preach") passes from John (1:4, 7) to Jesus (1:14) `[T]`.

**Extent.** Whether the prologue ends at 1:13 or 1:15 is disputed `[S]`. The ἄγγελος ("messenger, angel") and ἔρημος ("wilderness") frames close at 1:13 (Tool 3), and 1:14 opens with a change of time and place ("after John was handed over … into Galilee"). So this dig keeps 1:1–13 as the unit, with 1:14–15 as the hinge `[I]`, moderate.

**Heading or sentence.** The heading is a real syntactic question. Both NA28 and the SBLGNT print a full stop after 1:1 and a comma after 1:3, so they take καθὼς γέγραπται … ("just as it is written …") as introducing ἐγένετο Ἰωάννης ("John appeared", 1:4) `[T]`. Read that way, the beginning of the gospel *is* John's appearing, just as Scripture said. The alternative takes 1:1 as the title of the whole book. Both are grammatical `[I]`, and the choice affects nothing else in the unit.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1 | Heading | "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ": verbless, and governing what follows |
| 1:2–3 | Scripture speaks | God addresses "you" through Scripture: a messenger, a voice, a way to be prepared for the Lord |
| 1:4–8 | The messenger | John in the wilderness; all Judaea and Jerusalem come; dressed as Elijah; announces the Stronger One and a Spirit-baptism |
| 1:9–11 | The Son | Jesus comes; the heavens are torn; the Spirit descends; God addresses "you" from heaven |
| 1:12–13 | The wilderness | The Spirit drives him out; forty days; Satan, the beasts, the angels serving |

**Device: a frame, with a chain of fulfilments.**

- **The frame.** Messenger at the start (τὸν ἄγγελόν μου, "my messenger", 1:2) and angels at the end (οἱ ἄγγελοι, "the angels", 1:13), with ἔρημος ("wilderness") in the first and last sections (1:3–4; 1:12–13) `[T]`. The prologue is a wilderness frame opened and closed by God's ἄγγελοι ("messengers, angels") `[I]`, moderate–high.
- **The chain of fulfilments.** Each section fulfils the one before it:
  - the "voice in the wilderness" (1:3) appears as John "in the wilderness" (1:4);
  - "one is coming" (1:7) is met by "Jesus came" (1:9);
  - "he will baptise with the Holy Spirit" (1:8) is met by the Spirit coming *into* him (1:10), not yet *from* him;
  - the Son declared (1:11) is at once driven out (1:12).
  
  `[T]` for each link; `[I]` for the chain as design.

**The centre.** The scene at 1:9–11 is the only one in which heaven acts, and the Voice's sentence is the unit's only direct divine speech from outside Scripture. It is the prologue's goal, not its middle panel `[I]`.

### 4. Linking Words

- **καθώς ("just as", 1:2).** Ties the heading, or 1:4 on the NA28 punctuation, to Scripture. "The beginning of the gospel" happens *as it is written* `[T]`.
- **ὃς ("who", 1:2).** The messenger "who will prepare your way": the relative clause makes preparation the messenger's whole job.
- **καί ("and").** Parataxis runs throughout, as Mark's narrative "and". It is almost never logical, so it should not be preached as "and so" `[T]`.
- **δέ ("but", 1:8).** ἐγὼ … ὕδατι, αὐτὸς δέ … ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ ("I … with water, but he … with the Holy Spirit"). This is the unit's one adversative, and it sets John and the Stronger One apart `[T]`.
- **εὐθύς ("immediately", 1:10, 12).** It links the Voice to the driving out without a pause. See Tool 10 for the wordplay with 1:3.
- **Καὶ ἐγένετο ("and it came about", 1:9).** A biblical narrative transition, as in the LXX `[I]`. It marks the arrival of the one announced.

### 5. Parallels

- **Isa 40:3 as quoted (1:3).** "Prepare the way of the Lord ‖ make his paths straight." This is synonymous parallelism: the second line interprets the first, so the "way" is a straightened road for someone coming `[T]`.
- **John's antithesis (1:8).** "I baptised you with water ‖ he will baptise you with the Holy Spirit." The contrast lies in agent and element together `[T]`.
- **The Hebrew parallelism Mark does not keep.** In the Hebrew of Isa 40:3, בַּמִּדְבָּר ("in the wilderness") is paired with בָּעֲרָבָה ("in the desert") in the next line. So the Hebrew means "*In the wilderness* prepare the way … make straight *in the desert* a highway" `[T]`. The Masoretic accents agree, as Leningrad prints them in the WLC, where קוֹרֵא ("calls") takes a disjunctive `[T]`. This is a reading tradition, not authorial. See Tool 8 and the triage.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

The whole prologue is narrator's voice, and it carries the book's most authoritative framing.

- **1:1.** A heading no character speaks.
- **1:2–3.** The only citation formula in Mark spoken by the narrator. γέγραπται ("it is written") occurs seven times in Mark, and the other six are all Jesus speaking (7:6; 9:12, 13; 11:17; 14:21, 27) `[T]`, verified. The narrator quotes Scripture once, at the start, and then hands Scripture to Jesus.
- **1:6.** John's clothes and food are a narrator's aside that *shows* rather than tells who John is (Tool 11: Elijah).
- **1:13.** A summary with no dialogue: "and he was with the wild beasts". The narrator declines to give the content of the testing `[T]`.

### 7. Vocabulary

**Proper-noun inventory:** Ἰησοῦς ("Jesus"), Χριστός ("Christ"), Ἠσαΐας ("Isaiah"), Ἰωάννης ("John"), Ἰουδαία ("Judaea"), Ἱεροσολυμῖται ("the people of Jerusalem"), Ἰορδάνης ("Jordan", twice), Ναζαρέτ ("Nazareth"), Γαλιλαία ("Galilee"), σατανᾶς ("Satan"), and the number τεσσεράκοντα ("forty"). Triage:

- **Judaea, Jerusalem, Galilee:** routine scenery here. But Jerusalem's people come out to John now (1:5), while its scribes come down to oppose Jesus later (3:22; 7:1) `[T]`, and Galilee returns at 16:7.
- **Nazareth:** no Old Testament history. It returns at 1:24, 10:47, 14:67 and 16:6 as Ναζαρηνός ("Nazarene") (overview).
- **Jordan, Isaiah, Satan, forty and Ἰησοῦς:** candidates, and checked below.

**εὐαγγέλιον ("good news, gospel", 1:1).** A big Bible word. In Mark it occurs at 1:1, 14, 15; 8:35; 10:29; 13:10 and 14:9 `[T]`, verified. Its background here is the Isaiah chapter Mark names: ὁ εὐαγγελιζόμενος ("the one who brings good news", Isa 40:9 Swete, twice), whose message is "Behold your God" `[T]`. *High confidence* that the Isaianic sense is in view, given the formula in 1:2.

**βάπτισμα μετανοίας εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν ("a baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins", 1:4).**
- μετάνοια ("repentance") occurs only here in Mark, and the verb μετανοέω ("to repent") at 1:15 and 6:12 `[T]`.
- ἄφεσις ("forgiveness") occurs only at 1:4 and 3:29. ἁμαρτία ("sin") occurs only at 1:4, 1:5 and 2:5–10 `[T]`, verified.
- **What this shows.** The book names sins and forgiveness at its opening, and then speaks of how sins are dealt with only as ransom (10:45) and covenant blood (14:24). This is a sweep finding, and it is re-verified here.
- **A forward link.** βάπτισμα ("baptism") returns at 10:38–39, where it names Jesus' own death: "the baptism with which I am baptised" `[T]`. *Moderate–high* that 1:4 and 10:38 are meant to be heard together.

**ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου ("the one stronger than I", 1:7).** ἰσχυρός ("strong") occurs only at 1:7 and 3:27, where the strong man must be bound `[T]`, verified. The comparative has a partner in Isa 40:10 Swete, κύριος μετὰ ἰσχύος ἔρχεται ("the Lord comes with strength"), in the chapter just quoted *(moderate–high)*.

**πνεῦμα ἅγιον ("Holy Spirit", 1:8).** In canonical Swete the phrase "Holy Spirit" occurs only at Ps 50:13 and Isa 63:10–11, according to the claim audit `[S: audit]`. So the prophet's promise draws on the prayer of Isa 63, whose 63:19 lies behind 1:10 (Tool 11) *(moderate; context)*.

**ὁ ἀγαπητός ("the beloved", 1:11).** In Mark it occurs only at 1:11, 9:7 and 12:6 `[T]`, verified. In the LXX it renders יָחִיד ("only one") for Isaac at Gen 22:2, 12 and 16 (Swete, verified). "Beloved" therefore means "only". *High confidence* on the LXX usage; *moderate–high* that Gen 22 is heard.

**εὐδοκέω ("to be well pleased", 1:11).** It occurs only here in Mark, and it is absent from the Voice at 9:7 `[T]`, verified. See Tool 11 (Isa 42:1).

**ἐκβάλλω ("to drive out, cast out", 1:12).** The exorcism verb. The Spirit is its first subject, and its later objects are demons, the temple traders (11:15) and the beloved son of the parable: ἐξέβαλον αὐτὸν ἔξω τοῦ ἀμπελῶνος ("they cast him out of the vineyard", 12:8) `[T]`. *High* on the usage; the link to 12:8 is `[I]`, moderate.

**πειράζω and σατανᾶς ("to test"; "Satan", 1:13).**
- πειράζω occurs at 1:13; 8:11; 10:2 and 12:15 `[T]`, verified. After the wilderness, every "testing" of Jesus is done by Pharisees or their allies.
- σατανᾶς occurs at 1:13; 3:23, 26; 4:15 and 8:33 `[T]`, verified. The next time Jesus says the name directly to someone, it is to Peter: Ὕπαγε ὀπίσω μου, Σατανᾶ ("Get behind me, Satan", 8:33).

**θηρίον ("wild beast", 1:13).** It occurs only here in Mark `[T]`, verified. For its possible backgrounds, see Tool 11 and Difficult Verses.

**Name check, Ἰησοῦς ("Jesus / Joshua").** In the Greek Old Testament, Ἰησοῦς is Joshua, who led Israel across the Jordan (Josh 3:1 Swete, Ἰησοῦς … ἕως τοῦ Ἰορδάνου, "Joshua … to the Jordan") `[T]`. Here Ἰησοῦς comes to the Jordan (1:9). Heb 4:8 shows the NT can exploit the identity of the name `[T]`. But Mark gives no signal *(uncertain; do not preach it as the point)*.

**Name check, Ἰορδάνης ("Jordan").** Its first canonical occurrence is Gen 13:10–11 (Lot's choice). Its load-bearing events are three `[T]`, all verified in Swete:
- Israel crossing under Joshua (Josh 3–4);
- Elijah crossing before he is taken up, with the spirit of Elijah resting on Elisha (2 Kgs 2:6–15);
- Naaman, who ἐβαπτίσατο ἐν τῷ Ἰορδάνῃ ("dipped himself in the Jordan", 2 Kgs 5:14), the only LXX verse joining βαπτίζω ("to baptise, dip") to the Jordan.

The Elijah succession is the likeliest to be active, because 1:6 has just dressed John as Elijah. But Mark gives no verbal signal *(uncertain)*.

**Number check, τεσσεράκοντα ("forty").** See Tool 11: Deut 8:2; Exod 34:28; 1 Kgs 19:8.

### 8. Translations

**1:1: "the Son of God".** Both English versions print it. NA28 brackets it and the SBLGNT omits it. See Textual Variants.

**1:2: πρὸ προσώπου σου ("before your face").**
- NASB95 "ahead of You"; ESV "before your face".
- The ESV keeps the idiom of Exod 23:20 and Mal 3:1. The NASB95 loses it, but its capital "You" signals that the addressee is divine or messianic.
- **KJV:** "As it is written in the prophets" (from recall; not in the corpus). It follows the Majority reading τοῖς προφήταις ("the prophets"). See Textual Variants.

**1:4: "John the Baptist appeared … preaching" (NASB95) against "John appeared, baptizing … and proclaiming" (ESV).** The difference is not one of translation philosophy. The two versions render different Greek texts:
- The NASB95 follows the reading of B 33 (and the SBLGNT), ὁ βαπτίζων … κηρύσσων ("the Baptiser … preaching"), which makes "Baptiser" a title.
- The ESV follows the reading without the article, βαπτίζων … καὶ κηρύσσων ("baptising … and preaching"), found in A W and the Majority text, which makes baptising an activity.
- NA28 prints [ὁ] βαπτίζων … καὶ κηρύσσων ("[the] Baptiser … and preaching") `[T]`, verified in the apparatus.

**1:10: σχιζομένους ("being torn").**
- NASB95 "opening"; ESV "being torn open".
- The ESV keeps the verb that recurs at 15:38 (ἐσχίσθη, "was torn"). The NASB95's "opening" loses the frame and sounds like the LXX's ἀνοίγω ("to open") of Isa 64:1, which Mark does *not* use `[T]`.

**1:11: ἐκ τῶν οὐρανῶν ("from the heavens").**
- NASB95 "out of the heavens"; ESV "from heaven".
- The Greek plural is the same as in 1:10, and the NASB95 keeps it. The ESV's singular loosens the link between the heavens that are torn and the heavens that speak `[T]`.

**1:12: ἐκβάλλει ("drives out").**
- NASB95 "impelled Him to go out"; ESV "drove him out".
- The ESV is closer. Neither lets the listener hear the "cast out" of 1:34 and 1:39.

**1:13.** NASB95 "wild beasts"; ESV "wild animals". θηρίον ("wild beast") carries a note of danger that "animals" softens *(moderate)*.

**1:7 and 1:17: ὀπίσω μου ("behind me").**
- Both versions: 1:7 "after me", 1:17 "Follow me", 8:33 "Get behind me", 8:34 "come after me".
- The Greek phrase is the same each time `[T]`, verified. The English hides that the Stronger One who comes "behind" John calls disciples to come "behind" him.

**Ancient versions check.** The modern versions agree on "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (1:3). Mark's Greek attaches ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ("in the wilderness") to the voice, as the LXX does. The Hebrew of Isa 40:3 attaches it to "prepare" (Tool 5).

- **Triage: category 3, the NT's own text.** Mark reads Isaiah in its Greek phrasing, and the phrasing is load-bearing: the voice is *in the wilderness*, and John appears ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ("in the wilderness", 1:4) `[T]`.
- The Hebrew is not a correction of Mark. The Hebrew sense (the way itself is prepared in the wilderness) is also fulfilled, since the Son goes into the wilderness at 1:12 `[I]`.
- This is **not** a `Translation-tradition split`.
- The Vulgate and Peshitta readings were not checked, and none of the divergences below depends on them.

The three other Hebrew–Greek divergences in the unit are set out under Tool 11, each assigned to category 3.

**Pulpit divergence note (ESV, 2025 US).** The congregation will hear:

- "before your face" (1:2), which is better than the NASB95;
- "the heavens being torn open" (1:10), which is better, and should be tied aloud to 15:38;
- "drove him out" (1:12), which is better.

Three things they will *not* hear:

- **1:4.** That "John appeared, baptizing … and proclaiming" rests on a different Greek reading from the study text's "John the Baptist appeared" (Textual Variants). This is harmless, but worth knowing if someone compares Bibles.
- **1:11.** The link between the torn "heavens" and the Voice "from heaven". The ESV's singular hides the repeated plural.
- **1:7 and 1:17.** That "after me" and "Follow me" are the same Greek phrase.

### 9. Tone and Feel

The register is **portentous and brisk**. It is built from a heading without a verb, a thick opening of Scripture (three texts in two verses), and then short paratactic clauses linked by καί ("and") and εὐθύς ("immediately") `[T]`.

The prologue slows down only for two details:

- John's clothing and food (1:6). This detail is identification, not colour: see Tool 11.
- The Voice (1:11). This is the unit's only direct divine speech from heaven.

Everything else is compressed, most of all the wilderness: forty days in one verse, with no dialogue and no outcome `[T]`. *Soundtrack:* a single trumpet (the herald), a sudden fanfare from above (1:10–11), and then silence and distance, with the wild beasts at the edge of the frame. The preacher should keep the urgency and not sentimentalise the baptism, because the text moves straight on to conflict.

### 10. Repetition

All counts are from the SBLGNT index, for 1:1–13 unless stated otherwise.

- **σύ ("you"), six times** (1:2 ×2; 1:8 ×2; 1:11 ×2). In 1:2 and 1:11 it is God's address to the coming one `[T]`. See Headline Finding 1.
- **βαπτίζω ("to baptise"), five times** (1:4, 5, 8 ×2, 9), with βάπτισμα ("baptism") at 1:4 `[T]`. John's work, contrasted with the Stronger One's (1:8), is completed in Jesus' baptism (1:9).
- **ἔρημος ("wilderness"), four times** (1:3, 4, 12, 13). This is the setting word of the frame.
- **πνεῦμα ("Spirit"), three times** (1:8, 10, 12): promised, descending, driving out.
- **Ἰωάννης ("John"), three times** (1:4, 6, 9).
- **εὐθύς, three times** (1:3, 10, 12). This is one lemma in the index, though at 1:3 it is the adjective, εὐθείας ("straight"), and at 1:10 and 1:12 the adverb ("immediately") `[T]`, verified. Isaiah says to make his paths εὐθείας ("straight"), and then everything happens εὐθύς ("straightaway"). Mark uses the adverb 41 times in 1:1–16:8 (overview), so the effect may be accidental. As deliberate wordplay it is *uncertain*, but it is *moderate* as something a Greek listener would hear.
- **ἄγγελος ("messenger, angel"), twice** (1:2, 13). It frames the unit, as noted under Tool 3.
- **ὁδός ("way"), twice** (1:2, 3), opening the book's "way" thread, which runs through sixteen verses (overview, verified).
- **Other pairs:** φωνή ("voice"), twice (1:3, 11), the prophet's voice and God's voice. οὐρανός ("heaven"), twice (1:10, 11). Ἰορδάνης ("Jordan"), twice (1:5, 9). ἔρχομαι ("to come"), twice (1:7, 9). ὕδωρ ("water"), twice (1:8, 10). ἁμαρτία ("sin"), twice (1:4, 5). κηρύσσω ("to preach"), twice (1:4, 7).

**Idea-level repetition.** The prologue repeats *preparation* and *arrival*: the way prepared and the Lord coming; the herald and the Stronger One; water and Spirit; the Spirit promised and the Spirit given. Each preparation in the prologue is met by an arrival within a few verses `[I]`.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**The book's live sources (from Phase 0.5):** Isaiah; the Psalms (2, 22, 110, 118); Daniel; Zechariah; Malachi and the Elijah texts; Exodus; Deuteronomy (strengthened in v0.2). The prologue is where most of them first appear.

**Exod 23:20 + Mal 3:1 → 1:2** *(high: formula quotation)*

*Source context.*

- **Exod 23:20** comes at the close of the Book of the Covenant. YHWH promises: "Behold, I send an angel before you, to guard you on the way and to bring you into the place I have prepared" (Swete: ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω τὸν ἄγγελόν μου πρὸ προσώπου σου ἵνα φυλάξῃ σε ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ, "behold, I send my angel before your face, to guard you on the way"). A warning follows: obey his voice, for "my name is in him" (23:21) `[T]`. The addressee is Israel on its way out of Egypt.
- **Mal 3:1** answers a people who ask "Where is the God of justice?" (2:17). In the Hebrew, הִנְנִי שֹׁלֵחַ מַלְאָכִי וּפִנָּה־דֶרֶךְ לְפָנָי ("Behold, I send my messenger, and he will clear a way before me"). Then "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple", a coming that refines and judges (3:2) `[T]`.
- **Mark's line.** The first clause is Exod 23:20 LXX, without its ἐγώ ("I"). NA28 prints the text without ἐγώ, with B, D and Θ; the Majority text adds it. The second clause, ὃς κατασκευάσει τὴν ὁδόν σου ("who will prepare your way"), is Malachi's. But its verb stands nearer the Hebrew פִּנָּה ("he will clear") than Swete's ἐπιβλέψεται ὁδόν ("will look upon a way") `[T]`.
- **Triage: category 3.** The NT's own form of the text, reached through the Hebrew.

*Book usage.* This is the first use. Malachi returns in the Elijah conversation (9:11–13) and in the Lord's coming to his temple (11:11–17) `[T]`. Exodus returns at Sinai (9:2–7; the covenant blood at 14:24) `[T]`.

*OT-to-OT.* In the Hebrew, Mal 3:1 reuses Exod 23:20: "Behold, I send (שׁלח, 'to send') my messenger (מַלְאָךְ, 'messenger')". Mal 3:1 also shares Isa 40:3's "clear the way" (פָּנָה, "to clear" + דֶּרֶךְ, "way"). All four lemmas were verified in the WLC with `find.py`, and a negative control failed as it should `[T]`. So the three texts Mark joins were already joined in the Hebrew prophets. He did not create the conflation; he followed it `[I]`, moderate–high.

*What it adds.* An exodus guide *and* a temple visitation. The pronouns carry the Christology. In Exodus the "you" is Israel; in Malachi the way is "before me", YHWH. Mark's "your way" makes the one coming the one addressed, and the addressee is identified at 1:11 `[T]`/`[I]`, high.

**Isa 40:3 → 1:3** *(high: formula quotation)*

*Source context.* Isa 40:1–11 opens the comfort: Jerusalem's hard service is ended. A voice calls for YHWH's way to be prepared (40:3), and "the glory of the LORD shall be revealed" (40:5). "The word of our God stands for ever" (40:8). A herald of good news, ὁ εὐαγγελιζόμενος ("the one who brings good news", Swete, twice), is told to lift up his voice and say "Behold your God" (40:9). "Behold, the Lord comes with strength" (μετὰ ἰσχύος ἔρχεται, 40:10). He will shepherd his flock (40:11) `[T]`, verified in Swete.

- **The verse's function.** It is the first command in the comfort: the road for God's return.
- **Mark's text.** It follows Swete verbatim until the last words, where it has αὐτοῦ ("his") for τοῦ θεοῦ ἡμῶν ("of our God") `[T]`.
- **Triage: category 3.** Mark reads the Greek syntax, "a voice *in the wilderness*". In the Hebrew parallelism, "in the wilderness" goes with "prepare" (Tools 5 and 8).

*Book usage.* This is the first use of Isaiah, and Isaiah is named at 1:2 and 7:6 only `[T]`. The same chapter supplies the words Mark uses next:

- "good news" (40:9) behind 1:1;
- "the Lord comes with strength" (40:10) behind 1:7;
- the shepherd (40:11), which returns in the ποιμήν ("shepherd") of 6:34 and 14:27.

The links between chapter and book are `[I]`, moderate–high. The wording is `[T]` in each.

*OT-to-OT.* Mal 3:1 is itself a reading of Isa 40:3 (above). Isaiah 40's coming God and Malachi's Lord coming to his temple belong to one prophetic conversation about YHWH's return `[I]`.

*What it adds.* The way prepared is **YHWH's own coming**. Mark's αὐτοῦ ("his") lets κύριος ("Lord") stand for the one who comes next, and his heading borrows Isaiah's word for the herald's message. The "gospel" is that God is coming in person `[I]`, high.

**Isa 63:19 (Heb; Eng 64:1) → 1:10** *(moderate: through the Hebrew; as the overview has it)*

*Source context.* The long prayer of Isa 63:7–64:11 remembers the exodus, the shepherd and the Holy Spirit (63:11). It laments that God's people are "as those over whom you never ruled" (63:19a), and it breaks out: לוּא־קָרַעְתָּ שָׁמַיִם יָרַדְתָּ ("O that you would tear the heavens and come down", 63:19b) `[T]`. Both קָרַע ("to tear") and יָרַד ("to come down") were verified at Isa 63:19. Swete (numbered 64:1) has ἐὰν ἀνοίξῃς τὸν οὐρανόν ("if you open heaven") `[T]`. No form of σχίζω ("to tear") is used of heaven anywhere in Swete (overview, verified).

- **Audit ruling.** The sweep widened this link to Isa 63:11–14. The claim audit rejected that wider match, because Rahlfs 63:11 reads "out of the land" and the Hebrew of 63:14 has cattle going down. This dig accepts the ruling `[S: audit]`. The one surviving contact is the rare "Holy Spirit" (63:10–11), which lies beside 1:8 (Tool 7).
- **Triage: category 3**, reached through the Hebrew.

*Book usage.* σχίζω ("to tear") returns only at 15:38 `[T]`, verified: the veil is torn at the death.

*OT-to-OT.* No pairing is attempted here: the audit found the Isa 63:11–14 connection too weak.

*What it adds.* A prayer for God to break in and come down is answered at the Jordan, and answered again, differently, at the cross `[I]`.

**Ps 2:7 + Gen 22:2 + Isa 42:1 → 1:11** *(Ps 2:7: moderate–high, a changed form; Gen 22:2: moderate–high; Isa 42:1: moderate–high, raised from moderate)*

*Source context.*

- **Ps 2.** The nations rage against YHWH and his anointed. YHWH installs his king on Zion and decrees: בְּנִי אַתָּה ("You are my son"; Swete Υἱός μου εἶ σύ, "My son are you"), "today I have begotten you". The nations are given him as inheritance. The psalm ends, "Kiss the son … blessed are all who take refuge in him" `[T]`.
- **Gen 22.** God commands Abraham to take "your son, your only one" (יְחִידְךָ, "your only one"; Swete τὸν ἀγαπητόν, "the beloved") and offer him (22:2). At 22:12 and 22:16 the angel twice commends Abraham for not withholding τοῦ υἱοῦ σου τοῦ ἀγαπητοῦ ("your beloved son") `[T]`, verified.
- **Isa 42:1–9.** The first Servant song. In the Hebrew: הֵן עַבְדִּי … בְּחִירִי רָצְתָה נַפְשִׁי נָתַתִּי רוּחִי עָלָיו ("Behold my servant … my chosen, in whom my soul delights; I have put my Spirit upon him"). He will bring justice to the nations `[T]`, verified. Swete makes the Servant "Jacob … Israel" and has προσεδέξατο ("accepted") for "delights".

*Book usage.*

- **Ps 2.** It returns in the Voice at 9:7 (οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἀγαπητός, "this is my beloved Son") and in the high priest's question (14:61) `[T]`.
- **ἀγαπητός ("beloved").** Only at 1:11, 9:7 and 12:6, where the vineyard owner sends his "one beloved son" last, and he is killed `[T]`, verified.
- **Isaiah's Servant.** It returns in "many" at 10:45 and 14:24 (Isa 53; overview, moderate).
- **Mark's formula.** The Σὺ εἶ ("you are") form is Mark's own: 1:11; 3:11; 8:29; 14:61; 15:2 (sweep, verified). So the Voice's wording sets the pattern that demons, Peter, the high priest and Pilate later repeat or question `[T]`.

*OT-to-OT.* The three texts are already in canonical conversation:

- **Ps 2 and Isa 42.** Both are about God's appointed one and the nations `[T]`.
- **Isa 42 and Mark 1:10.** Isa 42:1's "I have put my Spirit upon him" is the reason the Spirit's descent stands directly before the Voice.
- **The Majority text at 1:10.** It reads ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν ("upon him"), which is Isa 42:1's own preposition in Swete (ἐπ᾽ αὐτόν). NA28 reads εἰς αὐτόν ("into / to him"), with B, D and ƒ¹³ `[T]`, verified in the apparatus. Scribes heard Isaiah here.
- **Gen 22 and Ps 2.** Gen 22 adds a son to be *offered*. Ps 2 has a son to be *enthroned* `[I]`.

*Triage.* Isa 42:1 is **category 3**. Mark's εὐδόκησα ("I am well pleased") answers the Hebrew רָצְתָה ("delights"), not the LXX, and Matt 12:18 translates the same verse with ὁ ἀγαπητός μου εἰς ὃν εὐδόκησεν ("my beloved, in whom he was well pleased") `[T]`, verified in the SBLGNT. That independent NT rendering is why the confidence is raised above the overview's *moderate*. Ps 2:7's word order is **category 1 at most**: the change is Mark's own formula, not a variant.

*What it adds.* One sentence declares the enthroned king (Ps 2), the only son to be offered (Gen 22) and the Spirit-anointed Servant (Isa 42). In Mark's plot, the order of the book follows the order of the sentence: the Son is declared, he is the Servant, and he is offered. The cross is already in the word "beloved", and 12:6 makes it explicit `[I]`, high.

**2 Kgs 1:8 → 1:6** *(high: distinctive clothing, near-verbatim)*

*Source context.* Ahaziah's messengers describe the man who turned them back. In the Hebrew, אִישׁ בַּעַל שֵׂעָר וְאֵזוֹר עוֹר אָזוּר בְּמָתְנָיו ("a hairy man, with a belt of leather bound round his waist"). The king: "It is Elijah the Tishbite." In Swete, Ἀνὴρ δασὺς καὶ ζώνην δερματίνην περιεζωσμένος τὴν ὀσφὺν αὐτοῦ ("a hairy man, and girded with a leather belt about his waist") `[T]`, verified in both languages. The chapter is a confrontation with a king who consults other gods, and fire falls on the troops sent to seize the prophet.

*Book usage.* Elijah is not named until 6:15. He returns at 8:28 and 9:4–13, where Jesus says "Elijah has come, and they did to him whatever they wished", and at 15:35–36 `[T]`.

*OT-to-OT.* Mal 3:23 Heb (Swete 4:4) promises Elijah before the day of the LORD. Mal 3:1 has just been quoted. So Mark cites Malachi's promise and then dresses John in 2 Kings' Elijah, and the reader identifies him without being told `[I]`, high.

- **Textual confirmation.** Codex D replaces τρίχας καμήλου ("camel's hair") with δέρριν ("a skin garment") and omits the belt. NA28 apparatus: "⸀ δερριν D a"; "⸋ D it" `[T]`, verified. δέρριν is Zech 13:4 Swete's word for the prophet's hairy mantle. A scribe assimilated John to the prophetic uniform.

*What it adds.* John is Malachi's Elijah. His dress also carries Elijah's confrontation with a king, and the next king in Mark (Herod, 6:14–29) kills him `[I]`.

**Deut 8:2–5 → 1:12–13** *(moderate: a cluster of words and a pattern)*

*Move 1.* Moses tells Israel to remember "all the way which the LORD your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness, … to test you" (Hebrew לְנַסֹּתְךָ, "to test you"; Swete ἐκπειράσῃ σε, "might test you"). Then "as a man disciplines his son, so the LORD your God disciplines you" (8:5) `[T]`, verified: נסה ("to test"), מִדְבָּר ("wilderness") and אַרְבָּעִים ("forty") at Deut 8:2.

Behind it lies Exod 4:22, Υἱὸς πρωτότοκός μου Ἰσραήλ ("Israel is my firstborn son", Swete) `[T]`.

Mark has the son, the wilderness, forty, and testing: πειραζόμενος ("being tested"), a cognate of ἐκπειράσῃ. The agent differs: in Deuteronomy God tests; in Mark, Satan does, and the Spirit sends `[T]`. *Moderate.* It is a pattern, not a citation, and the declared "Son" of 1:11 is the connection.

**1 Kgs 19:4–8 → 1:13** *(moderate: pattern, supported by Elijah at 1:6)*

*Move 1.* Elijah flees Jezebel into the wilderness (ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ, "in the wilderness", 19:4 Swete). An angel touches him twice: "Arise, eat" (19:5, 7). "In the strength of that food" he goes τεσσεράκοντα ἡμέρας καὶ τεσσεράκοντα νύκτας ("forty days and forty nights") to Horeb (19:8) `[T]`, verified.

### 12. Genre

Gospel narrative, opening with a **scriptural prologue**. It has a verbless heading, a formula citation, a prophet's speech, a theophany, and a summary without dialogue. Reading rules:

- These events happened: a baptism in the Jordan, a period in the wilderness.
- They are theologically shaped: the heavens, the Voice.
- The narrator's framing is authoritative.

The theophany is narrated from Jesus' point of view: εἶδεν ("he saw", 1:10), and the Voice speaks to "you" (1:11) `[T]`. Mark does not say that the crowd saw or heard anything. The genre rule is to read it as disclosure to Jesus and to the reader, not as a public spectacle `[I]`, high.

### 13. Copycat

- **John's call to repentance and baptism (1:4–5).** Descriptive. It is also the pattern of the gospel's summons, since Jesus repeats "repent" at 1:15 `[T]`. The call is normative; the method (a baptism in the Jordan) is unique to the forerunner.
- **The crowds confessing their sins (1:5).** Descriptive. They show the right response to the herald `[I]`.
- **John's humility (1:7).** Descriptive of his role. The prologue presents it as a fact about who is coming, not as a virtue for imitation.
- **Jesus' baptism (1:9–11).** A unique event. The Voice is not a promise that every believer will hear it.
- **The Son in the wilderness (1:12–13).** A unique event. Mark gives nothing to copy: no dialogue, no Scripture answers, no method. The *manner* is a sending by the Spirit into enemy ground, and the Son is served, not serving `[T]`. The posture it suggests is trust in the one who went, not imitation of how he resisted.

### 14. Bible Timeline

The events stand at the hinge between the Prophets' last promises and their fulfilment: the messenger (Malachi), the voice (Isaiah 40), and the torn heavens (Isaiah 63). The reader stands after the cross and resurrection, and so knows what the prologue's "beloved" and "baptism" (10:38) will cost. The reader also stands after Pentecost, which Mark does not narrate, so the Spirit-baptism promised at 1:8 is, for the reader, already given `[I]`. Nothing in the unit is an Old Testament passage, so the Tanak-position field does not apply. The Old Testament sources are listed in Tanak order under Tool 11's live-source line.

### 15. Who Am I?

- **Jesus.** The Son, the Servant and the Stronger One, and not a model believer. The wilderness shows *him* doing what only he does.
- **John.** The forerunner. His role is unique. A preacher may share his posture ("not worthy"), but must not claim his office.
- **The crowds.** Recipients of the call, and the nearest to "us": they hear the herald, confess, and wait for the Stronger One.
- **Satan.** The adversary.
- **The angels.** The Son's servants.
- **The reader.** Addressed *through* the narrator, and told who Jesus is before anyone in the story knows it.

### 16. So What?

**Author's intended response.** To receive the Jesus of the following chapters as the Lord whose coming Scripture promised and whom God himself calls Son. The reader should then read every later scene in that light, and should repent and believe (1:15) because the one who is coming has come `[I]`.

**Worldview.** The good news is not an idea but an arrival: God has kept his word (1:2–3) and come in person. The heavens have been torn open once (1:10), and will be again, differently, at the cross (15:38). The adversary has been confronted on his own ground (1:12–13; 3:27).

**Behaviour.**
- *Stop:* treating Jesus as a teacher or example we assess from outside. God has already said who he is.
- *Start:* reading the Gospel as those who know more than the disciples, and so are more accountable.
- *Motive:* the Son was sent into the wilderness for us, and the angels served him. He is the Stronger One, not our model under temptation.

**Four audiences.**
- *For me:* do I hear Mark's first chapter as God's own verdict on Jesus, or only as the start of a biography?
- *For a Christian friend:* one who feels abandoned in a "wilderness". The Son went there first, sent by the Spirit, and it is his presence there, not a technique, that is the comfort. This is a pastoral inference and should be made carefully `[I]`.
- *For the church:* a baptised people, baptised into the one on whom the Spirit came.
- *For an unbeliever:* the claim is not that Jesus was a good man, but that God has come as Isaiah said.

**Prayer.** Father, you have spoken over your Son. Tear open our hearing too, that we may know the Stronger One who came, and follow behind him.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.**
- **Time.** Under Herod Antipas in Galilee and a Roman prefect in Judaea `[S]`. Mark gives no date `[T]`.
- **Place.** The lower Jordan and the Judaean wilderness; Nazareth in Galilee. Galilee is a northern region under a Herodian ruler, distinct from Judaea `[S]`.
- **Politics.** Occupation; a temple run by a priestly aristocracy; the Pharisees' influence in the synagogues `[S]`.

**What the first hearers knew.**
- **Loosening the sandal strap** (1:7) was a slave's task `[S]`, commonly cited from later rabbinic sources (*uncertain* on the date of the evidence). The point stands from the text alone: John ranks himself below the lowest service `[I]`.
- **Locusts and wild honey** (1:6) were the food of the wilderness poor. Locusts are permitted food (Lev 11:22) `[S]` for the custom, `[T]` for Leviticus. They mark John as living off the land, outside the city economy.
- **A camel-hair garment and a leather belt** would read as prophetic dress to anyone who knew 2 Kings 1 (Tool 11).
- **Washing rites for Jewish purity** were widespread (the *mikveh*) `[S]`. John's baptism differs in that it is administered *by* him (ὑπ᾽ αὐτοῦ, "by him", 1:5), once, for repentance and forgiveness `[T]`.
- **The Jordan and the wilderness** carried exodus and conquest associations (Joshua 3–4) and prophetic associations (Elijah, 2 Kings 2) `[T]` for the texts.
- **Mark's likely first audience.** Gentile, Roman *(moderate; overview)*. They needed Jewish customs explained (7:3–4), but they are not given an explanation of John's dress. Mark expects them to know their Scriptures, or to learn them `[I]`.

**What this changes.** The prologue's scene is set *outside* Jerusalem and its temple. All Judaea and Jerusalem go *out* to the wilderness for forgiveness (1:5), and the temple is not mentioned. Forgiveness is offered at the river by a prophet, before the Lord comes to his temple (Mal 3:1) and pronounces judgement on it (11:15–17) `[I]`, moderate–high.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** Readers who know Torah, Prophets and Writings, and who hear Malachi's promise of a messenger and Elijah as the last words of the Prophets (Mal 3:23 Heb). Mark begins where the Prophets end `[I]`. Mark also names Isaiah, the prophet of comfort for the exiles, and his reader is still waiting for God's return in person.

**First hearers.** Most likely Gentile believers in Rome, possibly under persecution (overview, `[I]`/`[S]`). Some of them know the Scriptures in Greek.

- **Shocking.** God addresses a Galilean from Nazareth as "my beloved Son", in the words of Israel's enthronement psalm. And that Son is immediately *driven* by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tested by Satan.
- **Surprising.** The book gives no birth story and no pedigree, and the only witness to the heavenly Voice is Jesus himself.
- **Comforting.** For a persecuted community, the Son went into the enemy's wilderness first. The book's first act of the Spirit is to send the Son into danger, not away from it `[I]`.
- **Disturbing.** "All Judaea and Jerusalem" came out and confessed sins (1:5). The book will show the same capital rejecting the one John announced `[I]`.

**What we bring.** An individualised, private reading of baptism and temptation ("my baptism", "my wilderness"). A sentimental picture of the dove. And the habit of reading Mark through Matthew's and Luke's temptation dialogues, so that we supply what Mark leaves out.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. The return of the LORD / the new exodus.**
- **Where this passage sits:** the fulfilment stage. The way of YHWH (Isa 40) is prepared, and the one who comes is the Son.
- **Before:** Exod 23:20 (the angel guiding Israel), Isa 40 (the return from exile as God's own coming), Mal 3 (the Lord to his temple).
- **After:** the way (ὁδός) of 8:27–10:52; the Lord at his temple (11:11–17).
- **Contribution:** it tells us *who* is coming, namely the Lord himself in the Son.
- **Confidence:** high.

**2. The Spirit.**
- **Where this passage sits:** the Spirit comes to rest on the Messiah (Isa 42:1; 61:1 is the trajectory), before being poured out on others (the promise of 1:8).
- **Before:** Gen 1:2 (uncertain here); Isa 42:1; Isa 63:10–11 (the Holy Spirit in the exodus).
- **After:** 3:29 (blaspheming the Spirit); 13:11 (the Spirit speaking through disciples); outside Mark, Pentecost.
- **Contribution:** the Spirit who descends is the Spirit who *sends*. Spirit-anointing leads directly into conflict.
- **Confidence:** high for the Spirit on the Servant; moderate for Isaiah 63.

**3. The beloved son offered.**
- **Where this passage sits:** the Son declared.
- **Before:** Isaac (Gen 22); Israel as God's firstborn son (Exod 4:22); the king as God's son (Ps 2).
- **After:** 9:7; 12:6 (the beloved son killed); 15:39.
- **Contribution:** "beloved" carries the offering from the start.
- **Confidence:** moderate–high.

**Confluence: climactic convergence.** The three themes meet in one reality the passage names. The coming LORD is the Spirit-anointed Servant, and he is the only Son who will be offered. The prologue announces all three at the river, and the rest of the book shows how they belong together.

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection.** Mark is a New Testament passage, so Christ is named. The Christological work lies in the Old Testament patterns Mark uses:

- **Direct fulfilment** (Isa 40:3; Mal 3:1): prophecy.
- **Typology:** Isaac, the beloved son. It passes the category test (sonship and sacrifice) and the NT-precedent test (Rom 8:32; Heb 11:17–19, which is the same kind of connection). It passes the escalation test: the Son is actually given, where Isaac was spared. It partly passes the authorial-pattern test, since Gen 22 marks the "only son" three times. *High.*
- **Israel as the son in the wilderness** (Deut 8): trajectory and contrast. Israel was tested for forty years and failed; the Son is tested for forty days. Mark records no failure, but also no victory scene. *Moderate.*
- **Elijah and Elisha:** uncertain; do not press it.

**How the passage points to Christ.** The prologue identifies Jesus with *YHWH's* coming (the pronouns in 1:2–3) and with the *Son and Servant* (1:11). The Christological claim is not that he is like the prophets, but that the prophets prepared *his* way.

**Trajectory.** The passage looks for God to come and to rend the heavens. It is fulfilled in Jesus' arrival. What remains, both already and not yet, is the Spirit-baptism promised but not narrated, and the torn veil still to come.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like" temptation:** "Be humble like John", or "resist temptation like Jesus".
- **The gospel grounding:** the Son was sent, anointed and tested first, and for others.
- **Christ as hero, not example:** the Stronger One enters Satan's ground. The angels serve him, and the book will say what he achieved there (3:27).

**Confidence:** high on Isaiah and Malachi; high on Ps 2 and Gen 22; moderate on Deuteronomy 8.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**1:12–13: What happened in the wilderness?**
- **Category:** apologetic and pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** Mark gives no temptations, no answers and no outcome.
- **Common mishandling:** importing Matthew and Luke's three temptations and preaching them as though from Mark.
- **Rhetorical function:** a compressed scene of conflict between the Son and Satan, placed straight after the declaration. It functions as confirmation and commission, not as a moral test to pass `[I]`.
- **Options:** (a) the victory is assumed and explained later by 3:27; (b) the conflict is ongoing, and every later exorcism continues it.
- **Honest exegesis:** Mark leaves the outcome to the ministry that follows *(moderate)*.
- **Handling:** acknowledge the silence. Do not fill it from the other Gospels without saying so.

**1:13: "He was with the wild beasts."**
- **Category:** interpretive.
- **Options:** (a) danger and desolation; (b) Adam restored, with peace with the animals (Isa 11:6–9); (c) scenery.
- **Honest exegesis:** θηρίον ("wild beast") is a hapax in Mark with no further marker. Option (a) fits the wilderness and Satan best, but no option is textually secured *(uncertain)*.
- **Handling:** mention it lightly or leave it. Do not build the sermon on it.

**1:4: John's baptism "for the forgiveness of sins" before Jesus' death.**
- **Category:** doctrinal.
- **Rhetorical function:** preparation ("a baptism of repentance"). The forgiveness it looks to is announced with authority at 2:10 and grounded at 10:45 and 14:24 `[I]`.
- **Handling:** address briefly.

**1:10–11: Adoptionism.** Did Jesus *become* Son at the baptism?
- **Category:** doctrinal.
- **Honest exegesis:** the Voice says σὺ εἶ ("you *are*"), not "today I have begotten you". Mark drops Ps 2:7's second clause. The aorist εὐδόκησα ("I am well pleased") states a settled delight `[T]`, moderate on the aspect.
- **Handling:** a sentence, if the question is live in the congregation.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall: preaching Mark's wilderness with Matthew's script

- **What it looks like:** "Jesus answered each temptation with Scripture, and so must we."
- **Why it is wrong:** Mark gives no dialogue, no three tests and no Scripture answers. He gives a sending (ἐκβάλλει, "drives out"), the adversary, beasts, and angels serving the Son `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach 1:12–13 as the Son sent into Satan's territory, and let 3:27 explain it. If Matthew is used, say so from the pulpit.

### Pitfall: "Your wilderness season"

- **What it looks like:** the wilderness as a metaphor for the believer's hard times, with Jesus as the model of coping.
- **Why it is wrong:** Moses-is-me, again. The Son is there as the Stronger One, not as our example. The angels serve *him* `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** the comfort is that he went first and went for us. Draw the pastoral application from who he is, not from his technique.

### Pitfall: a gentle, sentimental baptism

- **What it looks like:** a peaceful river scene, a soft dove, and a warm moment of affirmation.
- **Why it is wrong:** the heavens are *torn* (σχίζω), as the veil will be torn at his death. The Voice calls him the "beloved" whom Gen 22 offers. The Spirit at once *drives* him into conflict `[T]`.
- **The corrective:** preach the baptism as the beginning of the way to the cross (10:38), with 15:38–39 in view.

### Pitfall: "Be like John"

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on humility and pointing away from oneself, and nothing more.
- **Why it is wrong:** the prologue's interest in John is in *whom he announces*. His dress identifies him as Malachi's Elijah; it is not a model of humility.
- **The corrective:** use John to point to the Stronger One, as the text does.

### Pitfall: building doctrine on 1:1's "Son of God"

- **What it looks like:** making 1:1 the proof-text for Mark's Christology.
- **Why it is wrong:** the words are textually uncertain (Textual Variants).
- **The corrective:** ground "Son of God" in 1:11, where God says it.
