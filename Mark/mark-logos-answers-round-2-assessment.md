## Question by Question

### 1. Ephphatha and the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 (Mark 7:34–35)

**What was found.**

- **1a (the Assistant).**
  - English, Hurtado, Garland, Cooper and Evans all link 7:31–37 to Isa 35:5–6, by μογιλάλος ("speaking with difficulty") or by the crowd's words at 7:37.
  - None examines the Hebrew of Isa 35:5 or compares it with the Septuagint.
  - Moyise has nothing on it.
  - Evans and Garland quote the English: "unstopped".
- **1b (the Assistant on Watts, pp. 171–175).** Watts derives μογιλάλον from Isa 35:6 LXX and quotes Isa 35:5 in English ("unstopped"). He does not compare the Hebrew פתח with the Greek "hear" `[S: Watts, via the Assistant]`.
- **1c (Patrick's reading).**
  - **France on 7:34** `[S: France]`: Εφφαθα "probably represents the Aramaic ethpeel imperative, ʾetpᵉtaḥ … 'Open up!'". On 7:35 he cites Isa 35:5–6 in English ("the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped").
  - **Williams on 7:34** `[S: Williams]`: διανοίχθητι is a "pronouncement imperative". Nothing on Isaiah.

**The corpus.**

- **The verb.** The Hebrew of Isa 35:5 is וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה ("the ears of the deaf will be opened"). That is the root פתח ("open"), passive. France's Aramaic ʾetpᵉtaḥ is the same root, also passive `[T]` for the Hebrew; `[S: France]` for the Aramaic form. So the word Mark keeps in Aramaic, and translates as διανοίχθητι ("be opened") and ἠνοίγησαν ("were opened", 7:35), is the cognate of Isaiah's Hebrew verb. The Greek of Isa 35:5, ἀκούσονται ("will hear"), loses it.
- **The idiom.**
  - **The baseline:** אֹזֶן ("ear") and פתח ("open") share a verse in seven places in the WLC.
  - **Only three have the ear as what is opened, all in Isaiah:**
    - 35:5, the ears of the deaf;
    - 48:8, Israel's ear "was not opened";
    - 50:5, the Servant: "the Lord GOD has opened my ear".
  - **The other four** join the two words in other constructions: eyes open and ears attentive (Neh 1:6; 2 Chr 6:40; 7:15), and opening a riddle (Ps 49:5) `[T]`.
  - So "opened ears" is an Isaianic idiom in the Hebrew Bible, and the only one of the three about healing the deaf is 35:5.

**Assessment.**

- **This is this work's own finding.** No commentator among those owned, nor Watts, has made the Hebrew point. That is a finding about these nine works, not about all the literature: Marcus, Lane and Guelich are not owned and were not checked.
- **It is strengthened.** The idiom is Isaiah's alone in the Hebrew Bible, and the Aramaic healing word shares its root.
- **The weakness.** "Be opened" is the natural word for curing deafness in any Semitic language, so an Aramaic speaker healing a deaf man might say it without any thought of Isaiah `[I]`. What makes the link more than coincidence:
  - Mark has already tied the scene to Isa 35 through the rare μογιλάλος at 7:32;
  - the crowd's response at 7:37 is Isa 35:5–6;
  - Mark chose to keep the Aramaic word and translate it.
- **Rating: moderate,** as a seventh case of Mark nearer the Hebrew (triage category 3: the NT's own text, reached through the Hebrew). It goes into the claim-audit queue before it enters the overview.
- **A recollection, unverified.** I recall an argument (Rabinowitz, 1960s) that εφφαθα may be Hebrew, not Aramaic: a niphal imperative, the very stem of Isa 35:5. If that is right, the link would be closer still. It is from memory and has not been checked, so it is a question for a later round, not a finding.

### 2. The strong man bound (Mark 3:27)

**What was found.**

- **Watts** `[S: Watts, pp. 147–152, 285–286, 372, via the Assistant]`:
  - He reads 3:27 as Jesus' own commentary on his exorcisms, through Isa 49:24–25's Yahweh-Warrior.
  - He concedes "no direct verbal correspondence" with Isa 49:24–25 LXX, but holds "the similarity in meaning is so great … nor is there any comparable picture elsewhere in the Old Testament".
  - He treats Isa 53:12 ("the spoils of the strong") cautiously: the plunder follows the Servant's suffering, which is absent here.
  - He does not discuss Tobit or 1 Enoch.
- **Commentaries** `[S: France 173–174; Hurtado 65–66; Garland 132–133; Bayer 520]`. Isa 49:24–25 is cited by Hurtado (as a likely allusion), Garland and Bayer. France notes that Tobit's binding comes after the expulsion, and that 1 Enoch and the Testament of Levi concern the imprisonment of the fallen angels or the end-time disabling of evil, not exorcism.
- **Watts's internal link** (from his exported text and the Assistant). "Satan", "Spirit" and "strong man" terminology occur together only at 3:27 and in the prologue, and he reads the Spirit's descent on "the stronger one" (1:7) as the answer to the prayer of Isa 63.

**The corpus.** Watts's internal link holds:

- ἰσχυρός ("strong"; 1:7 as ἰσχυρότερος, "stronger") occurs in Mark only at 1:7 and 3:27.
- Σατανᾶς ("Satan") occurs at 1:13; 3:23, 26; 4:15; 8:33.
- The Holy Spirit is named at 1:8 (with the Spirit at 1:10, 12) and at 3:29, and also at 12:36 and 13:11.

All three stand together only in 1:7–13 and 3:22–30 `[T]`, SBLGNT. That is a strong internal echo (Move 4) between the prologue and the Beelzebul controversy.

**Assessment.**

- **The verdict stands: uncertain for a verbal allusion.** Watts agrees there is none.
- **History of Interpretation is now strong.** The link is widely seen, on meaning.
- **France's point weakens the rival.** The binding traditions are mostly not about exorcism. But it does not supply binding to Isaiah.
- **What carries the weight is internal to Mark.** If 3:27 is Isaianic, it is because 3:22–30 picks up 1:7–13, and 1:10 is the Isa 63:19 link (Question 3). That chain is only as strong as its weakest link. It is reported as a Watts reading, `[I]`, not as a finding.

### 3. The torn heavens (Mark 1:10)

**What was found.**

- **France** (p. 77) `[S: France, via the Assistant]`. The Hebrew קָרַע ("tear") of Isa 63:19 would be well rendered by σχίζω ("tear"), but "the LXX in fact uses anoigō", so the allusion would be heard only by those reading Isaiah in Hebrew. This is the overview's own "nearer the Hebrew" case at 1:10, already in print.
- **Watts** (his exported text) `[S: Watts, verbatim]`:
  - The Greek's use of ἀνοίγω ("open") for קרע ("tear") at Isa 63:19 is "anomalous".
  - The baptism is "the only place in the NT where the descent of the Spirit is described by καταβαίνω" ("come down"), and "only in Isaiah 63 LXX is this verb associated with both the Exodus … and the descent of the Spirit" (63:14, κατέβη πνεῦμα παρὰ κυρίου, "the Spirit came down from the Lord").
  - Mark and Isaiah alike link the "descent" or "placement" of the רוּחַ ("Spirit"; Hebrew שׂים, "place", 63:11; Greek καταβαίνω, 63:14) with "coming up out of water" imagery.
  - The appeal to "rend the heavens" (63:19) "must surely be understood in the light of the immediately preceding" Exodus memory, "replete with רוּחַ language".
  - He reports Lentzen-Deis's three objections and answers them:
    - the passage does not fit a theophany form;
    - Mark has the Spirit descending, not God coming to destroy enemies;
    - judgement is not a possible sense of 1:10.
  - He extends the reading tentatively. The Beelzebul controversy recalls Israel grieving the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:29; Isa 63:10). Mark 4:35–5:43 may be "a pastiche reflecting Isaiah 63:7–65:25", which he calls "far from conclusive".
- **Others** `[S]`: Wessel (EBC), Garland, English, Cornerstone and Moyise (reporting Marcus) link 1:10 to Isa 64:1.
- **The 15:38 bracket** `[S: Schnabel 2:423; Williams 267; Bayer 691]`. Schnabel, Williams and Bayer join σχίζω ("tear") at 1:10 and 15:38. Williams notes it is used only twice in Mark, both times of an act of God.

**The corpus.**

- **Watts's verb claim holds narrowly.**
  - πνευμ- ("spirit") with a καταβ- form ("come down") stands in 8 verses of Swete.
  - **Only at Isa 63:14 is the Spirit of the Lord the subject of the verb.** At Num 11:17, 25 the Lord comes down and takes of the spirit. At Judg 14:19 Samson goes down. Ecc 3:21 is the spirit of the beast `[T]`.
  - All four Gospels use καταβαίνω for the Spirit at the baptism, so Watts's "only place in the NT" means the one event, told four times.
- **The rarity of the "Holy Spirit".** רוּחַ קָדְשׁוֹ ("his Holy Spirit") stands in the Hebrew Bible only at Isa 63:10, 11 and Ps 51:13 (WLC). That supports Watts's link of Mark 3:29 to Isa 63:10, where Israel "grieved his Holy Spirit" and God "turned to become their enemy" `[T]`. It goes into the queue.
- **Watts assembles his case across two texts.** He takes the Spirit's descent from the Greek of 63:14, and the "sea" and the placing of the Spirit from the Hebrew of 63:11. That is the very assembly claim audit 1 objected to. But Swete's apparatus (round 1 follow-up) shows that **the first hand of Vaticanus carries all three in one Greek witness**: the sea (63:11), the Holy Spirit (63:11) and the Spirit descending (63:14). Watts does not cite it, and it partly answers the audit's objection on his behalf.
- **Watts's Isa 64:2 readings at 4:41.** The fear-words he cites from Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion at Isa 64:2 are **not in Swete's apparatus** (Isa 64:2–3, as Swete numbers it). His source is presumably Field or the Göttingen edition, neither of which is in the library. They stay `[S: Watts]`.

**Assessment.**

- **The 1:10 / Isa 63:19 row.** History of Interpretation is now strong (France, Watts, Wessel, Garland, Cornerstone, Marcus via Moyise). The "nearer the Hebrew" point is a rediscovery and should be credited to France. The row stays moderate; a single word (σχίζω, "tear") carries it.
- **Claim audit 1, Claim 2 (Isa 63:11–14), is reopened.** Watts is a strong history of interpretation for it. Vaticanus's first hand answers the "no single text" objection. But the audit's other points remain:
  - the cluster of spirit, coming down and coming up is common (43 chapters of Swete);
  - Isaiah's verb is "bring up", Mark's "come up";
  - the full reading is one manuscript's first hand, corrected in that manuscript.

  It goes to the next claim audit at **uncertain–moderate**, and is not to be adopted here.
- **Watts's extensions are candidates, not findings:**
  - Mark 3:29 ← Isa 63:10 (the rare "Holy Spirit");
  - Mark 4:12 ← Isa 63:17 (the hardened heart);
  - Mark 4:41 ← Isa 64:2 (fear at awesome deeds);
  - Mark 4:35–5:43 as a pastiche on Isa 63:7–65:25.

  Watts himself rates the last "far from conclusive".

## What Schneck's List Shows About the Inventory

Watts quotes Schneck's list of Isaiah allusions "in every chapter of Mark" 1–8 `[S: Schneck, via Watts]`. Ten of its entries are **not in the overview's inventory**:

- 2:7 (Isa 43:25–26)
- 2:16–20 (Isa 58:2–7)
- 2:20 (Isa 58:8)
- 4:24 (Targum of Isa 27:8)
- 5:1–20 (Isa 65:1–7)
- 6:34–44 (Isa 55:1–3)
- 6:39–40 (Isa 25:6)
- 6:52 (Isa 6:9–10)
- 8:17b (Isa 6:9–10)
- 8:25 (Isa 42:6–7)

**Why this matters for the hypothesis.** The hypothesis test found Isaiah thin in the Galilee chapters: about 1 locus per 100 verses. Schneck's list puts nearly all its extra entries there. If even a few survive testing, the "front-weighting" finding of claim audit 2 would be partly an artefact of an inventory that never tested them. Most look thematic and some are surely weak, but they have not been tested at all. This is the most important gap these answers have exposed.

**Action.** Test the ten in the coming solo digs of Mark 1–8, or in a single claim audit. Until then, the substrate verdict carries the note *"Galilee inventory incomplete: Schneck's list untested"*.

## Effect on the Hypothesis

1. **Frame:** unchanged, high.
2. **Substrate:** unchanged in verdict. It now carries a declared gap, Schneck's untested Galilee entries, which could raise Isaiah's count where the inventory is thinnest.
3. **Architecture:** unchanged, open. Watts's reading of the prologue through Isa 63 is now on the record in his own words. It is the strongest single piece of his scheme, and it links the prologue (1:10), the Beelzebul controversy (3:22–30) and, tentatively, 4:35–5:43 to the one Isaianic prayer that, in your own Isaiah sweep, ends the new-exodus argument "in a question" (63:11). That convergence between Watts and the Isaiah sweep is worth a solo dig of Mark 1:9–13 with Isa 63:7–64:12 as its main OT text.

## Critical Assessment: Gaps

1. **"No commentator has seen it" covers nine owned works, not the field.** The *Ephphatha* claim is this work's own only relative to that library.
2. **The idiom count rests on one construction.** "Ear" as the object of "open" was judged by reading the seven verses. A different parsing of Ps 49:5 or the Chronicles verses would not change the result, but it was a judgement, not a mechanical count.
3. **Watts's own words now carry much of this assessment.** That is a gain over round 1. But Watts argues a thesis, and the audits should test his extensions as hard as they tested this work's own.
4. **Five rediscoveries in two rounds show a weakness in the method.** The literature was consulted only after the claims were made, by design. The design is right for independence, but it means the hypothesis test overstated what was new. Future reports should mark leads as "new to this work", not "new".
