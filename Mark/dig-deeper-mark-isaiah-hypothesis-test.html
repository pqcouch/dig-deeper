Hypothesis Test: Does Mark Build His Gospel on Isaiah?

Hypothesis (yours). Mark opens with “As it is written in Isaiah the prophet” (1:2), although the quotation that follows draws on Exodus and Malachi too. The hypothesis is that Mark builds his Gospel on the Book of Isaiah, just as he says. Primary texts. Mark from the SBLGNT with the MorphGNT index, counted in 1:1–16:8 only. The NA28 text and apparatus exports for readings that bear weight. The Greek Old Testament from Swete (observation) and the Rahlfs–Hanhart Isaiah export (citation). Post-audit note (6 November 2026). This report is left unchanged as a record. Claim audit 2 (Mark/dig-deeper-mark-claim-audit-2.md) has tested its six claims, and where the two disagree the audit supersedes this report. In brief:

  • Frame. It holds and is strengthened: Isaiah’s weighting towards the prologue is significant against the movements’ lengths.
  • Substrate. Isaiah is the most-used source and, with Exodus, the most widely spread. “Most evenly spread” is withdrawn, because the 0.29 benchmark was misread.
  • Architecture.
    • Isa 42:16 survives only as part of a Greek-led cluster (Isa 35:5–8; 40:3–4; 42:16–18). “The only text in Hebrew or Greek” fails across two verses, because of Jer 31:8–9 in the Hebrew.
    • The Isa 35 sequence is downgraded to uncertain.
    • Isa 52:7 is reframed as a herald complex (40:9–10; 52:7; 61:1).
    • Isa 49 / 53:12 at 3:27 stays uncertain.
    • The “way” phrase ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“on the way”) is the continuation of Exod 23:20, not the wording of Isa 40:3, so falsifier F2 is partly met.

Second post-audit note (9 November 2026). Claim audits 3 and 4 tested the Logos-round candidates and Schneck’s Galilee list.

  • Recount. Three of Schneck’s ten survive (5:1–20 from Isa 65:3–5; 6:52 and 8:17 from Isa 6:9–10). With them, Isaiah has 20 loci.
  • “Thin in Galilee” was an artefact of an untested inventory. Outside the prologue Isaiah is spread in proportion to the narrative (2.4–3.5 loci per 100 verses; fit to length p ≈ 0.98). The front-weighting is the prologue alone.
  • Frame: high.
  • Substrate: strengthened to moderate, but not yet compared fairly with the other books.
  • Architecture: open.

The Hypothesis and How It Was Tested

“Mark builds his Gospel on Isaiah” can mean three different things, and they need different evidence. They were tested separately.

  1. Frame (weak form). Mark names Isaiah as the lens through which the whole story is to be read. The evidence is chiefly 1:1–3.
  2. Substrate (middle form). Isaiah supplies more, and more load-bearing, citations and allusions than any other book, and they are spread across the whole Gospel.
  3. Architecture (strong form). Isaiah’s shape governs Mark’s shape. The best-known version is Rikki Watts’s: a new exodus in three stages, deliverance, the way, and arrival at Jerusalem. That account is recalled here, not read. See Questions for Logos.

Falsifiers set before any counting.

  • F1. The middle form fails if Isaiah’s links cluster in a few episodes, or are outweighed by the Psalms or Daniel at the Gospel’s turning points.
  • F2. The strong form fails if the “way” motif can be fully explained without Isa 40.
  • F3. The frame is weakened if the attribution at 1:2 is best explained as the ordinary habit of naming the more prominent source in a composite quotation.

Method.

  • The inventory is the overview’s, not a new one. The Intertextual Map of the Mark overview v0.2 has already been through a whole-book sweep and an independent claim audit of 21 allusions. Every row rated moderate or above was taken over unchanged, for Isaiah and for six control sources: the Psalms, Daniel, Exodus, Deuteronomy, Zechariah and Malachi. Using already-audited ratings means the comparison cannot be tilted towards Isaiah by how this run rates its own finds.
  • New candidates are kept apart. The few new Isaiah links found in this run are reported separately, and they do not enter the counts.
  • Distribution. Each locus was assigned to one of the overview’s six movements: Prologue 1:1–13 (13 verses); Galilee 1:14–8:21 (292); the Way 8:22–10:52 (117); Jerusalem 11:1–13:37 (113); the Passion 14:1–15:47 (118); the Tomb 16:1–8 (8). Concentration is measured with a Herfindahl index (HHI): 1.0 means every locus falls in one movement, and 0.17 is the floor for six movements. A spread exactly proportional to the movements’ lengths would score 0.29.
  • Searches. Greek searches were accent-stripped. Hebrew searches were made by lemma. Every count names its edition.

A correction to my opening proposal. I put forward Isa 63:19 behind Mark 1:10 as a promising new lead. It was not new. The Mark overview already carries it at moderate, through the Hebrew קָרַעְתָּ (“you tore”) against the Greek ἀνοίξῃς (“you open”). The claim audit (Claim 2) has already tested and rejected the wider match with 63:11–14. The folder was ahead of me, and this report builds on what it holds rather than re-deriving it.

Summary of Findings

Form Verdict In one line
Frame Holds — high Isaiah is the only prophet Mark names. His two named citations come one from each half of the book (29:13; 40:3). The name stands over a composite whose three texts were already joined in the Hebrew.
Substrate Holds in part — moderate Isaiah is the most widely spread source in Mark, and the most evenly spread of any source tested. It is not the dominant voice at the Gospel’s two climaxes: there the Psalms and Daniel speak. On high-confidence links alone, Isaiah and the Psalter are level.
Architecture Open, with one strong lead — uncertain–moderate The “way” is Mark’s own emphasis (it is concentrated in 8:22–10:52, and the parallels drop it). Its only scriptural anchor is Isa 40:3. One verse, Isa 42:16, is the only text in either the Hebrew or the Greek where God leads the blind along a way, and Mark’s Way section is framed by the healing of two blind men. That is a lead for the claim audit, not a finding.

The defensible thesis, on present evidence: Mark frames his Gospel with Isaiah and draws on the whole of Isaiah throughout. Whether Isaiah also supplies the Gospel’s plan is not yet shown. “Builds on” is stronger than the evidence so far supports. “Frames with, and reads through” is not.

Test 1 — The Frame: Mark 1:1–3

1. The name is the harder reading. NA28 and the SBLGNT read ἐν τῷ Ἠσαΐᾳ τῷ προφήτῃ (“in Isaiah the prophet”), with א B L Δ. The Majority text has ἐν τοῖς προφήταις (“in the prophets”) [T], verified in the 1:1–13 dig. A scribe who knew the first line was not Isaiah had every reason to change the name, and none to put it in. So the name is Mark’s.

2. Isaiah is the only prophet Mark names as a writer. In 1:1–16:8 the name occurs at 1:2 and 7:6 only. Moses is named in eight verses, as lawgiver. David is named as the speaker of Ps 110 (12:36). Elijah is named as a figure, not an author. No other prophet’s name occurs: there is no Jeremiah, Daniel or Zechariah, although Mark quotes Zechariah (14:27) and Jeremiah (11:17) [T], SBLGNT.

3. The two named citations come one from each half of Isaiah. 7:6–7 names Isaiah for 29:13, and 1:2–3 names him for 40:3 [T]. Mark puts the same name over a text in chapter 29 and a text in chapter 40. Nothing in Mark divides the book. This matches your single-volume reading of Isaiah, though it cannot prove it: Mark is simply using the name the scroll carried [I].

4. The γέγραπται (“it is written”) formulas. There are seven: 1:2; 7:6; 9:12; 9:13; 11:17; 14:21; 14:27 [T].

  • Isaiah: three (1:2; 7:6; 11:17), two of them named.
  • Zechariah: one (14:27).
  • Unspecified: three. Two concern the Son of Man’s suffering (9:12; 14:21) and one concerns Elijah (9:13).

No other book is cited under the formula more than once.

5. Is the name just “the principal source”? (F3).

  • The verbal share is level. Counted in the SBLGNT, the Exodus/Malachi line (1:2b) is 13 words and the Isaiah line (1:3) is 14 [T]. Isaiah is barely the larger part, and it comes second.
  • The convention would more naturally name the first source. A rule of naming the bigger or the first part does not explain why Mark leads with Exodus and Malachi and then credits the whole to Isaiah [I].
  • The three texts are already joined in the Hebrew. The 1:1–13 dig verified that Mal 3:1 shares Isaiah 40:3’s rare פָּנָה (“clear”) + דֶּרֶךְ (“way”), and that Mal 3:1 reuses Exod 23:20’s שׁלח (“send”) + מַלְאָךְ (“messenger”) [T]. Mark did not create the composite. He followed a link the prophets had already made, and he named it from Isaiah, the passage that supplies the “way of the Lord” [I], moderate–high.
  • What the corpus cannot settle. Whether such composites circulated ready-made under a single name (the “testimonia” hypothesis) is [S]. It goes to Logos.

Verdict on the frame: holds, high. F3 does not explain the evidence as well as the hypothesis does.

What the numbers show [T] for the counts, [I] for their reading:

  1. Isaiah is the most widely spread source in Mark, and the most evenly spread. Its HHI of 0.21 is the lowest of any source, and below the 0.29 of a length-proportional spread. Isaiah turns up wherever Mark goes. Only Exodus comes close (0.23).
  2. Isaiah is not clearly the most-used source. It leads on total loci (15 against 13), but the Psalms lead on links rated high (9 against 8). The overview’s phrase “the most-used source in Mark” should be qualified to “the most widely used”.
  3. Isaiah is densest where the book begins. It has 23 loci per 100 verses in the prologue, against 1.0 in Galilee, 1.7 on the Way, 3.5 in Jerusalem and 2.5 in the Passion. The prologue is where a reader is told how to read, and that is where Isaiah is concentrated. This is what the frame thesis predicts.
  4. The Psalms are concentrated in the Passion. Seven of their thirteen loci fall in chapters 14–15. Daniel is concentrated in chapter 13 and the trial. Each controls one zone of the book. Isaiah controls no zone but is present in all of them except the empty tomb.
  5. The Torah taken together outweighs Isaiah. Exodus, Deuteronomy, Genesis, Leviticus and Numbers together exceed Isaiah’s 15 loci, and Moses is named in eight verses. But the Torah is mostly cited as law, in disputes over what “Moses commanded” (1:44; 7:10; 10:3–5; 12:19, 26). Isaiah is cited as the story’s meaning. They do different work, so the comparison that matters is between individual books.

Isaiah leads at two turning points, shares four, and is absent from four. It is absent from the two that the overview identifies as the book’s hinges: “You are the Christ … the Son of Man must suffer” (8:29–31) and the cry from the cross (15:34). There the Psalms carry the weight. F1 is partly met.

A reception datum. The Majority text adds 15:28, “and the Scripture was fulfilled which says, ‘He was numbered with transgressors’” (Isa 53:12). The critical text does not have it [T], overview. Early copyists, in other words, felt that the cross should carry a marked Isaiah citation, and it did not. That is evidence about how Mark was read, not about how he wrote.

Verdict on the substrate: holds in part, moderate. Isaiah’s breadth is unique and its density at the opening is striking. Its claim to be the dominant voice fails at the climaxes.

Test 4 — The Architecture: the Way

1. The “way” is Mark’s own emphasis [T], SBLGNT.

  • It is not a matter of frequency. ὁδός (“way”) is used 1.44 times per 1,000 words in Mark, 1.20 in Matthew and 1.03 in Luke. Mark does not use the word much more often than they do.
  • It is a matter of placement. Seven of Mark’s sixteen uses fall in 8:22–10:52, a section that holds 18% of the book’s verses. The chance of seven or more falling there at random is about 1.4% (binomial test). The section’s bounds were set by the two healings of the blind, not by ὁδός, so the test is not circular.
  • The parallels drop it. At the five Way-section verses with parallels (8:27; 9:33–34; 10:17; 10:32; 10:52), Matthew and Luke keep “on the way” once out of ten (Matt 20:17). “On the way” is therefore Markan wording, not tradition shared by all three. That takes Markan priority for granted [S].
  • Mark gives the word one scriptural anchor, and it is the opening. The only places Mark draws ὁδός from Scripture are 1:2 (Malachi) and 1:3 (Isa 40:3). Every later use is narrative.
  • Jesus goes ahead on the way. προάγω (“to go ahead”) occurs at 6:45; 10:32; 11:9; 14:28; 16:7. At 10:32 he leads the way “going up to Jerusalem”. The book’s last promise is that he goes ahead of them to Galilee (16:7).

On this evidence F2 is not met. The way motif has no competing scriptural source in Mark, and it is distinctively Markan. But it is not proven Isaianic either: the evidence is placement, not shared wording beyond ὁδός itself.

2. A lead: the blind led on the way — Isa 42:16. This is new in this run, and unaudited.

  • The data. “I will lead the blind by a way they do not know” (NASB95). The Greek is ἄξω τυφλοὺς ἐν ὁδῷ (“I will lead the blind on a way”), the same in Swete and Rahlfs. The Hebrew is וְהוֹלַכְתִּי עִוְרִים בְּדֶרֶךְ.
  • The baseline. τυφλ- (“blind”) and ὁδ- (“way”) stand together in four Swete verses. Two are Deuteronomy’s curses on misleading the blind (27:18; 28:29) and one is Tobit. Isa 42:16 is the only one where God leads the blind [T]. In the WLC, the lemmas for blind and way share a verse only at Deut 27:18; 28:29 and Isa 42:16 [T], by lemma. The finding holds in both texts.
  • It takes up Isa 40:3–4. It ends ποιήσω … τὰ σκολιὰ εὐθεῖαν (“I will make the crooked straight”) and has τρίβους (“paths”), the vocabulary of 40:3–4. In the Hebrew, לְמִישׁוֹר (“into level ground”) occurs in Isaiah only at 11:4, 40:4 and 42:16 [T]. So within Isaiah, 42:16 is the way of 40:3 travelled by the blind.
  • The Markan side. τυφλός (“blind”) occurs in Mark only at 8:22–23 and 10:46–51, the two healings that frame the Way section [T]. The last of them ends ἠκολούθει αὐτῷ ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“he was following him on the way”, 10:52).

The match is conceptual and structural. The only shared Greek words are “blind” and “way”, so it must go to the claim audit before it is used. But it answers the question the strong form needs answered: is there an Isaianic text that joins blindness healed to the way? There is one, in both texts, and it is unique.

3. A second lead: Isaiah 35. This is also new and unaudited.

  • The chapter’s sequence. In one chapter: the eyes of the blind are opened and the ears of the deaf hear (35:5); the tongue of the mute speaks (35:6, μογιλάλων, the source of Mark 7:32’s NT hapax, already high); a way called holy (35:8); “the redeemed will walk there” (35:9, λελυτρωμένοι); and “the ransomed of the LORD will return and come … to Zion” (35:10) [T], Swete and Rahlfs, NASB95.
  • Mark’s sequence. 10:45 has λύτρον (“ransom”), 10:46–52 a blind man on the way, and 11:1 the approach to Jerusalem.
  • The baseline. The chapters of Swete that contain deaf, blind and way are Exod 4 and Isa 35, 42 and 43 [T]. Three of the four are Isaiah.
  • The weaknesses. The “redeemed” link is Greek only: the Hebrew of 35:9 is גְּאוּלִים, from גאל, not a ransom word. The order is not Mark’s, since Isaiah’s redeemed follow the healing and Mark’s ransom precedes it. And Jerusalem is not named in Isa 35.

Rating: uncertain–moderate, for audit.

4. Two more Isaianic candidates in the Galilee movement. Both are new and unaudited.

  • 1:14–15 and Isa 52:7.
    • The data. Mark has κηρύσσων τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ θεοῦ … ἤγγικεν ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ (“proclaiming the good news of God … the kingdom of God has come near”). Isaiah has εὐαγγελιζόμενος ἀγαθά … Βασιλεύσει σου ὁ θεός (“bringing good news … your God reigns”).
    • The baseline. Good news joined to God reigning: in Swete, bring good news + reign share a verse only here and at 2 Sam 18:26, where a king receives a runner’s news. In the WLC, בשׂר (“bring news”) + מלך (“reign”) share a verse only at Isa 52:7 [T], by lemma.
    • The caveat. Mark uses the noun, and Isaiah the verb.
    • Rating: moderate.
    • Bearing on the frame. It would put an Isaianic proclamation at the head of the Galilean ministry, two verses after the prologue’s Isaianic frame.
  • 3:27 and Isa 49:24–25 / 53:12 (the strong man plundered).
    • The data. Isaiah asks whether spoil can be taken from the mighty (49:24–25), and the Servant “will divide the spoil with the strong” (53:12, τῶν ἰσχυρῶν μεριεῖ σκῦλα).
    • Shared wording. Only ἰσχυρ- (“strong”) is shared. Mark’s τὰ σκεύη (“goods”) and διαρπάσαι (“plunder”) are not in either passage.
    • Rating: uncertain. This is the kind of conceptual match the claim audit has repeatedly cut down.

5. Watts’s three stages against the text. This is recalled, not read. As I understand it, Watts reads Isa 40:1–11 as a three-stage programme and maps Mark onto it: Galilee as deliverance, 8:22–10:52 as the way, and Jerusalem as the arrival, which turns to judgment because Jerusalem does not receive its Lord. Tested against the corpus:

Stage Mark Isaianic support found Strength
Deliverance 1:14–8:21 Isa 35:5–6 at 7:32, 37 (high); Isa 52:7 at 1:14–15 (new, moderate); Isa 6 and 29 on hardness (high); Isa 49 at 3:27 (uncertain) Mixed. The healings are Isaianic, but the “warrior” element is weak in wording
The way 8:22–10:52 ὁδός concentration (Markan); Isa 42:16 (unique in both texts); Isa 35 (sequence) The strongest of the three, but structural, not verbal
Arrival 11:1–16:8 Isa 56:7 and 5:1–7 at the temple and the vineyard (high), both turned against the city; Mal 3:1 (the Lord to his temple) Supports arrival-as-judgment, but Jeremiah, the Psalms and Daniel share the work

A caution that applies to the whole table. Galilee, journey, Jerusalem is also simply the plot. Matthew and Luke follow the same geography without Mark’s ὁδός emphasis. A three-stage reading is only Isaianic if Isaiah explains features that the geography does not. So far one such feature has been found, the blind on the way. That is promising, and it is not yet enough.

Verdict on the architecture: open, uncertain–moderate. F2 is not met. The positive case rests on one unaudited lead.

Critical Assessment: Gaps in the Argument

This section is here to guard against the echo-chamber effect. A report that finds what its commissioner hoped for should be read sceptically, and this one leans that way.

  1. The same model built the overview, the sweep and this test. The control comparison uses ratings that independent auditors checked, which limits the problem but does not remove it. All four new candidates (Isa 42:16; Isa 35; Isa 52:7; Isa 49 / 53:12) were found by the same author in one session. The project’s own record is that synthetic claims made at speed fail far more often than passage-level ones. None of them should be preached until the claim audit has run.
  2. The unit of counting is a choice. One locus per row treats a whole psalm and a single Isaiah verse alike. Counted by psalm, the Psalter is seven sources, not one. Counted by Mark verse, the Passion’s Psalm density rises. The headline (Isaiah broadest, Psalms level on high links) is stable across these choices. The margin of 15 to 13 is not.
  3. The movement boundaries are the overview’s. The HHI depends on them. A different division, for example ending Galilee at 8:26, moves one locus at most, but that has not been tested.
  4. Thematic is not verbal. The architecture case rests on placement and shape. The Hays criterion of volume is weak for every new lead except Isa 52:7.
  5. The argument from the parallels assumes Markan priority. If Matthew were first, the dropped “on the way” phrases would be Mark’s additions rather than retentions. That makes them more Markan, not less, so the conclusion survives. The assumption should still be stated.
  6. Watts is recalled, not read. The three-stage scheme above is my summary of what Watts argues. It may misstate him, and he may answer the geography objection. That is a question for Logos (below), and the right order under your method: inventory first, secondary literature second.
  7. “Builds on” is a claim about intention, and counts can only make it more or less probable. The strongest single datum for intention is the naming at 1:2, and it supports the frame, not the plan.
  8. Isaiah is absent from the ending. 16:1–8 has no Isaianic link, and its “going ahead” follows Zech 13:7 at 14:27–28. A Gospel built on Isaiah might be expected to end in Isaiah’s new creation (65:17) or Zion’s joy (35:10). Mark ends in fear and silence. This report has no answer to that, and the hypothesis needs one.

Questions for Logos

You own Watts, Isaiah’s New Exodus and Mark (WUNT 88), and Moyise, Jesus and Scripture. These questions are framed so that the answers can be tested against the inventory above, not adopted from it.

  1. Watts’s stages. How exactly does Watts divide Mark, and does he map the divisions onto Isa 40:1–11 alone or onto the whole of Isa 40–55? Does he answer the objection that Galilee, journey, Jerusalem is simply the plot?
  2. Watts on the way and the blind. Does he use Isa 42:16 or Isa 35 for the healings at 8:22–26 and 10:46–52? If he does, the lead here is a rediscovery, and the audit should say so.
  3. Watts on the ending. How does he account for 16:8, which ends in fear and silence, against an Isaianic arrival in Zion?
  4. The attribution at 1:2. Does Watts, Moyise or a commentary you own discuss the testimonia hypothesis, or a convention of naming one prophet for a composite? Matt 27:9 (Zechariah under Jeremiah’s name) is the obvious comparison.
