Mark: Logos Answers, Round 1 — Assessment

Answers assessed. Round 1 of the Mark questions (mark-logos-questions-round-1), as recorded in mark-logos-answers-round-1. That record has two parts:

  • Patrick’s own extracts from the library (Watts; Carson in the Expositor’s Bible Commentary; Witherington; Barnett; Evans);
  • the Research Assistant’s answers to nine questions.

Date. 9 November 2026. Method. Each answer is treated as a claim to test, not a finding. Wording claims were checked against the corpus:

  • Mark: SBLGNT;
  • the Greek OT: Swete, with the Rahlfs Isaiah verse file;
  • the Hebrew: the WLC, by lemma.

Cited views are recorded as [S: author].

How the Answers Were Read

Three kinds of material, weighted differently.

  1. Verbatim extracts in Part A: the 16:8 section and the testimonia footnote of Watts; the Carson, Witherington, Barnett and Evans passages. These are the authors’ own words, so they carry most weight.
  2. Third-person summaries in Part A. These are the paragraphs beginning “Watts suggests…” and “Watts discusses…”, with the lists A–D and A–C. They read like a generated summary, not like Watts’s prose, so they are treated as a summary, not as Watts.
  3. The Assistant’s answers in Part B. Each cites page numbers in Watts or in other works, which can be checked in Logos. One answer (on Moyise) is from general knowledge and is set aside.

One factual conflict between the sources, resolved by the verbatim text. On how Watts divides Mark:

  • Patrick’s verbatim extract (p. 123): 1:14/16–8:21/27; 8:21/27–10:45/11:1 (the Way); 10:45/11:1–16:8.
  • The Assistant: “10:46/11:1–16:18”.

Mark ends at 16:8, and the extract has 16:8. The Assistant’s “16:18” is a transcription error.

One conflict that needs a direct look.

  • The Assistant says Watts “does not appeal to Exodus 23:20 for understanding the ‘way’”.
  • The summary in Part A says that, for Watts, “the term ‘Way’ plays a programmatic role … aligning with the prophetic texts of Exodus and Malachi”.

These cannot both be right, and neither is Watts’s own wording. Settled 9 November by Watts’s own text, in favour of the summary. See Follow-up.

Question by Question

1.1 Watts’s three stages, and the “plot” objection

What Watts says [S: Watts, pp. 123, 135–136, 371].

  • Three movements. After a survey of 61 structural analyses, he finds three major sections. He matches them to the Isaianic new exodus schema:
    • Yahweh delivers his people from the nations and their idols;
    • he leads them along the Way;
    • he arrives in Jerusalem, enthroned in a restored Zion.
  • Isa 40–55, not 40:1–11 alone. Exorcisms are tied to Isa 49, and healings and feedings to Isa 35 and 29.
  • Against the “plot” objection. The match lies “not in a geographical opposition” but in the nature of Jesus’ acts. The only geographical point is that the deliverance happens outside Jerusalem.

Assessment. The answer to the objection is partial, for three reasons.

  1. The three-stage pattern is the first exodus’s before it is Isaiah’s. Exodus runs deliverance from Egypt, the way through the wilderness, then arrival. Isaiah’s new exodus is built on that pattern. So the scheme on its own cannot choose between Isaiah and Exodus [I]. Watts’s case rests on the Isaianic particulars, not on the three-part shape.
  2. The weakest stage is Watts’s first. Its chief Isaianic support for the exorcisms is Isa 49:24–25. Claim audit 2 rated that uncertain at 3:27: Mark shares no phrase with it, and Isaiah has no binding [S: audit].
  3. The survey of structural analyses shows agreement about Mark’s divisions. But that agreement is exactly what the objection grants. It shows Mark has three parts, not that the parts are Isaiah’s [I].

Effect on the hypothesis. Watts’s account of the stages agrees with what the corpus found stage by stage:

Stage Watts Corpus and audits
Deliverance Isa 49 (exorcisms); Isa 35, 29 (healings, feedings) Isa 35:5–6 at 7:32–37 is high. Isa 49 at 3:27 is uncertain
The Way Isa 42:16 Supported, Greek-led: Isa 42:16–18 ranks among the top partners of 8:22–26 and 10:46–52 in the corrected scan. But the framing phrase “on the way” is Exod 23:20’s
Arrival The Temple; the death explained Isa 56:7 and 5:1–7 are high, both turned to judgement

The strong form of the hypothesis stays open, uncertain–moderate. It now has the support of a published argument, which fills History of Interpretation, and the same weak point that argument has.

1.2 / 3.2 The blind on the way — a rediscovery

What Watts says [S: Watts, pp. 172–173, 292, 373, 246, 249–250].

  • Mark has two healings of the blind, and only two, and their position bracketing the Way section is integral to the section’s “wisdom” theme. Watts sees it as evoking Isa 42:16.
  • He reads Isaiah’s “way they did not know” as Yahweh’s unexpected means. In Isaiah that is Cyrus; in Mark it is a Messiah who must suffer.
  • Isa 35:5–6 and 29:18 lie behind the healings as such.

What this means for the audit trail. The hypothesis test presented Isa 42:16 as a lead found in that run. Watts had made the case in 1997. The lead was re-derived independently from the text, which is worth something as a check, but it is not new. Claim audit 2’s Claim 1 should carry the note “History of Interpretation: Watts, pp. 172–173, 292, 373”.

What the audits add to Watts, as far as these answers show:

  • the Hebrew/Greek split, which makes the case Greek-led;
  • ἀναβλέψατε (“look up, see again”) at Isa 42:18, Mark’s own verb;
  • Jer 31:8–9, a rival in the Hebrew;
  • the corrected contact scan;
  • Exod 23:20’s ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“on the way”), if Watts does not use it after all (see 1.3).

Agreement between auditors and Watts. Watts splits the two texts the same way claim audit 2’s auditors did: Isa 35 for the healing, Isa 42 for the way. Both auditors were blind to Watts. That agreement was reached independently, so it counts.

1.4 The ending at 16:8

What Watts says (verbatim extract).

  • Isa 40–55 carries a refrain of “fear not”. Jacob-Israel’s fear, and refusal to believe the good news, delayed the new exodus.
  • Mark ends on a fear that silences the first witnesses. That is a warning to his audience, and, with 16:7 pointing back to Galilee and the stilled sea, an encouragement.
  • He offers it as “an interesting parallel”, not as a structural proof.

The corpus.

  • The refrain is real, and Isaiah’s own.
    • In the WLC, אַל (“not”) with יָרֵא (“fear”) in one verse occurs in 13 verses of Isaiah, more than in any other book. Genesis has 8 and Jeremiah 7.
    • The Greek agrees: μή with an imperative or subjunctive of φοβέομαι (“fear”) occurs in 13 verses of Isaiah in Swete, again first. Genesis and Jeremiah have 8 each.
  • It runs through the whole book, not only chapters 40–55. The 13 Hebrew verses are 7:4; 10:24; 35:4; 37:6; 40:9; 41:10, 13, 14; 43:1, 5; 44:2; 51:7; 54:4. Four of them stand before chapter 40. One is Isa 35:4, in the chapter Mark uses at 7:32 [T]. This fits your reading of Isaiah as one book better than Watts’s confinement to 40–55.
    • Watts’s own list includes 44:8, 51:13 and 54:14. Those use other wording (“do not tremble”; “you fear”; “you will not fear”), so the lemma count leaves them out.
  • Mark’s side is thinner.
    • φοβέομαι (“fear”) is common in Mark: 12 verses.
    • At the tomb the young man says μὴ ἐκθαμβεῖσθε (“do not be alarmed”, 16:6), not Isaiah’s μὴ φοβεῖσθε (“do not fear”).
    • Mark’s own “fear not” sayings stand elsewhere: 5:36, and 6:50, where the Greek has ἐγώ εἰμι· μὴ φοβεῖσθε (“it is I; do not fear”) [T].

Assessment. It is a thematic link, uncertain–moderate. It is good for preaching, as Watts uses it, with the caution that the verbal link to 16:8 is only the common verb.

A new candidate. Mark 6:50 joins “it is I” (ἐγώ εἰμι) with “do not fear” (μὴ φοβεῖσθε). That pairing is Isaianic in shape: Isa 41:10 and 43:1–5 join “fear not” with God’s “I am with you”. This needs a baseline and an audit before it goes anywhere; see the queue below.

2.1 Why 1:2 names Isaiah

What the sources say.

  • Watts [S: Watts, pp. 85, 88–89; verbatim footnote] rejects the testimonia explanation: “no concrete evidence for Christian testimonia in this period”. He follows Dodd’s verdict that testimony books were “the result, not the presupposition” of early Christian exegesis. He then offers two answers, which he does not treat as exclusive:
    • Jewish convention of naming one author for a composite;
    • a deliberate frame, his “sandwich”: the ascription to Isaiah and the Isaiah text enclose the Exodus–Malachi lines.
  • Carson (EBC) [S: Carson] doubts that testimonia “ever existed as separate books”.
  • Atkins (Lexham Bible Dictionary) [S: Atkins] gives “naming only the most prominent source and/or signalling … the importance [of] this section of Isaiah”.

The corpus. Watts’s “sandwich” is true as a description of the verse: name (1:2a), Exodus–Malachi (1:2b), Isaiah (1:3) [T]. Whether it is a Markan intercalation is [I]. Mark’s other intercalations frame stories, not lines of a quotation.

The convention explanation would most naturally name the larger source. By the SBLGNT count the shares are level: 13 words from Exodus–Malachi, 14 from Isaiah (hypothesis test).

Assessment. Falsifier F3 is not decided, and should not be. The leading scholar of the frame reading also accepts the convention as possible. The two explanations are compatible: a convention can be used to make a point. The frame verdict of the hypothesis test stands, high. It rests on the text (the harder reading, Isaiah the only prophet named, the prologue weighting) and does not need the convention to be false.

Set aside: Moyise. The Assistant found no Moyise text on this question and answered from general knowledge. That paragraph is discarded. It named an article, Moyise, “Is Mark’s Opening Quotation the Key to His Use of Scripture?”. As far as I know this appeared in Irish Biblical Studies in 1998, but I have not verified that. It is the one source on this question worth seeking out.

2.2 Isa 52:7 behind 1:14–15

What the sources say.

  • Evans [S: Evans, Eerdmans 1068; BKBC 88–89]: “Jesus’ gospel derives from Isa. 40:9, 52:7, and 61:1.”
  • Watts [S: Watts, p. 119] gives the same group plus 41:27 and 60:6.
  • Barnett [S: Barnett, p. 34] quotes Isa 52:7.
  • Witherington [S: Witherington, p. 78] adds that 11QMelchizedek is a pesher on Isa 52:7 and makes the anointed one its herald.
  • Abernethy (NSBT) and Köstenberger–Goswell concur.

Assessment.

  • The audit’s reframing is confirmed. Claim audit 2 reframed Isa 52:7 as a “herald complex” of 40:9–10, 52:7 and 61:1, at moderate. Evans names the same three texts. History of Interpretation can now read checked, widely recognised.
  • 11QMelch is a Second Temple witness to the same reading, alongside the Psalms of Solomon 11 that audit 2 found. It is not in the corpus, so it stays [S].

A new Isaianic candidate for “has drawn near”. Witherington lists the LXX uses of ἐγγίζω (“draw near”) for the nearness of God or his judgement, and one of them is Isa 56:1. The corpus sharpens it:

  • The exact form. ἤγγικεν (“has drawn near”), Mark 1:15’s own form, occurs in only six verses of Swete: Isa 56:1; Jer 28:9 (Hebrew 51:9); Lam 4:19; Ezek 7:4; 9:1; 1 Macc 9:10.
  • Isa 56:1 is the only one of the six about salvation, not doom. It reads ἤγγικεν γὰρ τὸ σωτήριόν μου παραγίνεσθαι (“for my salvation has drawn near to come”). The Hebrew is כִּי־קְרוֹבָה יְשׁוּעָתִי (“for my salvation is near”) [T].
  • It is the chapter Mark quotes at 11:17 (Isa 56:7, high).

Claim audit 2 offered Lam 4:19 and Dan 7:22 for the “time” half of 1:15. Isa 56:1 is a better-fitting candidate for the verb, and it is unaudited. It goes to the queue.

1.5 / 3.3 The ransom saying (10:45)

The Assistant’s claim. “Isaiah 53 is closer in wording and theological substance to Mark 10:45 than Exodus 21:30 and 30:12.”

The corpus says otherwise on wording. Mark 10:45: δοῦναι τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν (“to give his life as a ransom for many”).

Text Give His life Ransom For many
Exod 21:30; 30:12 (Swete) δώσει / δώσουσιν τῆς ψυχῆς αὐτοῦ λύτρα —
Exod 21:30; 30:12 (WLC) נָתַן (“give”) נַפְשׁוֹ (“his life”) כֹּפֶר (“ransom”) —
Isa 53:10–12 (Swete, Rahlfs) δῶτε, “you give” (53:10) ἡ ψυχὴ αὐτοῦ (53:12, “handed over to death”); at 53:10 “your/our soul” — πολλοῖς, πολλῶν (53:11–12)
Isa 53:10–12 (WLC) תָּשִׂים (“make, set”) נַפְשׁוֹ (53:10) — (אָשָׁם, “guilt offering”) רַבִּים (53:11–12)

Baseline. “Give” + “ransom” + “his life” in one verse occurs in Swete only at Exod 21:30 and 30:12. A third hit, Ps 33:23, was a false friend: δούλων (“servants”) was caught as “give”. Within two verses, Ps 48:8–9 Swete (Hebrew 49:8–9) adds the same words in the negative: no one “will give to God … the price of the redemption of his soul” [T].

  • Isa 53 has no λύτρον (“ransom”), and λύτρον occurs in Isaiah only at 45:13.
  • Isa 53:12’s ἀνθ᾽ ὧν means “because”, not “in place of”, so it is no match for Mark’s ἀντί (“for, in place of”).

The Assistant’s own sources disagree with its headline.

  • Cornerstone [S: Turner–Bock, 262–263] gives Exod 30:12, Ps 49:7–9 “and especially Isaiah 53:10–12”.
  • Beale–Gladd [S] say Exodus “establishes the principle”, and Isa 53 “applies” it to a person.
  • EDNT [S: Balz–Schneider 2:365] warns that the saying “cannot be derived directly from Isaiah 53 on methodological grounds”.

Assessment.

  • The wording is Exodus’s: “give his life as a ransom”.
  • “For many” is Isaiah’s.
  • The conceptual bridge is Isa 53:10’s guilt offering of “his life”, which is in the Hebrew only.
  • Ps 49:8–9 is the foil: no man can give a ransom for his life, and the Son of Man gives his [I].

The overview’s row (Isa 53:10–12 at 10:45, moderate) should become, pending audit: Exod 21:30; 30:12 (give his life as a ransom) with Isa 53:11–12 (for many); cf. Ps 49:8–9 | 10:45 | moderate. The Assistant’s “closer in wording” is a case of the known fault: it reasons from theology and English and reports the result as wording.

Follow-up: Watts on Exodus 23:20 and the Way (9 November 2026)

Source. Two verbatim extracts from Watts that Patrick supplied after round 1 (Part C of the answers record): “OT Contexts: Exodus 23:20” and “The ‘Way’: 8:22/27–10:45/11:1”. Because these are Watts’s own words, they outweigh the Assistant’s summaries.

The conflict at 1.3 is settled against the Assistant.

  • Watts discusses Exod 23:20 at length. His own outline of Exod 23:20–33 has as its first part “1. On the way (vv. 20–21)”. He notes that the messenger’s task is “to keep Israel ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (LXX)” [S: Watts, verbatim]. So Watts saw the Greek phrase claim audit 2 relied on.
  • He reports the Exodus reading of the Way section. He cites Kelber, followed by Swartley, for the view that Mark’s ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“on the way”) draws attention to 8:27–10:52 as “the explanation of the NE ‘Way’ spoken of in 1:2f with its citation of Exodus 23:20 and repetition of ὁδός-terminology” [S: Kelber and Swartley, via Watts].
  • He does not reject that reading. His own conclusion is “wisdom/teaching and journey (quite possibly with Exodus/NE imagery)”. His controlling text for the healings of the blind stays Isa 42:16.
  • So the Assistant’s answer was wrong, and the Part A summary was right. The Assistant said Watts “does not appeal to Exodus 23:20 for understanding the ‘way’ itself”. Watts does not make Exod 23:20 his controlling text, but he reports and half-accepts the Exodus reading. The answer is another case of the Assistant’s known fault: a confident negative from a partial reading.

A second rediscovery. Claim audit 2’s Claim 5 made two findings that the literature already had:

  • “On the way” in the Way section is the continuation of Exod 23:20. This is Kelber’s link, reported by Swartley and by Watts. The audit did not know it.
  • Matthew and Luke drop the phrase at Mark’s frames. Swartley had this too, as Watts reports him:
    • seven uses of ὁδός (“way”) in the Way section (8:27; 9:33, 34; 10:17, 32, 46, 52);
    • in Matthew only two parallels (20:17; 20:30), in Luke only one (18:35);
    • outside the section, five of Mark’s uses are paralleled in both Matthew and Luke.
    Those seven verses are the same seven the SBLGNT count gives. Swartley’s parallels are the same as Auditor B’s once 10:46 is restored, which B said it should be [T].

What the audits still add. As far as these extracts show:

  • the field baseline: 28 of 109 Mark words of similar frequency cluster as strongly somewhere, so concentration alone is not design;
  • the control at other parallels: 14 of 16 kept (Auditor B’s figure);
  • the observation that Mark’s quotation at 1:2 stops before Exodus’s “to guard you on the way”. This is still [I] and untested, and nothing in the extracts mentions it.

The case against, which Watts reports and answers. Watts reports Gundry’s “root and branch attack” on a cohesive Way section [S: Gundry, via Watts]:

  • ὁδός means only “the road on which an event takes place”;
  • the way of 1:2–3 (repentance) is not the way of 8:27–10:52 (the cross);
  • Jerusalem is not named as the goal until 10:1;
  • crowds are often present;
  • the triads are only a quarter of the section;
  • the disciples’ blindness is mentioned only at 8:18, outside the section;
  • the disciples never come to see.

Watts concedes the last point and the point about crowds. His main reply is from the text: ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“on the way”) is used of Jesus travelling with his disciples only in this section.

The corpus check of Watts’s reply.

  • ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ stands in Mark at 8:3, 8:27, 9:33, 9:34, 10:32 and 10:52 (SBLGNT).
  • 8:3 is the crowd fainting “on the way” home, not Jesus’ journey. So Watts’s claim holds: every use of the phrase for Jesus’ journey with the disciples falls inside 8:27–10:52 [T].
  • Watts’s “10:32, 34” must be a slip, perhaps for 9:34: 10:34 has no ὁδός.
  • Gundry’s point about 8:18 is also correct: “having eyes, do you not see?” stands before 8:22 [T].

Effect.

  • Claim audit 2, Claim 5. Parts (b) and (c) keep their verdicts, and History of Interpretation now reads Swartley; Kelber; Watts. Part (d), Isaiah’s way specifically, stays uncertain–moderate. Now the published debate supports it too:
    • Watts and Marcus read the way as Isaiah’s;
    • Kelber and Swartley read it as Exodus’s;
    • Gundry denies a special way at all.
  • The hypothesis test’s architecture verdict stands: open. Gundry’s objections are the strongest form of the “it’s just the plot” objection. Watts answers the structural ones, but concedes that the disciples never see.

Critical Assessment: Gaps in This Assessment

  1. Most of Watts here comes through the Assistant. Only the 16:8 section and the testimonia footnote are his own words. Every other “Watts says” rests on the Assistant’s summary of the pages it cites. They are checkable, but not yet checked.
  2. The Part A summaries look generated. On the one point that could be checked, Exod 23:20, the summary proved right and the Assistant wrong. Even so, a summary is not the author’s text, and the other summary points (for example, Watts on Isa 63 at 1:10) still need Watts’s own words.
  3. Agreement with Watts is not proof. That Watts and the blind auditors agree on Isa 42 for the way and Isa 35 for the healing shows the reading is natural. It does not show that Mark intended it.
  4. The ransom table compares words, not intentions. A writer can draw on Isa 53’s ideas in Exodus’s vocabulary. The table shows where the wording comes from, and no more.
  5. The new candidates were found by the assessor in one sitting — the pattern the audits have repeatedly cut down. They are queued, not adopted.
