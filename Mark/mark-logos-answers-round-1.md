Watts’ analysis of the proposals form a ‘representative survey of 61 analyses of Markan structure revealed a considerable degree of uniformity in spite of the major structural units envisaged… there is substantial agreement as to the major Markan literary divisions’. ‘This suggests that Mark’s basic outline consists of three major sections: 1:14/16-8:21/27; 8:21/27-10:45/11:1 (The ‘Way’); and 10:45/11:1-16:8.’[^1]

Watts suggests that the prologue of Mark reveals Isaianic New Exodus themes:

A\) Mark's use of εὐαγγέλιον aligns with Isaiah's announcement of comfort (Is 40:9; 41:27; 52:7; 60:6; 61:1), suggesting a Palestinian origin rather than Hellenistic. Jesus embodies the 'messenger' tradition of Isaiah 61, proclaiming and effecting the New Exodus.

B\) The rending of the heavens and Spirit's descent (1:10) reflects Isaiah's lament over the delay of the New Exodus (Is 63:7-64:12), portraying Jesus as Israel's representative.

C\) Jesus is identified as the 'servant' of Isaiah 42:1, destined to deliver captives and restore sight, while also being the messianic heir of David.

D\) His preaching of fulfilled time includes a call to repentance (1:15), echoing Malachi's warning.

Watts discusses Mark's use of Isaiah 40 as a foundational framework for understanding his Gospel, emphasising the need for leaders and people to respond positively to the forerunner to avoid curses. The prologue is filled with textual icons from Israel's history, reinforcing Mark's narrative of Yahweh's intervention. Mark introduces the concept of the Gospel with the phrase Ἀρχη τοῦ εὐαγγελίου, linking it to the hope of Yahweh's coming. John the Baptist is portrayed as Malachi's forerunner, preparing the way for Jesus. The baptism scene signifies Yahweh's intervention, with Jesus depicted as both the Isaianic servant and David's messianic son. The prologue establishes a consistent interpretive framework for Mark's Gospel, suggesting that the themes introduced will influence the entire narrative.

Watts discusses the thematic connections between the Gospel of Mark and the Isaianic New Exodus framework, particularly focusing on the significance of the term ὁδός (way) in Mark 1:2.

A\) YHWH’s deliverance of His exiled people from the power of the nations and their idols.

B\) The journey along the ‘Way’ in which YHWH leads His people from their captivity among the nations.

\(C\) Arrival in Jerusalem, the place of His presence, where YHWH is enthroned in a gloriously restored Zion.

Scholars suggest that the term ‘Way’ plays a programmatic role in Mark's narrative, aligning with the prophetic texts of Exodus and Malachi, which emphasise Jerusalem and the Temple. The text highlights Jesus' actions, such as the cursing of the fig tree and the cleansing of the Temple, as fulfilling prophetic threats and indicating judgment. Mark's narrative structure reflects the Isaianic New Exodus schema, which includes Yahweh's deliverance, the journey along the 'Way', and the arrival in Jerusalem. The text argues that Mark's portrayal of Jesus' ministry is not merely geographical but focuses on the nature of Jesus' actions in relation to Yahweh's deliverance from the nations and idols. The expectation is set for exploring correlations between the Isaianic New Exodus schema and Mark's Gospel activities.

V. The Short Ending of Mark

The sudden and almost anti-climactic ending of Mark continues to be a source of fascination and has spawned a considerable literature. Although we do not intend entering this debate in any detail, we will nevertheless briefly mention an interesting parallel with the message of *Isaiah 40–55*.

Generally *Isaiah 40–55* is seen as announcing Israel’s deliverance from exile, and this is borne out by the prologue and the content of the various oracles, proclamations, and hymns which announce, detail, and celebrate the nation’s imminent salvation.[^2] But, as we have argued elsewhere, there is also an on-going and increasingly polarised altercation between Yahweh and Jacob-Israel concerning his power, his faithfulness, and his wisdom.[^3] One of the issues underlying this tension seems to be Jacob-Israel’s fear as is evident in the constant refrain of ‘fear not’ as Yahweh encourages his people to trust him and to accept that their salvation is near ([*Isa 40:9*](https://ref.ly/Is40.9); [*41:10*](https://ref.ly/Is41.10), [*13*](https://ref.ly/Is41.13), [*14*](https://ref.ly/Is41.14); [*43:1*](https://ref.ly/Is43.1), [*5*](https://ref.ly/Is43.5); [*44:2*](https://ref.ly/Is44.2); [*44:8*](https://ref.ly/Is44.8); cf. [*51:7*](https://ref.ly/Is51.7), [*13*](https://ref.ly/Is51.13); [*54:4*](https://ref.ly/Is54.4), [*14*](https://ref.ly/Is54.14)).<sup>376</sup>[^4] We also argued that it was ultimately because of Jacob-Israel’s ‘fear’ and refusal to accept, to believe, and to act on the ‘good news’ of the prophet’s message that the Isaianic New Exodus (INE) was delayed.

Apparently not much had changed in Malachi’s day nor, if our reading is correct, in that of the Markan Jesus. If we are right in seeing Mark’s Jesus as the one who inaugurates the long-awaited INE it is perhaps not surprising, as Chris Marshall has shown, that the question of ‘faith and unbelief’ is a central issue for Mark.[^5] It figures prominently in Jesus’ opening invitation to repent and believe,[^6] and also in the unbelief and lack of repentance that characterises Israel’s leadership.[^7] The disciples, too, respond in a mixture of ‘faith’ and ‘fear’.[^8] In commenting on the latter in *Mark 4:34* Marshall observes that ‘when believers fail to act in faith, they are not merely “small in faith”; they are actually succumbing to the power of the γενέα ἄπιστος that stands opposed to God’s rule’.[^9] Nevertheless, their unbelief does not prevent Jesus exercising his authority, even in the face of what appears to be a demonically inspired onslaught. Jesus calms the fearfully chaotic sea just as he heals the demonised lad.

If we are on the right track, this may explain why Mark’s gospel concludes rather abruptly with a statement about a ‘fear’ that results in silence. At the outset of the INE’s announcement, there is the urgent command to proclaim, to not hold back, to not be afraid in announcing the ‘good news’ ([*Isa 40:9*](https://ref.ly/Is40.9); cf. [*52:7–9*](https://ref.ly/Is52.7-9)). But in the end, it was ‘fear’ that threatened the full realisation of Yahweh’s promised deliverance. Perhaps Mark, recalling that these first witnesses of true Israel’s ‘resurrection’ where silenced by their fear, also has in mind the possibility that members of his audience might also be tempted, out of fear, to be silent, to succumb to the unbelief of the ‘faithless generation’. On the other hand, Mark has already explained that it was in Galilee (cf. [*16:7*](https://ref.ly/Mk16.7)) that Jesus prevailed over the power of the watery deep ([*4:35ff*](https://ref.ly/Mk4.35ff)), with all of its deathly connotations, and this in spite of the fear of the disciples. Perhaps, then, in addition to the warning, there is also a word of encouragement to his audience. Even if they too have been tempted to be silent, they need to recognise that Jesus has still conquered. And if, for Mark, ‘discipleship evidently involves a continuing struggle for the victory of faith over unbelief’[^10] (and we would add ‘fear’), then maybe being reminded of Jesus’ great victory will provide the added strength and encouragement they need to proclaim the gospel in the face of hostility and rejection.

1.  **Isa 52:7 and Mark 1:14–15.** Is the link recognised in the commentaries? It is common in the literature as far as I know, which is unverified.

There has been considerable controversy over how to render the verb ηγγικεν. The Aramaic equivalent is likely to be *qrb*. Despite the repeated arguments of C. H. Dodd, ηγγικεν normally refers to the approach or drawing near of something in time or space.<sup>33</sup>[^15] Notice how in the LXX this verb is regularly used when the nearness of God or of his judgment is the subject (Deut. 4:7; Isa. 41:21; 48:16; 51:5; 56:1; Jer. 23:23). It is thus unlikely that we should translate the text here “the dominion of God has come,” as Dodd argued. This does not mean that the reference is to something in the remote future. Rather, as J. Schlosser suggests, the reference is to something imminent or near.<sup>34</sup>[^16] In view of the use of this verb in Rom. 13:12, James 5:8, and 1 Pet. 4:7 (cf. Luke 21:18, 20), a translation such as “has drawn near” best captures the sense here. This is especially likely if this verse is no more than a summary of Jesus’ early preaching, when there still seems to have been influence by the Baptist. 11QMelch (especially lines 15ff., which offer a pesher on Isa. 52:7), which suggests that it is the task of God’s anointed one or *mashiach* to bring good news about God’s impending action for his people, may be of relevance here.<sup>35</sup>[^17] It may be suggested that Jesus could say the dominion of God was near precisely because he was near to those hearing this message. In short, Jesus saw the reign of God as being manifested in his life, and when others came under that reign there began to be a realm, a community where the dominion was manifest. An analogy with Mark 13:28 is perhaps apt.[^18]

The word ‘gospel’ was used by Gentile rulers to proclaim important and good news (see comment on 1:1). Gentile readers would have immediately recognised the word and, indeed, the point Mark was making, that Jesus was an emperor-like figure. But for his part Jesus chose the word ‘gospel’ because of its very pointed use in the Old Testament, in particular by the prophet Isaiah who promised a new exodus to the Jews exiled in Babylon:

How beautiful on the mountains  
are the feet of those who bring good news \[= gospel\],  
who proclaim peace,  
who bring good tidings,  
who proclaim salvation,  
who say to Zion,  
‘Your God reigns!’ (Isaiah 52:7)

Jesus was the proclaimer par excellence who brought the good news, or gospel, to Israel that her God reigns, that the kingdom of God had drawn near. Jesus was not teaching the general truth that God is king over history, but rather that the time has come for God to fix up the world, as the prophets had said he would. Zechariah, for instance, had prophesied: ‘The LORD will be king over the whole earth. On that day there will be one LORD, and his name the only name’ (Zechariah 14:9).[^19]

Jesus’ gospel derives from Isa. 40:9, 52:7, and 61:1. The underlying Hebrew word is basar, which means “good news.” In Greek the word is euaggelion (pronounced euangelion), from which we derive the obsolete evangel; the verb is euaggelizesthai (pronounced euangelizesthai), from which we derive evangelize. Whereas the Markan evangelist exploited the fact that “good news” conveyed as much significance to the Roman world as it did to the Jewish world (see comment on Mark 1:1; Matt. 1:1; Luke 1:1–4), there is little doubt that it is the meaning that gospel has in Isaiah that lies behind Jesus’ proclamation of the kingdom of God. Passages from Isaiah contributed to the substance and manner of Jesus’ preaching and teaching (cf. Matt. 11:2–6 = Luke 7:18–23; Luke 4:16–30).[^20]

## Part B — Research Assistant Answers

**Logos Questions 2**


**\> How exactly does Watts divide Mark, and does he map the divisions onto Isa 40:1–11 alone or onto the whole of Isa 40–55?**


Watts structures Mark’s Gospel around three movements that correspond to Isaiah’s New Exodus schema: deliverance from captivity (mapped to Jesus’ ministry in Galilee and beyond, 1:16–8:21/26), a journey along the ‘Way’ (8:22/27–10:45/52), and arrival in Jerusalem (10:46/11:1–16:18).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F605259)

Regarding the scope of Isaiah’s text, Watts draws on the broader New Exodus framework rather than confining his analysis to [Isaiah 40:1–11](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40.1-23.40.11) alone. While Mark’s emphasis on Isaiah’s New Exodus appears both in his introductory citation and throughout the prologue, the three-stage schema Watts identifies—deliverance, journey, and arrival—encompasses the fuller New Exodus vision that extends through [Isaiah 40–55](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40-23.55).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F604972) The three-fold structure echoes the New Exodus schema where Yahweh as Warrior and Healer delivers his people from bondage, leads the ‘blind’ along the New Exodus way of deliverance, and arrives at Jerusalem, with Jesus’ exorcisms linked to [Isaiah 49](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.49) and his healings and feedings to [Isaiah 35](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.35) and 29.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F360704)

The mapping is therefore not restricted to the opening chapter of Isaiah’s consolation but draws on multiple passages within the broader New Exodus complex, allowing Watts to demonstrate thematic coherence across Mark’s entire narrative structure through the lens of Isaiah’s extended vision of restoration.

**\> Does Watts answer the objection that Galilee, journey, Jerusalem is simply the plot?**


Yes, Watts does address this objection directly. Rather than treating the three divisions as a simple geographical progression from Galilee to Jerusalem, Watts emphasizes that the correspondence with Isaiah’s New Exodus schema is not based on straightforward national-geographical equivalence or a purely geographical opposition between receptive Galilee and resistant Jerusalem. Instead, the point of contact lies in the *nature* of Jesus’ actions—specifically his deliverance of people from spiritual powers, which parallels Yahweh’s deliverance from the powers of the nations and their idols.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F605860)

Watts further clarifies that the only geographical consideration is that this deliverance happens outside Jerusalem, as in Isaiah’s New Exodus, and that Jesus’ delivering actions extend beyond Galilee alone—consistent with an Isaianic Yahweh-Warrior working among a Jewish diaspora.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F606326) This distinction is crucial: the three-part structure reflects not merely Mark’s narrative plot but rather the *theological content* of each section—the type of redemptive work Jesus performs and how it corresponds to Isaiah’s schema of deliverance, journey, and arrival.

Watts supports this by noting that a survey of diverse literary analyses reveals two major narrative breaks that suggest Mark intentionally uses a three-part macro-structure, and that these sections display “broad parallels” with the New Exodus schema—implying deliberate theological architecture rather than incidental narrative convenience.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1030261) The argument, then, is that while the plot *happens* to move from Galilee to Jerusalem, the structure’s significance derives from its alignment with Isaiah’s redemptive pattern.

**\> Does Watts use Isa 42:16 or Isa 35 for the healings at 8:22–26 and 10:46–52?**


Watts uses [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16) for the blind healings at 8:22–26 and 10:46–52, arguing that these miracles evoke the Isaianic picture of Yahweh leading the blind along a path they do not know.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1035245) 

However, the search results also indicate that commentators widely recognize that Mark’s healing of the blind (8:22–26; 10:46–52) primarily reflects [Isaiah 35:5f](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.35.5$2B)[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F665404), alongside [Isaiah 29:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.29.18). The distinction Watts makes is that while these healings draw on the broader healing imagery of [Isaiah 35](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.35), their *placement* and *function* within Mark’s narrative structure—bracketing the “Way” section—deliberately invokes the wisdom theme of [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16), where Yahweh leads the spiritually blind along an unknown path.

Watts explains Mark’s inclusion of only two healings of the blind, positioned as they are, as integral to the wisdom themes of his ‘Way’ section, specifically evoking [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F875644) So Watts is not choosing between [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16) and [Isaiah 35](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.35); rather, he recognizes that both passages inform the healings, but he emphasizes [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16) as the controlling text for understanding why these particular miracles frame the disciples’ journey toward Jerusalem—a journey fundamentally about learning to accept Jesus’ unconventional path to messianic deliverance.

**\> How does Watts account for 16:8, which ends in fear and silence, against an Isaianic arrival in Zion?**


Watts accounts for Mark’s enigmatic ending—where the women tell no one and are seized with fear—as serving both a reminder of how fear had once hindered the New Exodus and as a warning against allowing it to do so again.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1024189)

This is a clever move that transforms the apparent tension between Mark’s abrupt conclusion and Isaiah’s triumphant vision of restoration. Rather than treating the ending as a failure of the Isaianic schema, Watts locates it within the New Exodus framework itself. Although Isaiah’s New Exodus reaches its culmination in the joyful reception and enthronement of Yahweh in a gloriously restored Jerusalem, Mark’s story concludes somewhat differently.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F880433) The difference, however, is not a departure from the Isaianic pattern but a recalibration of it.

Watts recognizes that fear functioned as an obstacle within Isaiah’s own New Exodus narrative—a historical reality that prevented the people from embracing restoration. By ending with fear and silence rather than celebration, Mark echoes this Isaianic pattern: the New Exodus has been inaugurated through Jesus’ death and resurrection, yet its full realization depends on the community’s willingness to overcome fear and proclaim it. The ending thus becomes not a contradiction of the Isaianic arrival but a challenge to readers to complete what the women failed to do—to embrace the New Exodus without allowing fear to paralyze testimony.

This reading preserves Watts’s overarching argument: the promise and warning inherent in Mark’s opening citation are brought to their climaxes, with Mark’s account of Jesus’ entry, his declaration of what the Temple should become, and his explanation of his death all at home within the framework of New Exodus fulfillment.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F882399) The ending extends rather than undermines this framework.

\[1\] Rikki E. Watts, *[Isaiah’s New Exodus and Mark*](https://ref.ly/res/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23:03:58Z/880433?len=194), ed. Martin Hengel and Otfried Hofius, Wissenschaftliche Untersuchungen Zum Neuen Testament (Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 1997), [88:295–296](https://ref.ly/res/LLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z/880433?len=2427), [88:368](https://ref.ly/res/LLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z/1024189?len=216).


  
**\> Does Watts discuss the testimonia hypothesis, or a convention of naming one prophet for a composite?**


Watts directly engages the testimonia hypothesis, noting that scholars commonly propose Mark uncritically adopted a pre-existing testimonium wrongly attributed to Isaiah, or made a simple mistake.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F527248) However, he argues against this view on three grounds: there is no concrete evidence for Christian testimonia in this period, Mark himself demonstrates familiarity with combined texts, and Mark appears aware of the significance of scriptural citations, placing them strategically throughout his narrative.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F527666)

Rather than dismissing the problem, Watts proposes two solutions. First, he suggests Mark may be following common Jewish practice in naming only one author with composite quotations.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F528146) This convention—naming a single prophet for a composite citation—would resolve the apparent discrepancy without requiring error or uncritical dependence.

More innovatively, Watts proposes that Mark employs his characteristic “sandwich” construction in the opening citation itself, with [Isaiah 40:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40.3) and its surrounding Isaiah material framing the inserted Exodus/Malachi conflation as an interpretative gloss.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F528252) This structure suggests that Mark’s “blanket” ascription to Isaiah reflects his larger theological framework, particularly if the sandwich structure is intentional.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F528949)

Watts acknowledges that while the insertion of the Malachi/Exodus combination between the Isaiah ascription and Isaiah text appears unprecedented, Mark’s characteristic synthesis method and the existence of testimonia collections make it difficult to determine whether this particular amalgamation originates with Mark or derives from earlier sources.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F522537) The key point is that Watts treats the composite citation not as evidence of carelessness but as a deliberate hermeneutical strategy reflecting either Jewish convention or Mark’s own interpretative technique.

\[1\] Rikki E. Watts, *[Isaiah’s New Exodus and Mark*](https://ref.ly/res/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23:03:58Z/522537?len=567), ed. Martin Hengel and Otfried Hofius, Wissenschaftliche Untersuchungen Zum Neuen Testament (Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 1997), [88:85](https://ref.ly/res/LLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z/522537?len=567), [88:88–89](https://ref.ly/res/LLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS/2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z/527248?len=1955).

**\> Does any commentary discuss the testimonia hypothesis, or a convention of naming one prophet for a composite?**


Yes, the search results discuss both the testimonia hypothesis and the convention of naming one prophet for composite citations.

[Mark 1:2–3](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.2-62.1.3) combines material from [Malachi 3:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.39.3.1), [Isaiah 40:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40.3), and [Exodus 23:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.23.20) yet attributes the entire quotation to Isaiah. Mark may be following standard Jewish practice of naming only the most prominent source, and/or signaling to the reader the importance that this section of Isaiah will have for the rest of his narrative.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBD?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBD%2F2025-04-29T00%3A09%3A20Z%2F18173272)

Regarding the testimonia hypothesis, composite quotations in Mark and other New Testament texts may not have been drawn directly from individual Old Testament books but from testimonia—collections of brief excerpts culled from various Old Testament passages and grouped by theme.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBD?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBD%2F2025-04-29T00%3A09%3A20Z%2F18173731) However, although no examples of testimonia that were actually used by New Testament writers are extant today, collections are known to have existed both before and after the New Testament period.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBD?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBD%2F2025-04-29T00%3A09%3A20Z%2F18174212)

Such “testimonies” were primarily thematic combinations for instructional and apologetic purposes and some may have circulated in written form during the apostolic period. However, the hypothesis that they were collected in a precanonical “testimony book” used by the Church in anti-Jewish apologetic is less likely.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NTINTERPRETATION?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANTINTERPRETATION%2F2014-10-08T02%3A47%3A35Z%2F661843)

Additionally, an introduction to a citation is not usually indicative of its source, nor does it mean that the citation has borrowed from elsewhere. Therefore, it requires an audience to identify those texts and contexts.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:THEMELIOS43$5F2?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATHEMELIOS43_2%2F2025-10-13T15%3A34%3A38Z%2F395425)

**\> Is the link between Isa 52:7 and Mark 1:14–15 recognised in the commentaries?**


Yes, the link between [Isaiah 52:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52.7) and [Mark 1:14–15](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.14-62.1.15) is recognized across multiple commentaries.

[Isaiah 40](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40), [52](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52), and 61 contributed to the substance of Jesus’ preaching, with [Isaiah 52:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52.7) promising the good news of God’s reign (“Your God reigns!”), which Jesus now proclaims in [Mark 1:15](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.15) (“the kingdom of God has come near”).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EERDMANSCOMMBBL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEERDMANSCOMMBBL%2F2014-10-03T22%3A55%3A27Z%2F6588346) Jesus’ use of the Isaianic tradition, especially 52:7 and 61:1–2, provides strong evidence that the proclamation of the kingdom of God as “good news” originated with Jesus and became standard in the Christian community.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EERDMANSCOMMBBL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEERDMANSCOMMBBL%2F2014-10-03T22%3A55%3A27Z%2F6589435)

[Isaiah 52:7–10](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52.7-23.52.10) bears witness to Christ, with Mark’s Gospel drawing upon the kingdom and good news language from 52:7 in the proclamation “the kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news” ([Mark 1:15](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.15)).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NSBT40?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANSBT40%2F2016-08-27T01%3A11%3A10Z%2F219573)

Mark announces “good news” (euangelion) from the outset, which in Isaiah is bound up with the Lord’s visitation of Zion and his rule ([Isaiah 52:7–8](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52.7-23.52.8)).[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BBLCLTHTHCPPRCH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABBLCLTHTHCPPRCH%2F2023-03-03T14%3A53%3A53Z%2F1894619)

Mark’s use of euangelion appears best understood in terms of the Isaianic announcement of comfort, namely the proclamation of Yahweh’s New Exodus coming to his people, drawing on [Isaiah 40:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.40.9), [41:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.41.27), [52:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.52.7), [60:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.60.6), and 61:1.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F574347)

The commentaries thus converge on recognizing that Jesus’ proclamation in [Mark 1:14–15](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.1.14-62.1.15) deliberately echoes and fulfills the Isaianic promise of good news centered on God’s kingdom and reign.

**\> Does Watts use Isa 42:16–18 or Exod 23:20 for the “way”?**


Watts uses [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16) for the “way” section, arguing that the two blind healings bracketing Mark’s journey narrative evoke the Isaianic picture of Yahweh leading the blind along an unknown path.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1035245) He does not appeal to [Exodus 23:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.23.20) for understanding the “way” itself.

Watts interprets the phrases in [Isaiah 42:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.42.16) describing an unknown way as referring to the unexpected means by which Yahweh accomplishes the New Exodus—specifically his scandalous choice of the pagan Cyrus as Israel’s deliverer.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F789011) Israel is “blind” and does not “know” this way because she refuses to accept Yahweh’s wisdom expressed in his choice of Cyrus.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F789348)

In Mark’s parallel structure, the “Way” to Jerusalem involves both a spatial component and a sapiential one—accepting that the Messiah must suffer. The disciples’ blindness and deafness language invokes Isaiah’s wisdom polemic, which concerns Jacob-Israel’s inability to accept the unconventional nature of Yahweh’s plan for deliverance.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F1034739) Just as Yahweh leads the blind along an unexpected path in Isaiah, so in Mark Jesus takes the initiative in calling disciples to follow him along a path they do not understand.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ISAIAHSNEWEXODUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3AISAIAHSNEWEXODUS%2F2024-01-08T23%3A03%3A58Z%2F795993)

The [Exodus 23:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.23.20) allusion appears in Mark’s opening composite citation but does not function as the primary interpretive lens for the “way” section itself.

**\> Which is closer in wording to Mark 10:45: Exod 21:30 and 30:12, “a ransom for one’s life”, or Isa 53.**


[Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53) is closer in wording and theological substance to [Mark 10:45](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.10.45) than [Exodus 21:30](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.21.30) and 30:12, though the commentaries recognize that all three texts inform the saying.

[Exodus 30:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.30.12) uses the term “ransom” in connection with a payment for life, establishing the principle that “payment or ‘ransom’ is symbolically exchanged for a ‘life.’”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781430091790?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781430091790%2F2026-05-13T16%3A28%3A37Z%2F23413) However, this represents a more limited conceptual parallel—a concrete financial transaction within Israel’s religious system.

[Isaiah 53:10–12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53.10-23.53.12), by contrast, “applies the principle of a ransom payment and sacrificial imagery to a person, an individual representing God’s people who suffers for their salvation,” prophesying that “this individual would suffer on behalf of the ‘many,’ and his ‘life’ would secure their redemption.”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781430091790?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781430091790%2F2026-05-13T16%3A28%3A37Z%2F23918) This captures the vicarious, redemptive dimension of Mark’s saying.

The concept of ransom in [Mark 10:45](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.62.10.45) probably draws on [Exodus 30:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.2.30.12), [Psalm 49:7–9](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.19.49.7-19.49.9), and especially [Isaiah 53:10–12](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53.10-23.53.12).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM61MT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM61MT%2F2009-06-10T20%3A49%3A37Z%2F822151) Yet even though the interpretation of the ransom saying cannot be derived directly from [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53) on methodological grounds, the undeniable echoes of the Old Testament text indicate the importance of [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53) for understanding the saying.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:46.10.26?pos=res%2FLLS%3A46.10.26%2F2019-07-08T21%3A11%3A47Z%2F4636960)

The distinction is that Exodus provides the vocabulary and basic concept, while [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.53) supplies the theological framework—the suffering servant who gives his life as an atoning sacrifice for the many. Mark’s formulation echoes Isaiah’s language of substitutionary suffering more directly than it echoes the Exodus census payment.
