Question 1: Ephphatha and the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 (Mark 7:34–35)

The claim being tested. In the Hebrew of Isa 35:5 the ears of the deaf are opened: וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה. The Greek has “will hear” (ἀκούσονται). Mark has Εφφαθα … Διανοίχθητι (“be opened”, 7:34) and “his ears were opened” (7:35).

If the link holds, it is a seventh place where Mark stands nearer the Hebrew than the Greek. The link to Isa 35:5–6 itself is already rated high, because μογιλάλος (“speaking with difficulty”) occurs only at Isa 35:6 in the Greek OT and at Mark 7:32 in the NT. What is new is the “opened” point.

1a. Scope: Mark commentaries, then OT-in-NT.

  • Ask: Which commentators on Mark 7:31–37 connect “Ephphatha, that is, be opened” and “his ears were opened” (7:34–35) with Isaiah 35:5? Do any of them point out that the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 says the ears of the deaf will be “opened”, where the Septuagint says they “will hear”?
  • Why: The audit found the Hebrew point. If no commentator has made it, it is a genuine finding of this work. If one has, it is a rediscovery and should be credited, as Watts was for Isa 42:16.
  • Watch for:
    • an answer about μογιλάλος only, which is already settled;
    • an answer that quotes the English of Isa 35:5 (“unstopped”, “opened”) and treats that as the Hebrew. Check which language the commentator actually argues from.

1b. Scope: Watts.

  • Ask: Does Watts discuss Mark 7:31–37 and Isaiah 35:5–6, and does he comment on the Hebrew or Greek wording of Isaiah 35:5?
  • Why: Watts links the healings to Isa 35 (round 1). Whether he argues it from the Hebrew decides how much the audit’s point adds.
  • Watch for: a general statement that “the healings echo Isaiah 35” with no verse or language given.

1c. No AI credits. In France (NIGTC) and Williams (EGGNT), read the comment on Mark 7:34 for ἐφφαθα and διανοίχθητι. Note whether either mentions the Hebrew פָּתַח (“open”) of Isa 35:5.

Question 2: The strong man bound (Mark 3:27)

The claim being tested. Mark 3:27: “no one can enter the strong man’s house and plunder his goods unless he first binds the strong man”. Two Isaiah texts have been proposed:

  • Isa 49:24–25: prey taken from the mighty man;
  • Isa 53:12: the Servant “will divide the spoils of the strong”.

Claim audit 2 rated both uncertain, for three reasons:

  • Mark shares no phrase with either text;
  • Isaiah has no binding;
  • Mark’s “goods” (σκεύη) is not Isa 53:12’s “spoils” (σκῦλα). Luke 11:22 has “spoils” and so is closer to Isa 53:12.

The binding itself is found in Tob 8:3, where Raphael binds the demon, and 1 Enoch 10:4 (“Bind Azazel”).

2a. Scope: Watts.

  • Ask: How does Watts connect Jesus’ exorcisms, and the saying about binding the strong man in Mark 3:27, with Isaiah 49:24–25? What wording does he point to, and does he discuss Isaiah 53:12 or the binding traditions of Tobit and 1 Enoch?
  • Why: Round 1 found that Watts ties the exorcisms to Isa 49, and this is the first of his three stages. It is the weakest stage on the audit’s evidence, so his own argument for it matters most.
  • Watch for:
    • an argument from the theme of rescue from the mighty, offered as if it were shared wording;
    • “the stronger one” (1:7) and “the strong man” (3:27) used as evidence for Isaiah. ἰσχυρός (“strong”) is Mark’s own word in both places.

2b. Scope: Mark commentaries.

  • Ask: What background do commentators give for binding the strong man and plundering his house in Mark 3:27? Do they cite Isaiah 49:24–25, Isaiah 53:12, Tobit 8:3, 1 Enoch 10:4, or the Testament of Levi 18:12? Which do they think most likely?
  • Why: The audit could not choose between Isaiah (the plundering) and the binding traditions (the binding). The commentaries may show whether anyone has weighed them against each other.
  • Watch for:
    • Isa 53:12 argued from Luke’s wording (“divides his spoils”) rather than Mark’s;
    • Testament of Levi 18:12, which is not in the corpus. Note it as [S].

Question 3: The torn heavens (Mark 1:10)

The claim being tested. At Mark 1:10 the heavens are “torn open” (σχιζομένους). The overview links this to Isa 63:19 (English 64:1), “Oh that you would rend the heavens and come down”, through the Hebrew only:

  • The Hebrew has קָרַעְתָּ (“you tore”).
  • Both Swete and Rahlfs have ἀνοίξῃς (“you open”).
  • No form of σχίζω (“tear”) is used of heaven anywhere in the Greek OT.

Claim audit 1 rejected a wider match with Isa 63:11–14. Swete’s apparatus has since shown that the first hand of Vaticanus reads “out of the sea” at 63:11, with the Hebrew, where every other codex reads “land”. The round-1 summary said Watts reads 1:10 against Isa 63:7–64:12, but that was a summary, not Watts’s own words.

3a. Scope: Watts.

  • Ask: What does Watts say about the tearing of the heavens and the descent of the Spirit in Mark 1:10, and how does he relate them to Isaiah 63:7–64:12? Does he discuss the Hebrew “rend” against the Greek “open” at Isaiah 63:19 (64:1), or Isaiah 63:11–14 (“brought them up out of the sea … put his Holy Spirit among them”)?
  • Why: This verifies the round-1 summary in Watts’s own terms. It is also the passage where Isaiah’s new-exodus argument “ends in a question” (63:11) in your own Isaiah sweep, so how Watts reads it bears on the whole hypothesis.
  • Watch for:
    • a reading of the whole prayer as background, with no verse-level wording;
    • “sea” at 63:11 treated as the Greek reading. In the Greek it is the first hand of Vaticanus only.
  • No AI credits: search Watts for “63:19” and “64:1” and export the pages it returns. That is more reliable than the Assistant’s summary.

3b. Scope: Mark commentaries, then OT-in-NT.

  • Ask: Which commentators connect “the heavens torn open” in Mark 1:10 with Isaiah 64:1 (Hebrew 63:19), “Oh that you would rend the heavens”, and do any note that the Septuagint there has “open” rather than “tear”? Do any connect σχίζω (“tear”) in 1:10 with the tearing of the temple curtain in 15:38?
  • Why: This fills the History of Interpretation criterion for a row the overview rates moderate. The 15:38 question is asked because the overview treats 1:10 and 15:38 as a bracket. That bracket is internal to Mark, but commentators who see it may also say what they make of the Isaiah link.
  • Watch for: the torn curtain (15:38) offered as proof of the Isaiah source. It is evidence of Mark’s own design, not of where 1:10 comes from.

  2. 3a, with its credit-free search, then 3b.

  4. 1b last. It matters only if 1a finds nothing.
