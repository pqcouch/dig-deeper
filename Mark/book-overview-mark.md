## The Greek Text — What Is Being Read

The overview reads Mark from the Greek. It reads it whole, in the SBLGNT, before any English was opened. Every count below is made by lemma against the MorphGNT index of that edition. Four textual decisions affect what "Mark" is, and each is stated here once so that the rest of the document can rely on it.

**1. The ending.** The SBLGNT and NA28 both end the text at 16:8, ἐφοβοῦντο γάρ ("for they were afraid"). Both print the "shorter ending" and 16:9–20 inside double brackets ⟦ ⟧, which marks them as later additions `[T]`. The NA28 apparatus gives the evidence:

- **Omit both endings:** א B 304, the Sinaitic Syriac, one Sahidic manuscript and Armenian manuscripts, with Eusebius and Jerome reporting manuscripts that end at 16:8.
- **16:9–20:** A C D K W Γ Δ Θ ƒ¹³ 𝔪, the Latin tradition and Irenaeus (in Latin).
- **Both endings:** L Ψ 083 099 579 `[T]`.

The NASB95 and ESV both print 16:9–20 in brackets.

**Rule adopted.** This overview treats **1:1–16:8** as the book. Every count excludes 16:9–20 unless it says otherwise. That matters because the SBLGNT index files the longer ending under Mark too, and it adds εὐαγγέλιον ("gospel"), πιστεύω ("to believe") and σημεῖον ("sign") to the totals. The longer ending is discussed where it bears on preaching (Trap 6, and the last preaching unit). Whether Mark *meant* to stop at 16:8, or his ending was lost, is `[S]` and contested. The text shows only that the earliest witnesses stop there.

**2. 1:1 — υἱοῦ θεοῦ ("Son of God").** NA28 prints [υἱοῦ θεοῦ], bracketed. The phrase is present in א¹ B D L W and absent in א* Θ 28, and the SBLGNT omits it `[T]`. The NASB95 and ESV both include it. **No finding in this overview rests on 1:1.** The Son-of-God frame is carried by 1:11 and 15:39, which are secure `[T]`.

**3. 1:41 — σπλαγχνισθείς ("moved with compassion") or ὀργισθείς ("angered").** This is the one place where the SBLGNT and NA28 part company on a word that shapes a scene. NA28 prints σπλαγχνισθείς, and the apparatus cites only D and three Old Latin witnesses for ὀργισθείς. The SBLGNT prints ὀργισθείς `[T]`. The NASB95 ("Moved with compassion") and ESV ("Moved with pity") follow NA28. A dig on 1:40–45 must settle this from Logos. Nothing in this overview depends on it.

**4. Verses absent from the critical text.** The SBLGNT has no 7:16, 9:44, 9:46, 11:26 or 15:28. The NASB95 brackets all five. This matters for one intertextual row: 15:28 is the Isa 53:12 citation ("He was numbered with transgressors"), which is not part of Mark on the critical text `[T]`.

**Verse count.** The SBLGNT has 661 verses in 1:1–16:8 `[T]`.

## Canonical Position

The NT equivalent of the Tanak block: position in the collection, what the book presupposes, what it hands on.

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Section** | The Gospels. Mark is second of four in the received order. The Western order found in Codex D (Matthew, John, Luke, Mark) puts it last `[S]`. The title κατὰ Μᾶρκον ("according to Mark") is not part of the text: NA28 reports it absent from the first hands of א and B, and supplied by later correctors `[T]`. |
| **Reading implication** | Read second, after Matthew, Mark looks like a shorter Matthew — a reading with an old pedigree (Augustine) `[S]`. Read on its own terms, it is a different book. It has no birth story and no genealogy, and it opens with Scripture and the wilderness. On the critical text it has no resurrection appearance and ends in silence and fear (16:8) `[T]`. **Prefer the second reading.** Its abruptness at both ends belongs to its shape and is not a lack. `[I]` |
| **Presupposes** | (1) **The Scriptures, before anything else.** Mark alone of the four Gospels puts a Scripture citation in its first sentence after the heading: καθὼς γέγραπται ("as it is written", 1:2). Checked against the openings of Matthew, Luke and John in the SBLGNT `[T]`. γέγραπται ("it is written") stands seven times: 1:2; 7:6; 9:12; 9:13; 11:17; 14:21; 14:27 `[T]`. (2) **Isaiah as the named prophet.** Ἠσαΐας ("Isaiah") is named at 1:2 and 7:6 and nowhere else `[T]`. (3) **The Elijah story, known well enough to be recognised unnamed.** John's dress at 1:6, ζώνην δερματίνην περὶ τὴν ὀσφὺν αὐτοῦ ("a leather belt around his waist"), is the wording of 2 Kgs 1:8 in Swete, ζώνην δερματίνην περιεζωσμένος τὴν ὀσφὺν αὐτοῦ ("girded with a leather belt about his waist"). The Hebrew is אֵזוֹר עוֹר ("a belt of skin"), and that verse ends by naming Elijah `[T]`. Mark does not name him until 6:15 and 9:11–13 `[T]`. (4) **Persons the audience already knows.** "Simon of Cyrene, the father of Alexander and Rufus" (15:21) identifies a man by sons who play no part in the story `[T]`. That they were known to the first readers is `[I]`, moderate–high. |
| **Handoff** | Forward, and outside the story. The gospel "must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10); what the anointing woman did "will be spoken of" wherever the gospel is preached "in the whole world" (14:9) `[T]`. The last instruction in 1:1–16:8 sends the disciples, "and Peter", to Galilee (16:7). No meeting there is narrated `[T]`. Within the canon, Mark hands on to Luke, whose prologue knows of "many" earlier accounts (Luke 1:1) `[T]`. That Mark is one of them is `[S]`. |
| **Neighbours** | Matthew before, Luke after. Most of Mark's narrative reappears in one or both `[S]`; this could be checked mechanically with `_texts/tools/synoptic_diff.py`, which was not run in this Draft. **The direction of dependence** (Markan priority, or Matthew first, as the Augustinian and Griesbach hypotheses hold) is a named open question. It is reported and not resolved, and nothing in this overview depends on it. One datum from the corpus is worth keeping: Mark's quotation of Isa 56:7 includes πᾶσιν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν ("for all the nations", 11:17), and both Matt 21:13 and Luke 19:46 lack it `[T]`. |

## Presenting Situation

**Author.** The book is anonymous. The narrator never speaks in the first person, and no author is named `[T]`. The attribution to Mark, and his connection with Peter, come from Papias as Eusebius reports him (*Hist. eccl.* 3.39) `[S]`. They are early, and they are not in the text. Peter is the most-named disciple (Πέτρος ("Peter") in 19 verses of 1:1–16:8, alongside the name Σίμων ("Simon") in 1:16–3:16 and at 14:37), and he is singled out in the last message of the book: "tell his disciples *and Peter*" (16:7) `[T]`. That this reflects Petrine testimony is `[S]`/`[I]`, moderate.

**Audience.** The text shows a Greek-reading audience that does not know Aramaic or Jewish custom:

- **Aramaic is glossed ten times**, nine with ὅ ἐστιν ("which is") and once with τοῦτ' ἔστιν ("that is"): 3:17; 5:41; 7:2; 7:11; 7:34; 12:42; 15:16; 15:22; 15:34; 15:42 `[T]`.
- **Jewish practice is explained** "for the Pharisees and *all the Jews*" (7:3–4), which is a narrator writing from outside that group `[T]`.
- **Latin loanwords run through the book** `[T]`: κεντυρίων ("centurion", 15:39, 44, 45), σπεκουλάτωρ ("executioner", 6:27), πραιτώριον ("praetorium", 15:16), λεγιών ("legion", 5:9, 15), κῆνσος ("poll-tax", 12:14), φραγελλόω ("to flog", 15:15) and ξέστης ("pitcher", 7:4). One of these is used to explain a Greek term: λεπτὰ δύο, ὅ ἐστιν κοδράντης ("two small coins, which is a quadrans", 12:42) `[T]`. A setting in Rome or the Latin west is suggested `[I]`, moderate. The tradition says Rome `[S]`.
- **The reader is addressed directly**: ὁ ἀναγινώσκων νοείτω ("let the reader understand", 13:14), and "what I say to you I say to all: stay awake" (13:37) `[T]`. The discourse steps out of the narrative to speak to its audience.

**Occasion.** It is not stated. Persecution is in view: θλῖψις ἢ διωγμός ("affliction or persecution", 4:17), a hundredfold reward μετὰ διωγμῶν ("along with persecutions", 10:30), and disciples handed over to councils, synagogues, governors and kings (13:9–13) `[T]`. That the first audience was suffering when the book was written is `[I]`, moderate–high. The date is `[S]` and disputed. Some put it before AD 70, reading 13:14 as still to come; others put it just after, reading it as recent. The text does not settle this, and the overview does not depend on it.

**Pressure.** The book holds two scandals together. The first is **a crucified Messiah**. The man declared "my beloved Son" (1:11; 9:7) is ἀποδοκιμασθῆναι ("rejected", 8:31), handed over, mocked as "King of the Jews" (15:2, 9, 12, 18, 26) and killed, and he dies crying "why have you forsaken me?" (15:34) `[T]`. The second is **failing disciples**. The Twelve are chosen "to be with him" (3:14). Their hearts are called hardened, as his opponents' were (πώρωσις ("hardness"), 3:5; πωρόω ("to harden"), 6:52; 8:17). They sleep when told to watch (14:37–41), and "they all left him and fled" (14:50). Peter denies him three times (14:66–72) `[T]`. The dominant tension, `[I]` high: **how can the one whom God calls Son be the one whom men reject and God forsakes — and how can those who deserted him still be summoned to follow?** The book's answer is its own δεῖ ("it is necessary", 8:31; 9:11; 13:7, 10, 14; 14:31) and its γέγραπται ("it is written", seven times), together with the ransom saying (10:45) and the message to "his disciples and Peter" (16:7) `[T]`.

**Purpose.** To bring the reader to the confession the centurion makes at the cross — "truly this man was God's Son" (15:39) — and to call the reader onto the way behind the Son, who gives his life as a ransom. That way means denying oneself, taking up the cross, and keeping watch while the Son of Man is away. `[I]`

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|--------|----------|----------|
| 1:1–13 | Prologue: the beginning in the wilderness | Scripture, forerunner and Son. The heavens are "torn", the Spirit descends, and the Voice declares "You are my beloved Son". The Son is then driven into the wilderness, tested by Satan, and served by angels. The reader is told what the characters will spend eight chapters trying to discover. **v0.4, the frame:** ἄγγελος ("messenger, angel") stands at 1:2 and 1:13 and not again until 8:38, and ἔρημος ("wilderness") four times (1:3, 4, 12, 13). The πνεῦμα ("Spirit") is promised (1:8), descends (1:10) and drives out (1:12); the next πνεῦμα in the book is an unclean one (1:23). The only divine speech is addressed to Jesus in the second person, through Scripture at 1:2 and from heaven at 1:11: σύ ("you") six times in thirteen verses `[S: dig 1:1–13]` |
| 1:14–8:21 | Galilee: authority, and who is this? | Three cycles. Each **opens with the disciples** and **closes on a hardness or unbelief** (see below). Authority over demons, disease, sin, Sabbath, sea and death. The identity question is asked again and again (1:27; 2:7; 4:41; 6:2–3; 6:14–16) |
| 8:22–10:52 | On the way: the Son of Man must suffer | Framed by two healings of the blind (8:22–26; 10:46–52), the only uses of τυφλός ("blind") in the book. Peter confesses "You are the Christ" (8:29). Three passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34) are each followed by misunderstanding and by teaching on discipleship. The Transfiguration comes at the centre. The movement ends on the ransom saying (10:45) and on Bartimaeus following ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ ("on the way", 10:52) |
| 11:1–13:37 | Jerusalem: the King and the temple | Entry. A fig tree is cursed around the temple action (11:12–25). Controversies with every party. The vineyard parable (12:1–12). The discourse on the temple's fall and the Son of Man's coming, ending "stay awake" (13:37) |
| 14:1–15:47 | The passion: handed over | Anointing, Passover, covenant cup and Gethsemane. Arrest, flight and trials. Peter's denial. Crucifixion among robbers, death, the torn veil, the centurion's confession. Burial |
| 16:1–8 | The empty tomb | "He has been raised; he is not here." "He is going ahead of you to Galilee; there you will see him." The women flee and say nothing, "for they were afraid" |

**The three Galilean cycles** `[T]` for the pattern and `[I]` for its reading:

| Cycle | Opens with the disciples | Closes on |
|---|---|---|
| 1:14–3:6 | The call of the four (1:16–20) | the **opponents'** πώρωσις ("hardness", 3:5) and the first plot to destroy him (3:6) |
| 3:7–6:6a | The appointment of the Twelve (3:13–19) | the **hometown's** ἀπιστία ("unbelief", 6:6) |
| 6:6b–8:21 | The sending of the Twelve (6:7–13) | the **disciples'** hardened heart (6:52; 8:17) and "Do you not yet understand?" (8:21) |

The circle of incomprehension closes inward, from enemies to kin to the Twelve themselves. πώρωσις and πωρόω stand in these three verses only: 3:5; 6:52; 8:17 `[T]`.

**Device.** A **two-part narrative** turning on **8:27–30**. The first half asks *who is this?*. The second half asks *what kind of Christ is he?*, and its answer is "the Son of Man must suffer" (8:31) `[T]`/`[I]`. Three **heaven-to-earth disclosures** of the Son mark the beginning, the middle and the end:

- **1:10–11:** the heavens σχιζομένους ("being torn"), and a voice: "You are my beloved Son".
- **9:7:** a cloud, and a voice: "This is my beloved Son".
- **15:38–39:** the veil ἐσχίσθη ("was torn"), and the centurion: "this man was God's Son".

σχίζω ("to tear") occurs in Mark only at 1:10 and 15:38. ἀγαπητός ("beloved") occurs only at 1:11, 9:7 and 12:6 `[T]`. The first two disclosures are made to Jesus and then to three disciples. The last is spoken by a Gentile soldier, the first human being in the book to call Jesus God's Son (the demons say it at 3:11 and 5:7) `[T]`.

**Other devices** `[T]`:

- **Intercalation ("sandwich").** One story is opened, a second is told inside it, and then the first is closed: 3:20–35; 5:21–43; 6:7–30; 11:12–25; 14:1–11; 14:53–72. What each pairing *means* is `[I]` and is work for the digs.
- **Pace.** εὐθύς ("immediately") is used as an adverb 41 times. Thirty-one fall before 8:27 and ten after. Eleven are in chapter 1 alone, and six in chapters 11–16. The story slows as it reaches Jerusalem: chapters 11–16 cover about a week, and 14:12–15:47 a single day and night.

**Contested seams.** Where the Galilean section ends (8:21, 8:26 or 8:30) and whether 8:22–26 belongs forwards or backwards are disputed `[S]`. This overview puts 8:22–26 at the head of the Way section, because the two blind men frame it. **The end of the prologue (1:13 or 1:15)** is also disputed `[S]`. The 1:1–13 dig keeps 1:13, because of the messenger-and-wilderness frame `[S: dig 1:1–13]`. But εὐαγγέλιον ("gospel") binds 1:1 to 1:14–15, and the herald passages of Isa 40 lie behind both, so 1:14–15 reads as a hinge that faces both ways `[I]`. The arc does not depend on either choice. Chiastic schemes for the whole book are `[S]`. None was tested, and none is adopted.

## Microscript

*The good news begins as Isaiah wrote: the Lord comes, and his way is prepared. Jesus, the beloved Son whom the heavens were torn open to announce, binds the strong man, forgives, heals and feeds with an authority no one can account for. Then he takes the way to Jerusalem, where the Son of Man must be handed over, rejected and killed, and gives his life as a ransom for many. At his death the veil is torn and a Gentile confesses him God's Son. The tomb is empty and he goes ahead of his scattered disciples to Galilee — and the story is left open, for the reader to follow him.*

**Keynotes, verified by lemma in the SBLGNT (1:1–16:8):**

- ὁδός ("way, road"): **16 verses**. They are 1:2, 3; 2:23; 4:4, 15; 6:8; 8:3, 27; 9:33, 34; 10:17, 32, 46, 52; 11:8; 12:14. Seven fall in 8:22–10:52, which is 117 of 661 verses (p ≈ 0.014), and the phrase ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ ("on the way") clusters there `[T]`. **v0.3:** that clustering is not evidence of design on its own, because 28 of the 109 lemmas found in 10–30 verses of Mark cluster as strongly in some movement. The design evidence is that Matthew and Luke drop "on the way" at Mark's frames while keeping ὁδός elsewhere `[S: audit 2]`.
- παραδίδωμι ("to hand over, betray"): **20 occurrences in 19 verses**. It is used of John (1:14), of Jesus (3:19; 9:31; 10:33 twice; 14:10, 11, 18, 21, 41, 42, 44; 15:1, 10, 15) and of the disciples (13:9, 11, 12). It is also used once of the harvest (4:29) and once of tradition (7:13) `[T]`.
- ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ("the Son of Man"): **14 occurrences in 13 verses**. They are 2:10, 28; 8:31, 38; 9:9, 12, 31; 10:33, 45; 13:26; 14:21 (twice), 41, 62. The plural "sons of men" at 3:28 is excluded `[T]`.
- δεῖ ("it is necessary"): **6 verses**. They are 8:31; 9:11; 13:7, 10, 14; 14:31. MorphGNT files δεῖ under δέω, which also carries "to bind" in 8 verses. The two were separated by parse code `[T]`.
- εὐαγγέλιον ("gospel"): **7 verses**. They are 1:1, 14, 15; 8:35; 10:29; 13:10; 14:9. This excludes 16:15 in the longer ending `[T]`.

**Test against the movements.** The prologue prepares "the way of the Lord" (1:2–3) and announces the εὐαγγέλιον (1:1, 14–15). In Galilee the Son of Man has authority on earth (2:10, 28), and the one "stronger" than the strong man binds him (1:7; 3:27). The Way section is the ὁδός itself, and there the Son of Man δεῖ ("must") suffer (8:31) and gives his life as a ransom (10:45). Jerusalem shows the rejected stone becoming the head of the corner (12:10) and the gospel going to all nations (13:10). The passion is the handing over (14:41; 15:1, 15) of the Son who must drink the cup (14:36). The tomb sends the disciples on to Galilee (16:7). Every movement serves the microscript.

## Intertextual Map

Mark is **citation-moderate and allusion-dense**. Marked quotations are few: γέγραπται ("it is written") seven times; "Moses said / wrote / the book of Moses" at 7:10, 10:3–5, 12:19 and 12:26; "have you not read" at 2:25, 12:10 and 12:26; "David himself said" at 12:36 `[T]`. The allusions cluster in two places, the prologue and the passion, and much of the passion is told in the words of the Psalms without a single formula (15:24, 29, 34, 36) `[T]`.

**How Mark uses his Scriptures.** This is a text-first finding for the whole sweep. Where Mark **quotes with a formula**, he follows the Greek closely: Isa 40:3 at 1:3; Isa 29:13 at 7:6–7, including the LXX's μάτην ("in vain"), where the Hebrew has no such word; Isa 56:7 at 11:17; Ps 117:22–23 LXX at 12:10–11 `[T]`. Where he **alludes**, or quotes without a formula, he several times stands nearer the Hebrew than any Greek text in the corpus `[T]`:

- **1:2.** κατασκευάσει τὴν ὁδόν ("will prepare the way"). The Hebrew of Mal 3:1 is וּפִנָּה־דֶרֶךְ ("and he will clear a way"), and the LXX reads ἐπιβλέψεται ὁδόν ("will look upon a way"). The Hebrew verb פָּנָה ("to turn, clear") is the verb of Isa 40:3, פַּנּוּ דֶּרֶךְ ("clear the way"). Both verified by lemma (6437) with `find.py`. So the two texts Mark joins under Isaiah's name share a Hebrew phrase that the Greek does not show.
- **1:10.** σχιζομένους τοὺς οὐρανούς ("the heavens being torn"). Isa 63:19 in the Hebrew reads קָרַעְתָּ שָׁמַיִם ("you tore the heavens"; English 64:1). Both Swete and Rahlfs have ἐὰν ἀνοίξῃς τὸν οὐρανόν ("if you open the heaven"). No form of σχίζω ("to tear") is used of heaven anywhere in Swete.
- **1:11.** ἐν σοὶ εὐδόκησα ("in you I am well pleased"). Isa 42:1 in the Hebrew reads רָצְתָה נַפְשִׁי ("my soul delights"). The LXX reads προσεδέξατο αὐτὸν ἡ ψυχή μου ("my soul has accepted him").
- **4:29.** θερισμός ("harvest"). Joel 4:13 in the Hebrew reads קָצִיר ("harvest"). The LXX reads τρυγητός ("vintage"; Swete 3:13).
- **14:27.** πατάξω τὸν ποιμένα ("I will strike the shepherd"), in the singular. Zech 13:7 in the Hebrew reads הַךְ אֶת־הָרֹעֶה ("strike the shepherd"). **Both Swete and Rahlfs have the plural**, πατάξατε τοὺς ποιμένας ("strike the shepherds"). The BHS apparatus (13:7, note b) proposes reading the verb as first person, "I will strike", citing some LXX manuscripts, the Armenian, and Matt 26:31. This is a finding that rests on an apparatus note, as BHS prints it.
- **15:34.** Ἐλωῒ ἐλωῒ λεμὰ σαβαχθάνι ("Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani") is Aramaic. The Hebrew of Ps 22:2 reads עֲזַבְתָּנִי ("you have forsaken me"). Mark's own translation, εἰς τί ἐγκατέλιπές με ("why have you forsaken me?"), does not take the LXX's added πρόσχες μοι ("attend to me") or its ἵνα τί ("why").

**Consequence for the digs.** In Mark, Tool 11 Move 2 should read Swete, Rahlfs **and** the WLC. A search of the Greek alone will miss 1:2, 1:10 and 14:27. `[I]`

**A formula quotation that follows the Greek in a way that matters (v0.4).** At 1:3 Mark has φωνὴ βοῶντος ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ("a voice of one crying in the wilderness"), as the LXX does. In the Hebrew, בַּמִּדְבָּר ("in the wilderness") is paired with בָּעֲרָבָה ("in the desert") in the next line, so it goes with "clear the way". Mark keeps the Greek phrasing, and the phrasing is load-bearing: John appears ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ("in the wilderness", 1:4). The citation formula at 1:2 is also the only γέγραπται ("it is written") in Mark spoken by the narrator; the other six are in Jesus' mouth `[S: dig 1:1–13]`.

**Tested and rejected as a seventh case (v0.2).** The sweep proposed that Mark's γυμνὸς ἔφυγεν ("naked, he fled", 14:52) follows the Hebrew of Amos 2:16, עָרוֹם יָנוּס ("naked he will flee"), against the Greek ὁ γυμνὸς διώξεται ("the naked one will pursue"). The claim audit found Gen 39:12 closer: Rahlfs καταλιπὼν τὰ ἱμάτια αὐτοῦ … ἔφυγεν ("leaving his garments … he fled"), with the seizing and the garment. The list above stays at six `[S: audit]`.

**Tested and rejected as a seventh case (v0.3).** Claim audit 2 proposed *Ephphatha*, "be opened" (7:34), as a seventh case, on the Hebrew of Isa 35:5, וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה ("and the ears of the deaf will be opened"). Audit 3 withdrew it. The Greek of the same verse already has τότε ἀνοιχθήσονται ὀφθαλμοὶ τυφλῶν ("then the eyes of the blind will be opened", Swete and Rahlfs). Greek Isaiah also opens ears at 42:20; 48:8; 50:5. So Mark need not have read the Hebrew here. The list stays at six `[S: audit 3]`.

Sources are listed in **Tanak order** (BHS). Locations were found by a systematic 4-gram sweep of all of Mark against all of Swete. This was supplemented by a deliberate hunt for type-scene, name and number allusions that share no wording. Every row's verbal overlap was then checked verse against verse in Swete, accent-stripped, and in the WLC where the Greek and Hebrew diverge. Swete's versification is used where it differs, and flagged. Confidence: *high* means a quotation or distinctive shared wording; *moderate* means shared wording plus conceptual fit; *uncertain* means conceptual only.

## Christological Trajectory

Mark is a Gospel, so Christ is its subject rather than its horizon. The question for the overview is *how* the book presents him. It does so in three registers at once.

- **Ground — the Son in the place of YHWH.** The book's first citation prepares ὁδὸν κυρίου ("the way of the Lord"; Hebrew דֶּרֶךְ יְהוָה, "the way of YHWH"), and the one who comes is Jesus (1:2–3, 9) `[T]`. The book's first claim about him is God's, in God's own words, before any human speaks. Malachi's "before *me*" becomes "your way", Isaiah's "our God" becomes "his", and God addresses the "you" twice, through Scripture at 1:2 and from heaven at 1:11 `[T]` for the wording, `[I]` for the identification `[S: dig 1:1–13]`. He forgives sins, the prerogative of εἷς ὁ θεός ("God alone", 2:7). He is κύριος ("Lord") of the Sabbath (2:28). He rebukes the sea as the LORD does and walks on it as God alone does (Job 9:8). He "passes by" as the LORD passed by Moses, and says ἐγώ εἰμι ("I am", 6:48–50) `[T]`/`[I]`. He is David's Lord (12:35–37) and the one enthroned at the right hand of Power (14:62) `[T]`. Mark does not argue the point; he shows it through the Scriptures he chooses, and leaves the reader to draw the conclusion. `[I]`, high.
- **The necessary sufferer.** The one with all authority *must* (δεῖ) be rejected and killed (8:31). He gives his life as λύτρον ("ransom") ἀντὶ πολλῶν ("in the place of many", 10:45). His blood is the covenant blood poured out ὑπὲρ πολλῶν ("for many", 14:24). He drinks the cup his Father does not take away (14:36), and he dies forsaken (15:34) `[T]`. The Scripture behind this is Isa 53 ("many", "his life"; at 10:45 combined with Exodus's ransom vocabulary, turned round, and Isa 43:3–4), Zech 13:7 (the struck shepherd), Ps 22 and Ps 118 (the rejected stone) — see the Intertextual Map. The cross is where the Son is disclosed, not a detour on his way to glory: the veil is torn and the confession is made at the moment of death (15:37–39), not at the empty tomb `[T]`/`[I]`.
- **Pattern.** "If anyone wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me" (8:34). The Son of Man "did not come to be served, but to serve" (10:45) `[T]`. Christ is the pattern only as the one who first *ransomed*. The discipleship teaching of 8:34–38, 9:35–37 and 10:42–44 is each time grounded in a passion prediction that comes first (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34). The indicative leads, and the imperative follows `[T]` for the order, `[I]` for the reading.

**Son of God — and who says it.** The narrator says it (1:1, if the text stands), and God says it (1:11; 9:7). Demons say it (3:11; 5:7), and Jesus half-says it in a parable (12:6) and in reply to the high priest (14:61–62). The one human being in the narrative who says it is a Roman executioner at the moment of death (15:39) `[T]`. The disciples never do. Peter confesses "the Christ" (8:29) and then rebukes the suffering that the title will cost `[T]`. **The prologue creates the book's irony** `[S: dig 1:1–13]`. Χριστός ("Christ", 1:1) is not said again until Peter says it at 8:29. The Spirit-baptism promised at 1:8 is never narrated in 1:1–16:8. So the reader hears the whole of Galilee knowing what no character knows `[T]`.

**Moralism check.** Mark invites two forms of moralism. **"Be like the disciples — follow Jesus"** fails, because the disciples in Mark are the book's warning as much as its model. They misunderstand, sleep and flee; one betrays and one denies `[T]`. **"Be like the faith-heroes"** (the woman with the flow of blood, Bartimaeus, the widow, the anointing woman) fails too, because each of them is a recipient before they are an example, and the book's last human act is fear and silence (16:8) `[T]`. The grounding is the ransom (10:45) and the promise "he is going ahead of you" (16:7). The failed are restored by the one who goes before them, not by recovering their nerve. Christ is hero, not merely example. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **The messianic secret as a technique.** This preaches Jesus' commands to silence (1:25, 34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26, 30; 9:9) as modesty, crowd management, or a strategy to avoid a political misreading, and stops there. *Bites at:* 1:40–45; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9. *Why it is wrong:* the book tells us when the silence ends, "until the Son of Man has risen from the dead" (9:9), so the secret is about *what kind* of Christ he is. It is also reversed at the close: told to speak, the women say nothing (16:8; cf. 1:44). *Corrective:* preach the silences in view of the cross, which is where the title can be understood. The *why* of the secret is `[S]`-heavy (Wrede and after) and should not be preached as settled.

2. **Disciples as simple models, or simple failures.** The first preaches "be like Peter, leave your nets", and the second preaches "don't be like Peter". *Bites at:* 1:16–20; 6:7–13; 8:27–33; 10:28–31; 14:26–72. *Why it is wrong:* Mark keeps both true at once. The Twelve are called, appointed and sent, and they still end hardened (8:17), asleep and scattered. *Corrective:* preach the disciples as those whom Christ keeps calling "behind me" (1:17; 8:33, 34), and let 16:7 ("and Peter") carry the grace.

3. **Miracles as proofs, or as promises of the same.** Either "Jesus did miracles, so he is God", or "bring your storm, and he will still it". *Bites at:* 4:35–41; 5:21–43; 6:45–52; 9:14–29. *Why it is wrong:* in Mark the miracles raise the question "who is this?" (4:41); they do not settle it. And the one who stills storms is not rescued from his own cross (15:30–32). *Corrective:* preach each miracle as a disclosure that points to the cross, and read the stilling of the storm with Jonah (see the Intertextual Map).

4. **Mark 13 as a timetable.** This reads the discourse as a chart of the end-times, or as nothing but AD 70. *Bites at:* 13:1–37, especially 13:14, 24–27, 30, 32. *Why it is wrong:* the discourse is framed by βλέπετε ("watch out", 13:5, 9, 23, 33) and ends "stay awake" (13:37). Its purpose is vigilance, not calculation, and "of that day or hour no one knows" (13:32). Its vocabulary of watching is then acted out in Gethsemane (see the Echo Table). *Corrective:* preach 13 and 14 together.

5. **"Why have you forsaken me?" as abandonment without purpose, or with its answer supplied too quickly.** This either explains the cry away by jumping to the end of Ps 22, or preaches it as unrelieved despair. *Bites at:* 15:33–39. *Why it is wrong:* Mark gives the cry in Aramaic, then translates it, and then lets it be misheard as a call to Elijah (15:35). The answer is in the narrative, not in the rest of the psalm: the veil is torn and the centurion confesses (15:38–39). *Corrective:* keep the cry and the confession together, as the text does.

6. **The ending — either ignored or overcorrected.** One way preaches 16:9–20 as Mark (snake-handling, poison-drinking, "these signs will follow"). The other apologises for 16:8 as though the Gospel had failed. *Bites at:* 16:1–8 and [16:9–20]. *Why it is wrong:* the earliest witnesses end at 16:8 (see *The Greek Text*). And 16:8 is not an ending without hope. It follows the promise "you will see him, just as he told you" (16:7; 14:28), and the book's first line calls all of it only the ἀρχή ("beginning", 1:1). *Corrective:* preach 16:1–8 as the book's ending, with the promise at its centre. If 16:9–20 is read aloud, say from the pulpit what it is. The pastoral landmine lies in 16:17–18.

7. **Preaching Mark with another Gospel's script** (v0.4, from the 1:1–13 dig) `[S: dig 1:1–13]`. This fills Mark's spare scenes from Matthew or Luke: "Jesus answered each temptation with Scripture, and so must we", or "your wilderness season". *Bites at:* 1:12–13 first; anywhere Mark is shorter than its parallels `[I]`. *Why it is wrong:* Mark gives no dialogue, no three tests and no Scripture answers. He gives a sending (ἐκβάλλει, "drives out", the exorcism verb), the adversary, the beasts, and angels serving *the Son* `[T]`. The wilderness is the Stronger One entering Satan's territory, which 3:27 explains; it is not a pattern for coping. *Corrective:* preach Mark's scene as Mark tells it, and if Matthew is drawn in, say so from the pulpit. A related misstep is to build Christology on 1:1's "Son of God", which is textually uncertain (see *The Greek Text*); ground it in 1:11, where God says it.

**Addressee differentiation.** The prologue names distinct parties: the forerunner, the Son, the Spirit, Satan, the wild beasts and the angels (1:2–13). The first episode then names four men (1:16–20). Three resolve specifically:

- **John** — his arrest (1:14) and death (6:17–29) rehearse the Son's (9:13).
- **The four** — named together at 1:16–20 and 1:29, and not again until 13:3, where they are the private audience of the watch-discourse. Of them, **Peter** alone is named in the last message (16:7).
- **The angels who served** (1:13) — they are answered by the women who served (15:41). The only angel-figure at the end is a νεανίσκος ("young man", 16:5).

Hold 1:1–20 open against 13–16: the book ends where it began, in Galilee, with the Son going ahead and disciples called "behind" him. `[I]`

**What the text-first reading adds, or nuances, against the usual account:**

- The **three-cycle Galilean structure** is widely taught `[S]`. The text adds that the cycles close on a πώρωσις / πωρόω ("hardness") chain of exactly three verses, moving inward from enemies to disciples (3:5; 6:52; 8:17).
