Mark: Questions for the Logos Research Assistant, Round 1

Purpose. These are questions for the Research Assistant, drawn from two reports in this folder:

  • the hypothesis test, Does Mark build his Gospel on Isaiah? (dig-deeper-mark-isaiah-hypothesis-test);
  • claim audit 2 (dig-deeper-mark-claim-audit-2).

They follow logos-research-assistant-guidelines in the Dig Deeper folder.

The questions have two aims:

  • To fill the “history of interpretation” criterion. Both audits recorded it as not checked, and these questions fill it for the links now rated moderate or higher.
  • To test Watts. The hypothesis test set out Watts’s argument from recall only. These questions check it against what he actually wrote.

Use.

  • Ask one question per turn.
  • For section 1, set the scope to Watts, Isaiah’s New Exodus and Mark (WUNT 88), alone.
  • For the other sections, set the scope to your Mark commentaries, adding Jones, OT Quotations and Allusions in the NT, and Moyise, Jesus and Scripture.
  • Paste each answer back with its footnotes, under the question as asked.

1. A Named Commentator’s View: Watts

1.1 Watts’s three stages

  • Ask: In Isaiah’s New Exodus and Mark, how does Watts divide the Gospel of Mark, and how does he map those divisions onto Isaiah? Does he base the scheme on Isaiah 40:1–11 alone, or on Isaiah 40–55 as a whole?
  • Why: The hypothesis test summarised Watts’s scheme from recall: Galilee as deliverance, 8:22–10:52 as the way, Jerusalem as arrival. That summary may misstate him, and the strong form of the hypothesis was tested against it.
  • Watch for:
    • a summary with no page numbers;
    • stages named in the Assistant’s own words rather than Watts’s.
  • Then ask: Does Watts answer the objection that Galilee, journey, Jerusalem is simply the plot of the story, and so need not come from Isaiah?

1.2 Watts on the healings of the blind

  • Ask: How does Watts interpret the two healings of blind men in Mark 8:22–26 and 10:46–52? Which Isaiah texts does he connect them with — Isaiah 35:5, Isaiah 42:16–18, or others — and does he mention Jeremiah 31:8–9?
  • Why: Claim audit 2 reframed the “blind on the way” link as a cluster: Isa 35:5–8 for the theme, Isa 40:3–4, and Isa 42:16–18 for the wording, which the Greek carries best. If Watts already makes this case, the audit’s lead is a rediscovery, and the audit should say so.
  • Watch for: whether he argues from the Greek (ἀναβλέπω, “to see again”, at Isa 42:18) or the Hebrew. The audit found the case stronger in the Greek.

1.3 Watts on Exodus 23:20 in Mark 1:2

  • Ask: What does Watts say about Exodus 23:20 in the composite quotation of Mark 1:2–3? Does he discuss the rest of that verse, “to guard you on the way”, or the part Exodus plays alongside Malachi 3:1 and Isaiah 40:3?
  • Why: Claim audit 2 found that Mark’s framing phrase in 8:27–10:52, ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ (“on the way”), is the continuation of Exod 23:20 in the Greek, not the wording of Isa 40:3. This bears directly on whether the Way section is Isaiah’s way or Exodus’s.
  • Watch for: an answer that folds Exodus into “the new exodus” without separating the verse from the theme.

1.4 Watts on the ending at 16:8

  • Ask: How does Watts account for the ending of Mark at 16:8, where the women flee in fear and say nothing, within his reading of an Isaianic arrival in Zion?
  • Why: The hypothesis test named this as the gap it could not close. Isaiah has nothing in Mark 16:1–8, and an Isaianic plan might be expected to end in Zion’s joy (Isa 35:10) or new creation (Isa 65:17).
  • Watch for: whether he treats the delay or failure of the new exodus as Isaianic in itself, and on which texts.

1.5 Watts on the ransom saying

  • Ask: Which Old Testament texts does Watts connect with “to give his life as a ransom for many” in Mark 10:45? Does he discuss Exodus 21:30 or 30:12, Isaiah 43:3–4, or Isaiah 53:10–12?
  • Why: The overview rates Isa 53:10–12 at 10:45 as moderate. Claim audit 2 found Exod 21:30 and 30:12 verbally closer: λύτρα τῆς ψυχῆς (“a ransom for one’s life”). The noun λύτρον occurs in Isaiah only at 45:13.
  • Watch for: “many” (Isa 53:11–12) treated as decisive without discussing λύτρον.

2. The State of a Debate

2.1 Why Mark 1:2 names Isaiah

  • Ask: What are the main explanations of why Mark 1:2 attributes to “Isaiah the prophet” a quotation that begins with Exodus 23:20 and Malachi 3:1, who holds each, and what evidence do they give? Do any compare Matthew 27:9, where Zechariah is quoted under Jeremiah’s name?
  • Why: This is falsifier F3 of the hypothesis test. Three kinds of explanation were named there:
    • a convention of naming the principal source;
    • a collection of proof-texts already circulating under one name (the “testimonia” hypothesis);
    • a deliberate frame for reading the whole Gospel.
    The corpus could not settle which.
  • Watch for:
    • an answer that assumes the “in the prophets” reading of the Majority text;
    • one that counts words, which the corpus has already done (13 words from Exodus/Malachi, 14 from Isaiah).

2.2 Mark 1:14–15 and its background

  • Ask: What Old Testament background do commentators give for “proclaiming the gospel of God … the time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has come near” in Mark 1:14–15? Do they cite Isaiah 52:7, Isaiah 40:9 or Isaiah 61:1 for the first half, and Daniel 7:22 for the second?
  • Why: Claim audit 2 reframed Isa 52:7 as a group of herald texts (Isa 40:9–10; 52:7; 61:1) at moderate. It flagged Dan 7:22, “the time arrived and the saints took possession of the kingdom”, as a candidate for the “time” half.
  • Watch for: “gospel” explained from the Roman imperial usage alone. That is background, not an Old Testament source.

3. Has Anyone Seen This Allusion?

For this section, ask Jones’s OT Quotations and Allusions in the NT first, then the commentaries.

3.1 Isaiah 35:5 behind “Ephphatha” (Mark 7:34–35)

  • Ask: Which commentators discuss a link between Mark 7:34–35 (“Ephphatha, that is, be opened … his ears were opened”) and Isaiah 35:5? Does anyone note that the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 says the ears of the deaf will be “opened”, where the Greek says they “will hear”?
  • Why: Claim audit 2 found this new point. If it holds, it is a seventh case in the overview’s list of places where Mark stands nearer the Hebrew. The link to Isa 35:5–6 is already rated high because of μογιλάλος (“speaking with difficulty”). What this question tests is the Hebrew point.
  • Watch for: an answer about μογιλάλος only, which is already settled.

3.2 The blind on the way

  • Ask: Which commentators discuss a link between the healings of the blind in Mark 8:22–26 and 10:46–52 and Isaiah 42:16–18, Isaiah 35:5 or Jeremiah 31:8–9? What wording do they point to, and does anyone reject the link?
  • Why: This is the same lead as 1.2, asked of the commentaries rather than of Watts.
  • Watch for: whether the link is to the healing (Isa 35:5) or to the way (Isa 42:16). The audit found that they rest on different texts.

3.3 Exodus 21:30 and 30:12 behind Mark 10:45

  • Ask: Which commentators discuss a link between “a ransom for many” in Mark 10:45 and the ransom for one’s life in Exodus 21:30 and 30:12? Does anyone compare these with Isaiah 53:10–12, and which do they prefer?
  • Why: This is a new candidate from claim audit 2, unaudited. If no commentator has seen it, that is not a demotion. It still goes to the next claim audit.
  • Watch for: answers that treat λύτρον (“ransom”) as Isaianic without citing an Isaiah verse.

3.4 The strong man bound (Mark 3:27)

  • Ask: Which commentators discuss the Old Testament or Jewish background of binding the strong man in Mark 3:27? Do they point to Isaiah 49:24–25, Isaiah 53:12, Tobit 8:3, or 1 Enoch 10:4?
  • Why: Claim audit 2 left this uncertain. Isaiah has the plundering but no binding, and the binding comes from Tobit and 1 Enoch.
  • Watch for: Isaiah 53:12 cited from Luke 11:22’s wording (“divides his spoils”), not from Mark’s.

3.5 The torn heavens (Mark 1:10)

  • Ask: Which commentators discuss a link between the heavens “torn open” in Mark 1:10 and Isaiah 63:19 (English 64:1), “Oh that you would rend the heavens”? Do they note that the Greek of Isaiah has “open” rather than “tear”?
  • Why: The overview rates this moderate, through the Hebrew only. A history of interpretation would complete it.
  • Watch for: the torn veil (15:38) brought in as proof. That is an internal echo, not evidence of the source.
