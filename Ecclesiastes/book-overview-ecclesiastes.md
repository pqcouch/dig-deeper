## What this document is

This is the whole-book planning layer for Ecclesiastes. It exists to make the passage digs sharper, not to settle them in advance.

Ecclesiastes poses a particular problem for an overview, and it should be stated at the top. **No book in the canon is harder to give a "structure" to, and no book's overall message is more contested.** Readers disagree on whether the speaker is a believer, a sceptic, or a character the frame narrator presents in order to answer him. They also disagree on whether the book has a plan at all. An overview that picks one of those readings and presents it as the book would be doing the digs' work for them, and doing it badly.

So this document separates three kinds of claim more sharply than usual:

- **What the page shows** — the vocabulary, the refrains, the frame, the counts. These are firm, and they are verified.
- **What the page suggests** — the movements and the microscript. These are provisional.
- **What interpreters have argued** — the major readings, set side by side rather than decided.

| Field | |

| **Reading implication** | In the Writings, Ecclesiastes is read as one of the scrolls, not as the middle term of a Solomonic trilogy. The reader has already come through Psalms, Job and Proverbs. ## Presenting Situation

**The voices.** The book has two speakers, and telling them apart is the first exegetical decision any passage requires. `[T]`

- A **frame narrator** speaks *about* "Qohelet" in the third person: "says Qohelet" (1:2; 7:27; 12:8). He also evaluates him at the end: "In addition to being a wise man, Qohelet also taught the people knowledge" (12:9–10).
- **Qohelet** speaks in the first person from 1:12 ("I, Qohelet, have been king over Israel in Jerusalem") to 12:7.
- The epilogue (12:9–14) then addresses a "my son" (12:12). That is the voice of Proverbs 1–9.

**The name.** קֹהֶלֶת ("Qohelet"; NASB95 "the preacher") occurs **7×**, all in this book and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible: 1:1, 2, 12; 7:27; 12:8, 9, 10 (lemma 6953, WLC). `[T]` It is a **feminine participle** of the root קהל ("to assemble"). It takes the article once (12:8, הַקּוֹהֶלֶת, "the Qohelet"), which suggests a title or office rather than a personal name. `[T]` for the form; `[I]` for "office". **At 7:27 the verb is feminine** (אָמְרָה קֹהֶלֶת, "says Qohelet"). BHS proposes redividing it as אָמַר הַקֹּהֶלֶת, "says the Qohelet", on the analogy of 12:8 and the Greek (*BHS app.* 7:27 ᵃ⁻ᵃ). **That is an editorial proposal printed in BHS, not a manuscript reading.** The LXX's Ἐκκλησιαστής ("member or leader of an assembly") is the source of the English title. `[T]` for the Greek (Swete 1:1).

**Author.** The book names "the words of Qohelet, son of David, king in Jerusalem" (1:1). `[T]` for the superscription. **Identifying Qohelet with Solomon is `[I]`.** It is a strong inference, because only Solomon was both David's son and king "over Israel in Jerusalem" (1:12). The Solomonic colouring of 1:16 and 2:4–9 supports it (see the Intertextual Map). **Yet the book never uses the name.** Whether Qohelet is Solomon himself, a later sage speaking in a Solomonic persona, or a persona the frame narrator presents, is **`[S]` and contested.** Authorship and date belong to the scholars, not to the page.

**Date.** The book gives none. Two things are `[T]` for their presence:

- **Two Persian loanwords:** פַּרְדֵּס ("park", 2:5; elsewhere only Song 4:13 and Neh 2:8) and פִּתְגָם ("sentence, decree", 8:11; elsewhere only Esth 1:20).
- **A heavy use of the relative particle שֶׁ־ ("which, that")** in place of the older אֲשֶׁר.

The inference from these to a Persian-period or later date is `[S]`, and it is widely held. The traditional ascription to Solomon is also `[S]`. **Neither is a text-observation.**

**Audience.** Three horizons need to be kept apart:

1. **The narrative-world audience** is the people Qohelet "taught knowledge" (12:9). `[T]`
2. **The compositional audience** is "my son" (12:12), a pupil in the wisdom school. `[T]` that the address is there; `[I]` for what it implies.
3. **The canonical audience** is the reader of the Writings, who has Torah, Prophets, Psalms, Job and Proverbs behind them. `[I]`

Interpretation defaults to the third.

**The pressure.** The book states its own question at the start, and repeats it: **"What advantage does man have in all his work which he does under the sun?"** (1:3; again 3:9; 5:16). `[T]` The keyword is יִתְרוֹן ("advantage, gain, profit"). It occurs **10× in 9 verses, and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible** (1:3; 2:11, 13 ×2; 3:9; 5:9, 16; 7:12; 10:10, 11 — lemma 3504, WLC). `[T]` **The pressure is death.** It is the one "fate" (מִקְרֶה, "fate, event") that falls alike on the wise and the fool (2:14–16), on man and beast (3:19), and on the righteous and the wicked (9:2–3). Death cancels every gain toil, wisdom or righteousness might claim. `[T]` A second pressure runs underneath: **the world's moral order does not visibly hold.** The righteous perish in their righteousness and the wicked prolong their lives (7:15; 8:14). `[T]`

**Purpose.** The purpose is to walk the wise reader to the edge of what wisdom can secure, and to make them admit that neither toil nor wisdom yields a lasting יִתְרוֹן ("advantage") against death. The book then redirects them to three things: receiving their חֵלֶק ("portion") as God's gift with joy (2:24; 3:13; 5:19), fearing God (3:14; 5:7; 12:13), and trusting that he will bring every deed into judgement (3:17; 11:9; 12:14). `[I]` — and see the contested readings under *Preaching Traps*, which this purpose statement does not settle.

## The Greek Text of Ecclesiastes

A short section, because the Greek of this book has a distinctive profile that downstream digs need to know about.

**It is mechanically literal.** Swete's text renders the Hebrew object marker אֶת (which marks the direct object) with **σύν plus the accusative** — ungrammatical Greek — **10 times**. The ten places, in Swete's numbering, are 2:17; 3:17 ×2; 7:27 and 7:30 (= Heb 7:26, 29); 8:8, 15, 17; 11:7; 12:9 (accent-insensitive search, Swete). `[T]` That feature is the one usually cited in associating the Greek Ecclesiastes with **Aquila** or the Aquila-like revision **(Barthélemy)** `[S]`. It is not verified here against Rahlfs-Hanhart or Göttingen.

**Its keywords track the Hebrew closely:**

- ματαιότης ("vanity") occurs **38×** in Swete, the same total as הֶבֶל ("breath, vanity") in the WLC (38). `[T]` for both counts. The totals match; **a verse-by-verse one-to-one mapping was not checked.**
- ὑπὸ τὸν ἥλιον ("under the sun") occurs **30×** in Swete, against **29** occurrences of תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ ("under the sun") in the Hebrew (27 verses, twice each at 8:15 and 9:9). The one extra Greek occurrence is at **2:3**. There the WLC reads תַּחַת הַשָּׁמַיִם ("under the heavens"), and **BHS records that two manuscripts, the Greek and the Syriac read הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ ("the sun")** (*BHS app.* 2:3 ᶜ). BHS also notes the reverse question at 1:13 (*app.* 1:13 ᵇ: "ℭ mlt Mss 𝔖𝔗𝔙 הַשָּׁמֶשׁ"). `[T]` for the counts and the apparatus entries.

**Consequence for the digs.** The Greek is a near-transparent window on its Hebrew *Vorlage*, so it will rarely yield an independent reading. Where it does differ, the difference is probably significant. **A dig on 1:12–18 or 2:1–11 should check whether "under heaven" or "under the sun" is the text it is describing.** *Confidence: high on the counts; the Aquila association is `[S]`.*

## Structural Arc Map

Reading with the chapter divisions stripped away, these seams show on the page:

- **The frame.** 1:2 and 12:8 are near-identical: הֲבֵל הֲבָלִים … הַכֹּל הָבֶל ("vanity of vanities … all is vanity"). `[T]`
- **The first-person turn** at 1:12. `[T]`
- **The first second-person address to a "you"** at 5:1 (Heb 4:17), where imperatives begin. `[T]`
- **The refrain "vanity and striving after wind"** (רְעוּת רוּחַ, "striving after wind"). It occurs **7×, all in this book, the last at 6:9**: 1:14; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6; 6:9 (lemma 7469, WLC). `[T]`
- **The exact midpoint.** Verses 1:1–6:9 are **111 of the book's 222 verses** (WLC count). `[T]`
- **"Who knows?" and "cannot find out" questions.** From 6:10 onward, questions about what man cannot know or find out begin to dominate. `[T]` / `[I]`

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1:1–11 | **Title and prologue** | Superscription. The thesis in the frame voice: הֲבֵל הֲבָלִים ("vanity of vanities", 1:2). The book's question: יִתְרוֹן ("advantage", 1:3). A poem on the ceaseless round of generation, sun, wind and rivers, ending on "no remembrance" (1:4–11). |
| 1:12–2:26 | **The king's experiment** | "I, Qohelet, was king" (1:12). He tests wisdom (1:12–18), pleasure and great works (2:1–11), wisdom against folly in the face of death (2:12–17), and toil left to an heir (2:18–23). The first word that good comes "from the hand of God" follows (2:24–26). |
| 3:1–6:9 | **God's times and man's toil** | The times poem, 28 occurrences of עֵת ("time") in 3:2–8 (lemma 6256, WLC), and God's unfindable work (3:1–15). Injustice and the one fate of man and beast (3:16–22). Oppression, envy, the lone man, the king (4:1–16). The turn to imperatives before God: worship and vows (5:1–7). The ill of wealth, and God's gift of enjoyment (5:8–20). The man given wealth without the power to enjoy it (6:1–9). **Closes on the last "striving after wind" at 6:9 — the book's midpoint.** |
| 6:10–8:17 | **What is good — and who can find it?** | The hinge questions: "who knows what is good for a man … who can tell a man what will be after him?" (6:10–12). "Better … than" sayings (7:1–14). The limits of righteousness and wisdom (7:15–29), with מָצָא ("find") **8× in 7:23–29** (of 17 in the book; lemma 4672, WLC). The king, and justice delayed (8:1–15). "Man cannot find out the work" (8:16–17). |
| 9:1–11:6 | **The one fate, and the wisdom that still serves** | All things are "in the hand of God", yet one fate comes to all (9:1–6). "Go, eat your bread in happiness" (9:7–10). Time and chance, and the poor wise man forgotten (9:11–18). Sayings on folly and rule (10:1–20). Act without knowing — "you do not know the work of God" (11:1–6). |
| 11:7–12:7 | **Rejoice and remember** | "The light is pleasant" (11:7–8). "Rejoice, young man … yet know that God will bring you to judgement" (11:9–10). "Remember your Creator" before the darkening, and the poem of ageing and death (12:1–7), ending with the dust returning to the earth and the spirit returning to God. |
| 12:8–14 | **Epilogue** | The frame voice repeats 1:2 (12:8). Qohelet is appraised as a sage (12:9–10). The words of the wise are "given by one Shepherd" (12:11). "My son, be warned" (12:12). "Fear God and keep his commandments … God will bring every act to judgement" (12:13–14). |

**Binding device.** The binding is a **frame (inclusio) around a two-half body**, with **refrains** doing the work that argument does in other books. `[T]` for the frame and the refrains; `[I]` for calling it the book's design.

- **The frame** is 1:2 ‖ 12:8, with the third-person voice also surfacing at 7:27.
- **The first half** (1:12–6:9) is marked by the "striving after wind" refrain. It asks what *profit* there is.
- **The second half** (6:10–12:7) is marked by "who knows / cannot find out". It asks what *knowledge* there is.
- **Across both halves** run the **enjoyment passages** (see the Echo Table). Each says, in varying words, that there is "nothing better" than to eat, drink, rejoice and see good in one's toil, and that this is God's gift. The lemmas for "eat" and "drink" appear together at 2:24; 3:13; 5:18 (Heb 5:17); 8:15; 9:7 (WLC). The joy-vocabulary carries the same note at 3:12, 22 and 11:7–10.

**The structural options, stated rather than chosen.** *Confidence in the table above: moderate.*

1. **A two-halves reading turning on 6:9/6:10, with verbal markers (A. G. Wright)** `[S]`. The midpoint count and the last "striving after wind" at 6:9 are verified here. Wright's finer numerical scheme is not.
2. **A frame-narrator reading (M. V. Fox)** `[S]`. On this reading the structure that matters is the frame and the body, and the body is not further planned.
3. **A reading with no discernible plan beyond loosely linked reflections** `[S]`. This is a long-standing position in the commentaries.

**The digs should test option 1 first**, because it is the one the text gives most purchase to.

## Microscript

*Under the sun, everything is הֶבֶל ("breath, vanity"). No toil and no wisdom secures a יִתְרוֹן ("advantage") against death, and no one can find out the work of God. So receive your חֵלֶק ("portion") — bread, wine, work and the wife you love — as God's gift, with joy. Fear God, and keep his commandments, for he will bring every deed into judgement.*

**Keyword: הֶבֶל ("breath, vanity").** It occurs **38 times in 30 verses** — more than half of its 73 occurrences in the Hebrew Bible (lemma 1892, WLC). The verses are 1:2; 1:14; 2:1, 11, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23, 26; 3:19; 4:4, 7, 8, 16; 5:7, 10; 6:2, 4, 9, 11, 12; 7:6, 15; 8:10, 14; 9:9; 11:8, 10; 12:8 (5:7, 10 = Heb 5:6, 9). `[T]`

**Structural pulse:** *no gain → no finding out → receive the portion → fear God → judgement.*

**Supporting keynotes:**

- **"Under the sun"**: תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ, **29×, all in this book** (surface and skeletal passes agree, WLC). `[T]`
- **God, only ever as אֱלֹהִים ("God")**: **40× in 36 verses**, while **the name יהוה ("the LORD") occurs 0×** in the whole book (lemmas 430 and 3068, WLC). `[T]`
- **עָמָל ("toil")**: the noun occurs 22× (lemma 5999), with the verb (lemma 5998) and the adjective עָמֵל ("toiling"; lemma 6001) alongside (WLC). `[T]`
- **טוֹב ("good")**: 52×. `[T]`
- **חֵלֶק ("portion")**: 8×. `[T]`

**What the absence of the divine name means.** The absence is a finding, not a gap. Qohelet speaks of God as the Creator and giver who stands over "the sons of man" (1:13; 3:10). He never speaks of him as Israel's covenant LORD, and בְּרִית ("covenant") and תּוֹרָה ("law") are also absent (0× each, WLC). `[T]` for the absences; `[I]` that this is a deliberate universal register. **Do not preach it as evidence that Qohelet is irreligious.** He speaks of God forty times.

**Test against the movements:**

- **Prologue** — the thesis and the question.
- **The king's experiment** — no gain, even for the greatest.
- **Times and toil** — God's work is fitting but unfindable, so receive the portion.
- **What is good?** — no one can find it out.
- **The one fate** — so eat, drink and work with your might.
- **Rejoice and remember** — joy under judgement, before death.
- **Epilogue** — fear God.

Each movement serves the spine. The epilogue's "keep his commandments" (12:13) is the only element the body does not anticipate verbally, apart from the one earlier occurrence of מִצְוָה ("commandment") at 8:5, where it refers to the king's command. **That is either the frame's resolution or its correction — see Trap 1.**

## Intertextual Map

**Ecclesiastes is citation-light.** It has no formula citations, and no passage names its source. Its argument is carried mainly by **internal repetition** — refrains, keywords and early-to-late returns. **Consequence for the sweep: the digs will lean on Move 4 (internal echo) far more than Move 2 (book usage).** Genesis 1–3, Deuteronomy and the Solomon narrative of Kings are the three live external sources. `[T]` / `[I]`

## Christological Trajectory

**Type of connection:** mainly **trajectory and contrast**, with one **typological** figure and one **eschatological** hinge. This is a wisdom book; it predicts nothing, and nothing here should be preached as prophecy.

**1. The son of David, king in Jerusalem — and the one greater than Solomon.** Qohelet presents himself as the wisest and greatest king (1:16; 2:9). He found no gain that death could not cancel, and could not find out the work of God (8:17). **The trajectory runs through that confession of limit.** Jesus presents himself as "something greater than Solomon" (πλεῖον Σολομῶνος, Matt 12:42 — verified SBLGNT), in whom "all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden" (Col 2:3). *Tests:*

- **Category:** kingship and wisdom — passes.
- **NT precedent:** explicit for Solomon, not for Ecclesiastes itself.
- **Escalation:** the son of David who could not find out God's work gives way to the Son who makes the Father known (John 1:18).

*Confidence: moderate-high as trajectory; do not claim the NT reads Ecclesiastes itself.* `[I]` / `[S]`

**2. The one fate, and the answer to יִתְרוֹן ("advantage").** The book's question — what gain survives death? — is left open by design. Qohelet's "who knows whether the spirit of man ascends upward?" (3:21) is met in the frame only by "the spirit will return to God" (12:7). **The New Testament's answer is resurrection, and it answers in Qohelet's own terms.** Paul ends his resurrection chapter: "your κόπος ('toil') is not κενός ('in vain') in the Lord" (1 Cor 15:58, verified SBLGNT). And in the Greek, the creation was "subjected to ματαιότης ('futility')" — **the LXX's word for Qohelet's הֶבֶל ('vanity') — in hope** (Rom 8:20; ματαιότης is used 3× in the NT: Rom 8:20; Eph 4:17; 2 Pet 2:18, verified SBLGNT). *Confidence: moderate.* The vocabulary tie is verified; whether Paul has Ecclesiastes in view is `[S]` and untested. `[I]`

**3. The rich fool and the heir.** Jesus' rich man says "eat, drink, be merry" (φάγε, πίε, εὐφραίνου, Luke 12:19). Those are the same three verbs as the Greek of Eccl 8:15, where Swete has φαγεῖν … πιεῖν … εὐφρανθῆναι ("to eat … to drink … to be merry") (verified in both). God's reply — "whose will these things be?" (Luke 12:20) — is Qohelet's complaint of 2:18–21. **The parable takes Qohelet's enjoyment and removes God from it, and that is exactly its folly.** *Confidence: moderate.* `[I]`

**4. The one Shepherd.** "Given by one Shepherd" (12:11) is the one place the book names the source of wisdom's words. **If** the Shepherd is God, and **if** Ezekiel's "one shepherd" (34:23; 37:24) is in the ear, the line anticipates the Shepherd who says "there will be one flock, εἷς ποιμήν ('one shepherd')" (John 10:16, verified SBLGNT). *Confidence: uncertain* — both "ifs" are contested. **Do not build a sermon on it.** `[I]`

**5. The judgement of every hidden thing.** "God will bring every act to judgement, everything which is hidden" (12:14; cf. 3:17; 11:9) is fulfilled in the day "when God will judge the secrets of men through Christ Jesus" (Rom 2:16) and before "the judgement seat of Christ" (2 Cor 5:10). *Confidence: high as trajectory.* `[I]`

**Moralism check.** Ecclesiastes tempts preachers toward two opposite moralisms. The **first is "enjoy life while you can"**: the gift becomes the goal and God becomes a footnote. The **second is "life is meaningless without God, so get religion"**: the book becomes an apologetic trap, and its honesty about the righteous perishing is dismissed.

**The book's own ground is neither.** It points to **God as giver** — "from the hand of God" (2:24), "the gift of God" (3:13; 5:19) — and to **God as judge** (12:14), before whom the reader is to stand in fear. The gospel grounding is that the giver has entered the "one fate" and come out the other side. **Joy under the sun is therefore not denial of death but trust in the one who has defeated it.** And the fear of God becomes the fear of those whom the Judge has already justified.

**Preach Christ as the answer to the question the book honestly leaves open. Do not preach him as a correction of a book that failed to ask it.** `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

**1. Deciding who Qohelet is before reading him.** This is the book's master trap, and there are three live options `[S]`:

- **(a) A frame reading.** Qohelet is a sceptic whose "under the sun" wisdom the frame narrator presents, and then corrects, in 12:13–14 (e.g. Longman) `[S]`.
- **(b) An affirmation reading.** Qohelet is an orthodox "preacher of joy", and the enjoyment passages are the book's positive message (e.g. Whybray) `[S]`.
- **(c) An ironic-epistemology reading.** The body shows autonomous observation running into contradiction, with the "remember / fear God" passages as the alternative starting point (e.g. Bartholomew) `[S]`.

*None of these commentators was consulted in this run. The labels are recalled and should be checked in Logos before they are cited.*

**How it sounds:** "Everything Qohelet says is the view of a man without God; ignore it until chapter 12." Or: "Qohelet is simply telling you to enjoy yourself." **Why it is wrong:** either verdict applied in advance flattens the tension the book deliberately sustains. God is present throughout the body (40× אֱלֹהִים, "God"). Yet the body also says things the frame goes beyond (3:21 against 12:7). **Where it bites:** 1:12–2:26; 3:16–22; 9:1–10. **Corrective:** declare, per dig, which voice is speaking and which reading the passage has been tested under.

**2. Carpe diem without the Giver.** **How it sounds:** "Life is short — enjoy it." **Why it is wrong:** every enjoyment passage grounds the joy in God's gift (2:24–26; 3:13; 5:18–20; 8:15; 9:7) or places it under judgement (11:9). Ecclesiastes has no "for tomorrow we die" — that is Isa 22:13 and 1 Cor 15:32. **Where it bites:** 9:7–10 and 11:9–10 especially. At 11:9 the preacher who stops before "yet know that God will bring you to judgement" has preached the opposite of the verse.

**3. Rendering הֶבֶל ("breath, vanity") as "meaningless", and preaching nihilism.** **How it sounds:** "Qohelet says life has no meaning." **Why it is wrong:** הֶבֶל is "breath, vapour" — transient and insubstantial, not senseless. The book uses it alongside real goods: the "days of your הֶבֶל ('fleeting life')" are also to be enjoyed with the wife you love (9:9). **Note for the preacher — translations diverge, and the congregation hears the version:** the **NIV84 renders it "meaningless" in 28 verses, including all four at 1:2**; the **ESV has "vanity" nearly throughout**; the **NASB95 varies** — "vanity" (1:2; 12:8), "futility" through most of the book, "emptiness" (5:7) and "fleeting" (9:9). **Where it bites:** 1:2 and every refrain. **Pastoral landmine:** in ministry among the elderly and the bereaved, "meaningless" lands very differently from "fleeting".

**4. Turning 3:1–8 into a calendar poem.** **How it sounds:** a funeral reading or time-management talk that stops at 3:8. **Why it is wrong:** the poem is the premise of 3:9–11. The times are God's, man "cannot find out the work which God has done", and the point is humility, not scheduling. **Where it bites:** 3:1–15.

**5. The woman of 7:26–28.** **How it sounds:** either a misogynist proof text or an embarrassed skip. **Why it is wrong:** the passage sits inside the "find / not find" cluster (מָצָא, "find", 8× in 7:23–29), where the finding that matters is universal: "God made *man* upright, but *they* have sought out many devices" (7:29). The text also carries an editorial question at 7:27, where BHS proposes redividing the feminine verb (see Presenting Situation). **Where it bites:** 7:23–29. **Corrective:** preach it within the cluster; name the difficulty; don't build on the verb.

**6. Allegorising 12:1–7 too confidently.** **How it sounds:** "the grinders are the teeth, the windows the eyes …" as settled fact. **Why it is wrong:** some images plausibly depict the ageing body; others depict a household in mourning or a cosmic darkening (12:2). The text is also textually difficult in several places (*BHS app.* 12:4, 5, 6 — at 12:6 the ketiv יִרְחַק ("is removed") stands against the qere יֵרָתֵק ("is snapped"); NASB95 "is broken"). **Where it bites:** 12:1–7. **Pastoral landmine:** this is the Bible's poem of old age; preach it to the aged with tenderness, not wit.

**7. Preaching 12:13–14 as the whole book — or cutting it off.** **How it sounds:** "The point of Ecclesiastes is: obey God." Or, from the other side: "The epilogue is a pious later addition." **Why it is wrong:** the first skips the journey the frame summarises. The second imports a composition-critical judgement as though it were a reading. **Note:** BHS marks 12:9 "prb add (gl)" (*app.* 12:9 ᵃ). That is the editors' conjecture, not a manuscript witness, and it should not be cited as "the text shows". **Where it bites:** 12:8–14.
