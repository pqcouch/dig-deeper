One Covenant, Two Kinds of Commandment? The Tripartite Division of the Mosaic Law Reconsidered

A critical engagement with Stephen J. Wellum, Brad Green and Ardel Caneday, and Justin Dillehay

Draft, 8 October 2026

Abstract

In Reformed theology the Mosaic law has traditionally been divided into moral, ceremonial and judicial law. Under that division the first is binding for ever, the second is abrogated and the third has expired except for its “general equity”. This paper sets two recent critiques of the division against a recent defence. The critiques are the extract from Gentry and Wellum’s Kingdom through Covenant and an essay by Green and Caneday. The defence is Dillehay’s article, which builds on Philip Ross. Each side’s strongest arguments are presented and tested against the Hebrew and Greek texts.

The critics establish that Scripture treats the Sinai covenant as a single covenant, ratified as a whole and brought to its end as a whole in Christ. The defenders establish that the Torah itself discerns different kinds of commandment, so that the New Testament’s different treatment of them is principled rather than arbitrary. Neither side’s account fits all the evidence.

The paper argues, tentatively, that the text supports a twofold distinction within one covenant. On one side stand commandments that express the created moral order and bind humanity as such. On the other stand commandments that constitute Israel as a separated, priestly nation. The judicial law is better understood as the polity’s application and sanction of both than as a third kind. The Decalogue is the covenant’s distinctive core and a reliable summary of the first kind, but its Fourth Word belongs to both kinds at once. The question of the division therefore comes down chiefly to the Sabbath, where the evidence remains genuinely divided.

1. Introduction

1.1 The question

Every Christian reader of the Pentateuch has to answer a practical question: why do Christians keep “you shall not steal” but not “you shall not eat the pig”? The classical Reformed answer divides the law into three. Moral law is summarised in the Decalogue and binds all people at all times. Ceremonial law (sacrifice, priesthood, purity, food and calendar) prefigured Christ and is abrogated. Judicial law (the civil statutes of Israel’s polity) “expired together with the State of that people”, and still obliges others only so far as “the general equity thereof may require”.1

Over the last century many biblical scholars have rejected the division as an imposition on a text that knows no such categories. The debate is not merely academic. It decides how a pastor answers the questioner holding Leviticus 18:22 in one hand and Leviticus 11:7 in the other, the Sabbatarian, and the Hebrew Roots enthusiast who points out that the Torah calls its laws “everlasting”.

1.2 Three contributions

Three recent pieces represent the main positions.

  1. Stephen J. Wellum, writing with Peter Gentry, rejects the division in four pages near the end of Kingdom through Covenant.2 He argues that Scripture views the law-covenant as a unit, that the covenant was temporary by design, and that Christians are no longer “under the law” as a covenant. Moral norms come to the Christian through “the law of Christ”.
  2. Brad Green and Ardel Caneday argue in a short essay that the law is “one indivisible law covenant with three discernible aspects”. They accept the categories as aspects and reject them as divisions.3
  3. Justin Dillehay defends the division in an article for The Gospel Coalition. Following Philip Ross, he argues from natural law, the Pentateuch, the prophets, the Gospels and Paul that the New Testament’s different treatment of different laws “reflects a difference in nature that already existed during the Old Testament and could have been discerned even then”.4

The three pieces differ in genre and apparatus. Wellum’s is the summary section of a monograph, with primary and secondary sources cited. Dillehay’s is a well-sourced popular article. Green and Caneday’s is a popular essay without notes. The aim here is not to grade them. It is to bring out the strongest form of each side’s case and test it against the text.

1.3 Method

The argument works from the original languages. Hebrew is cited from the Westminster Leningrad Codex, the Greek Old Testament from Swete’s diplomatic edition, and the New Testament from the SBL Greek New Testament.5 English versions are consulted for reference only, because every translation, the Septuagint included, already embodies interpretive decisions. Counts are reproducible and their method is stated. Old Testament passages are cited in Hebrew versification, with English versification in square brackets where the two differ. Patristic, medieval and confessional sources are cited from general knowledge unless otherwise noted. They are not load-bearing for the conclusion, but they should be verified before the paper is quoted.

2. The Tradition in Brief

The common claim that the division was “derived from Aquinas”6 does not hold. What is ancient is a twofold distinction. Irenaeus separates the “natural precepts” of the Decalogue, implanted in humanity from the beginning, from the precepts “of bondage” given after the golden calf.7 Tertullian argues for “a law unwritten” known to the fathers before Moses.8 Augustine distinguishes precepts for living (vitae agendae) from precepts that signify (vitae significandae).9 The rabbis, from a different direction, distinguish mišpāṭîm, laws reason would have required (murder, theft), from ḥuqqîm, decrees without evident rationale (pork, mixed fabrics, the red heifer).10

The threefold form, with praecepta moralia, caeremonialia, iudicialia, is the systematisation of Thomas Aquinas.11 Calvin adopted it.12 The Westminster Confession codified it.

Two features of the confessional form matter for what follows. First, the Confession does not ground the moral law’s authority in the division itself, or in Sinai. It grounds it in the law given to Adam and written on the heart. Sinai is that law “delivered” again “in ten commandments” (19.1–2). Second, the Confession itself calls the Sabbath commandment “positive, moral, and perpetual” (21.7). On the Confession’s own terms, then, moral and natural do not mean the same thing. Both points come back below.

3. The Case against the Division

3.1 One covenant, ratified as a whole

The critics’ first and strongest claim is that Scripture presents the Sinai law as a single covenant instrument, not as an anthology of separable codes. Exodus supports this. Moses recounts to the people “all the words of the LORD and all the judgements” (Exod 24:3: כָּל־דִּבְרֵי יְהוָה וְאֵת כָּל־הַמִּשְׁפָּטִים). That is the Decalogue together with the case law of 21:1–23:19. He then reads “the book of the covenant” (Exod 24:7: סֵפֶר הַבְּרִית). The blood is thrown “in accordance with all these words” (Exod 24:8: עַל כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה). The mišpāṭîm are not an appendix to the covenant; they are part of its stated terms.13

The New Testament assumes the same unity. Paul warns that the man who accepts circumcision as entry into the covenant ὀφειλέτης ἐστὶν ὅλον τὸν νόμον ποιῆσαι, “is a debtor to do the whole law” (Gal 5:3). Paul also quotes Deuteronomy 27:26 so as to include πᾶσιν τοῖς γεγραμμένοις ἐν τῷ βιβλίῳ τοῦ νόμου, “everything written in the book of the law” (Gal 3:10). James adds: ὅστις γὰρ ὅλον τὸν νόμον τηρήσῃ, πταίσῃ δὲ ἐν ἑνί, γέγονεν πάντων ἔνοχος, “whoever keeps the whole law but stumbles in one point has become guilty of all” (Jas 2:10).

3.2 The priesthood and the law

The single most important text for the critics is Hebrews 7:11–12, and Wellum rightly puts it at the centre. The author says the people received the law on the basis of the Levitical priesthood (Heb 7:11: ὁ λαὸς γὰρ ἐπ᾽ αὐτῆς νενομοθέτηται). He then infers that μετατιθεμένης γὰρ τῆς ἱερωσύνης ἐξ ἀνάγκης καὶ νόμου μετάθεσις γίνεται, “when the priesthood is changed, of necessity a change of law also takes place” (Heb 7:12). Nothing in the argument limits the “change of law” to ceremonial statutes. The law as given rests on the priesthood, so a new priest means a new legal order. Hebrews 8:13 draws the covenantal conclusion: the first covenant is πεπαλαίωκεν, “made old”, and ἐγγὺς ἀφανισμοῦ, “near to vanishing”.

3.3 Temporary by design

Paul presents the law as having a terminus built into its purpose. It “came 430 years after” the promise and does not annul it (Gal 3:17). It “was added because of transgressions until (ἄχρις οὗ) the seed should come” (Gal 3:19). It was a παιδαγωγός “until Christ”, and ἐλθούσης δὲ τῆς πίστεως οὐκέτι ὑπὸ παιδαγωγόν ἐσμεν, “now that faith has come, we are no longer under a guardian” (Gal 3:24–25). Christ is τέλος νόμου (Rom 10:4).

Paul describes himself as μὴ ὢν αὐτὸς ὑπὸ νόμον, “not myself under law”, and yet as μὴ ὢν ἄνομος θεοῦ ἀλλ᾽ ἔννομος Χριστοῦ, “not without God’s law but in-lawed to Christ” (1 Cor 9:20–21). Here Paul distinguishes God’s law from the Mosaic law without letting the former disappear.

The critics have one further text that the defenders seldom face. In Romans 7:6 Paul says believers κατηργήθημεν ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου, “have been released from the law”. The very next verse names the law in question by quoting the tenth commandment: οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις, “you shall not covet” (Rom 7:7). Whatever “released” means, the law from which the believer is released includes the Decalogue.

3.4 An interwoven text

Green and Caneday observe that the Holiness Code sets “love your neighbour as yourself” (Lev 19:18) beside the prohibition of mixed breeding, mixed seed and mixed fabric (Lev 19:19). The Hebrew paragraphing confirms the observation. In the Leningrad Codex, Leviticus 19:1–19 is one open paragraph; the chapter’s first petuchah falls after verse 19.14 The ancient scribal division gives no sign that verses 18 and 19 belong to different kinds of law.

Jesus’ own citations cut across the categories too. In Mark 7:10 he quotes the fifth commandment and, in the same breath, Exodus 21:17, a capital case law: Τίμα τὸν πατέρα σου καὶ τὴν μητέρα σου, καί· Ὁ κακολογῶν πατέρα ἢ μητέρα θανάτῳ τελευτάτω. Mark calls both “Moses said”, and Matthew 15:4 has “God said”. Both count as “the commandment of God” (Mark 7:8–9).

3.5 Moral obligation grounded in God, not in Sinai

The critics do not deny that Christians are morally bound. They locate the ground of that obligation elsewhere. Green and Caneday’s treatment of Ephesians 6:1–3 is their best passage. Paul quotes the fifth commandment to Gentile churches that were never under Sinai. On their reading he does so because the commandment “codifies morality that was true from the beginning”, not because the Decalogue as a covenant document remains in force.

The Greek strengthens their case in a way they do not notice. Greek Exodus 20:12 promises long life ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς τῆς ἀγαθῆς ἧς Κύριος ὁ θεός σου δίδωσίν σοι, “upon the good land which the Lord your God gives you”. Deuteronomy 5:16 is near-identical. Paul stops at ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς (Eph 6:3). He keeps the command and the promise but cuts the promise loose from Canaan. This is a redemptive-historical transposition made inside a single verse.15

3.6 Where the critics overreach

Three overstatements weaken the critique.

The claim that nobody distinguished. Green and Caneday conclude that “no faithful Israelite and no New Testament author ever tried to pull [the threads] apart”. The rabbinic ḥuqqîm/mišpāṭîm distinction contradicts the first half. Every New Testament citation of Leviticus 19:18 contradicts the second: Matthew 22:39, Mark 12:31, Romans 13:9, Galatians 5:14 and James 2:8 all take verse 18 and leave verse 19 behind.

The claim about the source of moral law. Wellum quotes Douglas Moo: “our source for determining God’s eternal moral law is Christ and the apostles, not the Mosaic law or even the Ten Commandments”.16 This sits awkwardly with how the apostles actually work. In the chapter Wellum calls “crucial”, eleven verses before his key text, Paul asks ἢ καὶ ὁ νόμος ταῦτα οὐ λέγει; “does not the law also say these things?” (1 Cor 9:8). He then quotes the ox-muzzling statute of Deuteronomy 25:4 and draws from it the minister’s right to support: δι᾽ ἡμᾶς γὰρ ἐγράφη, “for our sake it was written” (1 Cor 9:10). In 2 Corinthians 13:1 the two-or-three-witness rule of Deuteronomy 19:15 governs church discipline. In 1 Corinthians 5:13, Deuteronomy’s refrain after capital sanctions, “purge the evil from your midst” (cf. Deut 17:7), becomes the formula of excommunication. The apostles are the authoritative interpreters of the moral law, but they interpret it from Moses.

The claim about “keeping” the law. Wellum says Christians “fulfil” the law rather than “do” or “keep” it. Yet Paul writes that ἡ περιτομὴ οὐδέν ἐστιν… ἀλλὰ τήρησις ἐντολῶν θεοῦ, “circumcision is nothing… but keeping the commandments of God” (1 Cor 7:19). He also speaks of the uncircumcised who τὰ δικαιώματα τοῦ νόμου φυλάσσῃ, “keeps the righteous requirements of the law” (Rom 2:26).

4. The Case for the Division

4.1 Distinctions within the Torah

Dillehay’s most important contribution, following Ross, is to show that the distinction is not imported into the Old Testament from the New. The Torah supplies it. Three groups of texts are relevant. The first two are his. The third is added here.

Laws grounded in Israel’s status, and explicitly not binding on outsiders. Deuteronomy 14:21 forbids Israel to eat an animal that died of itself. In the same verse Israel may give it to the sojourner to eat or sell it to the foreigner: כִּי עַם קָדוֹשׁ אַתָּה לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, “for you are a holy people to the LORD your God”. The ground is Israel’s consecration, and the outsider is expressly excluded. Leviticus 17:15 handles the same food differently. There the sojourner who eats it, like the native, must wash and is unclean until evening. That shows the “sojourner” category is not uniform across the Torah. But in neither book is the law universal.17

Laws whose breach defiled the land under the nations. The sexual prohibitions of Leviticus 18 bind “the native and the sojourner” alike (18:26). The chapter then grounds them in history. The previous inhabitants committed “all these abominations” and the land “vomited out the nation that was before you” (18:27–28). The Canaanites were never under Sinai, yet they were judged by these standards.

Laws whose stated purpose is separation. Leviticus 20:24–26 grounds the clean/unclean food distinction in God’s prior act. “I am the LORD your God, who has separated you from the peoples. You shall therefore separate between the clean beast and the unclean” (20:24–25). The food laws mark Israel off from the nations by design, and the nations were never judged for breaking them. Genesis 9:3–4 confirms this from the other side. Noah, and through him all humanity, is given “every moving thing that lives” for food, with the single exception of blood. The food restrictions are therefore later and narrower than the creation-wide permission.

These texts separate commandments by addressee and stated rationale. Some bind humanity, and the nations were held accountable to them. Others constitute Israel as a separated people.

4.2 The primacy of morality over the cult

The prophets rank the law’s demands. Dillehay cites Hosea 6:6, and the Hebrew supports his reading of it as a statement of priority, not of abolition. The first colon is absolute in form: “steadfast love I desire, and not sacrifice” (חֶסֶד חָפַצְתִּי וְלֹא־זָבַח). The parallel colon is explicitly comparative: “knowledge of God more than burnt offerings” (וְדַעַת אֱלֹהִים מֵעֹלוֹת). The Greek renders both cola comparatively (ἔλεος θέλω ἢ θυσίαν). Matthew’s two citations follow the Hebrew form, καὶ οὐ θυσίαν (Matt 9:13; 12:7).

Psalm 51 has the same structure. The psalm that says “you do not delight in sacrifice” (Ps 51:18 [16]) ends “then you will delight in right sacrifices” (51:21 [19]). The strongest text of this kind is Jeremiah 7:22–23: “I did not speak to your fathers, nor command them… concerning burnt offering and sacrifice; but this thing I commanded them: Obey my voice.” Jesus’ “weightier matters of the law”, τὰ βαρύτερα τοῦ νόμου (Matt 23:23), draws on this prophetic tradition. Dillehay suggests Micah 6:8 as the source. Zechariah 7:9, which contains all three Hebrew roots (mišpāṭ, ʾĕmet, ḥesed), is at least as likely.

4.3 The Decalogue’s distinct status

The Torah sets the Ten Words apart from the rest of the legislation in several ways:

  • They alone were spoken by God directly to the whole assembly, “and he added no more” (Deut 5:22: וְלֹא יָסָף).
  • They alone were written “by the finger of God” on stone (Exod 31:18).
  • Deuteronomy alone calls them “the ten words” (Deut 4:13; 10:4; cf. Exod 34:28), a designation found nowhere inside either Decalogue.18
  • The tablets were placed inside the ark (Deut 10:5: בָּאָרוֹן), while “this book of the torah” was placed beside it (Deut 31:26: מִצַּד אֲרוֹן).

This is the textual root of the tripartite intuition. The critics cannot simply set it aside. It should be noted, however, that this is a distinction of status within the covenant. It does not by itself establish that the Decalogue’s content is coextensive with the moral law (see §5.2).

4.4 The New Testament discriminates among the commandments

The New Testament does not treat the commandments of the law as interchangeable.

  • 1 Corinthians 7:19 contrasts one commandment (circumcision, Gen 17; Lev 12:3) with “keeping the commandments of God”. Dillehay’s observation is apt: unlike the commandments Paul goes on to enforce, circumcision did not exist before Abraham.
  • Romans 13:9 quotes four Decalogue prohibitions and then sums up “any other commandment” in Leviticus 19:18. That is a deliberate selection from a single paragraph.
  • 1 Timothy 1:9–10 arranges a vice list in Decalogue order: πατρολῴαις καὶ μητρολῴαις (fifth), ἀνδροφόνοις (sixth), πόρνοις, ἀρσενοκοίταις (seventh), ἀνδραποδισταῖς (eighth, as man-stealing, cf. Exod 21:16), ψεύσταις, ἐπιόρκοις (ninth). Paul is still structuring Christian moral instruction by the Ten after the cross.
  • 1 Corinthians 5:1 condemns a union “not found even among the Gentiles” (ἥτις οὐδὲ ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν), namely having one’s father’s wife, as in Leviticus 18:8. Paul assumes that the Gentile conscience knows what Leviticus 18 forbids.
  • Ephesians 6:2 calls the fifth commandment ἐντολὴ πρώτη ἐν ἐπαγγελίᾳ, “the first commandment with a promise”. Paul counts within the Ten as a recognised series.

4.5 “General equity” in apostolic practice

The texts in §3.6 that tell against Moo’s dictum also illustrate the Confession’s category of “general equity”. In 1 Corinthians 9:9–10, 2 Corinthians 13:1 and 1 Corinthians 5:13, statutes of Israel’s polity are carried by analogy into the life of the church. The apostles neither reject these statutes as obsolete nor apply them as they stand. This is the strongest New Testament evidence for the judicial category, though, as §4.6 argues, it does not show that the judicial law is a separate kind of law.

4.6 Where the defenders overreach

Two kinds, not three. Every line of Dillehay’s Old Testament evidence separates one pair: the universal or moral from the Israel-specific or cultic. None separates the judicial law as a third kind. Indeed the Torah fuses the moral and the judicial. Leviticus 20:10 attaches the death penalty (מוֹת־יוּמַת) to the adultery forbidden in Leviticus 18. The judicial category therefore does not divide one set of laws from another. It divides a single law into its rule and its sanction. Dillehay’s own definition shows this: the judicial law binds others “only insofar as its general equity reflects the natural law”. On that account it has no force of its own.

Matthew 5:18–20. Dillehay applies the verse to the moral law’s abiding force. Jesus, however, speaks of ἰῶτα ἓν ἢ μία κεραία, “one iota or one stroke”, and of “one of the least of these commandments” (5:19). On any reckoning that includes ceremonial detail. The verse either covers the whole law, fulfilled in different modes (Ross’s eschatological, soteriological and moral fulfilment, which Dillehay also cites), or it does not distinguish among commandments at all.

The antitheses. Dillehay shows convincingly that Jesus’ teaching on anger, lust and love of enemies has Old Testament roots (Exod 23:4–5; Prov 25:21–22; Job 31:1; 2 Kgs 6:22). He passes over the two antitheses that bear on judicial law. On oaths, the Torah regulates vows (e.g. Deut 23:22 [21]) while Jesus says μὴ ὀμόσαι ὅλως, “do not swear at all” (Matt 5:34). On retaliation, Jesus takes the court’s rule of proportion (Exod 21:24) and applies it to personal retaliation (Matt 5:38–42).

5. Five Test Cases

The two cases rest largely on different texts. Five further sets of evidence test both, and none of the three papers adequately addresses them.

5.1 What the Torah calls “for ever”

The Torah repeatedly calls its laws perpetual. A phrase search of the whole Leningrad Codex for ḥuqqat ʿôlām and ḥoq ʿôlām, “a perpetual statute / everlasting due”, finds 34 occurrences. Thirty-two are in Exodus–Numbers.19 Every one of the Torah occurrences attaches to cultic or priestly law. They concern:

  • Passover and Unleavened Bread;
  • the lampstand;
  • the priests’ garments, washing and portions;
  • fat and blood;
  • the Day of Atonement;
  • the festivals;
  • the trumpets;
  • the red heifer;
  • the Levites’ service.

None is attached to the Decalogue or to the ethical commands of Leviticus 19. Circumcision (Gen 17:13) and the Sabbath (Exod 31:16) are each called a בְּרִית עוֹלָם, “an everlasting covenant”.

This cuts in two directions. Against the popular tripartite instinct, it shows that the Torah’s own language of permanence attaches to the ceremonial, not the moral. Against the critics, it shows that the belief Wellum attributes to Second Temple Judaism, that the law was eternal and unchangeable, came from reading the Torah’s own wording rather than from innovation. Any account in which these institutions end in Christ owes an account of ʿôlām.20 Paul’s treatment of circumcision suggests the shape of such an account. He sets aside an “everlasting covenant” sign as “nothing” (1 Cor 7:19; Gal 5:6; 6:15) without contradicting Genesis 17. On this reading, what was everlasting for the order it served gives way to what that order signified.

5.2 The Sabbath

All three papers point to the Sabbath as the hinge, though only Dillehay says so explicitly: “were it not for the fourth commandment’s inclusion, I suspect no one would object to seeing the Ten Commandments as a codification of permanent moral law.”

Evidence that it belongs to the created order:

  • Genesis 2:2–3: God ceased, blessed and sanctified the seventh day.
  • Exodus 20:11 grounds the command in that creation pattern, though with a change of verb from שָׁבַת to נוּחַ.
  • Exodus 16 shows the Sabbath in force before Sinai.
  • Hebrews 4:9: ἄρα ἀπολείπεται σαββατισμὸς τῷ λαῷ τοῦ θεοῦ, “a sabbath-rest remains for the people of God”.
  • It stands in the Ten.

Evidence that it belongs to Israel’s constitution as a separated people:

  • The noun שַׁבָּת does not occur in Genesis. Its first occurrence is Exodus 16:23, and Genesis records no human Sabbath-keeping.21
  • Exodus 16:29 presents it as a gift to Israel: “See, the LORD has given you the Sabbath” (נָתַן לָכֶם הַשַּׁבָּת).
  • Nehemiah 9:14 says God “made known” his holy Sabbath at Sinai.
  • It is called a “sign between me and the sons of Israel” (Exod 31:13, 17; Ezek 20:12).
  • Deuteronomy grounds it in Israel’s redemption from Egypt, not in creation (Deut 5:15).
  • Isaiah 1:13 lists it with new moons and offerings among the cultic acts God cannot endure when divorced from justice.
  • In the New Testament it is grouped among things that are σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων, “a shadow of what was to come” (Col 2:16–17).
  • It is left to conscience in Romans 14:5.
  • Jesus’ second appeal to “mercy, not sacrifice” is a ruling in a Sabbath dispute (Matt 12:7).

The evidence is genuinely divided. Two consequences follow for the debate.

The first concerns the defenders’ criterion. Dillehay’s test for moral law, “one had always been wrong; the other hadn’t”, does not cleanly sort the Fourth Word. The Westminster Confession’s own description of the Sabbath command as “positive, moral, and perpetual” (21.7) concedes that a perpetual command can be one nature does not teach. If so, natural law cannot be the criterion that identifies the moral law. The only remaining marker is inclusion in the Decalogue, which is what the natural-law argument set out to establish independently.

The second concerns the patristic witnesses. Irenaeus and Tertullian, both quoted by Dillehay, treat the Sabbath as a sign that the righteous before Moses did not keep.22 They support a natural-law Decalogue without the Sabbath.

5.3 The stone tablets: 2 Corinthians 3

None of the three papers discusses 2 Corinthians 3, though it bears directly on the Decalogue. Paul speaks of ἡ διακονία τοῦ θανάτου ἐν γράμμασιν ἐντετυπωμένη λίθοις, “the ministry of death, carved in letters on stones” (3:7). That description fits only the tablets. He then calls it τὸ καταργούμενον, “what is being set aside” (3:11), using the same verb as Ephesians 2:15. The reading of “the old covenant” is veiled until ἐν Χριστῷ καταργεῖται, “it is set aside in Christ” (3:14).

At first sight this is the critics’ strongest text. A tripartite reply is available. What is set aside is the ministry or covenant (διακονία, διαθήκη), not the moral content. And the contrast of 3:3, οὐκ ἐν πλαξὶν λιθίναις ἀλλ᾽ ἐν πλαξὶν καρδίαις σαρκίναις, “not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts”, is a contrast of where the law is written, not whether. On this reading 2 Corinthians 3 supports neither “the Decalogue endures as covenant document” nor “the Decalogue’s content is retired”. It supports a third position: the covenant administered on stone has ended, and what it inscribed is now inscribed by the Spirit.

5.4 Acts 15 and the sojourner laws

The only New Testament passage that formally asks the question at issue is Acts 15. Believing Pharisees insist that Gentile converts be circumcised and told τηρεῖν τὸν νόμον Μωϋσέως, “to keep the law of Moses” (15:5). The council refuses to put on the disciples “a yoke… which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear” (15:10). It nevertheless requires four abstentions (15:20, 29): from what is polluted by idols, from πορνεία, from what is strangled, and from blood.23

It has often been proposed that these four correspond to the laws Leviticus 17–18 imposes on “the sojourner who sojourns among them” (וּמִן־הַגֵּר הַגָּר בְּתוֹכָם):

  • sacrifice only at the tent (Lev 17:8–9);
  • no eating of blood (17:10–12);
  • draining the blood of game (17:13);
  • the sexual prohibitions (18:26).

The correspondence is an inference, not a certainty. If it holds, the apostles answered the question by neither the tripartite scheme nor the “only what is repeated” rule. They used the Torah’s own distinction between what it required of Israel and what it required of the Gentile living among Israel. That is the line §4.1 found inside the Torah.

5.5 New-covenant torah

The prophets’ new-covenant promises are not lawless. Jeremiah 31:33 reads נָתַתִּי אֶת־תּוֹרָתִי בְּקִרְבָּם, “I will put my torah within them”. Ezekiel 36:27 reads בְּחֻקַּי תֵּלֵכוּ וּמִשְׁפָּטַי תִּשְׁמְרוּ, “you will walk in my statutes and keep my judgements”. That is the exact Levitical pair of Leviticus 18:4–5, 26. Hebrews 8:10 quotes Jeremiah in the Greek form νόμους μου, “my laws”, plural for the Hebrew singular. The critics must explain why the new covenant is promised as the internalisation of torah rather than its retirement. The defenders must explain why the internalised torah does not include the sign-laws Ezekiel himself names (Ezek 20:12). The most natural reading of both texts is that the new covenant writes on the heart what the old covenant wrote on stone. That is the reading §5.3 reached from 2 Corinthians 3.

6. Evaluation

The table below summarises how each side’s main claims fare against the evidence.

Claim Verdict Key texts
The Sinai law is one covenant, ratified as a whole (critics) Sustained Exod 24:3–8; Gal 3:10; 5:3; Jas 2:10
The whole covenant ends as covenant in Christ (critics) Sustained Heb 7:11–12; 8:13; Gal 3:19–25; Rom 7:6–7; 2 Cor 3:7–14
Moral obligation is grounded in God and creation, not in Sinai (both sides) Common ground Gen 9:3–6; Lev 18:24–28; Rom 1:32; 2:14–15; Eph 6:1–3
No one, ancient or apostolic, distinguished kinds of law (Green and Caneday) Fails Rom 13:9; 1 Cor 7:19; Matt 23:23; the rabbinic ḥuqqîm/mišpāṭîm
Christ and the apostles, not Moses, are the source of moral norms (Wellum, via Moo) Needs restating 1 Cor 9:8–10; 2 Cor 13:1; 1 Cor 5:13; 1 Tim 1:9–10
The Torah itself discerns universal and Israel-specific laws (defenders) Sustained Deut 14:21; Lev 18:26–28; 20:24–26
The prophets rank morality above cult (defenders) Sustained Hos 6:6; Jer 7:22–23; Ps 51:18, 21
The judicial law is a distinct third kind (defenders) Not shown Lev 18 with 20:10 (rule and sanction fused)
The Decalogue has a unique status within the covenant (defenders) Sustained Deut 5:22; 10:5; 31:26; Exod 31:18
The Decalogue’s content is coextensive with natural moral law (defenders) Contested at the Fourth Word Exod 16:29; 31:13–17; Ezek 20:12; Col 2:16–17; WCF 21.7

Two things stand out. The critics win the covenantal argument: the Sinai covenant is one, and it has ended as a covenant. The defenders win the taxonomic argument: the law contains discernible kinds of commandment, discernible already in the Old Testament. Neither side has the whole truth. Each fails where it tries to make its own argument settle the other question. The critics try to infer from the covenant’s unity that its contents cannot be distinguished. The defenders try to infer from the contents’ distinctness that one part of the covenant survives as a code.

7. A Tentative Conclusion

On the question “tripartite or not?”, the evidence surveyed here leads to a qualified answer.

First, against the critics. The tripartite tradition is right that the Old Testament itself distinguishes kinds of law. It is also right that the New Testament’s different treatment of them is principled. Jewett’s claim that the distinction “rests upon the teaching of the New Testament, not upon location in the Old Testament”24 does not survive Deuteronomy 14:21, Leviticus 18:24–28 and Leviticus 20:24–26. Nor does Green and Caneday’s claim that no one ever drew the distinction. On this point Ross and Dillehay have the better argument.

Second, against the defenders. The text does not support the threefold form. The distinction it does support is twofold:

  • commandments expressing the created moral order, binding on humanity as such and attested before and outside Sinai (Gen 9; Lev 18:24–28; Rom 1–2);
  • commandments constituting Israel as a separated and priestly people (food, purity, cult, calendar and the covenant signs), marked by the language of separation (Lev 20:24–26) and of perpetuity within Israel’s generations (§5.1).

The judicial law is not a third kind. It is the polity’s application and sanction of both kinds. The apostles carry it into the church by analogy, which is what the Confession means by “general equity”. This was, in substance, the position of Irenaeus and Tertullian.

Third, against both. The Decalogue is neither simply “the moral law” nor simply “the old covenant”. It is the covenant’s distinctive core, set apart in its giving, its writing and its deposit. Nine of its Words reliably summarise the created moral order, and the New Testament keeps using them in exactly that way (Rom 13:9; Eph 6:2–3; 1 Tim 1:9–10). The Fourth Word belongs to both kinds at once. It is grounded in creation, yet given to Israel as a sign. It is honoured in the promise of a remaining rest, yet called a shadow. As a covenant document the Decalogue shares the fate of the covenant (2 Cor 3:7–11). As a summary of creation morality it does not, because that morality preceded Sinai and is now written on the heart (Jer 31:33; 2 Cor 3:3).

The tentative verdict, then, is as follows. The tripartite division is a serviceable teaching heuristic. Its core intuition, that the law contains kinds of commandment discernible in the text, is better grounded than its critics allow. In its classical form it claims two things the text does not support: that the judicial law is a separate kind, and that the Decalogue’s boundaries are exactly those of the moral law. The critics are right about the covenant and wrong about the contents. The defenders are right about the contents and over-precise about the categories.

The practical outcomes of the two positions are almost identical, with one exception: the Sabbath. On the textual evidence that remains an open question, not a settled one. A position closest to the evidence might be called one covenant, two kinds of commandment.

Three questions remain for further work:

  1. A full exegesis of 2 Corinthians 3:3–18 against Exodus 34 and Jeremiah 31.
  2. A test of the proposed Leviticus 17–18 → Acts 15 correspondence against the Hebrew and the textual history of the decree.
  3. A study of ʿôlām in its covenantal settings (Gen 9:16; 17:7, 13; Exod 31:16; 2 Sam 23:5; Isa 55:3; Jer 32:40), asking whether “everlasting covenant” language in the prophets transfers the Mosaic institutions or replaces them.
