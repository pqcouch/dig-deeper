Headline Findings

  1. 2:16–23 is the “therefore” of 2:9–15, and it closes the frame that 2:8 opened. [T] “The elements of the world” stands at 2:8 and 2:20 only, “of men” at 2:8 and 2:22 (with “according to” at both), so 2:8–23 is one polemic, bracketed (SBLGNT, by lemma). Inside the bracket every rule is answered by something 2:9–15 has already said: the decrees (δόγμα, 2:14) are what the rival now “decrees” (δογματίζομαι, 2:20, the verb’s only NT use); the head over all rule (2:10) is the head the rival does not hold (2:19); the dying with Christ in baptism (2:12–13) is the premise of “if you died with Christ” (2:20). — Surfaced by: Phase 0.6, Tool 3, Tool 4 (οὖν, 2:16; the first-class “if”, 2:20), Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 4. Why it matters: a sermon on 2:16–23 that has not preached 2:9–15 has nothing for the “therefore” to stand on, and a sermon that stops at 2:19 never reaches the reason (2:20).

  2. The passage turns on σῶμα (“body”), used three times in eight verses in three senses — and it refuses to let the third stand in for the first two. [T] The substance that belongs to Christ (2:17, NASB95 “substance”), the church that grows from its head (2:19, “the entire body”), and the physical body treated harshly (2:23, “severe treatment of the body”); σωματικῶς (“bodily”) at 2:9 stands just before. 2:23 then sets σῶμα (“body”) against σάρξ (“flesh”): severity to the body does nothing to the flesh. That contrast holds on every reading of the 2:23 crux (Tool 8; Difficult Verses) [I], high. — Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 3, Original Language Observations, Tool 8 (only the KJV lets the congregation hear “body” at 2:17). Why it matters: the rival offers a bodily regimen; Paul answers with the body of Christ — his substance and his church — and says the problem was never the body but the flesh.

  3. The warnings put a human being in the umpire’s seat; 3:15 puts Christ’s peace there with the same verb, uncompounded — and 3:12–16 reclaims two of the warning’s words. [T] for the forms; [S: audit 1] and [S: audit 2] for the ratings. “Let no one disqualify you” (μηδεὶς ὑμᾶς καταβραβευέτω, 2:18) is answered by “let the peace of Christ rule (βραβευέτω) in your hearts” (3:15). Each verb occurs once in the NT, and the simple verb answers the compound — a compound-against-simple play Paul uses elsewhere in the language of judging (διακρίνω, κρίνω, κατακρίνω — “discern”, “judge”, “condemn” — 1 Cor 11:29–32) (SBLGNT) [T]. The warning of 2:18 is the last of a four-clause “no one … you” series in three moods (2:4, 8, 16, 18) [T]. That “judge” (2:16) and “disqualify” (2:18) are imperatives, and so share the letter’s four third-person imperatives with 3:15 and 3:16, is a grammatical coincidence, not a designed frame: 3:16’s ἐνοικείτω (“let … dwell”) is an indwelling verb, kin to κατοικέω at 1:19 and 2:9, not a verb of judging [S: audit 2]. Two words return with their value reversed. ταπεινοφροσύνη, the rival’s “self-abasement” (2:18, 23), becomes the “humility” God’s chosen ones put on (3:12) — these two are its only pejorative uses in the NT (SBLGNT) [T]. And the regulations’ mere “word of wisdom” (2:23) becomes “the word of Christ … in all wisdom” (3:16) — λόγος with σοφία stands in one verse only at 2:23 and 3:16 in the letter (SBLGNT) [T]. The “teaching” of 3:16 answers 1:28, not the “teachings” of 2:22: Col 1:28 and 3:16 are the only NT verses joining νουθετέω (“admonish”) and διδάσκω (“teach”), both “in all wisdom” — the apostle’s ministry handed on to the church (SBLGNT) [T]. σύνδεσμος (“ligament”, 2:19 → “bond”, 3:14) keeps its value and is continuity, not reversal [S: audit 2]. Confidence: high on the words; moderate–high on the umpire reversal (2:18 → 3:15; audits 1 and 2); moderate on the two reclaimed words; low–moderate on 3:12–16 as a designed reversal of the whole passage, since 3:12–17 shares no more of this passage’s rarer words than other stretches do, and a control passage (1:26–2:4) shows as many reversals (Tool 11) [S: audit 2]. Pulpit (ESV): “disqualify” / “rule” (2:18; 3:15) and “asceticism” / “humility” (2:18, 23; 3:12) hide both links; the preacher has to supply them. — Surfaced by: Tool 4, Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 4.

  4. The verdict on the regulations is given three times in the letter’s own formula of identification — “which are”. [T] ἅ ἐστιν σκιά (“which are a shadow”, 2:17), ἅ ἐστιν πάντα εἰς φθοράν (“which are all for perishing”, 2:22) and ἅτινά ἐστιν λόγον … ἔχοντα σοφίας (“which have a word of wisdom”, 2:23) are the letter’s only plural “which are” clauses. The same relative-plus-“is” formula named Christ the image (1:15), the beginning (1:18), the head (2:10), and named the mystery “Christ in you” (1:27) and love “the bond of perfection” (3:14) (SBLGNT). — Surfaced by: Tool 3, Tool 4, Tool 10, Tool 7. Why it matters: the passage does not condemn the calendar or the food; it defines them — a shadow, perishable, a word without value — and only Christ is defined as substance.

  5. The rules are human-made, and the passage says so three ways. [T] “According to the commandments and teachings of men” (2:22) cites Isaiah 29:13 in its Greek form — ἔνταλμα (“commandment”) with διδασκαλία (“teaching”) stands in one verse of the whole LXX, Isa 29:13, and Colossians keeps the LXX order against the Gospels’ form [S: audit 1], independently confirmed here. ἐθελοθρησκία (“self-made religion”, 2:23) occurs nowhere in the NT or in Swete’s LXX before this verse. And the slogans of 2:21 — “do not handle, do not taste, do not touch” — are addressed in the second person singular, the only singular “you” in a passage that otherwise speaks to “you” plural (SBLGNT) [T]. — Surfaced by: Tool 11 (the Citation Triad), Tool 7, Tool 8 (the Hebrew of Isa 29:13 has no “teachings” at all — the Greek is the NT’s own text), Tool 10. Why it matters: the passage’s quarrel is not with discipline but with religion invented by people and imposed on persons one at a time, outside the body.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level [S: overview], confirmed by the text: to keep a young Gentile church walking “in Him” as they began (2:6), by setting before them the Christ in whom all the fullness dwells, so that no rival “philosophy” can persuade them they lack anything (2:8–10).

Passage level. Two commands and a question, with their grounds. The commands are both negative and both third-person: “let no one judge you” (2:16) and “let no one disqualify you” (2:18) [T]. The question is a rebuke: “if you died with Christ … why … do you submit to decrees?” (2:20) [T]. The passage contains no second-person command at all — the readers are not told to do anything here except refuse other people’s verdicts on them and see the absurdity of submitting [T] (the imperatives of Colossians 2 are περιπατεῖτε, 2:6; βλέπετε, 2:8; κρινέτω, 2:16; καταβραβευέτω, 2:18 — SBLGNT parsing). The positive commands wait for 3:1 (“keep seeking the things above”). So the passage’s purpose is to free the church from a rival tribunal and a rival regimen, by placing both under Christ — the calendar under his substance (2:17), the visionary under his headship (2:19), the regimen under his death (2:20) [I], high.

The passage serves the book’s purpose by making the “fullness” concrete: if all the fullness dwells in Christ and you have been filled in him (2:9–10), then a shadow cannot add to the substance (2:17), and growth comes from the head, not from a visionary (2:19) [I].

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 2:6–15 laid the ground: walk in him as you received him (2:6–7); see that no one takes you captive “according to the tradition of men, according to the elements of the world, and not according to Christ” (2:8); for in him the fullness dwells, and you are filled in him, who is head over all rule and authority (2:9–10); you were circumcised, buried, raised, made alive and forgiven with him; the certificate “consisting of decrees” was cancelled and nailed to the cross; the rulers and authorities were stripped and paraded (2:11–15) [T]. 2:8 named the threat in general; 2:9–15 gave the reason it has no hold. What 2:8–15 has set up and not yet provided is the particulars — what the “tradition of men” and “the elements of the world” actually look like when they arrive at Colossae, and what the readers are to do about them [I].

Necessity answer. 2:16–23 exists here because the argument now has its ground and can name its target. οὖν (“therefore”, 2:16) draws on 2:9–15, and the passage then closes the bracket 2:8 opened: “the elements of the world” (2:8 → 2:20), “of men” with “according to” (2:8 → 2:22) [T]. Every specific in the passage is answered by something already said:

Why here and not elsewhere. The passage must come after 2:9–15 (it has no ground without it) and before 3:1–4 (whose matching conditional, Εἰ οὖν συνηγέρθητε, “therefore if you have been raised”, answers 2:20’s Εἰ ἀπεθάνετε, “if you died”) [T]. The letter’s logic of union runs died-with → raised-with, so the negative half (what you died to: the elements, the decrees, the regimen) comes before the positive (what you seek: the things above) [I], high. The overview calls 2:20 and 3:1 “two matching conditionals” that “turn the warning into the new life” [S: overview]; the text confirms it, and 3:3 repeats ἀπεθάνετε (“you died”) — ἀποθνῄσκω stands only at 2:20 and 3:3 in the letter (SBLGNT) [T].

Implication. The passage is the hinge on which chapter 2 turns into chapter 3. Preached on its own it sounds like a list of prohibitions about prohibitions. Preached in position it is the moment the cross of 2:14–15 is applied to the church’s calendar, menu and spiritual ambition — and the moment the letter turns from what Christ has done to how the church lives (3:1) [I].

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 2 finds no earlier use of Isaiah 29 or of the calendar texts in Colossians, so the book-usage tracking adds nothing external. Move 4 sharpens the answer: the passage is planted with words the letter takes up again in 3:5–16 (ταπεινοφροσύνη, σύνδεσμος, σῶμα, the umpire, word + wisdom + teaching, “living in”), so 2:16–23 is not only the end of the warning but the negative template for the new life [I], moderate–high.

Immediately before: 2:6–15, the ground (above). Immediately after: 3:1–4 — “Therefore if you have been raised up with Christ, keep seeking the things above” — and then 3:5, “Therefore consider the members of your earthly body as dead” (NASB95), the letter’s own answer to what severity to the body could not do (Tool 11, Move 4). Section: the polemic of chapter 2 (2:6–23), of which this is the applied half [T]/[I]. NA28 starts paragraphs at 2:16 and 2:20 [T: as NA28 prints it].

Widely read out of context? Yes, 2:16–17, in Sabbath debates (Preaching Pitfalls; Difficult Verses). The immediate context — the “therefore” of 2:9–15, and the shadow/substance verdict in 2:17 — governs the verse, not the later debate [I].

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
2:16–17 First warning: the calendar and the menu “Let no one judge you”; ground: they are a shadow, the substance is Christ’s
2:18–19 Second warning: the visionary umpire “Let no one disqualify you”; the umpire described by four participles; the ground: he does not hold the head, from whom the body grows
2:20–22 The rebuke: why submit? “If you died with Christ … why … do you submit to decrees?”; the slogans quoted; their verdict: perishable, human
2:23 The final verdict A “word of wisdom” in self-made religion, humility and severity to the body — “no value” against the flesh

Device. Two parallel warnings (2:16–17; 2:18–19), each a third-person imperative with a ground, then a rhetorical question that gathers both (2:20–23) [T]. NA28’s paragraphing (2:16; 2:20) matches the turn from imperative to question [T: as NA28 prints it].

Three “which are” verdicts give the passage its spine [T]: ἅ ἐστιν (2:17), ἅ ἐστιν (2:22), ἅτινά ἐστιν (2:23). The first closes the first warning; the second and third close the question. These are the only plural relative “which are” clauses in Colossians; the singular forms identify Christ (1:15, 18; 2:10), the church (1:24), the mystery (1:27) and love (3:14) (SBLGNT) [T].

2:23 recapitulates 2:18 [T]. θέλων (“delighting”) … ταπεινοφροσύνῃ … θρησκείᾳ (2:18) returns as ἐθελοθρησκίᾳ (“self-made religion” — a compound of the same two roots) καὶ ταπεινοφροσύνῃ (2:23); and both verses end on “the flesh” — τοῦ νοὸς τῆς σαρκὸς αὐτοῦ (“his fleshly mind”, 2:18) and πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός (“against fleshly indulgence”, 2:23) [T]. The passage is framed internally as well as externally: the rival’s piety begins in the flesh (2:18) and cannot touch the flesh (2:23) [I], high.

The outer frame is 2:8 → 2:20–22 (Headline 1) [T].

Central assertions, by section: - 2:16–17 — command: let no one judge you; truth: the observances are a shadow, the body is Christ’s. - 2:18–19 — command: let no one disqualify you; truth (in the participle): such a man does not hold the head, from whom the whole body grows. - 2:20–23 — truth framed as a rebuke: you died with Christ to the elements, so submitting to human decrees is a contradiction; the decrees are perishable, human, and powerless against the flesh.

Warrant: the layout rearranges whitespace only [T]; the mood tags are from the SBLGNT parsing [T]. The one judgement call is whether the four participles of 2:18–19 describe the would-be umpire (taken here) or state the means of the disqualifying (the NASB95’s “by delighting … taking his stand …”). The Greek allows both; the masculine singular participles agree with μηδείς (“no one”) either way [T].

4. Linking Words

  • οὖν (“therefore”), 2:16 [T]. Inferential, drawing on 2:9–15 (Phase 0.6). It is the letter’s second οὖν after 2:6, and the third, 3:1, opens the answering conditional; the fourth (3:5) and fifth (3:12) launch the commands of the new life (SBLGNT) [T]. The inference runs: in Him you are filled, the decrees are cancelled, the powers are stripped → therefore let no one judge you.
  • ἅ ἐστιν (“which are”), 2:17 — relative of verdict; its antecedent is the whole list of 2:16 (neuter plural with feminine and neuter nouns: the things) [T]/[I].
  • δέ (“but”), 2:17 — the pivot: shadow ⟷ body [T]. The clause is verbless, τὸ δὲ σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ: “but the body (is) Christ’s”.
  • καί (“and”), 2:19 — “and not holding the head” adds the fourth and decisive participle; the καί makes the failure part of the same portrait, not a separate charge [T].
  • ἐξ οὗ (“from whom”), 2:19 — masculine, though κεφαλή (“head”) is feminine: the relative goes past the metaphor to the person, Christ [T] (grammar), high. The growth comes from him, not from the visionary’s experiences.
  • Εἰ (“if”), 2:20 — first-class condition with an aorist indicative: evidential, assumed true — “since you died” (Tool 4’s “evidential if”) [T]/[I], high. 3:1’s Εἰ οὖν is the same construction [T].
  • τί (“why”), 2:20 — the rhetorical question is the rebuke [T].
  • ὡς (“as if”), 2:20 — the counterfactual frame: they are not living in the world in the sense the decrees assume [T]/[I].
  • κατά (“according to”), 2:22 — the standard of the decrees: human. It answers 2:8’s three κατά (“according to the tradition of men, according to the elements of the world, and not according to Christ”) [T].
  • μέν … (no δέ), 2:23 — λόγον μὲν ἔχοντα (“having, to be sure, a word”) sets up a contrast the sentence never formally completes; the contrasting clause comes as οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι (“not of any value”) without a δέ [T]. The broken construction is part of why 2:23 is the letter’s hardest clause (Difficult Verses).
  • πρός, 2:23 — “against” (NASB95, ESV, NIV) or “for, towards” (its common sense). The meaning of the whole clause turns on this preposition (Tool 8; Difficult Verses) [T].

Translation note. The NASB95 and ESV both keep “therefore” (2:16), “but” (2:17) and “if … why” (2:20). The NASB95’s “such as” (2:20) and both versions’ parenthesis at 2:22 are punctuation decisions the Greek does not mark; the NA28 prints no parenthesis [T].

5. Parallels

Pauline prose, not Hebrew poetry, but three parallel constructions carry meaning [T]:

  • The two warnings (2:16–17 // 2:18–19). Each opens with a negative third-person imperative governing ὑμᾶς (“you”): μὴ … τις ὑμᾶς κρινέτω // μηδεὶς ὑμᾶς καταβραβευέτω. Each closes with a statement about Christ and “body”: τὸ δὲ σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ (“the body is Christ’s”) // ἐξ οὗ πᾶν τὸ σῶμα … αὔξει (“from whom the whole body grows”). The parallel is antithetical in its effect: the rival offers a calendar and a vision; each warning ends on Christ’s body [I], high.
  • The slogans (2:21). Three aorist prohibitions joined by μηδέ … μηδέ (“nor … nor”): μὴ ἅψῃ μηδὲ γεύσῃ μηδὲ θίγῃς — handle, taste, touch. The third verb (θιγγάνω) is close in sense to the first (ἅπτω); the triple is an escalation of the trivial, not a progression of thought [I]. Read against 2:19, it is a grim parallel by contrast: the rival forbids handling and touching (ἅπτω, θιγγάνω) while he himself does not hold (κρατέω) the head [I], moderate — a contrast of the passage’s grasping verbs, not a wordplay on one root.
  • The shadow/body antithesis (2:17). σκιά (“shadow”) // σῶμα (“body”) in a μέν-less, δέ-pivoted pair. The two terms stand together nowhere in the LXX (no verse in Swete has both) [T]; the pairing is Hellenistic idiom (Philo; Josephus) [S], moderate, and Heb 8:5; 10:1 make the same “shadow of what is to come” move (Tool 11) [T].

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A — epistle, not narrative. The nearest equivalent is the passage’s three verdict clauses (“which are …”, 2:17, 22, 23), in which the author steps back from command to pronounce on the things themselves (Tool 3) [T].

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. Χριστός (“Christ”, 2:17, 20) and θεός (“God”, 2:19). No place names, personal names or significant numbers. Routine; no canonical freight beyond the obvious [T].

σῶμα (“body”), 2:17, 2:19, 2:23 [T] — high confidence on the occurrences; moderate–high on the play between them. Colossians uses σῶμα in eight verses: 1:18, 1:22, 1:24, 2:11, 2:17, 2:19, 2:23, 3:15 (SBLGNT, by lemma) — three of the eight in this passage, with σωματικῶς (“bodily”) at 2:9. The senses run in a sequence: 1. 2:17 — τὸ δὲ σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ: the reality that casts the shadow (“substance”, NASB95 and ESV). Wherever else Paul writes “the body of Christ” (σῶμα with the genitive Χριστοῦ), he means Christ’s own body — crucified (Rom 7:4), shared in the Supper (1 Cor 10:16), or the church (1 Cor 12:27; Eph 4:12) (SBLGNT; read in each of the nineteen NT verses that have σῶμα and Χριστός together, most of which are not this construction) [T]. A Pauline ear could not hear τὸ σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ without hearing “the body of Christ” [I], moderate–high. 2. 2:19 — πᾶν τὸ σῶμα: the church, growing from its head, as at 1:18 and 1:24 [T]. 3. 2:23 — ἀφειδίᾳ σώματος, “severity to the body”: the physical body [T].

The rival’s piety is about the body (what goes into it, what it touches, how hard it is treated); Paul’s answer is that the substance is Christ’s body, and that growth belongs to his body the church [I], moderate–high. Read as a design, the triple is synthetic and should be marked so; read as a fact about this paragraph’s vocabulary it is simply there [T].

σάρξ (“flesh”), 2:18, 2:23 [T] — high confidence. Nine verses of the letter (1:22, 1:24, 2:1, 2:5, 2:11, 2:13, 2:18, 2:23, 3:22, SBLGNT). In this passage both occurrences close a unit: “the mind of his flesh” (2:18) and “the indulgence of the flesh” (2:23), the passage’s last word. σῶμα and σάρξ are not the same thing in 2:23 [T]: the regimen is severe to the body, and of no value against the flesh. In the letter σάρξ can be neutral (“in the flesh” = in person, 2:1, 5) or the sphere of the fallen self (2:11, 13, 18, 23); 2:18’s νοῦς τῆς σαρκός (“mind of the flesh”) — νοῦς with σάρξ stands in one other NT verse, Rom 7:25 (SBLGNT) — makes the second sense certain here [T]/[I]. The visionary is fleshly in his mind, not his appetites.

ταπεινοφροσύνη (“self-abasement” / “humility”), 2:18, 2:23 (→ 3:12) [T] — high confidence on the facts; uncertain on the precise sense at 2:18. Seven NT verses: Acts 20:19; Eph 4:2; Phil 2:3; Col 2:18, 2:23, 3:12; 1 Pet 5:5 (SBLGNT) — three of the seven in Colossians. The noun does not occur in Swete’s LXX at all; the adjective ταπεινόφρων occurs once (Prov 29:23, “the LORD upholds the humble-minded with glory”) [T]. Elsewhere in the NT it is always a virtue. Here it is the rival’s — and, at 3:12, the church’s again: the same word, false and true (overview Echo #18) [S: overview], re-verified. The sense at 2:18 is disputed: a self-humbling attitude, or a technical term for fasting as preparation for visions (Hermas uses the word of fasting) [S], moderate. The ESV’s “asceticism” has decided for a practice (Tool 8).

θέλων ἐν (“delighting in”), 2:18 [T]/[I] — moderate–high. θέλω with ἐν + dative is a Septuagintal idiom, “to take pleasure in” (Hebrew חָפֵץ בְּ, “to delight in”): 1 Sam 18:22 (“the king delights in you”); 2 Sam 15:26; 1 Kgs 10:9 (3 Reigns 10:9); Ps 146:10 (“He does not delight in the strength of the horse”) (Swete) [T]. The NASB95 (“delighting in”) and the NIV84 (“who delights in”) read it so; the ESV’s “insisting on” takes θέλων as “willing, wishing”; the KJV’s “in a voluntary humility” takes it adverbially [T]. The idiom favours the NASB95 [I].

θρησκεία τῶν ἀγγέλων (“the worship of the angels”), 2:18 [T] — high confidence on the facts; uncertain on the genitive. θρησκεία: four NT verses (Acts 26:5; Col 2:18; Jas 1:26, 27) [T]; in Swete four verses, all in two books (Wis 14:18, 27; 4 Macc 5:6, 13) [T]. The genitive is ambiguous, and the LXX has both kinds: Wis 14:27, “the worship of nameless idols” — the idols are worshipped (objective); 4 Macc 5:6, “the religion of the Jews” — the Jews practise it (subjective) [T]. So the grammar cannot decide whether the angels are worshipped (objective) or are the worshippers whose worship the visionary claims to join (subjective) [I]. ἄγγελος occurs only here in Colossians [T]. See Difficult Verses.

ἃ ἑόρακεν ἐμβατεύων (“entering into what he has seen”), 2:18 [T] — uncertain on the sense. ἐμβατεύω: once in the NT (SBLGNT). In Swete seven verses, with two senses [T]: (a) to enter a territory and take possession — Josh 19:49, 51 (the land as inheritance); 1 Macc 12:25; 13:20; 14:31; 15:40; (b) to “enter into” a subject in detail — 2 Macc 2:30, where the historian’s task is “to enter in, to walk about in the material, and to busy oneself with the particulars” (τὸ μὲν ἐμβατεύειν καὶ περίπατον ποιεῖσθαι λόγῳ καὶ πολυπραγμονεῖν ἐν τοῖς κατὰ μέρος). The NASB95’s “taking his stand on” leans on (a); the ESV’s “going on in detail about visions” and the NIV84’s “goes into great detail about what he has seen” are sense (b) [T]. A third sense — “entering” a sanctuary as an initiate, from second-century inscriptions at the oracle of Apollo at Claros — is the basis of the mystery-initiation reading [S], moderate. ὁράω in the perfect stands in the letter at 2:1 (“who have not seen my face”) and here (SBLGNT) [T]; the contrast — Paul unseen, the visionary standing on what he has seen — is noticed and not pressed [I], low.

φυσιόω (“to puff up”), 2:18 [T] — high confidence. Seven NT verses: six in 1 Corinthians (4:6, 18, 19; 5:2; 8:1; 13:4) and Col 2:18 (SBLGNT). The only use outside Corinth. 1 Cor 8:1: “Knowledge makes arrogant (φυσιοῖ, ‘puffs up’), but love edifies” (NASB95) — the same diagnosis of a spirituality of higher knowledge [I]. εἰκῇ (“without cause, in vain”): Rom 13:4; 1 Cor 15:2; Gal 3:4 (twice); 4:11; Col 2:18 — and Gal 4:11 stands immediately after the calendar of Gal 4:10 (Tool 11) [T].

καταβραβεύω (“to disqualify, defraud of the prize”), 2:18 (→ βραβεύω, 3:15) [T] — high lexically; moderate–high as intended wordplay [S: audit 1]. Each verb once in the NT; both third-person present imperatives. The βραβεύς was the umpire of the games [S]. Claim audit 1 (C4) confirmed the pair with nuance and found the four-imperative frame (2:16, 18 → 3:15, 16) [S: audit 1]. Neither the noun nor the compound is in Swete by word-beginning; the audit found the simplex at Wis 10:12 (ἐβράβευσεν) by a stem-anywhere search [S: audit 1].

κρατέω (“to hold fast”), 2:19 [T] — high confidence. Only here in Colossians. κρατέω with κεφαλή (“head”) stands in one NT verse, this one (SBLGNT) [T]. In Mark 7:3, 8 the same verb is used of “holding” the tradition of the elders / of men; in 2 Thess 2:15 of holding the apostolic traditions (SBLGNT) [T]. The rival “holds” his regimen and does not hold the head [I].

ἁφή, σύνδεσμος, ἐπιχορηγέω, συμβιβάζω, αὔξω, αὔξησις (“joint, ligament, supplied, knit together, grow, growth”), 2:19 [T] — high confidence. ἁφή and αὔξησις occur in the NT only in Eph 4:16 and Col 2:19; αὔξω only Eph 2:21 and Col 2:19; σύνδεσμος in Acts 8:23, Eph 4:3, Col 2:19 and 3:14 (SBLGNT). In Colossians συμβιβάζω stands only at 2:2 (hearts “knit together in love”) and 2:19; σύνδεσμος only at 2:19 and 3:14 (love, “the perfect bond of unity”) [T]. So each of the body’s two binding words has a love-twin elsewhere in the letter — the 1:24–2:5 dig drew the chain 2:2 → 2:19 → 3:14 [S: 1:24–2:5 dig], re-verified here. The growth is τοῦ θεοῦ (“of God”), a genitive of source: God gives it [I], high — compare 1 Cor 3:6–7, “God was causing the growth” [T].

τὰ στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου (“the elements of the world”), 2:20 — fully worked in the 2:6–15 dig (Tool 7; Difficult Verses) [S: 2:6–15 dig] with Patrick’s first Logos round [S: Logos 1]: three senses held (physical elements; elementary principles; elemental spirits), “spirits” the recent majority (Arnold), the evidence before the second century being for spirits associated with the elements rather than for the word used of spirits. Uncertain, unchanged. What this passage adds [T]: “you died … from (ἀπό) the elements” — ἀποθνῄσκω with ἀπό (“to die away from”) stands nowhere else in the NT (SBLGNT; Rom 6:2 and Gal 2:19 use the dative). The elements are something one can be removed from by death, which fits a power or a regime better than a physical substance [I], moderate. The calendar and food rules of 2:16, 21 are, on Paul’s argument, instances of living under the elements — exactly the move of Gal 4:3, 9–10 (Tool 11) [I], high.

δογματίζομαι (“to submit to decrees”), 2:20 [T] — high confidence. Once in the NT. Its noun δόγμα (“decree”) stands at 2:14: the certificate “consisting of decrees” was cancelled and nailed to the cross (2:6–15 dig, Echo 2:14 → 2:20) [S: 2:6–15 dig], re-verified. In Swete the verb occurs twice, Esth 3:9 (“let it be decreed that they be destroyed”) and Dan 2:15 in the Old Greek (“why is the decree so harsh?”) — both of a king’s decree; a word-beginning search also returns Theodotion’s Dan 3:12 and 6:13, but those are the noun δόγμα in the dative, and are dropped [T]. The form is middle or passive: “do you submit to decrees?” (NASB95, ESV) or “are you being decreed at?” [T]; either way the readers are the ones on whom the decrees fall.

The three slogans, 2:21 [T] — ἅπτω (“handle, touch”), γεύομαι (“taste”), θιγγάνω (“touch”). θιγγάνω occurs in three NT verses (Col 2:21; Heb 11:28; 12:20) and once in Swete’s LXX, at Exod 19:12, beside ἅπτω — the boundary around Sinai: “do not go up the mountain or touch (θιγεῖν) its border; whoever touches (ἁψάμενος) the mountain shall surely be put to death” (Rahlfs export agrees) [T]. Col 2:21 is the only NT verse with both verbs. The pairing of eat and touch in a prohibition is purity-law idiom: Lev 11:8 and Deut 14:8 (“you shall not eat of their flesh nor touch their carcasses”) and Gen 3:3 (“you shall not eat from it or touch it”) (Swete; Rahlfs agrees at Gen 3:3 and Lev 11:8) [T]. What the slogans forbid is unspecified — food, impurity, perhaps sex [I]; 2:22 says the things forbidden “perish in the using”, which points to food and drink [I], high.

ἀπόχρησις (“using up”), φθορά (“perishing”), 2:22 [T] — ἀπόχρησις once in the NT, absent from Swete. φθορά: eight NT verses (Rom 8:21; 1 Cor 15:42, 50; Gal 6:8; Col 2:22; 2 Pet 1:4; 2:12, 19). The things regulated are consumed in being consumed — the rule-makers legislate about what goes into the stomach and out [I], moderate (cf. Mark 7:19, “it does not go into his heart, but into his stomach, and is eliminated”).

ἐθελοθρησκία (“self-made religion”), 2:23 [T] — high confidence on the facts; moderate on the sense. Once in the NT; absent from Swete’s LXX; it is not known from any Greek text before this verse [S], moderate–high. The compound joins ἐθελο- (“willing, self-chosen”) with θρησκεία (“worship”): the θέλων … θρησκείᾳ of 2:18 compressed into one word (Tool 3) [T]. Sense: worship that the worshipper has chosen for himself — “will-worship” (KJV) — or “would-be worship” [S]. It is the passage’s single sharpest word: religion whose origin is the human will [I].

ἀφειδία σώματος (“severe treatment of the body”), 2:23 [T] — ἀφειδία once in the NT; in Swete only the adverb ἀφειδῶς (“unsparingly”) — of the righteous man’s generosity (Prov 21:26) and of slaughter without mercy (2 Macc 5:6, 12) [T]. “Not sparing the body.”

τιμή, πλησμονή (“value/honour”, “indulgence/satisfaction”), 2:23 [T] — uncertain on the sense of the clause. τιμή, forty NT verses, “honour” or “price, value” [T]. πλησμονή: once in the NT; twenty-eight verses in Swete, nearly all of eating or drinking “to the full” — Exod 16:3, 8 (bread to the full by the fleshpots); Lev 25:19; 26:5; Ps 77:25 (78:25, “bread of angels … to the full”); Isa 55:2; Ezek 39:19 — and Hos 13:6 of satiety that forgets God (Swete) [T]. In biblical Greek the word means “satisfaction, satiety”, mostly neutral, sometimes ominous [T]/[I]. That neutrality is part of the crux (Difficult Verses): “of no value against indulgence” requires πρός = “against” and πλησμονή = “gratification”; the plainer senses of both words point the other way.

λόγος … σοφίας (“a word of wisdom”), 2:23 [T] — λόγον μὲν ἔχοντα σοφίας, “having a reputation (word, appearance) of wisdom”. σοφία runs through the letter: 1:9, 1:28, 2:3, 2:23, 3:16, 4:5 (SBLGNT) — this is its only negative use [T]. λόγος with σοφία in one verse: 2:23 and 3:16 only in Colossians [T]. The “word of wisdom” the rival has (2:23) is answered by “the word of Christ … in all wisdom” (3:16) — and all the treasures of wisdom are hidden in Christ (2:3) [T]; compare 2:4, the warning against πιθανολογία (“persuasive argument”) [I].

Familiar words used in a Bible-specific way. “Judge” (κρίνω, 2:16) here means “pass a verdict on your standing”, not “form an opinion” [I]; “flesh” (σάρξ) is the fallen self, not the body (above); “world” (κόσμος, 2:20) is the order of things the believer has died out of, not the planet [I].

Ancient versions check. Two places trigger it — the moderns agree on a weight-bearing rendering:

  • 2:23, the last clause. The NASB95, ESV and NIV84 all make it “of no value against / in stopping / in restraining” the flesh’s indulgence. The Vulgate reads, as recalled, non in honore aliquo ad saturitatem carnis (“not in any honour, to the satisfying of the flesh”), and the KJV follows that shape [S] — uncertain: the Vulgate wording is recalled, not checked; verify in Logos (Clementine Vulgate). Translation-tradition split: the ancient Latin keeps πρός as “for, towards” and τιμή as “honour”, which allows the reading that the regulations serve the flesh’s satisfaction; the modern consensus reads πρός as “against” and τιμή as “value”. Preaching implication: on the modern reading, asceticism fails to restrain the flesh; on the older reading, the regimen is itself a form of the flesh’s self-satisfaction. Both make the same point about the regimen’s powerlessness, by different routes (Difficult Verses).
  • 2:18, “what he has seen”. The NASB95, ESV and NIV84 agree (“visions he has seen” or similar). The KJV’s “which he hath not seen” follows the majority Greek text; the Vulgate is recalled as quae non vidit (with the negative) [S], uncertain — verify. Here the split is not a translation question but a textual one, and it belongs under Textual Variants: the NA28’s shorter text has the better witnesses (𝔓⁴⁶ א* A B D*) [T: as NA28 prints it].

The Hebrew and the Greek (three-way triage). The passage cites one OT text, Isa 29:13 (2:22), and the divergence is real: the Hebrew reads “their fear of me is a commandment of men learned by rote” (מִצְוַת אֲנָשִׁים מְלֻמָּדָה, “a commandment of men, taught”), and has no “in vain” and no noun “teachings”; the LXX reads “in vain they worship me, teaching commandments of men and teachings” (Swete; Rahlfs export agrees) [T]. Colossians’ διδασκαλίας (“teachings”) exists only in the Greek. Category 3 — the Greek as the NT’s own text: it stands, and is reported under Tool 11, not as a translation error. Whether the LXX’s “in vain” reflects a different Hebrew reading (for example תֹּהוּ, “emptiness”, for the MT’s וַתְּהִי, “and it is”) is a category 2 question [S], uncertain; nothing in Colossians turns on it, since 2:22 cites the “commandments and teachings” half, not the “in vain”.

Pulpit divergence note (ESV). Three places where the ESV has decided something the congregation should know about:

  1. 2:18 “sensuous mind” for τοῦ νοὸς τῆς σαρκός. The congregation will hear “sensuous” as indulgent. But the man described is an ascetic, delighting in self-abasement; Paul’s point is that the ascetic mind is fleshly — self-centred and self-inflating — precisely while it denies the senses (2:18, 23). “Sensuous” turns the irony into a contradiction. The NASB95’s “fleshly mind” keeps it. Tell the congregation it is “the mind of the flesh” [I], high.
  2. 2:18 “insisting on asceticism”. “Insisting on” is a possible reading of θέλων, but the LXX idiom favours “delighting in” (NASB95), and “asceticism” names a practice where ταπεινοφροσύνη names an attitude (“lowliness of mind”) — the ESV has taken a side in the fasting debate (Difficult Verses) and, with “humility” at 3:12, has hidden the fact that the false humility and the true are the same word [S: overview], re-verified.
  3. 2:20 “submit to regulations” after 2:14 “legal demands”. The Greek ties the two with δόγμα / δογματίζομαι: the rival imposes the very “decrees” that were nailed to the cross. The NASB95 (“decrees … decrees”) lets the congregation hear it; the ESV does not [T].

Two smaller notes: 2:18 “disqualify” creates, in English only, an echo of the ESV’s “who has qualified you to share in the inheritance” (1:12) — the Greek words are unrelated (ἱκανόω; καταβραβεύω), so the echo is the translator’s, not Paul’s, and should not be preached as a wordplay [T]. 2:19 “knit together” — the ESV keeps 2:2’s “knit together in love” audible; the NASB95 (“held together”) breaks it [S: 1:24–2:5 dig], re-verified. Here the pulpit text serves better.

9. Tone and Feel

Protective, then scornful. The register toward the church is protective — “let no one …”, twice — and the warnings assume the church is not yet captured (2:5, “your good discipline”) [T]/[I]. The register toward the rival is scorn, and the devices are deflationary [T]/[I]:

  • Caricature by participles. The would-be umpire is drawn in four strokes: delighting in his own lowliness, entering into his visions, puffed up “without cause”, not holding the head (2:18–19). “Puffed up by the mind of his flesh” says the humble man is swollen [T].
  • Mimicry. The slogans are quoted in staccato — “Don’t handle! Don’t taste! Don’t touch!” (2:21) — three bare aorist subjunctives, in the singular, as a teacher would bark them [T]/[I].

It closes the polemic that 2:8 opened, cashing every specific against 2:9–15; and it plants almost every one of its distinctive words in chapter 3, where several return — two of them with their value reversed. 3:5–16 reads as the positive of 2:16–23’s negative: the right death (3:3, 5), the right humility (3:12), the right umpire (3:15), the right word and wisdom (3:16) [I], moderate (v1.1, lowered from moderate–high [S: audit 2]). For the overview’s Echo Table, the new rows are 1:9 → 2:18; 2:17, 19 → 3:15; 2:22–23 → 3:16; 2:20 → 3:7; 2:23 → 3:5; 2:16 → 3:13 (low).

12. Genre

Pauline epistle — polemical exhortation. Two features matter for reading [T]/[I]: - Quoted slogans. 2:21 is almost certainly the rival’s own language quoted back — the singular “you”, the bare prohibitions, the change of register — though the Greek has no quotation marks (the NA28 prints none; the English versions supply them) [T]/[I], high. - Rhetorical question as rebuke (2:20), in diatribe style [S], moderate.

The genre forbids reconstructing the rival teaching in more detail than the text gives. Paul describes features, not a system (overview, Trap 2) [S: overview].

13. Copycat

Largely N/A — an epistle, with no narrative characters. One application-check: the rival is a negative example (Tool 13’s trichotomy), and the commands are direct commands to the church — but negative ones, directed at others’ claims: “let no one …”. The prescriptive content is refusal, not a counter-regimen [T]/[I]. Readers who turn 2:21 into its opposite (“touch, taste, handle!”) make a regimen of liberty and miss the point (2:23 is about the flesh, not the menu).

14. Bible Timeline

The passage is itself a timeline statement. “A shadow of the things to come (τῶν μελλόντων)” (2:17) speaks from the standpoint of the calendar’s institution, looking forward; “the body is Christ’s” says the forward-looking has arrived [T]/[I]. Where the passage sits: this side of the cross, after Christ’s death and the believers’ death with him (2:20), and before the revealing (3:4) [T]. Where we are: the same. What has happened between Sinai’s calendar and Colossae’s: the substance has come; the shadow is not abolished but outshone — its function was to point [I].

Isaiah 66:23’s “from new moon to new moon … all flesh will come to worship” and Ezek 45:17’s eschatological calendar show the OT itself expected the calendar to have a future; Col 2:17 says that future belongs to Christ (Tool 11) [I], moderate. Common timeline misreadings here (Preaching Pitfalls): either carrying the calendar across as binding (“Christians must keep the feasts”), or treating the OT calendar as if it had been a mistake.

15. Who Am I?

  • “You” (plural) — the church at Colossae. Direct address; the congregation is the “you” [T]. Note the corporate frame: the rival addresses individuals (“you”, singular, 2:21); Paul addresses the church and speaks of the body (2:19) [T]/[I].
  • The rival (“no one”, “anyone”). A foil and a warning, unnamed and indefinite [T]. The trap for us is not only being judged but being the judge. Rom 14 shows believers judging one another over food and days; the hearer should ask whether he is the one “insisting”, not only the one being insisted upon [I], high.
  • Christ. The substance (2:17), the head (2:19), the one with whom we died (2:20). Not an example to imitate here, but the reality to hold [T].

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. That the church refuse any human tribunal over its standing with God — calendar, menu, vision or regimen — and hold fast to Christ the head, living as people who have died with him to the whole order of “do not handle, do not taste, do not touch” [I], high.

Stage 2 — worldview. The decisive categories are shadow and substance (2:17), head and body (2:19), body and flesh (2:23). Shadows are real and were given by God; but you do not hug a shadow when the body is in the room. Growth comes from the head (2:19), not from technique or experience. And the problem of the human heart is the flesh, which no rule about the body can reach (2:23); its answer is death and resurrection with Christ (2:20; 3:1–5) [I].

Behaviour. - Stop: accepting, or imposing, spiritual rankings based on observances, diets, disciplines or experiences; being impressed by claims of special access (“what he has seen”); using rules about the body as if they dealt with the flesh [I]. - Start: holding the head — consciously drawing life, growth and standing from Christ; receiving God’s gifts with thanksgiving (2:7; 1 Tim 4:3–4); putting to death the earthly practices of 3:5 by union with Christ, not punishing the body [I].

Motivation. Not “be free because rules are tiresome”, but “you died with Christ” (2:20), “you have been filled in Him” (2:10), “the substance belongs to Christ” (2:17) [T]. The freedom is a consequence of union; the command is grounded in the indicative of 2:9–15.

Four audiences. - For me: Where do I look for a sense of spiritual standing — in what I abstain from, what I keep, what I have experienced? Do I sit in judgement on others’ food, days or devotional habits? - For a Christian friend: the believer who has been made to feel second-class by a more “serious” Christian — the fasting, the special days, the dramatic experience — needs to hear “let no one disqualify you”; the substance is Christ’s, and he has filled you. - For the church: a congregation grows “with a growth which is from God” through every joint and ligament (2:19) — not through a spiritual elite with experiences the rest lack. The body language is corporate; the slogans are individual. - For an unbeliever: religion that offers a regimen — of diet, ritual, mindfulness or self-denial — may have “a reputation for wisdom” and still be “of no value against the flesh”. The Christian claim is not a better regimen but a death and a resurrection with Christ.

Prayer. Lord Jesus, you are the substance and we are your body. Keep us holding fast to you. Forgive us for judging one another by shadows, and for being cowed by those who judge us. Put to death in us what is earthly, not by our severity but by your death and life; and make us grow with the growth that comes from God.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period: the letter’s own setting is Paul’s imprisonment (4:3, 10, 18) [T]; date and place are not stated [S].
  • Location: Colossae, in the Lycus valley of Phrygia, near Laodicea and Hierapolis (2:1; 4:13) [T]. A substantial Jewish population had lived in Phrygia since Antiochus III settled Jewish families there (Josephus, Ant. 12.147–153) [S], moderate–high; Cicero records the confiscation of Jewish temple-tax gold at Laodicea (Pro Flacco 68) [S], moderate.
  • Religious context: Phrygia was known for ecstatic and mystery cults [S], moderate.

What the original audience knew

  1. The calendar of Israel. “Festival, new moon, Sabbath” was a recognisable Jewish triad (twelve verses in the Greek OT, Tool 11) [T]. Gentile converts in a town with a synagogue community would know it from their neighbours [I].
  2. Food and Sabbath as Jewish identity markers. In the Greek Jewish literature closest to this passage’s vocabulary (the scan, Tool 11), refusing to “taste” forbidden food and keeping sabbaths and feasts are the marks of fidelity under pagan pressure: the Seleucid ban meant one could “neither keep the Sabbath nor observe the feasts of the fathers” (2 Macc 6:6); Eleazar refused to taste pork (2 Macc 6:18–20; 4 Macc 5:2, 6); the seven brothers were forced to “touch” (ἐφάπτεσθαι) unlawful swine’s flesh (2 Macc 7:1) (Swete) [T]. In 4 Macc 5:6 the tyrant calls Jewish observance θρησκεία and mocks it as not “philosophy” (φιλοσοφεῖν) — the only verse in Swete with both the word for “worship” of 2:18 and the “philosophy” word of 2:8 [T]. A first-century Jewish reader could hear food abstention and calendar-keeping as a philosophy and a religion in exactly the terms Colossians uses [I], moderate–high. (Josephus likewise calls the Jewish schools “philosophies”, Ant. 18.11 [S].)
  3. The athletic umpire. The βραβεύς awarded the prize at the games; to be “umpired against” was to be disqualified [S], high (common knowledge); Paul uses the prize image elsewhere (1 Cor 9:24; Phil 3:14) [T].
  4. Angels in Jewish piety. Second-Temple Jewish texts are full of angels as mediators, guardians of the nations, and heavenly worshippers whose liturgy the righteous may glimpse (the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice at Qumran; 1 Enoch; Ascension of Isaiah) [S], moderate–high.
  5. Angels in Phrygian piety, later. The Council of Laodicea (4th century) forbade Christians to “name angels” (canon 35); Theodoret, commenting on Col 2:18, says the vice persisted in Phrygia and Pisidia; a famous shrine of the archangel Michael stood at Chonae near Colossae [S], moderate — all of it centuries later, and evidence of a regional tendency, not of the first-century teaching.
  6. Vision-and-initiation language. ἐμβατεύω appears in second-century inscriptions from the oracle of Apollo at Claros of initiates “entering” after receiving the mysteries [S], moderate; the dating and the inference are contested.
  7. Ascetic schools. Abstinence from meat and wine, and regimens of the body, were practised by Pythagoreans and others; “humility” was not a pagan virtue, so its prominence here points to a Jewish or Christian setting [S]/[I], moderate.

What this changes about how we read

  • The rival teaching need not be exotic to be dangerous. Every feature in 2:16–23 has a ready home in Diaspora Jewish piety — food laws, calendar, angels, visionary worship, fasting — and some features fit Phrygian folk religion as well [I]. The text does not let us choose between “Jewish mysticism” (Francis; Lincoln; Sappington), “syncretistic folk religion” (Arnold) and other reconstructions [S]; the Preaching Pitfall is to choose one and preach it (overview, Trap 2).
  • The calendar and the menu were identity-markers before they were rules. For a Gentile believer, taking them up meant joining a people and a “philosophy”; for Paul, it meant accepting another tribunal over one’s standing (Gal 4:9–10) [I], high.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: Pauline corpus, prison letters; the canonical reader has just read Ephesians [S: overview]. For this passage the reader also knows Romans 14 and Galatians 4 — Paul’s earlier rulings on food, days and the elements (Tool 11) [T].
  • What the canonical reader knows: that the Law’s calendar and food laws were given by God (Lev 11; Num 28–29), that the prophets judged their misuse (Isa 1; Hos 2), and that Jesus spoke of “the commandments of men” from Isa 29:13 (Mark 7; Matt 15) [T].
  • Canonical register: shadow/substance language is shared with Hebrews (8:5; 10:1) [T].

The first hearers

  • Who they were: a mainly Gentile church (1:21, 27; 2:13) [T]/[I], founded by Epaphras (1:7), in good order (2:5) [T].
  • Their situation: a teaching “in the air” offering more — stricter observances, angelic worship, visionary experience — and the implied verdict that they were missing something [T]/[I].
  • What they brought: a pagan past in which the gods and powers had to be placated with rites and abstentions [I]; perhaps Jewish neighbours whose discipline was visibly impressive [I].

Where the passage fits their world

  1. “Let no one judge you” lands in an honour-shame setting: to be judged deficient by a religious expert was public loss of standing [I], high.
  2. “Disqualify” lands in a games culture: the umpire’s verdict cancelled a race already run [I].
  3. “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch” lands as familiar — the shape of every purity code, pagan or Jewish [I].

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: that the God-given calendar is called “a shadow” [I] — and to Jewish ears, that observances martyrs died for are said to perish “in the using” (2:22).
  • Surprising: that the rigorous ascetic is called fleshly (2:18, 23) — the most self-denying man in the room is the one ruled by the flesh [T]/[I].
  • Comforting: “let no one disqualify you” — the church already has the substance; nothing it lacks can be supplied by a vision or a fast (2:9–10, 17) [I].
  • Disturbing: “why … do you submit?” (2:20) — the present tense suggests some were already submitting [T]/[I].

What we bring that they didn’t

  • Individualism — we hear “let no one judge you” as personal self-esteem; it is corporate standing in Christ, and the remedy is the body growing together (2:19) [I].
  • Sabbath debates — post-Reformation and Seventh-day controversies make 2:16 a proof-text; they did not have that debate (Pitfalls) [I].
  • Wellness culture — diets, detoxes, disciplines and “spirituality” offered as regimens for a better self: the same shape as 2:21–23 in modern dress [I].

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. Sabbath, festival and the shadow of what is to come

  • Where this passage sits: the NT reading of Israel’s calendar in the light of Christ.
  • What comes before: creation rest (Gen 2:1–3); the calendar given at Sinai (Lev 23; Num 28–29); prophetic critique of the calendar kept without righteousness (Isa 1:13–14) and its suspension in judgement (Hos 2:11); the eschatological calendar of the restored temple (Ezek 45:17) and of all flesh (Isa 66:23) [T].
  • What comes after: Jesus “Lord of the Sabbath” (Mark 2:28); the Sabbath rest that remains for the people of God (Heb 4:9); “a shadow of the good things to come” (Heb 10:1) [T].
  • What this contributes: the calendar was neither error nor eternal requirement; it was a God-given shadow cast forward by the body that was coming. Its substance is Christ’s [I], high.
  • Confidence: high (the trajectory); moderate on any implication for the Lord’s Day, which the text does not address.

2. Clean and unclean — food, touch and the body of Christ

  • Where this passage sits: the NT’s release of believers from purity regulation as a measure of standing.
  • What comes before: the food laws (Lev 11; Deut 14), with their “do not eat … do not touch” (Lev 11:8; Deut 14:8) [T]; Daniel’s refusal of the king’s food (Dan 1) and the Maccabean martyrs (2 Macc 6–7) as heroic fidelity [T].
  • What comes after: “Thus He declared all foods clean” (Mark 7:19); Peter’s vision (Acts 10:15); Rom 14:14, 17; 1 Tim 4:3–5 [T].
  • What this contributes: the things regulated “perish in the using” (2:22); holiness is not located in what enters the body, but in union with Christ, and it shows in what comes out of the heart (3:5–9) [I], high.
  • Confidence: high.

3. Head, body and growth from God

  • Where this passage sits: the church as Christ’s body, growing from its head.
  • What comes before: God as the giver of growth (1 Cor 3:6–7, “God who causes the growth”) [T]; 1:18 (head of the body, the church) [T].
  • What comes after: Eph 4:15–16; Col 3:15 (“called in one body”) [T].
  • What this contributes: spiritual growth is corporate and derived — “from whom the whole body … grows with a growth which is from God” (2:19). A spirituality that bypasses the head and the body for private experience cannot grow [I], high.
  • Confidence: high.

Confluence. The three themes meet in a climactic convergence on one word: σῶμα. The shadow’s substance is Christ’s body (2:17); the clean-and-unclean question is answered by union with his body, not by regulating ours (2:20–23); and growth belongs to his body, the church (2:19). The passage’s three senses of σῶμα are the three themes’ meeting point [I], moderate–high.

Typology (the calendar as shadow) and direct statement (Christ as head, as the one with whom believers died).

How the passage points to Christ

  • What the passage anticipates: the revealing of Christ, and of believers with him (3:4) [T].

Difficult / Contested Verses

2:16–17 — Does this abolish the Sabbath?

  • Category: doctrinal (tribal landmine); weaponised.
  • The difficulty: Sabbatarians, Seventh-day Adventists and opponents of any Lord’s Day observance all claim the verse.
  • Common misreadings: (a) “The Sabbath is abolished, so Sunday has no special status”; (b) “Only the ceremonial sabbaths are meant, not the weekly Sabbath”; (c) “Paul condemns the OT calendar as legalism.”
  • Rhetorical function in context: the verse is a warning against being judged, grounded in a verdict on what the observances are (a shadow). It is not a ruling on Christian worship days [T]/[I].
  • The main interpretive options: σαββάτων (plural, “sabbaths”) at the end of the triad most naturally includes the weekly Sabbath, since the triad in the OT does (Ezek 45:17; Hos 2:11) [T]/[I], moderate–high; whether the Fourth Commandment’s creation basis survives as a principle is a question the text does not raise [I].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: no one may judge a believer’s standing by these observances; they were a shadow; the substance is Christ’s (2:16–17) [T]. Rom 14:5–6 shows Paul allowing day-observance as a matter of conscience before the Lord [T].
  • Suggested handling for the preacher: address briefly; do not make the sermon a Sabbath debate (overview, Trap 3). Preach the shadow and the substance, and the refusal of any human tribunal.

2:18 — “The worship of the angels”

  • Category: doctrinal / historical (an exegetical crux).
  • The difficulty: the genitive may be objective (angels worshipped, venerated or invoked) or subjective (the worship the angels offer, which the visionary claims to join).
  • Rhetorical function in context: the phrase is one feature of the umpire who “does not hold the head” (2:19); either way, the angelic is put where only Christ belongs [I].
  • The main interpretive options [S]: (a) objective — veneration or invocation of angels for protection (Arnold; much older commentary); (b) subjective — participation in heavenly angelic worship through visionary ascent prepared by fasting (Francis; Lincoln; Sappington; Dunn inclines this way); (c) a mixture.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the grammar cannot decide — the LXX has θρησκεία with an objective genitive (Wis 14:27, idols) and a subjective one (4 Macc 5:6, the Jews) (Tool 7) [T]. Colossians answers both: the angels were created through Christ (1:16) and the powers stripped by him (2:15), so neither their worship nor their liturgy can add to what is in the head [I], high.
  • Suggested handling: acknowledge both readings in a sentence; preach the answer, which covers both.

2:18 — ἃ ἑόρακεν ἐμβατεύων

  • Category: exegetical crux; textual (the μή variant).
  • The main interpretive options: (a) “taking his stand on visions he has seen” (NASB95; entering/occupying, Josh 19:49); (b) “going on in detail about visions” (ESV; 2 Macc 2:30); (c) “entering (as an initiate) what he has seen” (the Claros inscriptions) [S]; (d) emendations of the Greek, proposed because the phrase is hard [S], low.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the text, without μή, has a man whose authority is his own visionary experience [T]/[I], high; how he uses it — stands on it, expounds it, enters it — is uncertain.
  • Suggested handling: preach “his authority is what he has seen”; do not hang anything on one gloss of ἐμβατεύω.

2:23 — “of no value against the indulgence of the flesh”

  • Category: exegetical crux — the hardest clause in the letter.
  • The difficulty: a μέν with no δέ; πρός, which usually means “towards, for”, here translated “against”; τιμή, “honour” or “value”; πλησμονή, usually neutral “satisfaction” (Tool 7).
  • Rhetorical function in context: the passage’s final verdict on the regulations, after “perish in the using” (2:22). It must say they fail — but fail how?
  • The main interpretive options:
    1. “of no value against the indulgence of the flesh” (NASB95, ESV, NIV84) — the regulations look wise but cannot restrain the flesh. Requires πρός = “against” [S].
    2. “not of any honour, [serving only] for the satisfaction of the flesh” (the KJV’s shape; the Vulgate as recalled) — the regimen does not honour God; it gratifies the flesh’s self-importance [S]/[I].
    3. “severity to the body, not in any honour [to it], for the satisfying of the flesh” — joining οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι to the body: the regimen dishonours the body, which deserves honour, and serves the flesh [S], low–moderate.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: on every reading, the regimen is powerless as a remedy for the flesh — it either fails to restrain it (1) or feeds it (2, 3) [I], high. Readings 2 and 3 fit 2:18 (“puffed up … by the mind of his flesh”) well: the ascetic’s pride is the flesh’s satisfaction [I], moderate. Reading 1 fits 3:5 (“put to death”) and the modern consensus [S].
  • Suggested handling: preach the point all readings share — severity to the body does not touch the flesh — and, if the clause is preached, say honestly that it is difficult.

2:20 — “the elements of the world”

  • See the 2:6–15 dig (Difficult Verses) and the Pulpit Note: the ESV’s “elemental spirits” has decided the question for the congregation [S: 2:6–15 dig] [S: Logos 1]. This passage adds the “died away from” construction (Tool 7).

2:23 — “severe treatment of the body” (pastoral)

  • Category: pastoral.
  • The difficulty: some hearers live under regimes of harsh self-denial or self-punishment — around food, exercise or the body — sometimes with a religious colouring.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: Paul calls severity to the body of no value for holiness [T]/[I]. That is liberating, not condemning.
  • Suggested handling: speak gently; do not make the hearer’s struggle a sermon illustration; make clear that the remedy is not a counter-regimen but Christ, and be ready to offer pastoral help afterwards.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Turning 2:16–17 into a Sabbath sermon

  • What it looks like: “Paul has abolished the Sabbath — and anyone who keeps Sunday special is a legalist”; or the reverse, a long explanation of why “sabbaths” here cannot include the weekly Sabbath.
  • Why it’s wrong: the verse forbids anyone to judge a believer by these observances, because they are a shadow (2:17). It does not rule on Christian worship days, and the Fourth Commandment debate is not its question (overview, Trap 3).
  • The corrective: preach shadow and substance and the refusal of a human tribunal; mention the Sabbath question in a sentence and point to Rom 14:5–6 (day-observance before the Lord is a matter of conscience).

Pitfall: Lecturing on a reconstructed “Colossian heresy”

  • What it looks like: twenty minutes on Jewish mysticism, Phrygian angel cults or Claros, followed by “and that is what Paul was against”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the text gives features, not a system; the reconstructions are [S] and disputed. The features are enough (overview, Trap 2).
  • The corrective: name the features from the text — calendar, food, humility, angels, visions, slogans, severity to the body — and let the congregation recognise its own “something more”.

Pitfall: Preaching against discipline itself

  • What it looks like: “Fasting, rules and self-control are legalism; Christian freedom means you can eat, drink and do as you like.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the target is regulation as a measure of standing and a remedy for the flesh, imposed by human authority (2:16, 20–23). Colossians commands the putting to death of earthly practices immediately afterwards (3:5), and Paul fasted (2 Cor 11:27). Making a regimen of liberty (“touch! taste! handle!”) is still a regimen.
  • The corrective: distinguish the body (σῶμα) from the flesh (σάρξ): severity to the body cannot touch the flesh; death and resurrection with Christ can (2:20; 3:1–5).

Pitfall: “Judge not” — a general rule against moral assessment

  • What it looks like: using 2:16 like a misused Matt 7:1, to silence any moral or doctrinal correction.
  • Why it’s wrong: the judging in view is a verdict on a believer’s standing based on observances and experiences (2:16, 18). The same letter commands the church to “teach and admonish one another” (3:16).
  • The corrective: preach the specific judgement forbidden — the shadow made a test of fellowship.

Pitfall: Making “shadow” mean “worthless”

  • What it looks like: “The OT feasts were empty rituals.”
  • Why it’s wrong: a shadow is cast by a body; the calendar was given by God (Num 28:2, “My feasts”) and, in the prophets, had a future (Ezek 45:17; Isa 66:23). Paul’s word is not Hosea’s judgement but a forward-pointing verdict (Tool 11).
  • The corrective: “the shadow was real, and it was shaped like Christ.”

Pitfall: Preaching Gen 3:3 behind 2:21

  • What it looks like: “Eve added ‘nor touch it’ to God’s command — the first legalism — and the Colossian rule-makers do the same.”
  • Why it’s wrong: “eat … touch” is a common purity-law pairing (Lev 11:8; Deut 14:8); nothing in Colossians points to Genesis 3 (Tool 11, not adopted).
  • The corrective: if the point is wanted, make it from 2:22 — “commandments and teachings of men” — which is the text’s own diagnosis.
