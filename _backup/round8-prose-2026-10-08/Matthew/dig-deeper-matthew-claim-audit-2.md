## Method

The method is the first audit's, which was the Mark audit's (29 September 2026). There were **three independent auditors**. Each was a fresh subagent with no access to the overview, to the sweep, to the first audit, or to one another's work. They were given the claims as proposals to break, not to defend, with only the proposed rating attached. To limit cost, they ran **one after another, not in parallel**.

| Part | Scope | Claims |
|---|---|---|
| A | Torah, Former Prophets and the structural claims | A1–A13 |
| B | Latter Prophets, including the shepherd-sequence design claim | B1–B12 |
| C | The Twelve and the Writings | C1–C16 |

Each auditor worked only from the corpus, and did seven things for every claim:

1. Read the Matthew verse in the SBLGNT, with the NA28 where a reading mattered.
2. Read the Synoptic parallel where there was one, and **said what Matthew inherited from Mark and what he added**. This check changed eight ratings in the first audit; here it decided A13 and C13.
3. Read the source in the WLC, in Swete **and** in Rahlfs.
4. Took a frequency baseline for every shared word or phrase, in Swete and in the New Testament.
5. Searched for the strongest rival source.
6. Scored the claim on the Hays criteria.
7. Gave a verdict and a final rating.

**Searches.** Every search was accent-insensitive. Hebrew was checked by lemma or by stripped consonants. Every absence was preceded by a positive control.

**Sources.** No commentaries and no web sources were used, so "History of Interpretation" is recorded as *not checked* throughout.

**Spot checks.** The main session then re-ran the evidence each verdict turns on. Twenty-four spot checks were made, and **all twenty-four reproduced**. These included:

- the Swete hits for the plural "I am with you" (Gen 23:4; Hag 1:13; 2:4);
- Gen 49:33's κατέπαυσεν ("ceased");
- Sus Th 46 as the only "innocent of the blood" verse in Swete;
- ἀπὸ τότε ἤρξατο ("from then on … began") at 4:17 and 16:21 only;
- ὀμνύω ("to swear") in chapter 23;
- Ps 21:9 Swete ῥυσάσθω ("let him deliver");
- the four Swete verses with ῥαπ- ("strike");
- the plural "lost sheep" at Ps 118:176 and Jer 27:6 only;
- τὸ πλανώμενον ("the straying one") at Ezek 34:4, 16 only;
- "great … in the sea" at Jonah 1:4 only;
- ἐνδεδυμένος ἔνδυμα ("clothed in a garment") at Zeph 1:8 only;
- εἰς νῖκος ("to victory") in eight Swete verses;
- θλῖψις οἵα ("tribulation such as") at Theodotion Dan 12:1 only.

One auditor's arithmetic was checked and corrected: Part C's reply counted three "Confirmed" while its own summary said two; the file's verdict lines give three, and those are the figures used below.

**Two limits** are recorded by the auditors:

- **Susanna.** The Rahlfs exports contain the canonical chapters of Daniel only, so every Susanna reading rests on Swete alone.

## Positional Frame (Phase 0.6)

The audit asked the two positional questions before scoping any claim.

**What has the book set up that these claims serve?** Matthew opens by naming the story it finishes (1:1–17). It cites its Scriptures in the narrator's voice ten times, and it ends with the presence formula (28:20). Most of the sweep's candidates are claims that this frame is wider than the formula citations. They propose that Matthew's own wording draws silently on Torah, Prophets and Writings at the turning points of the narrative: the temptation, the Sermon, the mission, the feast parables, the trial, the empty tomb.

**Why does this matter here?** Silent allusions are where an overview most easily overreaches. The first audit found that a third of the overview's allusions needed reframing, and that the Synoptic check was decisive. So the question for each claim was not only "does the wording match?". It was also "is this Matthew's own hand, or Mark's, or simply common Greek?"

### Claim A1: Gen 28:14–15 → Matt 28:19–20 (and 24:30)

**Proposal:** "Behold, I am with you … I will not leave you until I have done all that I have spoken" to Jacob, and "all the tribes of the earth", lie behind the Great Commission and 24:30. **Proposed rating:** moderate–high (on wording: ἕως ("until"), πάντα ὅσα ("all that"), "I am with you").

**Evidence.**
- Matthew (no Synoptic parallel, so this is Matthew's own wording): καὶ ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ μεθ' ὑμῶν εἰμι ("and behold, I am with you") πάσας τὰς ἡμέρας ἕως τῆς συντελείας τοῦ αἰῶνος ("all the days until the end of the age"), and πάντα ὅσα ἐνετειλάμην ("all that I commanded"), 28:20 [T]. Matthew's frame is the inclusio with 1:23 Μεθ' ἡμῶν ὁ θεός ("God with us") [T].
- Gen 28:15 in Swete: ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ μετὰ σοῦ ("behold, I am with you") … οὐ μή σε ἐγκαταλείπω ("I will not forsake you") ἕως τοῦ ποιῆσαί με πάντα ὅσα ἐλάλησά σοι ("until I have done all that I have spoken to you") [T]. Rahlfs is the same apart from the aorist ἐγκαταλίπω ("forsake") [T]. WLC: וְהִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עִמָּךְ ("and behold, I am with you") … עַד אֲשֶׁר ("until") [T]. Subject: God's travelling presence with Jacob until the land promise is fulfilled.
- Baselines (Swete): ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ μετά ("behold, I with") 2 verses (Gen 28:15; 1 Sam 14:7); πάντα ὅσα ἐλάλησα ("all that I spoke") 2 (Gen 28:15; 1 Sam 3:12) [T]. These are the strongest points for the proposal. But the shared items differ in function: Genesis's ἕως ("until") limits God's promise until it is fulfilled; Matthew's ἕως ("until") stretches the presence to the end of the age. Genesis's πάντα ὅσα ("all that") governs what God *spoke*, while Matthew's governs what Jesus *commanded* [T/I].
- Rivals (Tanak order): Deut 31:23 / Josh 1:7–9 (a commissioning, "as Moses commanded", μετὰ σοῦ ("with you")) [T]; Isa 43:5 μετὰ σοῦ εἰμι ("I am with you") with gathering east and west [T]; Hag 1:13 Ἐγώ εἰμι μεθ' ὑμῶν ("I am with you", plural) and Hag 2:4 μεθ' ὑμῶν ἐγώ εἰμι ("with you I am") — the only Swete verses with the plural form and εἰμι ("I am") (Rahlfs agrees) [T]; 2 Chr 36:23, the last verse of the Tanak (authority given by the God of heaven, a command, "let him go up") [T]. The presence formula has many sources, and Hag 1:13 matches Matthew's form more closely than Gen 28:15.
- 24:30: πᾶσαι αἱ φυλαὶ τῆς γῆς ("all the tribes of the earth") is Matthew's addition to Mark 13:26 [T]. In Swete the exact phrase occurs at Gen 12:3, Gen 28:14, Ps 71(72):17 and Ezek 20:32 [T]. It is the patriarchal blessing formula, combined with κόψονται ("they will mourn") from Zech 12:10–12. Gen 28:14 is one of three blessing loci and has no special claim [I].

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | Genesis LXX |
| Volume | Possible | ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ μετά ("behold, I with") is rare, but the shared words work differently in each text |
| Recurrence | Weak | No other Jacob-Bethel echo is evident in Matthew |
| Thematic Coherence | Possible | Divine presence on a journey fits the commission, but Emmanuel (1:23) is Matthew's own frame |
| Historical Plausibility | Strong | — |
| History of Interpretation | not checked | no commentaries used |
| Satisfaction | Weak | The commission is more satisfyingly explained by the 1:23 inclusio and the commissioning-presence formula as a type (Deut 31; Josh 1; Hag 1) |

**Verdict:** Needs reframing. **Rating:** moderate–high → low–moderate
**Reasoning:** 28:20 uses the biblical presence formula, which Matthew ties to 1:23. Gen 28:15 contributes at most one strand. Hag 1:13 is formally closer, and the "until / all that" words do different jobs in the two texts. In 24:30 the phrase is the Gen 12:3 / 28:14 blessing formula, not a Bethel echo.
**Spot-check line:** `swph('εγω ειμι μεθ υμων'), swph('μεθ υμων εγω ειμι')` → `['Gen 23:4', 'Hag 1:13'] ['Hag 2:4']` (the plural form is absent from Gen 28).

### Claim A2: Gen 49:33 → Matt 11:1 (and 7:28; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1)

**Proposal:** Jacob "finished charging his sons" is the model for "when Jesus had finished commanding his twelve disciples", and so for the discourse-ending formula. **Proposed rating:** candidate, unrated.

**Evidence.**
- Matthew: all five endings read καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς ("and it happened when Jesus finished") + an object/participle: τοὺς λόγους τούτους ("these words", 7:28; 19:1), τὰς παραβολὰς ταύτας ("these parables", 13:53), πάντας τοὺς λόγους τούτους ("all these words", 26:1), διατάσσων τοῖς δώδεκα μαθηταῖς ("commanding the twelve disciples", 11:1) [T]. ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν ("when he finished") occurs only in these five NT verses (SBLGNT) [T]. The formula is Matthew's own. διατάσσω ("command") occurs once in Matthew (SBLGNT) [T].
- Gen 49:33 in WLC: וַיְכַל יַעֲקֹב לְצַוֺּת אֶת־בָּנָיו ("and Jacob finished commanding his sons") [T]. Swete has a different verb: κατέπαυσεν Ἰακὼβ ἐπιτάσσων ("Jacob ceased commanding") [T]. Rahlfs is identical [T]. **The Greek OT does not use a τελέω ("finish") verb here**, so any link runs through the Hebrew idiom alone [T/I].
- Baseline: כלה ("finish") + infinitive is a stock narrative transition. It occurs at Gen 17:22; 18:33; 24:15; 49:33; Exod 31:18; 34:33; Num 16:31; Deut 32:45, among others (WLC; `find('lemma','3615','Genesis')` gives 16 verses in Genesis alone) [T].
- Rivals: Num 16:31 (WLC כְּכַלֹּתוֹ לְדַבֵּר אֵת כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה, "when he finished speaking all these words"; Swete ὡς δὲ ἐπαύσατο λαλῶν πάντας τοὺς λόγους τούτους, "when he ceased speaking all these words") [T]. Deut 31:1 Swete reads συνετέλεσεν Μωυσῆς λαλῶν πάντας τοὺς λόγους τούτους ("Moses finished speaking all these words"), against WLC וַיֵּלֶךְ ("and he went"). Deut 32:45 WLC reads וַיְכַל מֹשֶׁה לְדַבֵּר אֶת־כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים הָאֵלֶּה ("Moses finished speaking all these words") [T]. The Moses texts match the *series*, and especially 26:1 πάντας τοὺς λόγους τούτους ("all these words"), far better than Gen 49:33 [T/I].
- For Gen 49:33 the best case is 11:1 alone: a "commanding" verb plus twelve hearers, matching Jacob and his twelve sons [I].

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | — |
| Volume | Weak | No shared Greek verb. Only the Hebrew idiom is shared, and that idiom is common |
| Recurrence | Weak | The other four endings point to Moses' "all these words", not Jacob |
| Thematic Coherence | Possible | Twelve sons / twelve disciples at 11:1 only |
| Historical Plausibility | Possible | It requires Matthew to be working from the Hebrew [I] |
| History of Interpretation | not checked | no commentaries used |
| Satisfaction | Weak | The Deuteronomic Moses formula explains the whole series |

**Verdict:** Needs reframing. **Rating:** candidate → low (Gen 49:33). The formula's background is better described as Mosaic (Deut 31:1 LXX; 32:45 MT; Num 16:31): moderate.
**Reasoning:** The idiom is a Pentateuchal transition, and for the series as a whole Moses' "finished speaking all these words" is the best match. Gen 49:33 can be no more than a resonance at 11:1, and it lacks Greek support.
**Spot-check line:** `sw('Gen 49:33','Deu 31:1')` → Gen 49:33 reads κατέπαυσεν ("ceased"); Deu 31:1 reads συνετέλεσεν … πάντας τοὺς λόγους τούτους ("finished … all these words").

### Claim A3: Exod 30:11–16 → Matt 17:24–27; Exod 30:12 / Num 3:12 λύτρα → Matt 20:28

**Proposal:** The didrachm is the half-shekel λύτρα τῆς ψυχῆς ("ransom for the life"); the λύτρα ("ransom") of Exod 30 / Num 3 feeds λύτρον ("ransom") at 20:28. **Proposed rating:** moderate–high (17:24–27); strands (20:28).

**Evidence.**
- 17:24–27 is Matthew-only [T]. τὰ δίδραχμα ("the two-drachma tax") and στατήρ ("stater") are each NT hapaxes in SBLGNT. Matthew also has κῆνσον ("census tax", 17:25) and the substitution phrase ἀντὶ ἐμοῦ καὶ σοῦ ("for me and you", 17:27) [T]. **Matthew never uses λύτρα ("ransom") in the pericope** [T].
- Exod 30:12 Swete: δώσουσιν ἕκαστος λύτρα τῆς ψυχῆς αὐτοῦ ("each shall give a ransom for his life") at the census. 30:13: τὸ ἥμισυ τοῦ διδράχμου ("half of the didrachm"). 30:15–16: ἐξιλάσασθαι περὶ τῶν ψυχῶν ("to make atonement for your lives") [T]. WLC 30:12 כֹּפֶר נַפְשׁוֹ ("ransom of his life") [T]. Rahlfs agrees [T]. Exod 30 is the Torah's only per-head sanctuary levy [I].
- Mismatch: in the LXX the δίδραχμον ("didrachm") is the *shekel*, so the levy is *half* a didrachm. Matthew's "didrachma" is the contemporary coin value of the half-shekel. So the link is institutional, not lexical [T/I].
- Baselines: διδραχμ- ("didrachm") 19 verses in Swete, mostly valuations (Lev 27). λύτρα ("ransom", plural) 13 verses. λύτρον ("ransom", singular) once only, at Prov 13:8 [T].
- 20:28 = Mark 10:45 verbatim, so it is inherited [T]. Num 3:12's λύτρα αὐτῶν ἔσονται ("they shall be their ransom") is an **LXX plus**, absent from WLC [T]. Rahlfs has it [T]. The ἀντί ("in place of") + λύτρα ("ransom") pairing there is suggestive [I]. Rival frames for 20:28: Exod 21:30 (λύτρα τῆς ψυχῆς, "ransom for the life", identical to 30:12) and Num 35:31 (λύτρα περὶ ψυχῆς, "ransom for a life") show the phrase is legal stock. Isa 53:10–12 supplies ψυχή ("life/soul") given in death for πολλῶν ("many") [T]. The many-ness of 20:28 is better explained by Isa 53 [I].

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | — |
| Volume | Possible (17:24) / Weak (20:28) | Only δίδραχμ- ("didrachm") is shared, and its value differs; λύτρα ("ransom") is stock legal vocabulary |
| Recurrence | Possible | 17:27 ἀντί ("for") and 20:28 ἀντί ("for") with λύτρον ("ransom") within three chapters [I] |
| Thematic Coherence | Strong (17:24) | Sons are free, yet the Son pays a census-levy "for" Peter |
| Historical Plausibility | Strong | The temple tax was the Exod 30 levy as practised |
| History of Interpretation | not checked | no commentaries used |
| Satisfaction | Possible | Clear as institutional reference; the ransom freight is inferential |

**Verdict:** Confirmed with nuance. **Rating:** moderate–high → moderate overall. Exod 30 as the institutional referent of 17:24–27: high. The λύτρα ("ransom") reading of 17:24–27: moderate. The 20:28 strand: low–moderate (inherited from Mark; Isa 53 is stronger).
**Reasoning:** The tax is the Exod 30 levy beyond reasonable doubt. Reading it as "ransom" depends on readers supplying Exod 30:12, because Matthew does not say the word. The Num 3:12 strand depends on an LXX-only clause.
**Spot-check line:** `swre(r' λυτρον ')` → `['Pro 13:8']` (the singular λύτρον, "ransom", is not in Exod 30 or Num 3).

### Claim A4: Deut 8:2–5 → Matt 4:1–11

**Proposal:** Beyond the quotation of Deut 8:3, the wider Deut 8:2–5 frame shapes the temptation: being led, wilderness, forty, testing, hunger, son. **Proposed rating:** moderate–high.

**Evidence.**
- Matthew 4:1–4 vs Luke 4:1–4 (Q) and Mark 1:12–13: the wilderness, the forty days, πειράζω ("test"), hunger and "if you are the Son of God" all occur in Matthew's sources [T]. **Matthew's own increments:** καὶ νύκτας τεσσεράκοντα ("and forty nights", 4:2), and the fuller quotation of Deut 8:3, ἀλλ' ἐπὶ παντὶ ῥήματι ἐκπορευομένῳ διὰ στόματος θεοῦ ("but on every word proceeding from the mouth of God"), which Luke lacks [T]. ἀνήχθη ("he was led up") echoes ἤγαγεν ("he led") only loosely [T].
- Deut 8:2–5 Swete: ἤγαγέν σε … ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ("he led you … in the wilderness"), ἐκπειράσῃ σε ("to test you"), ἐλιμαγχόνησέν σε ("he let you hunger"), τεσσεράκοντα ἔτη ("forty years", 8:4), ὡς εἴ τις παιδεύσαι ἄνθρωπος τὸν υἱὸν αὐτοῦ ("as a man disciplines his son", 8:5) [T]. WLC 8:2 has זֶה אַרְבָּעִים שָׁנָה ("these forty years"), which Swete and Rahlfs lack in 8:2 but carry at 8:4 [T]. Subject: Israel, God's son, tested in the wilderness and fed by the word.
- Baselines: ἐλιμαγχόνησεν ("he let hunger") occurs only at Deut 8:3, and Matthew uses ἐπείνασεν ("he hungered") instead [T]. παιδεύσαι ἄνθρωπος ("a man disciplines") occurs only at Deut 8:5 [T].
- Rival or complement: "forty days and forty nights" occurs in 10 Swete verses, including the flood, Exod 24:18; 34:28; Deut 9:9, 18, 25; 10:10 and 1 Kgs 19:8 [T]. Exod 34:28 and Deut 9:9 add ἄρτον οὐκ ἔφαγεν ("he ate no bread") [T]. Matthew's own "nights" therefore pulls toward Moses' fast in Deut 9, the next chapter, without displacing Deut 8 [I].

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | — |
| Volume | Strong | Explicit quotation plus four or five frame elements from the same paragraph |
| Recurrence | Strong | Deut 6:13, 16 are quoted in the same pericope, so Matthew stays within Deut 6–8 |
| Thematic Coherence | Strong | The Son tested in the wilderness as Israel was, after 3:17 |
| Historical Plausibility | Strong | — |
| History of Interpretation | not checked | no commentaries used |
| Satisfaction | Strong | — |

**Verdict:** Confirmed with nuance. **Rating:** moderate–high → moderate–high
**Reasoning:** The Deut 8 frame is real, but it belongs mostly to the Q tradition. Matthew's own additions (the fuller 8:3, and "forty nights") strengthen it and add a Mosaic co-frame (Deut 9:9 / Exod 34:28) that the proposal does not mention.
**Spot-check line:** `swph('τεσσερακοντα ημερας και τεσσερακοντα νυκτας')` → 10 verses including Exo 34:28 and Deu 9:9.

### Claim A5: Deut 15:9 → Matt 6:22–23

**Proposal:** The "evil eye" is the grudging eye towards a needy brother in the year of release. **Proposed rating:** moderate–high.

**Evidence.**
- 6:22–23 is Q (Luke 11:34, almost verbatim), so it is inherited [T]. Matthew's hand lies in the placement: between treasure (6:19–21) and God/Mammon (6:24) [T]. Matthew's own clear use is 20:15, ἢ ὁ ὀφθαλμός σου πονηρός ἐστιν ὅτι ἐγὼ ἀγαθός εἰμι ("or is your eye evil because I am good?"), Matthew-only and explicitly about generosity [T]. In the NT the phrase occurs only in Matt 6:23 and 20:15, plus Mark 7:22 (SBLGNT) [T].
- Deut 15:9 Swete: πονηρεύσηται ὁ ὀφθαλμός σου τῷ ἀδελφῷ σου τῷ ἐπιδεομένῳ ("your eye be evil toward your needy brother"), καὶ οὐ δώσεις αὐτῷ ("and you do not give to him") [T]. Rahlfs is the same [T]. WLC וְרָעָה עֵינְךָ ("and your eye be evil") [T].
- Baselines: in WLC the evil-eye idiom (רע + עין) occurs in Deut 15:9; 28:54, 56; Prov 23:6; 28:22 [T]. Prov 22:9 has the antonym "good eye" (WLC טוֹב־עַיִן, "good of eye") for the one who gives to the poor [T]. In Greek, the exact noun phrase ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός ("evil eye") occurs in Sir 14:10 and Sir 34:13 (Swete numbering), and Sir 14:8–10 concerns stinginess with bread [T]. Deut 15:9 has a verb (πονηρεύσηται, "be evil"), not Matthew's adjective [T].
- So the idiom of stinginess is well attested. Deut 15:9 is its Torah anchor, but Matthew's Greek wording is closer to Sirach [I].

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | — |
| Volume | Weak | Only "eye" + "evil" is shared, the grammatical form differs, and the idiom is common |
| Recurrence | Possible | 20:15 uses the idiom of generosity, but makes no Deut 15 contact |
| Thematic Coherence | Possible | The money context of 6:19–24 fits. The year of release does not appear |
| Historical Plausibility | Strong | — |
| History of Interpretation | not checked | no commentaries used |
| Satisfaction | Possible | The idiom's meaning (stinginess) is secure; a *targeted* allusion to Deut 15:9 is not |

**Verdict:** Needs reframing. **Rating:** moderate–high → low–moderate (as a specific allusion); the idiom-meaning "grudging/stingy" is high.
**Reasoning:** Matthew uses a Hebrew idiom whose classic legal locus is Deut 15:9, but nothing points to that verse rather than the idiom. Sir 14:8–10 is the closest Greek wording. The sermon value is the idiom, not the allusion.
**Spot-check line:** `sw('Sir 14:10')` → ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρὸς φθονερὸς ἐπ' ἄρτῳ ("an evil eye is grudging over bread").

### Claim A6: Deut 18:16 → Matt 17:5–6

**Proposal:** Beside the accepted Deut 18:15 ἀκούετε αὐτοῦ ("listen to him"), the Horeb fear of 18:16 shapes the disciples' reaction. **Proposed rating:** moderate–high.

**Evidence.**
- ἀκούετε αὐτοῦ ("listen to him") is in Mark 9:7 and Luke 9:35, so it is inherited [T]. **Matthew alone** has 17:6–7: ἀκούσαντες … ἔπεσαν ἐπὶ πρόσωπον αὐτῶν καὶ ἐφοβήθησαν σφόδρα ("hearing, they fell on their faces and were greatly afraid"), then ἁψάμενος … Ἐγέρθητε καὶ μὴ φοβεῖσθε ("touching … rise, and do not be afraid") [T].
- Deut 18:16 Swete: Οὐ προσθήσομεν ἀκοῦσαι τὴν φωνὴν Κυρίου ("we will not again hear the voice of the Lord") … οὐδὲ μὴ ἀποθάνωμεν ("lest we die"), at Horeb [T]. Rahlfs agrees [T]. Parallels at Exod 20:19 and Deut 5:25 [T]. The sequence "listen to him" (18:15) followed by fear at the voice (18:16) does match Matthew's order (voice → hearing → fear) [I].
- But the Deut verse has no falling, no face, no touch, no "do not fear" [T].
- **Rival (stronger for 17:6–7):** Dan 10:9–12 (Theodotion, Swete "Dat"): ἐν τῷ ἀκοῦσαί με ("when I heard") … τὸ πρόσωπόν μου ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν ("my face to the ground") … χεὶρ ἁπτομένη μου ("a hand touching me") καὶ ἤγειρέν με ("and raised me") … Μὴ φοβοῦ ("do not fear") [T]. ### Claim A12: ἀπὸ τότε ("from then on") at 4:17, 16:21, 26:16 as a third structural hinge

- **Form.** 4:17 and 16:21 are identical in structure: sentence-initial Ἀπὸ τότε ἤρξατο ὁ Ἰησοῦς + infinitive ("from then on Jesus began to …": κηρύσσειν, "preach"; δεικνύειν, "show"), with programmatic content (the kingdom; the passion) [T]. 26:16 is different: καὶ ἀπὸ τότε ἐζήτει εὐκαιρίαν ("and from then on he sought an opportunity"). In all three, ἀπὸ τότε ("from then on") is Matthew's addition. That makes 26:16 deliberate as wording [T/I]. Luke 22:6 shares εὐκαιρίαν ("opportunity") with Matthew against Mark, a minor agreement [T].
- **Competing markers.** The five-fold ending formula (καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε ἐτέλεσεν, "and when he had finished") stands at 26:1, fifteen verses before 26:16. It, not 26:16, opens the passion block [T]. ἤρξατο ("began") with Jesus as subject also occurs at 11:7 and 11:20 (Τότε ἤρξατο, "then he began") [T]. τότε ("then") alone occurs 89× in Matthew against 6× in Mark (SBLGNT), so the adverb is a Matthean habit [T].

### Claim C1: Hos 1:10 Swete (MT 2:1) → Matt 5:9

**Proposal:** κληθήσονται … υἱοὶ θεοῦ ("they shall be called sons of God") in the peacemakers' beatitude echoes Hosea's reversal of Lo-ammi.  **Proposed rating:** moderate–high

### Claim C2: Amos 8:9 / Exod 10:22 → Matt 27:45

**Proposal:** The darkness at the sixth hour echoes Amos 8:9 (the sun going down at noon) and Exod 10:22 (darkness over all the land). The darkness is inherited from Mark 15:33.  **Proposed rating:** moderate

**Evidence.**
- Mark 15:33 has σκότος ἐγένετο ἐφ’ ὅλην τὴν γῆν ("darkness came over the whole land"), and Luke 23:44 has the same. Matthew alters this to σκότος ἐγένετο ἐπὶ πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν ("darkness came over all the land"). Elsewhere Matthew happily writes ὅλος ("whole"), e.g. ὅλην τὴν Συρίαν ("all Syria", 4:24). So the change is not just his habit, and it moves him towards Exod 10:22 ἐγένετο σκότος … ἐπὶ πᾶσαν γῆν Αἰγύπτου ("there came darkness … over all the land of Egypt"; Rahlfs identical). [T]/[I]
- Amos 8:9:
  - WLC: וְהֵבֵאתִי הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ בַּצָּהֳרָיִם ("I will make the sun go down at noon").
  - Swete and Rahlfs: δύσεται ὁ ἥλιος μεσημβρίας, καὶ συσκοτάσει ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς ("the sun will set at noon, and it will grow dark over the earth").
  - There is no σκότος ("darkness") noun, and no "all". The match is conceptual: noon matches the sixth hour.
  - Move 1: the darkness is the Day of the LORD on Israel. Amos 8:10 continues ὡς πένθος ἀγαπητοῦ ("as mourning for a beloved [only] son"), which coheres strongly with a crucifixion. [T]/[I]
- Baselines:
  - ἐπὶ πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν ("over all the land") occurs in 19 Swete verses, so it is common. What is rare is the combination σκότος + ἐγένετο + "over all the land", which occurs only in Exod 10:22.
  - μεσημβρία ("noon") occurs in 7 Swete verses.
  - Matthew has ἐπὶ πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν ("over all the land") only here. [T]
- Rival: Jer 15:9 ἐπέδυ ὁ ἥλιος … ἔτι μεσούσης τῆς ἡμέρας ("the sun went down while it was still midday") is a weaker rival. Its subject is the mother of seven. [T]

Four of the eight proposed chains are inherited from Mark, and three are mis-sourced:
   - ὀμνύω ("to swear") recurs in chapter 23 first;
   - ψευδομαρτυρία ("false testimony") and ἀρνέομαι ("to deny") do not come from the Sermon;
   - ῥύομαι ("to deliver") at 27:43 comes from Ps 22:9 (Swete 21:9).

   **What survives** is a handful of deliberate Matthean correspondences. Jesus keeps his own teaching under trial:
   - γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου ("your will be done", 6:10 → 26:42);
   - ῥαπίζω ("to slap", 5:39 → 26:67), with Isa 50:6 behind both;
   - Ἀπόστρεψον τὴν μάχαιράν σου ("put your sword back", 26:52, Matthew's own), which enacts 5:39.

   **Rating for the design:** low–moderate.
2. **The shepherd sequence is real, but Ezekiel 34 is its hub, not Ezekiel 34 with Zechariah 13 (B12).** Each link draws on its own text: 2 Sam 5:2 (2:6), Num 27:17 (9:36), Jer 50:6 (10:6; 15:24), Ezek 34 (18:12–14; 25:32) and Zech 13:7 (26:31). The bridge from Zech 13:7 to 18:14 ("the little ones") holds in the WLC and Swete. Rahlfs reads ἐπὶ τοὺς ποιμένας ("upon the shepherds") instead. **Rating for the design:** moderate.
3. **The Hebrew is again where Matthew's departures point.** Several of the strongest results rest on the Hebrew and not on either Greek text:
   - **Isa 22:22 (16:19).** The "key" is in the WLC only. Swete has τὴν δόξαν Δαυείδ ("the glory of David"), and Rahlfs drops the open/shut clause.
   - **Hab 1:4 (12:20).** Only Hab 1:4 joins "go forth", לָנֶצַח ("for ever") and מִשְׁפָּט ("justice") in the WLC. The translators render לָנֶצַח as εἰς νῖκος ("to victory") in six of the seven verses checked.
   - **Zeph 1:3 (13:41)** is a new candidate, and it rests on the Hebrew too: "the stumbling-blocks with the wicked", which the LXX lacks.

   This supports the sweep's third headline from a different direction.
