## The Passage

A whole-book sweep cannot quote the sixty-one chapters. The load-bearing frame of the book — the texts every tool keeps returning to — is set out here in ESV; the tools below range across the whole Gospel.

**The opening (1:1):** "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham."

**The naming (1:21–23):** "She will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins … 'Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel' (which means, God with us)."

**The proclamation (4:17):** "From that time Jesus began to preach, saying, 'Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.'"

**The closing (28:18–20):** "And Jesus came and said to them, 'All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me. Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you. And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age.'"

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools:

1. **The Gospel is bracketed by a "God with us" inclusio (1:23 *Immanuel* → 28:20 "I am with you always"), and everything between is arranged to disclose gradually who that "us-accompanying God" is.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Structure (bookends), Vocabulary (Immanuel), Repetition ("with"), Quotation/Allusion (Isa 7:14 Move 4 internal echo), Christological Reading. The name promised at the start is enacted, in person, at the end — no departure scene, unlike Mark, Luke, or John. Preach Matthew as the book that opens and closes on presence.

2. **Matthew's controlling claim is that in Jesus the whole story of Israel's Scriptures reaches fulfilment — and this is carried structurally, not just asserted.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Author's Purpose, Narrator's Comment (the ~10–12 "this was to fulfil" formula quotations, four of them clustered in chs. 1–2), Repetition (*plēroō*, "fulfil"), Quotation/Allusion (the OT is quoted more densely here than in any other Gospel). The fulfilment formula is Matthew's own authorial voice breaking in to interpret his story.

3. **The book is built to make its reader a disciple — and the ending turns every disciple into a disciple-maker.** `[T]` for the disciple-shaping arc; `[S: Cooper]` for the sharpened claim that "disciples who are disciple-makers" is *the* single controlling purpose. Surfaced by: Author's Purpose, Structure (the five teaching discourses framed by narrative), So What?, Who Am I? (the reader is cast as a learner at Jesus' feet), Repetition ("disciple", *mathētēs*). The Great Commission's "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (28:20) points back over the shoulder to the five discourses — the book is designed to be re-read.

4. **Jesus' divine identity is unveiled gradually — Son of David/Messiah at the start (1:1), full divine authority "in heaven and on earth" at the end (28:18) — so that the reader who began with a human king finishes worshipping God.** `[T]` for the trajectory; `[S: Cooper]` for the explicit "gradual unveiling" frame. Surfaced by: Structure, Vocabulary (the title-clusters: Christ, Son of David, Son of God, Son of Man, Lord/*kyrios*), Repetition (worship, *proskyneō*), Christological Reading. Worship of Jesus runs from the magi (2:2, 11) to the mountain (28:17), and it is never rebuked.

5. **Jesus takes on the role of Isaiah's Servant of the LORD, and this is the interpretive key to both his suffering and his mission to the nations.** `[T]` Surfaced by: Quotation/Allusion (the two direct Servant-song citations, 8:17 ← Isa 53:4 and the longest OT quotation in the book, 12:18–21 ← Isa 42:1–4; the baptismal and transfiguration voice, 3:17/17:5, echoing Isa 42:1), Biblical-Theological Themes (Servant), Christological Reading. The Servant who is "a light for the nations" (Isa 42:6; 49:6) is why the book that opens in Bethlehem ends commissioning "all nations."

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level thrust.** Matthew writes to present Jesus of Nazareth as the Messiah, Son of David and Son of Abraham (1:1), in whom the Law and the Prophets reach their fulfilment, and to bring his readers — through a close narrative encounter with this Jesus — to become his disciples, and through his teaching to be formed as disciples who make further disciples of all nations (5–7; 10; 13; 18; 24–25; 28:16–20). `[T]` The purpose is unusually accessible for a biblical book because Matthew states his frame at both ends: the genealogy anchors Jesus in the covenant story (1:1–17); the Commission discloses where the whole book is heading (28:18–20).

Two features of the book are the load-bearing evidence for this purpose, and no other reading accounts for both together: (a) the sheer density of **fulfilment quotation**, which shows Matthew's concern to place Jesus inside Israel's Scriptures; and (b) the **five great teaching discourses**, which show that Matthew is casting Jesus as authoritative teacher and his readers as learners under instruction. `[T]`

**Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper sharpens (b) into a single controlling purpose — "to make disciples of Jesus who are in turn disciple-makers" — and argues the book is best read as a "missionary training narrative" in which narrative draws the reader in and the discourses form and equip them. `[S: Cooper]` This is a legitimate and text-warranted sharpening; the four wide-scope calls he traces (4:17; 11:28–30; 16:24; 28:19–20) do form a real sequence in the text `[T]`. But note it is a *sharpening*, not the only defensible statement of purpose: Matthew's fulfilment concern (a) is at least co-equal with the disciple-making concern (b), and a purpose statement that foregrounds only disciple-making risks under-weighting the Christological-fulfilment spine that occupies chapters 1–2 and the passion. Hold both.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

At book level, the two Positional Necessity questions become: *why does this book stand where it does, and how does its own opening set up its own ending?*

**Preceding movement (canonical position).** Matthew opens the New Testament, and it is engineered to. The genealogy (1:1–17) is a deliberate hinge from the Old Testament to the New: it reaches back to Abraham and David and forward to "the Christ" (1:17), and its explicit third panel — "and from the deportation to Babylon to the Christ, fourteen generations" — frames the whole New Testament as the answer to the still-unresolved exile. `[T]` The canonical reader arrives at 1:1 having read a story that ended (in the Christian ordering) with Malachi's promise of a coming messenger and a still-absent kingdom; Matthew's first word (*biblos geneseōs*, "book of the genealogy / of the beginning") answers that expectation and even echoes Genesis. `[I]`

**Necessity answer (internal).** The book's ending is present in its beginning. What 1:21 promises — "he will save his people from their sins" — is enacted at 26:28, "my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." `[T]` What 1:23 promises — "God with us" — is delivered at 28:20, "I am with you always." `[T]` What the devil offers and Jesus refuses in 4:8–9 (all the kingdoms of the world) the Father gives in 28:18 (all authority in heaven and on earth). `[T]` The book exists as a single arc from promise (chs. 1–2) to fulfilment (chs. 26–28), and its climactic Commission is only intelligible as the resolution of tensions planted in the opening.

**Implication.** Matthew should never be preached as a loose anthology of pericopes. Each unit sits inside a book with a beginning that predicts an ending. The genealogy is not throat-clearing before the "real" material; it is the departure point that makes everything after it legible.

**General context.** Immediate literary neighbours within the book matter as much as canonical position: the birth narrative (1–2) is background; 3:1–4:11 commissions and tests Jesus; 4:12 opens the Galilean proclamation. Every discourse is embedded in a narrative setting that shapes it — the Sermon on the Mount (5–7) lands with authority only because 1–4 has established *who is speaking* (the new-Moses, tested-in-the-wilderness Son of God). `[T]` **Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper (following Weren, and against Kingsbury's threefold "from that time" division) proposes a four-part structure — Background (1–2); 3:1–4:11 commissioning; 4:12–10:42 kingdom-proclamation pattern; 11–28 Jesus completing the Servant's mandate. `[S: Cooper]` See Tool 3.

### 3. Structure

Two structural systems run simultaneously in Matthew, and both are text-signalled rather than imposed.

**(a) The five-discourse formula (the clearest datum).** Five great blocks of Jesus' teaching each close with a near-identical formula — "And when Jesus had finished these sayings/instructing…" (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). `[T]` The five discourses are, roughly: the Sermon on the Mount (5–7), the Mission discourse (10), the Parables of the kingdom (13), the Community discourse (18), and the Olivet/eschatological discourse (24–25). This fivefold shaping is one of the most secure observations in Matthaean scholarship and is often read as an evocation of the five books of the Torah, casting Jesus as a new Moses giving teaching from a mountain. `[T]` for the fivefold formula; `[I]` for the Pentateuch echo (widely held but a matter of inference).

| Discourse | Chapters | Theme |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| Sermon on the Mount | 5–7 | Life and righteousness of the kingdom |
| Mission | 10 | Sending the Twelve to proclaim the kingdom |
| Parables | 13 | The kingdom's hidden, mixed, surprising growth |
| Community | 18 | Humility, discipline, forgiveness among disciples |
| Olivet | 24–25 | Perseverance and watchfulness to the end |

**(b) The narrative geography and the "from that time" pivots.** Alongside the discourses runs a narrative spine with a strong geographical shape: Galilee (up to ch. 16), the journey up to Jerusalem (16–20), Jerusalem itself (21–27), and a return to Galilee (28:16–20). Two occurrences of "From that time Jesus began…" (4:17; 16:21) mark turning points — the start of the public proclamation, and the turn toward the cross after Peter's confession. `[T]`

**Bookends and framing.** Two major inclusios structure the whole: *Immanuel*/"God with us" (1:23) with "I am with you always" (28:20) `[T]`; and the summary of Jesus' ministry ("teaching… proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom… healing every disease," 4:23) repeated almost verbatim at 9:35 `[T]`, bracketing the Sermon and the miracle-cycle (chs. 5–9) as a single unit of word-and-deed proclamation. The genealogy's own threefold 14-14-14 pattern (1:17) is a miniature of Matthew's fondness for triadic arrangement. `[T]`

**Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper's four-section macro-structure (Background 1–2; 3:1–4:11; 4:12–10:42; 11:1–28:20) `[S: Cooper]` is a genuine alternative organising principle to the discourse-count, keyed to the *repeatable pattern* of kingdom-proclamation (Jesus first, then multiplied through the Twelve in ch. 10) which is then "worked out in the particular" across 11–28. It is compatible with, not a rival to, the five-discourse reading — Cooper folds the discourses inside his four sections (his Figure 4). The preacher can use both: the four sections give the narrative arc; the five discourses give the teaching skeleton. **Single-source note:** Cooper leans on Weren for this; the four-fold division is a minority framing against the more common two-fold (narrative/discourse alternation) and three-fold (Kingsbury) proposals — hold it as one good option, not the settled consensus.

### 4. Linking Words

At book level the decisive connectors are the structural formulae rather than sentence-level conjunctions. Four are load-bearing:

- **"From that time" (*apo tote*, 4:17; 16:21)** — narrative pivots, not mere temporal markers: the beginning of proclamation, and the turn to the passion. `[T]`
- **"Therefore" in the Commission (*oun*, 28:19)** — "All authority… has been given to me. *Go therefore*." The mission is grounded in the conferred authority: because Jesus now holds all authority, the nations can be discipled. Drop the "therefore" and the Commission floats free of its Christological ground. `[T]`
- **The fulfilment formula ("this took place *to fulfil*…", *hina/hopōs plērōthē*)** — functions as a logical connector between event and Scripture; see Tool 6. `[T]`
- **"For" (*gar*) in the beatitude-grounds and the Commission's close** — e.g. each beatitude's "for theirs is…", and "*and behold* I am with you always," which grounds the whole command in Christ's promised presence. `[T]`

Within discourse material the sentence-level connectors reward the standard clause-flow treatment (e.g. the "but I say to you" antitheses of 5:21–48; the "therefore" of 6:25 grounding "do not be anxious" in the preceding teaching on treasure). These are best worked at pericope level in a solo dig-deeper rather than catalogued here.

### 5. Parallels

Poetic parallelism is not the dominant register of a Gospel, but Matthew preserves and shapes several strongly parallel units, and the tool is *not* N/A:

- **The Beatitudes (5:3–10)** — eight parallel "Blessed are… for…" lines, framed by an inclusio ("for theirs is the kingdom of heaven," 5:3, 10) that marks them as a single unit. `[T]`
- **The Lord's Prayer (6:9–13)** — parallel petitions, with the first triad God-ward ("your name… your kingdom… your will") and "on earth as it is in heaven" bridging the two halves. `[T]`
- **The antitheses (5:21–48)** — six parallel "You have heard… but I say to you" constructions. `[T]`
- **Wisdom-style antithetical pairs** — the two builders (7:24–27), the two gates and two ways (7:13–14), wheat and weeds, sheep and goats (25:31–46). Matthew's teaching is saturated with binary parallelism serving a two-ways moral vision. `[T]`

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Far from N/A — narratorial intrusion is one of Matthew's signature devices, and it is where his theology is most explicit.

- **The fulfilment formulas** are the primary case: roughly ten to twelve editorial asides in which the narrator steps outside the action to say "this took place to fulfil what the Lord had spoken by the prophet." `[T]` Four cluster in the infancy narrative (1:22–23 ← Isa 7:14; 2:15 ← Hos 11:1; 2:17–18 ← Jer 31:15; 2:23 ← "he shall be called a Nazarene"), with others at 4:14–16 (Isa 9:1–2); 8:17 (Isa 53:4); 12:17–21 (Isa 42:1–4, the longest OT quotation in the Gospel); 13:35 (Ps 78:2); 21:4–5 (Zech 9:9); 27:9–10 (Zech 11:12–13/Jer). These are the narrator's authoritative interpretation of his own story. `[T]`
- **Translation asides:** "Immanuel (which means, God with us)" (1:23); "Golgotha (which means Place of a Skull)" (27:33); the citation and translation of Jesus' cry, "Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?" (27:46). `[T]`
- **Editorial verdicts and explanations:** "for he taught them as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (7:29); the note that at Jesus' death "the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom" (27:51) — an event the narrator reports without interpretation, leaving the reader to weigh it. `[T]`

**Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper reads the longest quotation, 12:17–21 (Isa 42:1–4), as Matthew's own commentary confirming "explicitly that Jesus has taken on the role of Isaiah's Servant of the Lord" — a narratorial hinge for the whole 11–28 movement. `[S: Cooper]` This coheres with the text-first observation that the formula quotations are the narrator's theological voice.

### 7. Vocabulary

Six word-clusters materially shape the whole book.

**(a) *Christos* / Son of David / Son of Abraham (1:1).** "Christ" (Anointed One, = Messiah) frames the book at 1:1 and is used sparingly thereafter (about fourteen times). "Son of David" carries the royal, Davidic-covenant freight (2 Sam 7); "son of Abraham" the promise-to-the-nations freight (Gen 12:3). `[T]` **Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper notes Matthew *never* puts "Christ" on Jesus' own lips and uses it sparingly, perhaps because of its capacity for political misunderstanding (cf. 16:13–23). `[S: Cooper]` — a plausible inference the text supports (Jesus deflects the title toward "Son of Man" precisely at Caesarea Philippi). `[I]`

**(b) Son of Man (*ho huios tou anthrōpou*).** Jesus' preferred self-designation, used ~30 times, beginning at 8:20. In the Hebrew Scriptures the phrase can mean simply "human being" (Ps 8:4; Ezekiel), but Jesus' usage — most obviously at 26:64 ("you will see the Son of Man… coming on the clouds of heaven") — alludes to the vindicated, authority-receiving figure of Daniel 7:13–14. `[T]` The title uniquely holds together suffering *and* vindication, which is why Jesus chooses it over "Messiah" as he moves toward the cross.

**(c) *Kyrios* ("Lord").** Deliberately ambiguous: it can mean "Sir" (a term of respect), but it is also the LXX rendering of the divine name YHWH. Matthew exploits the ambiguity — John prepares the way of "the Lord" (3:3, quoting Isa 40:3, where "Lord" is YHWH), and the one who comes is Jesus; "Lord, save me!" (14:30) echoes the psalmists' cry to YHWH. `[T]` / `[S: Cooper]` for the specific catalogue of instances.

**(d) "Kingdom of heaven" (*hē basileia tōn ouranōn*).** Matthew's characteristic phrase (~32×), where the other Gospels say "kingdom of God." Matthew *also* uses "kingdom of God" four times (12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43). `[T]` **Commentary note (Cooper) — single-source, flag:** Cooper follows Jonathan Pennington in arguing that the plural "heavens" is significant and that "kingdom of the heavens" is *not* simply a reverential circumlocution for "kingdom of God" but denotes a specific future reality — the kingdom established *in* the heavens coming down so that heaven and earth are reunited (cf. 6:10). `[S: Cooper/Pennington]` This is a distinctive, contestable reading: the older and still-common view is that "kingdom of heaven" is a reverential Jewish periphrasis avoiding the divine name, functionally equivalent to "kingdom of God." Pennington's spatial reading is well-argued but a minority position; flag it as a live option, not a settled result, and do not build a whole sermon's weight on the singular/plural distinction without commentary corroboration. See Open Questions.

**(e) *Dikaiosynē* ("righteousness").** A key Matthaean term (7×, e.g. 3:15; 5:6, 10, 20; 6:1, 33) largely absent as a theme from Mark. The "greater righteousness" of 5:20 and "be perfect" of 5:48 are load-bearing and pastorally dangerous (see Difficult Verses). `[T]`

**(f) Names as theology.** *Jesus* ("YHWH saves") is explicitly decoded — "you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins" (1:21) `[T]`; the Greek *Iēsous* is also the LXX form of *Joshua*, an identity the NT elsewhere exploits (Heb 4:8). *Immanuel* ("God with us," 1:23) is decoded by the narrator and paid off at 28:20. `[T]` The proper-noun inventory of the book is dense with canonical freight: **Bethlehem** (city of David, Mic 5:2, cited 2:6), **Egypt** (2:15, retracing the Exodus route), **Ramah** (2:18, Jer 31:15, exile weeping), **Nazareth**/**Galilee of the nations** (4:15), **Jordan**, **wilderness**, **Zion/Jerusalem**. Each is load-bearing and re-read below under Tool 11.

### 8. Translations

At book level the comparisons that recur:

- **"Kingdom of heaven" vs "kingdom of God."** All the major English versions render Matthew's *basileia tōn ouranōn* as "kingdom of heaven," obscuring the plural "heavens" that Cooper/Pennington make weight-bearing. `[T]` A finding that hangs on the plural should note the versions unanimously flatten it; NASB/ESV/KJV give no help here, and the point can only be made from the Greek. Flag rather than assert.
- **5:3 "poor in spirit"** — stable across ESV/NASB/KJV; the interpretive difficulty is exegetical, not text-critical (see Difficult Verses).
- **6:11 "daily bread" (*epiousios*)** — a famously rare Greek word (arguably coined); ESV/NASB "daily," KJV "daily," margins offer "for tomorrow" / "needful." A place where translations agree only by convention over a genuinely uncertain word. `[T]` *Uncertain — consider verifying* (BDAG; NET notes).
- **16:18 "on this rock I will build my church"** — the *Petros*/*petra* wordplay is a translation and exegesis crux (see Difficult Verses). `[T]`

**Ancient versions check.** The trigger fires at 1:23: the modern versions agree on "virgin" for *parthenos* (following Matthew's Greek), and the reading is weight-bearing. The underlying question runs back to Isaiah 7:14, where the Hebrew *‘almāh* ("young woman") was rendered *parthenos* ("virgin") by the **LXX** — the version Matthew quotes. `[T]` **Translation-tradition consideration:** the split is not between ancient and modern *NT* versions but between the Hebrew of Isaiah and its Greek (LXX) rendering that Matthew follows; the LXX's *parthenos* is the fulcrum of Matthew's citation and of centuries of debate. *High confidence* that Matthew quotes the LXX; the *‘almāh*/*parthenos* question itself is a standing crux (see Difficult Verses, Open Questions).

**Pulpit edition note:** Pulpit text (ESV Anglicised) matches report default; no exegetically load-bearing divergence in the frame texts.

### 9. Tone and Feel

Matthew's dominant register is **authoritative and majestic** — the Gospel reads with a certain formal, ordered gravity (the careful genealogy, the balanced discourses, the recurring formula quotations). But the tone modulates markedly across the book, and honouring the modulation is a preaching discipline:

- **Solemn fulfilment** in the infancy narrative — wonder shadowed by threat (magi worship; Herod's massacre; the Ramah weeping, 2:18). `[T]`
- **Programmatic authority** in the Sermon on the Mount — "but I say to you," climaxing in the crowds' astonishment "for he taught them as one who had authority" (7:28–29). `[T]`
- **Escalating conflict and prophetic indignation** through chs. 11–23, cresting in the seven woes on the scribes and Pharisees (ch. 23) — sustained, searing denunciation ("hypocrites… blind guides… whitewashed tombs… you serpents, you brood of vipers"). This is Isaiah-5 / Amos-5 register; it should not be preached mildly. `[T]`
- **Restrained horror and pathos** in the passion — the betrayal, the cry of dereliction (27:46), the tearing of the curtain and the earthquake. `[T]`
- **Triumphant commission** at the close — but note the deliberate note of realism: "but some doubted" (28:17) sits inside the resurrection worship. `[T]`

The book's emotional architecture moves from threatened-birth, through authoritative-teaching and mounting-conflict, to death-and-vindication, to sending. A sermon series should track that arc rather than flatten every scene to one mood.

### 10. Repetition

Matthew is a deliberate repeater; the drumbeats are the argument.

- **"Kingdom of heaven / God"** — ~36× total; the master theme, announced by John (3:2) and Jesus (4:17) in identical words and running to the end. `[T]`
- **"Your/their Father in heaven" (or "heavenly Father")** — a Matthaean signature (~20×, concentrated in the Sermon), grounding ethics and prayer in filial relationship. `[T]`
- **"That it might be fulfilled" / *plēroō*** — the formula quotations plus wider fulfilment language; the repeated claim that Jesus completes the Scriptures. `[T]`
- **"You of little faith" (*oligopistos*)** — a distinctive Matthaean tag for the disciples (6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8; 17:20), charting their half-formed, wavering faith. `[T]`
- **"Weeping and gnashing of teeth"** — six times (8:12; 13:42, 50; 22:13; 24:51; 25:30), a relentless refrain of final judgment unique in its frequency to Matthew. `[T]`
- **"Hypocrites" / "woe to you"** — the drumbeat of ch. 23 and the Sermon's polemic. `[T]`
- **Worship (*proskyneō*)** — recurring and structurally placed: magi (2:2, 8, 11), the leper (8:2), the disciples in the boat (14:33), the women and the Eleven at the resurrection (28:9, 17). Never rebuked when directed to Jesus. `[T]`
- **"Behold" (*idou*)** — a narratorial attention-marker studded through the book, including the final "behold, I am with you always." `[T]`
- **Mountains** — sermon-mountain (5:1), transfiguration-mountain (17:1), commission-mountain (28:16); the mountain is Matthew's revelation-site. `[T]`

**Near-repetition worth noting:** 4:23 and 9:35 are almost verbatim, framing chs. 5–9 (Tool 3). And John's and Jesus' proclamations are *identical* (3:2 = 4:17), binding forerunner and Messiah into one message. `[T]`

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

Matthew's OT usage is the densest in the New Testament; a whole-book sweep can only run the triad on the load-bearing citations and record the internal-echo (Move 4) network. Matthew's **confirmed live sources** are, above all, **Isaiah** (formula quotations at 1:23; 4:15–16; 8:17; 12:18–21; and the Servant frame throughout), the **Psalms** (2:7 at the baptism; 8/110 in the passion; 22 at the cross; 118 at the entry), **Deuteronomy** (the three wilderness temptation ripostes, 4:4, 7, 10, all from Deut 6–8), **Daniel 7** (Son of Man), and the **Minor Prophets** (Hosea 11:1; Micah 5:2; Zechariah 9:9; 11:12–13). Any further echo from these has an elevated prior.

**Isaiah 7:14 → Matthew 1:23** *(high confidence)*
- *Source context:* Isaiah 7 — Ahaz, faithless king of Judah, is offered a sign amid the Syro-Ephraimite threat; the sign of "Immanuel" is given as pledge that God is with his people to preserve the Davidic line through crisis. The sign's original horizon is a child born in Isaiah's own day, but the name "God with us" carries a surplus the Davidic covenant keeps alive.
- *Book usage:* This is the first of Matthew's formula quotations; it establishes Isaiah as the dominant source for the whole book and plants the "God with us" theme that Matthew will not resolve until 28:20. `[T]`
- *OT-to-OT:* Isaiah 7's Immanuel is developed within Isaiah itself (8:8, 10; and the child of 9:6–7, "Mighty God"), so citing 7:14 activates the wider Isaianic Immanuel/royal-child complex. `[I]`
- *What it adds:* Matthew reads the virginal conception as the moment "God with us" becomes literal and permanent, not merely a dynastic pledge. The LXX *parthenos* (for Hebrew *‘almāh*) is the hinge (see Tool 8). This is the book's opening bracket.

**Hosea 11:1 → Matthew 2:15** *(high confidence)*
- *Source context:* Hosea 11:1 — "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" — is a *retrospective* statement about the Exodus and God's fatherly love for the nation Israel, immediately followed by Israel's apostasy (11:2). It is not, in its own context, a prediction.
- *Book usage:* Matthew has already called Jesus God's "Son" (implied in the Davidic frame; explicit at 3:17). Applying Hosea 11:1 to the child Jesus casts Jesus as the true Israel who re-walks Israel's story (down to Egypt, up out of Egypt) but succeeds where the nation failed. `[T]`
- *OT-to-OT:* Hosea's "son" language draws on Exodus 4:22 ("Israel is my firstborn son"), so the citation reaches back through Hosea to the Exodus sonship motif. `[I]`
- *What it adds:* This is Matthew's clearest **typological** move: Jesus recapitulates Israel. It licenses the wilderness-temptation reading (Jesus, tested forty days, quotes Deuteronomy back at the tempter where Israel, tested forty years, failed). The citation works by pattern and sonship, not by prediction — a key for teaching how Matthew uses the OT.

**Isaiah 42:1–4 → Matthew 12:18–21** *(high confidence — the longest OT quotation in the Gospel)*
- *Source context:* The first Servant Song. The LORD's chosen Servant, Spirit-endowed, will bring forth justice to the nations — gently ("a bruised reed he will not break"), not by crushing opposition, persisting until he establishes justice, and the coastlands (nations) wait for his law.
- *Book usage:* Isaiah is Matthew's spine; the Servant frame is set up already at the baptism (3:17 echoes Isa 42:1, "my beloved… with whom I am well pleased") and 8:17 (Isa 53:4, "he took our illnesses"). By 12:18–21, placed as Jesus withdraws from lethal opposition, the narrator makes the Servant identification explicit and total. `[T]`
- *OT-to-OT:* Isaiah's Servant Songs (42; 49; 50; 52–53) form an internal sequence; citing 42 activates the whole arc, including the Servant as "a light for the nations" (42:6; 49:6) and as sin-bearer (53). `[T]`
- *What it adds:* This is the interpretive key to the whole second half of the book (Cooper: chs. 11–28 as the Servant completing his mandate). The Servant who is gentle to the bruised and a light to the nations is *why* the Gospel ends commissioning all nations, and *why* Jesus meets opposition with withdrawal rather than judgment "at this time."

**Daniel 7:13 → Matthew (Son of Man), esp. 26:64** *(high confidence)*
- *Source context:* Daniel 7 — after four beastly kingdoms crush the holy people, "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds to the Ancient of Days and receives everlasting dominion; the vision's interpretation vindicates the persecuted saints of the Most High.
- *Book usage:* Jesus' preferred self-title (~30×) is claimed most overtly before the Sanhedrin (26:64), turning his trial into the moment of Danielic vindication-in-reverse: the judged one is the coming judge. `[T]`
- *OT-to-OT:* Daniel 7 itself reworks earlier throne-theophany and kingdom material; and at 26:64 Jesus fuses Daniel 7:13 with Psalm 110:1 ("seated at the right hand of Power"), two texts already read together in second-Temple messianism. `[I]`
- *What it adds:* "Son of Man" lets Matthew hold suffering and glory together (Cooper, via France: "after his humiliation and suffering, Jesus will receive… the vindication… given to 'one like a son of man' in Daniel 7"). It supplies the eschatological authority that becomes "all authority in heaven and on earth" at 28:18. `[T]` / `[S: Cooper]`

**Psalm 2:7 (+ Isa 42:1) → Matthew 3:17; 17:5** *(high confidence)*
- *Source context:* Psalm 2 — the LORD's Anointed (Messiah) is enthroned as "my Son" and will inherit the nations; Isaiah 42:1 — the Servant is upheld and delighted in. The heavenly voice fuses royal Sonship (Ps 2) with Servant-vocation (Isa 42).
- *Book usage:* The voice recurs at the transfiguration (17:5), bracketing the central section and confirming the Son-Servant identity precisely as Jesus turns to the cross. `[T]`
- *What it adds:* From the outset the reader is told the King (Ps 2) will reign *as* the Servant (Isa 42) — royal and suffering vocations are welded at the baptism and never separated. `[T]`

**Move 4 — Internal echoes (the book answering itself):**
- *Internal (answers §1:21 / planted-and-resolved):* "he will save his people from their sins" (1:21) → "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins" (26:28). *High confidence.* `[T]`
- *Internal (answers §1:23):* "God with us" (1:23) → "I am with you always" (28:20). The book's outer bracket. *High confidence.* `[T]`
- *Internal (answers §4:8–9):* the devil offers "all the kingdoms of the world" (4:8–9), refused → the Father gives "all authority in heaven and on earth" (28:18). *High/moderate confidence* — a striking inversion the text invites. `[T]`/`[I]`
- *Internal (answers §2:2, 11):* magi (Gentiles) worship the newborn king → the nations are discipled (28:19); Gentile worship brackets the book. *Moderate confidence.* `[I]`
- *Internal (planted for resolution):* "You are the light of the world" (5:14) → "make disciples of all nations" (28:19); the Servant's light-to-the-nations vocation is shared with the disciples. *High confidence* (the link is Cooper's too). `[T]`/`[S: Cooper]`
- *Internal (identical proclamation):* John (3:2) and Jesus (4:17) preach the same words — forerunner and fulfiller bound together; and the disciples are sent to preach the same kingdom (10:7). *High confidence.* `[T]`

### 12. Genre

**Gospel** — a subset of ancient *bios* (biography), but tightly composed and theologically driven: historical narrative interwoven with extended discourse, framed by fulfilment commentary, aimed at eliciting discipleship. `[T]`/`[S: Cooper via Burridge]` Reading rules: the events are presented as having happened (including the miracles as "signs" that really occurred); the discourses are Jesus' authoritative teaching, not merely reported opinion; the parables (esp. ch. 13) are comparison-stories to be read for their main point, not allegorised in every detail; the Olivet discourse (24–25) mixes prophetic and apocalyptic registers and must be read with vision-not-video discipline. Matthew also embeds sub-genres: **genealogy** (1:1–17, theological architecture — note who is included, strikingly four women, and the exile as a structural pivot), **hymn/beatitude** (5:3–12), **parable**, and **apocalyptic** (24). **Commentary note (Cooper):** Cooper's "missionary training narrative" is a functional refinement of the *bios* genre — the *purpose* to which Matthew bends biography. `[S: Cooper]`

### 13. Copycat

At book level, the descriptive/prescriptive question is acute because Matthew is explicitly a teaching book whose closing command is "teaching them to observe all that I have commanded" (28:20) — so the discourses are, by the book's own claim, largely *prescriptive* for post-resurrection disciples. But careful distinctions are needed:

- **Jesus is not, at the deepest level, a model to copy but a Saviour to trust.** His "taking up the cross" as Servant/curse-bearer (chs. 26–27) is unique and unrepeatable — the disciples "made precisely zero contribution" (Cooper) `[S: Cooper]` / `[T]` (the passion narrative gives them no saving role). What Jesus does *for* his people (atonement, 1:21; 26:28) is the ground, not the template.
- **Yet 16:24 ("take up your cross and follow me") is genuinely prescriptive** for disciples — but it means sacrificial, cruciform discipleship *in response to* and *presupposing* Jesus' own cross, not a re-performance of the atonement. Cooper's discussion (against the "interim ethic" view) is worth heeding: the discourses presuppose the death and resurrection even though delivered before them. `[S: Cooper]` / `[I]`
- **The disciples are a mixed model** — commended when they confess (16:16) and worship (14:33; 28:17), warned when they show "little faith," misunderstand the cross (16:22–23, where Peter is aligned with Satan), and scatter (26:56). They are chiefly *us* — learners in formation — not heroes to imitate wholesale. `[T]`
- **The opponents are a negative example and, uncomfortably, sometimes a mirror of the religious reader** — the danger of a righteousness that is outward, self-secure, and blind to its need of mercy (chs. 15, 23). `[T]`
- **Genuinely unique/foundational moments** not to be made into method: the virginal conception, the transfiguration, the resurrection, the Commission's apostolic sending. `[T]`

### 14. Bible Timeline

Matthew sits at the **hinge of the whole timeline**. It narrates the arrival of the Christ — the fulfilment toward which the entire OT strained (Law, Prophets, Writings) — and it closes by opening the church age: "make disciples of all nations… to the end of the age" (28:19–20). `[T]` The reader is located *after* the decisive events (incarnation, cross, resurrection, conferral of all authority) but *before* the consummation ("the end of the age," "the close of the age," 13:39–40, 49; 24:3; 28:20). This "already/not-yet" location is the book's own frame for its ethics: Jesus' teaching applies to post-resurrection disciples (Cooper) `[S: Cooper]`, but with awareness of what has changed — e.g. the temple (17:24–27; 27:51) and the Israel-only mission of ch. 10 (contrast 10:5–6 with 28:19) belong to a moment now superseded, so the *principles*, not every detail, carry across. `[S: Cooper]` / `[I]`

For OT-fulfilment reading: Matthew is itself the paradigm of how to read the OT forward — through typology (Jesus as true Israel, new Moses, Son of David, Servant) rather than flat prediction-and-proof alone.

### 15. Who Am I?

Matthew's narrative strategy positions the reader as **a disciple, or a potential disciple** — one who sits under the teaching of the authoritative Jesus and must respond. `[T]` **Commentary note (Cooper, via Donaldson):** the book "invites readers to learn alongside the disciples… joining with them as they listen to Jesus's teaching." `[S: Cooper]`

- **Jesus** is the one to be *worshipped and followed and trusted* — the type of nothing prior (he is the antitype: true Israel, greater Moses, Son of David, Servant, Immanuel-God-with-us). We do not identify *with* Jesus; we receive from him and follow him. `[T]`
- **The disciples/Twelve** are chiefly *us* — learners of little faith being formed. We identify with them as recipients of the call and the teaching, and as those warned against misunderstanding. `[T]`
- **The crowds** are the not-yet-committed — amazed, following, but undecided — the reader before conversion. `[I]`
- **The opponents** function as foil and warning: the reader's temptation to a self-secure, mercy-less religiosity. `[T]`
- **John the Baptist** is the forerunner/Elijah-figure, not a template. `[T]`

A book-level "Moses-is-me" caution: do not read Jesus' *unique* acts (the miracles as Servant-signs, the atoning death) as "what I should do," while *do* hear the discourses' commands as addressed to disciples now.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** Matthew wants the reader to *become a disciple of Jesus who is in turn a disciple-maker* (Cooper's summary, which the text warrants). `[S: Cooper]` / `[T]` Concretely, the book aims to produce: (a) *worship and allegiance* to Jesus as Messiah and Lord — recognising who he is (16:16; 28:17); (b) *trust* in his atoning work — "he will save his people from their sins" (1:21) is received, not achieved; (c) *transformed living* under his teaching — the "greater righteousness" that is filial, prayerful, merciful, and visible (5:16, 20); and (d) *participation in the mission* to all nations, teaching what he commanded, sustained by his promised presence (28:19–20).

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motivation.**
- *Worldview:* history has turned on Jesus' death and resurrection; the kingdom of heaven is near and coming; there is a real judgment ("weeping and gnashing of teeth" is not decorative); the risen Jesus holds all authority and is present with his people.
- *Behaviour (start/stop):* start living the cruciform, merciful righteousness of the Sermon (chs. 5–7); start making and teaching disciples; stop the self-secure hypocrisy of ch. 23 and the anxious "little faith" that forgets the Father.
- *Motivation (guard against moralism):* the commands flow from gospel realities — you have a Father in heaven (6:9); your sins are forgiven through his blood (26:28); he is with you always (28:20). Application severed from these becomes the very Pharisaic righteousness Matthew condemns.

**Four audiences.** *For me:* am I a learner under this Jesus, or self-secure? *For a Christian friend:* the "little faith" disciple needs the assurance of the present Christ. *For the church:* ch. 18's community of humility, discipline, and forgiveness; the plural "you" of the Sermon is corporate. *For the unbeliever:* the crowds' question — who is this authoritative teacher, and what will I do with his call?

**Prayer** flows naturally: worship of the present Christ; confession of Pharisee-shaped religion; petition for the nations and for boldness in disciple-making.

**Grammatical/syntactical features.**
- **The "you have heard… but I say to you" antitheses (5:21–48)** — the emphatic *egō de legō hymin* ("but *I* say to you") foregrounds Jesus' personal authority over against the tradition; the pronoun is emphatic. `[T]` *High confidence.*
- **"Make disciples" (*mathēteusate*, 28:19)** is the single main imperative of the Commission; "go," "baptising," "teaching" are participles hanging off it (an aorist participle + three subordinate participial/imperatival forms). The grammar makes disciple-*making* the central command, with going, baptising and teaching describing how it is done. `[T]` *High confidence* — and exegetically load-bearing for how the Commission is preached.
- **"I am with you" (*egō meth’ hymōn eimi*, 28:20)** — emphatic pronoun again, echoing the divine "I am with you" assurances of the OT (e.g. Exod 3:12; Josh 1:5) and paying off *Immanuel*. `[I]`

**Recommended verification:** *epiousios* (6:11) — genuinely uncertain lexis; consult BDAG / NET notes. The singular/plural "heaven(s)" argument — verify against Pennington directly and a second commentary before making it load-bearing.

### Textual Variants

Book-level flags a preacher of Matthew should hold ready:
- **6:13b — the doxology of the Lord's Prayer** ("for thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever, Amen") is absent from the earliest manuscripts; ESV/NIV footnote it, KJV includes it. Do not build teaching on it; it is a (venerable) later liturgical addition. *High confidence.*
- **28:19 — the Trinitarian baptismal formula** is textually secure in all manuscripts and versions; despite older critical speculation, there is no manuscript warrant for omitting it. Preach it with confidence. *High confidence.*
- **16:2b–3 (signs of the times), 17:21 (this kind… by prayer and fasting), 18:11 (the Son of Man came to save the lost), 23:14 (the widows' houses woe)** — each is absent from the earliest manuscripts and bracketed/footnoted in modern versions; note when a hearer's KJV/NKJV differs. *High/moderate confidence.*
- **27:16–17 — "Jesus Barabbas"** — some manuscripts give Barabbas the first name "Jesus," heightening the substitution irony; textually uncertain, worth a footnote-level mention at most. *Moderate confidence.*

Otherwise Matthew's text is stable; most variants are below preaching level.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Written in Greek, most probably in the latter first century (a date before *or* after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70 is defensible; the absence of an explicit description of the siege does not settle it either way). `[S: Cooper]` Authorship is traditionally the apostle Matthew/Levi the tax-collector (10:3; cf. Mark 2:14); the text is anonymous, and Cooper (with Brown) leans toward an author of scribal formation — "a teacher of the law made a disciple for the kingdom" (13:52) — while holding apostolic authorship as possible. `[S: Cooper]`

**What the first audience knew / brought.** Cooper's reconstruction (heuristic, not certain) imagines the first hearers as **Greek-speaking Jewish people and Gentile God-fearers**, perhaps in a place like **(Syrian) Antioch** — a centre of Hellenistic Judaism and early Christian mission (Acts 13–14). `[S: Cooper]` Whether or not Antioch is right, the internal evidence that the audience *knew the Hebrew Scriptures well* is strong — the whole fulfilment argument presupposes it. `[T]` They inhabited a world where the **shadow of death pressed close** (high infant mortality, short life expectancy, urban insecurity) — Cooper connects this to Matthew's citation of Isaiah's "light… in the land of the shadow of death" (4:16) and to the desperate suppliants of chapters 8–9. `[S: Cooper]`

**The opponents.** Two rough groupings: the establishment (**chief priests, elders, Sadducees** — the last denying the resurrection, 22:23–33) and the popular reform movement (**Pharisees**, often linked with the "scribes/teachers of the law," esp. ch. 23). `[T]`/`[S: Cooper]` Matthew's portrait of the Pharisees is unremittingly negative — the point being not ethnic (the writers are themselves Jewish) but their representative failure to recognise God's merciful intervention in Jesus and their self-secure trust in heritage and law-keeping. `[S: Cooper]` The strong **Galilee/Judea (north/south) social divide** (a Galilean challenging the Jerusalem establishment; Peter's northern accent, 26:73) gives the journey to Jerusalem its dramatic charge. `[S: Cooper]`

**What this changes.** Reading Matthew against a synagogue-attending, Scripture-literate, death-haunted first audience recovers the *force* of the fulfilment claim (it is an argument that Jesus is the promised one, aimed at people who cared) and of the kingdom proclamation (light dawning on those under death's shadow). *Confidence:* the audience-reconstruction is heuristic — flag Antioch and the specific social details as *moderate/uncertain*; the Scripture-literacy inference is *high*.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** The reader of Matthew arrives having read (in the Christian canonical ordering) the whole Old Testament, ending on the note of promise-still-outstanding (Malachi's coming messenger; no Davidic king reigning; exile theologically unresolved). Matthew is engineered to be read *first* in the New Testament precisely as the answer to that outstanding promise (the genealogy's third panel runs "to the Christ"). `[T]`/`[I]`

**The first hearers** (see Historical Background) were likely Jewish and God-fearing, Scripture-literate, living under Roman power with the memory or imminence of Jerusalem's fall, and — if they obeyed the Commission — soon meeting Pharisaic opposition as they made disciples. `[S: Cooper]`

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**
- *Shocking:* a Messiah who is baptised among sinners, befriends tax-collectors, touches lepers, and is crucified (maximum shame); Gentile magi worship the newborn king while Jerusalem is troubled; four women (some Gentile, some scandalous) named in the royal genealogy. `[T]`
- *Surprising / subverting expectation:* the kingdom comes hidden — like seed, leaven, treasure (ch. 13) — not in obvious cosmic overthrow; the Messiah suffers before he reigns; the mission crosses to *all nations* (28:19), not Israel only. `[T]`
- *Comforting:* "God with us" bracketing the book; "Come to me, all who labour… and I will give you rest" (11:28–30); the Father who feeds the birds and clothes the lilies (6:26–30). `[T]`
- *Disturbing:* the woes of ch. 23; the frequency of judgment ("weeping and gnashing of teeth"); "I never knew you; depart from me" (7:23); the crowd's "his blood be on us and on our children" (27:25). `[T]`

**What we bring that they didn't.** Individualism (the Sermon's "you" is largely plural/corporate); a heaven-vs-hell frame Matthew does not use (Cooper: the book's operative pairing is *heaven and earth*, and judgment is real but not framed as a "choice between heaven and hell"); `[S: Cooper]` and a tendency to read "kingdom growth" into ch. 13 that Cooper argues Matthew would not recognise (see Pitfalls). `[S: Cooper]`

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Three themes most illuminate the book; they converge climactically (relation type: **climactic convergence**) in the closing scene.

**Kingdom of God/heaven.**
- *Where it sits:* Matthew narrates the *inauguration* — "the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (4:17) — of the reign anticipated from creation, promised to David (2 Sam 7), and awaited through exile.
- *Before / after:* draws on the Danielic everlasting kingdom (Dan 2:44; 7:14) and the Davidic covenant; points forward to consummation ("the close of the age," 28:20; the Son of Man's coming, ch. 24–25). `[T]`
- *Payoff:* the kingdom comes hidden and contested now (ch. 13), fully at the end; this reframes both the Sermon's ethics (kingdom-living now) and the disciple's perseverance.

**Servant of the LORD (Isaiah).**
- *Where it sits:* Jesus is identified as Isaiah's Servant (3:17; 8:17; 12:18–21) — sin-bearer (Isa 53) *and* light to the nations (Isa 42:6; 49:6).
- *Payoff:* this single theme accounts for both halves of the book's logic — the cross (Servant as curse-bearer, chs. 26–27) and the mission (Servant's light reaching the nations, 28:19). The disciples are *incorporated* into the Servant's light-vocation (5:14 → 28:19). `[T]`/`[S: Cooper]`

**Immanuel / God's presence (temple trajectory).**
- *Where it sits:* "God with us" (1:23) inaugurates a new mode of divine presence — no longer the temple (whose curtain is torn, 27:51) but the risen Christ himself, present "always, to the end of the age" (28:20). `[T]`
- *Payoff:* Matthew relocates the presence of God from the temple to the person of Jesus and his gathered disciples ("where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them," 18:20). `[T]`

**Confluence.** These three meet in the Great Commission: the enthroned King (kingdom) who is the vindicated Servant (Servant, now a light to *all nations*) promises his abiding presence (Immanuel). The convergence is climactic, not merely parallel — 28:18–20 is where the three streams pour into one reality. *Confidence: high.*

**N/A** — Matthew is not in the Torah (Genesis–Deuteronomy); the Schnittjer narrative-reading pass is reserved for Pentateuchal passages. (Matthew's *evocation* of the Pentateuch through the five discourses is handled under Structure and Christological Reading.)

### Christological Reading

Matthew is a NT book in which Christ is named throughout, so the extension's task is to surface the **OT typological patterns Matthew deliberately deploys** — the raw material for Christ-centred, non-moralistic preaching.

- **Jesus as true Israel (typology; passes category + NT-precedent + authorial-pattern tests).** Hosea 11:1 (2:15), the descent to and return from Egypt, and the wilderness testing (4:1–11, answered from Deuteronomy 6–8) present Jesus recapitulating and succeeding where Israel failed. *High confidence.*
- **Jesus as new Moses (typology).** The five discourses evoking the five books; teaching from a mountain; the infancy echoes of Moses (a tyrant killing infants, 2:16; "those who sought the child's life are dead," 2:20 ≈ Exod 4:19). *High confidence* on the pattern; the Pentateuch-count is `[I]`.
- **Jesus as Son of David / Messianic king (direct + typology).** 1:1; the Davidic covenant (2 Sam 7); entry to Jerusalem on a donkey fulfilling Zech 9:9 (21:5). *High confidence.*
- **Jesus as the Servant (direct citation + trajectory).** 8:17; 12:18–21; the passion as Isa 53 sin-bearing. *High confidence.*
- **Jesus as Son of Man / Danielic vindicated one (direct allusion).** Dan 7:13 at 26:64. *High confidence.*
- **Jesus as Immanuel / the divine presence (trajectory + escalation).** The gradual unveiling from human Messiah (1:1) to "all authority in heaven and on earth" (28:18), with worship never refused. *High confidence.*

**Moralism check.** The perennial danger in Matthew is preaching the Sermon on the Mount as achievable moral programme ("be like this") divorced from the gospel — the very Pharisaic righteousness the book condemns. The corrective is the book's own frame: the teaching is addressed to those already given the kingdom (5:3), whose sins Jesus bears (1:21; 26:28), whose Father they already have (6:9), and whose obedience is *response*, not *purchase*. Even 16:24's cross-bearing presupposes Christ's own cross. Christ is hero and Saviour first; example second.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

- **28:19 — "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."** *Category: doctrinal.* Contested between paedo- and credo-baptist traditions and (secondarily) over the Trinitarian formula's authenticity (textually secure — see Variants). *Rhetorical function:* the participle describes how disciples are made; the accent is on incorporation into the triune name. Handle: preach the substance (disciple-making incorporation) without importing the whole baptism debate.
- **5:20; 5:48 — "greater righteousness" / "be perfect."** *Category: doctrinal/pastoral.* Misread as either legalistic works-righteousness or as a "deliberately impossible standard to drive us to grace." *Rhetorical function:* addressed to those already in the kingdom (5:3), with a *missionary* purpose — righteousness that shines before others for the Father's glory (5:16). Cooper argues persuasively against the "deliberately impossible" reading. `[S: Cooper]` Handle: neither moralism nor a purely convicting function; kingdom-righteousness as grateful, Spirit-enabled response.
- **10:5–6 vs 28:19 — "go nowhere among the Gentiles" vs "make disciples of all nations."** *Category: apologetic.* Apparent contradiction. *Function:* ch. 10 is a specific, historically bounded mission to Israel; the Commission universalises it after the resurrection. Handle via Bible Timeline — the principle carries, the Israel-only restriction does not. `[S: Cooper]`/`[I]`
- **23 and 27:25 — the Pharisees and "his blood be on us and on our children."** *Category: ethical/pastoral (antisemitism).* Matthew's polemic has been weaponised. *Honest exegesis:* the critique is intra-Jewish and theological (failure to recognise God's mercy in Jesus), not ethnic — the writers and first disciples are Jewish. `[S: Cooper]` Handle with explicit care; name and disarm the antisemitic misuse from the pulpit.
- **16:18 — "on this rock I will build my church."** *Category: doctrinal (Catholic/Protestant).* The *Petros*/*petra* wordplay and the referent of "this rock" (Peter / Peter's confession / Christ). Handle: name the options; the immediate context is Peter's confession of Jesus as Christ.
- **24 — the Olivet discourse and AD 70.** *Category: doctrinal/apologetic.* Preterist/futurist/mixed readings; the relation of the temple's fall to the "coming of the Son of Man." Cooper cautions against making the temple's physical destruction the focus. `[S: Cooper]` Handle: genre-aware (prophetic/apocalyptic), do not over-specify.
- **Hell / "weeping and gnashing of teeth."** *Category: pastoral.* Frequent and severe in Matthew. Handle with sobriety and without sensationalism; note Cooper's point that the operative contrast is heaven-and-earth, not a "heaven-or-hell" sales pitch. `[S: Cooper]`
- **11:28–30 / 1:23 / 28:20 — comfort texts.** Not "difficult" but pastorally load-bearing; guard against sentimentalising "God with us" away from its costly, cross-shaped context.

## Preaching Pitfalls

Common ways Matthew gets mishandled, and how to avoid them:

### Pitfall: Preaching the Sermon on the Mount as achievable moralism (or as merely impossible)

- **What it looks like:** "Here are Jesus' rules for a better life — try harder"; or the opposite, "these are deliberately impossible so you'll despair and run to grace."
- **Why it's wrong:** The Sermon is addressed to those already given the kingdom (5:3), with a missionary purpose (5:16) — righteousness that shines for the Father's glory. Both moralism and the "impossible standard" reading miss the filial, grace-grounded, Spirit-enabled shape of the obedience.
- **The corrective:** Preach kingdom-righteousness as grateful response, grounded in the atonement (1:21; 26:28) and the fatherhood of God (6:9). (Cooper is especially good here.)

### Pitfall: "Growing / building / expanding the kingdom"

- **What it looks like:** Reading the mustard seed and leaven (13:31–33) as a programme for the church incrementally "growing the kingdom."
- **Why it's wrong:** In Matthew the kingdom of heaven is God's coming reign, established *by God* from heaven — not a thing the church enlarges by effort (Cooper: Matthew would give "a very puzzled look" at "kingdom growth"). `[S: Cooper]` The parables concern the kingdom's hidden, surprising, mixed manifestation, not our expansion of it.
- **The corrective:** Preach the confidence the parables actually give — the kingdom advances despite appearances, by God's power, through the sown "word of the kingdom."

### Pitfall: Turning the Great Commission into "go" (missions-as-travel) rather than "make disciples"

- **What it looks like:** The sermon centres "GO" as the imperative and the point.
- **Why it's wrong:** The single main verb is *make disciples* (*mathēteusate*); "going," "baptising," and "teaching" are subordinate participles describing how. `[T]`
- **The corrective:** Centre disciple-making (which includes teaching "all that I have commanded" — pointing back to the whole book), grounded in Christ's conferred authority (the "therefore" of v.19) and his promised presence (v.20).

### Pitfall: Antisemitic mishandling of the Pharisee-polemic and 27:25

- **What it looks like:** Reading Matthew's critique of the Pharisees, or "his blood be on us and on our children," as an indictment of the Jewish people as such.
- **Why it's wrong:** The polemic is intra-Jewish and theological (failure to receive God's mercy in Jesus), from Jewish writers; it is not an ethnic charge. `[S: Cooper]`/`[T]`
- **The corrective:** Name the misuse explicitly and disarm it; direct the warning where Matthew aims it — at self-secure religiosity in *any* reader.

### Pitfall: Preaching pericopes as detached gems, ignoring the book's arc

- **What it looks like:** A stand-alone sermon on a miracle or parable with no sense of where it sits between promise (1–2) and Commission (28).
- **Why it's wrong:** Matthew is a single engineered arc (Positional Necessity Check); the parts serve the whole.
- **The corrective:** Locate every passage in the book's movement toward the Servant's cross and the disciple-making Commission.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

- **The plural "heavens" (Pennington/Cooper).** Whether "kingdom of the heavens" is a distinct spatial concept or a reverential periphrasis for "kingdom of God" is genuinely contested. Verify against Pennington directly and a second major commentary (e.g. France, Davies–Allison) before making the singular/plural distinction load-bearing in a sermon. *Uncertain — consider verifying.*
- **Date and provenance.** Pre- vs post-AD 70 dating, and the Antioch hypothesis, are unresolved and heuristic. Nothing in the sweep should hang on a firm answer; flag as background, not foundation. *Uncertain.*
- **Authorship.** Apostolic (Matthew/Levi) vs a scribal disciple in the Matthaean tradition — Cooper leans to the latter but holds the former possible. Not exegetically decisive. *Moderate/uncertain.*
- **Isaiah 7:14 *‘almāh*/*parthenos*.** The Hebrew "young woman" vs LXX/Matthaean "virgin" is a standing crux with a large literature; preach 1:23 aware of it. *Moderate confidence* that Matthew intends the virginal-conception reading via the LXX. Consider NET notes / a critical commentary.
- **6:11 *epiousios* ("daily bread").** Lexically uncertain ("daily" / "for tomorrow" / "needful"). *Uncertain — consider a lexicon.*
- **Cooper as sole source for several framing claims** (Antioch; the four-section structure via Weren; the plural-heavens reading via Pennington). Each would benefit from a second commentary before being taught as settled.

## Book-Overview / Commentary (Cooper) Tensions

Surfaced, not resolved — places where the text-first sweep and Cooper's introduction pull differently or need cross-checking:

1. **Single controlling purpose vs dual spine.** Cooper makes "disciples who are disciple-makers" *the* overarching purpose. The text-first sweep finds the disciple-making arc genuinely present, but co-equal with (not subordinating) the Christological-fulfilment spine that dominates chs. 1–2 and 26–28. Consider preaching purpose as *twofold and integrated* (fulfilment-in-Christ → disciples-who-make-disciples) rather than single. **Tension: emphasis, not contradiction.**
2. **"Kingdom of the heavens" as a distinct spatial reality.** Cooper/Pennington's reading is distinctive and contestable against the older reverential-periphrasis consensus. The text warrants the observation that Matthew *prefers* the phrase and also uses "kingdom of God" four times; it does not compel the full spatial thesis. **Tension: a single-source reading needing corroboration.**
3. **Four-section structure (via Weren).** A minority macro-structure. Useful, but hold alongside the more securely text-signalled five-discourse framework rather than as its replacement. **Tension: one good option among several.**
4. **Antioch / audience reconstruction.** Cooper is admirably candid that this is heuristic; the sweep agrees the *Scripture-literacy* of the audience is well-warranted while the *location* is not. **No real tension — Cooper flags it himself.**
5. **"The Beatitudes are not blessings" / "salt is not social preservative" / other "ears to hear" surprises.** Cooper lists several arresting counter-readings in his introduction that this book-level sweep did not independently test (they are passage-level claims). They are flagged here as **candidates for solo dig-deeper** before adoption — do not import them wholesale into a sermon on Cooper's authority alone.
