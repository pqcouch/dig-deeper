The Greek Text — What Is Being Read

The overview reads Matthew in the NA28. It read the whole book in that edition before any English was opened. Counts are made by lemma in the SBLGNT index and then confirmed in the NA28 wording; where the two editions differ in a way that touches a count, the difference is stated. Eight textual facts affect what “Matthew” is, and each is stated here once.

1. Verse count. The NA28 and the SBLGNT both have 1,068 verses [T]. Neither prints 17:21, 18:11 or 23:14. The NASB95 prints all three in square brackets, which gives it 1,071 verses; the ESV omits them [T]. The SBLGNT text has 18,329 words [T].

2. What the NA28 prints in brackets. Three whole passages and two shorter readings are printed but marked as doubtful [T]:

  • 12:47 (the report that his mother and brothers stand outside). It is omitted by א* B L Γ ff¹ k syˢ·ᶜ sa.
  • 16:2b–3 (the red sky at evening and morning). It is omitted by א B Γ ƒ¹³ 579 syˢ·ᶜ sa mae boᵖᵗ, and by Origen.
  • 21:44 (the one who falls on the stone). It is omitted by D 33 it syˢ, Origen and the Syriac Eusebius.
  • 6:33 [τοῦ θεοῦ] (“of God”). The SBLGNT omits it. So the NA28 has five instances of “the kingdom of God” (6:33; 12:28; 19:24; 21:31; 21:43), and the SBLGNT four.
  • 27:16–17 [Ἰησοῦν] (“Jesus”) before Βαραββᾶν (“Barabbas”). The full name is read by Θ ƒ¹ 700* syˢ, and Origen knew it. If it stands, Pilate’s question is “Jesus Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ?” (27:17).

Nothing in this overview rests on any bracketed text, except one intertextual row (21:44, Daniel 2), which is flagged where it stands.

3. Ἀσάφ (“Asaph”, 1:7–8) and Ἀμώς (“Amos”, 1:10). NA28 prints the names of a psalmist and a prophet where 1 Chr 3:10, 14 has the kings Asa and Amon (Swete 1 Chr 3:10 Ἀσά (“Asa”); 3:14 Ἀμνών (“Amnon”, for Amon)) [T]. NA28’s Ἀσάφ (“Asaph”) is read by 𝔓¹ᵛⁱᵈ א B C ƒ¹·¹³ 700; the majority text reads Ἀσά (“Asa”). Its Ἀμώς (“Amos”) is read by א B C Γ Δ Θ ƒ¹ 33; the majority reads Ἀμών (“Amon”) [T]. The ESV prints “Asaph” and “Amos”; the NASB95 prints “Asa” and “Amon”. Whether the evangelist meant to evoke the Psalms and the Prophets, or the forms are spelling variants of the kings’ names, is [S] and contested. Nothing rests on it, but the pulpit text will say “Asaph”, and someone will ask.

4. 6:13 — the doxology. NA28 ends the Lord’s Prayer at ἀπὸ τοῦ πονηροῦ (“from the evil one / from evil”), with א B D Z 0170 ƒ¹ lat and Origen [T]. The doxology, ὅτι σοῦ ἐστιν ἡ βασιλεία… (“for yours is the kingdom…”), is read by K L W Δ Θ ƒ¹³ 33 𝔪, and the apparatus cross-refers it to 1 Chr 29:11–13. The NASB95 prints it in brackets; the ESV omits it.

5. 27:49 — the spear. A sentence borrowed from John 19:34 (the soldier pierces his side and water and blood come out) stands in א B C L Γ. NA28 does not print it [T]. This is a place where NA28 sets aside its two chief witnesses, and it is worth knowing before 27:45–56 is preached.

6. 24:36 — οὐδὲ ὁ υἱός (“nor the Son”). NA28 prints it, with א*·²ᵇ B D Θ ƒ¹³; the majority text omits it [T]. Both English versions include it.

7. 27:9 — Ἰερεμίου (“of Jeremiah”). The name is all but unanimous. A handful of witnesses read Ζαχαρίου (“of Zechariah”, 22 syʰᵐᵍ) or Ἠσαΐου (“of Isaiah”, 21 l), or drop the name (Φ 33 a b syˢ·ᵖ) [T]. The attribution is original on the evidence. The words quoted are mostly Zech 11:12–13 (see the Intertextual Map).

8. The title. NA28 prints κατὰ Μαθθαῖον (“according to Matthew”). It is absent from the first hands of א and B and supplied by their correctors [T]. The title is not part of the text.

Rule adopted. This overview treats the NA28 text as the book, brackets included, and says so wherever a bracket matters.

Canonical Position

The NT equivalent of the Tanak block: position in the collection, what the book presupposes, what it hands on.

Field
Section The Gospels. Matthew stands first of the four in the received order, and so first in the New Testament. It is also first in the Western order found in Codex D (Matthew, John, Luke, Mark) [S].
Reading implication Read first, Matthew is the hinge between the Testaments, and its first line says so. Βίβλος γενέσεως (“book of the genesis”, 1:1) occurs in Swete only at Gen 2:4 and 5:1, and the second of these opens a genealogy (Hebrew סֵפֶר תּוֹלְדֹת (“book of the generations”), Gen 5:1) [T]. Matthew’s own genealogy then follows the line of 1 Chr 1–3 and Ruth 4:18–22 [T]. The book reads forward from the end of the Scriptures. It also reads forward from both of the ends those Scriptures have. (a) In the BHS order, the Tanak closes with Chronicles. Chronicles opens with a genealogy from Adam (1 Chr 1:1) and closes with Cyrus: כָּל־מַמְלְכוֹת הָאָרֶץ נָתַן לִי (“all the kingdoms of the earth he has given me”)… וְהוּא־פָקַד עָלַי (“and he has charged me”)… יְהוָה אֱלֹהָיו עִמּוֹ (“the LORD his God be with him”), and then the last word, וְיָעַל (“and let him go up”, 2 Chr 36:23) [T]. Matthew closes: ἐδόθη μοι πᾶσα ἐξουσία (“all authority has been given to me”)… πορευθέντες (“go”)… ὅσα ἐνετειλάμην ὑμῖν (“all that I commanded you”)… ἐγὼ μεθʼ ὑμῶν εἰμι (“I am with you”, 28:18–20) [T]. The claim audit lowered this to low–moderate [S: audit], for four reasons. First, it depends on Chronicles standing last, which it does only in the BHS (Talmudic) order. Second, the Greek does not carry it evenly: Swete reads the dative, πάσαις ταῖς βασιλείαις τῆς γῆς (“to all the kingdoms of the earth”), so that Cyrus speaks to the kingdoms; only Rahlfs, πάσας τὰς βασιλείας τῆς γῆς ἔδωκέν μοι (“all the kingdoms of the earth he has given me”), and the Hebrew make them what is given [T]. Third, each shared element has a closer source of its own. For 28:18–19 the source of record is Dan 7:14 OG, ἐδόθη αὐτῷ ἐξουσία … πάντα τὰ ἔθνη (“authority was given to him … all the nations”), the only ἐδόθη (“was given”) with ἐξουσία (“authority”) in Swete. For 28:20 the closer sources are Hag 1:13, ἐγώ εἰμι μεθ᾽ ὑμῶν (“I am with you”), and Hag 2:4, μεθ᾽ ὑμῶν ἐγώ εἰμι (“I am with you”), in the rebuilding after Cyrus, and Gen 28:15, ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ μετὰ σοῦ (“behold, I am with you”) [T]. Fourth, the genealogy follows Ruth 4:18–22 and takes its heading from Genesis as much as it follows Chronicles [T]. The cluster of giving, commanding, presence and going recurs at about seven places in Swete. Keep (a), if at all, as a resonance at the scale of the canon that depends on one order, not as an allusion. (b) In the English order, the Old Testament closes with Malachi, whose last words name Moses and Elijah (Mal 3:22–23 Hebrew; Swete 4:4 for Elijah) [T]. In Matthew, John is “Elijah who is to come” (11:14; 17:10–13), and Moses and Elijah appear together on the mountain (17:3) [T]. Prefer neither order to the exclusion of the other. The BHS sequence is the Talmudic one, and Codex Leningradensis itself puts Chronicles first among the Writings (see _texts/README.md). The reading in (a) depends on that sequence [S]. The Malachi reading (b) was not audited and stands as written.
Presupposes (1) The Scriptures as a story that has an ending still to come. The narrator says ἵνα πληρωθῇ τὸ ῥηθέν (“that what was spoken might be fulfilled”) or its equivalent ten times: 1:22; 2:15; 2:17; 2:23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9 [T]. γέγραπται (“it is written”) stands nine times, of Scripture (2:5; 4:4, 6, 7, 10; 11:10; 21:13; 26:24, 31); the tenth γράφω (“to write”, 27:37) is the charge over the cross [T]. Jesus asks οὐκ ἀνέγνωτε (“have you not read?”) or its equivalent at 12:3, 5; 19:4; 21:16, 42; 22:31 [T]. (2) Abraham and David as the two names of promise (1:1): Ἀβραάμ (“Abraham”) in 6 verses (1:1, 2, 17; 3:9; 8:11; 22:32), Δαυίδ (“David”) in 15 verses (17 occurrences) [T]. (3) The exile. μετοικεσία Βαβυλῶνος (“deportation to Babylon”) occurs four times, all in 1:11–17 [T]. The genealogy is told as a history that has passed through Babylon. (4) The Law and the Prophets as one whole: 5:17; 7:12; 11:13; 22:40 [T]. Μωϋσῆς (“Moses”) is named in 7 verses [T]. (5) Daniel 7. The Son of Man “coming on the clouds of heaven” (24:30; 26:64) is assumed to be known, not explained [T].
Handoff Forward, to the nations and the end of the age. The gospel of the kingdom will be preached ἐν ὅλῃ τῇ οἰκουμένῃ (“in the whole world”, 24:14), and the woman’s deed will be told ἐν ὅλῳ τῷ κόσμῳ (“in the whole world”, 26:13) [T]. The last command is μαθητεύσατε πάντα τὰ ἔθνη (“make disciples of all the nations”, 28:19), and the last promise runs ἕως τῆς συντελείας τοῦ αἰῶνος (“to the end of the age”, 28:20) [T]. The book has no ascension and no departure scene: it ends with Jesus present [T]. Within the canon it hands on to Mark.
Neighbours Before it: Malachi (English order) or Chronicles (Tanak order) — see Reading implication. After it: Mark. Most of Mark’s narrative reappears in Matthew [S]. The direction of dependence (Markan priority, which most hold; or Matthew first, with the Augustinian and Griesbach hypotheses) is a named open question. It is reported here and not resolved, and nothing in this overview depends on it. One datum from the corpus belongs here: Mark’s quotation of Isa 56:7 includes πᾶσιν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν (“for all the nations”, Mark 11:17); Matthew’s (21:13) lacks it [T]. Yet Matthew is the Gospel that ends with πάντα τὰ ἔθνη (“all the nations”). The phrase is kept for the last line [I].

Presenting Situation

Author. The book is anonymous. The narrator never speaks in the first person [T]. Two details sit at the place a reader would look for an author [T]:

  • At the call of the tax collector, Matthew names him Μαθθαῖον (“Matthew”, 9:9), where Mark has Λευίν (“Levi”, Mark 2:14).
  • In the list of the Twelve, Matthew alone adds ὁ τελώνης (“the tax collector”) after his own name (10:3; compare Mark 3:18).

Both were checked in the SBLGNT. The attribution to the apostle Matthew comes from Papias as Eusebius reports him: Matthew “compiled the sayings in the Hebrew dialect” (Hist. eccl. 3.39.16) [S]. What that sentence means, and how it fits a Greek Gospel, is disputed [S]. The picture of the scribe μαθητευθεὶς τῇ βασιλείᾳ τῶν οὐρανῶν (“discipled for the kingdom of heaven”), who brings out καινὰ καὶ παλαιά (“new things and old”, 13:52), is often read as the author’s self-portrait [S]. That is attractive and unprovable, moderate.

Audience. The text shows readers who stand inside Jewish practice, and a community that stands apart from the synagogue:

  • Jewish custom is not explained. Matthew’s handwashing dispute (15:2) has no gloss. Mark at the same point explains the practice “of the Pharisees and all the Jews” (Mark 7:3–4) [T]. Semitic terms pass unglossed: ῥακά (“raca”, 5:22), μαμωνᾶς (“mammon”, 6:24), κορβανᾶς (“the temple treasury”, 27:6) [T]. Matthew glosses only three times: Ἐμμανουήλ (“Immanuel”, 1:23), Γολγοθᾶ (“Golgotha”, 27:33), and the cry from the cross (27:46). Mark has ten such glosses [T].
  • The Sabbath is still kept. “Pray that your flight may not be … on a Sabbath” (24:20) [T]. That this assumes readers who observe it is [I], moderate–high.
  • The synagogue is “theirs”. Jesus teaches ἐν ταῖς συναγωγαῖς αὐτῶν (“in their synagogues”, 4:23; 9:35; also 10:17; 12:9; 13:54), and to the scribes and Pharisees it is “your synagogues” (23:34) [T]. The narrator uses Ἰουδαῖοι (“Jews”) in his own voice only once, and from outside: the guards’ story “has been spread παρὰ Ἰουδαίοις (”among Jews“) to this day” (28:15) [T]. Elsewhere “King of the Jews” is spoken only by Gentiles (2:2; 27:11, 29, 37). The chief priests say “King of Israel” (27:42) [T]. The narrator writes as a Jewish believer at a distance from the synagogue, [I], moderate–high.
  • There is an ἐκκλησία (“church, assembly”). It is built on “this rock” (16:18) and given a procedure for discipline (18:15–17) [T].
  • The reader is placed after the events. The field is called “the Field of Blood ἕως τῆς σήμερον (”to this day“)” (27:8), and the guards’ tale is told μέχρι τῆς σήμερον ἡμέρας (“to this day”, 28:15) [T]. The book was written at some remove from what it tells, [I], high.

Occasion. It is not stated. The text shows several pressures at once [T]:

  • persecution “in their synagogues” and before governors and kings (10:17–18; 23:34);
  • false prophets and ἀνομία (“lawlessness”), in four verses (7:23; 13:41; 23:28; 24:12), with love growing cold (24:12);
  • a mixed community of wheat and weeds that must not be weeded early (13:24–30, 36–43);
  • sin within the brotherhood, and forgiveness (18);
  • “little faith”. ὀλιγόπιστος (“of little faith”) occurs at 6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8, and ὀλιγοπιστία (“littleness of faith”) at 17:20. Doubt is named at the very end (28:17);
  • a rival account of the empty tomb still current (28:11–15).

The date is [S] and disputed. Many put it after AD 70, reading the king who “burned their city” (22:7) and 24:1–2 as looking back; some put it before, citing the temple tax (17:24–27) and the altar (5:23–24) as live practice. The provenance (Antioch is the usual proposal) is [S]. Nothing here depends on either.

Pressure. The dominant tension, [I] high: How can Jesus — rejected by Israel’s leaders, handed to the Gentiles and crucified — be Israel’s Messiah, the son of David and son of Abraham in whom the Law and the Prophets are fulfilled; and how is his community to live, with a righteousness greater than the scribes’, as a mixed and doubting body that must now go to the nations? The book’s answers are its fulfilment formulae, its δεῖ (“it is necessary”, 16:21; 26:54), the ransom (20:28) and the covenant blood “for the forgiveness of sins” (26:28), and the risen Lord’s authority and presence (28:18–20) [T].

Purpose. To show that the crucified and raised Jesus is God-with-us, the one in whom the Scriptures are fulfilled, and to form the reader as a disciple who hears his words and does them (7:24), and who makes disciples of all the nations under his authority and in his presence (28:18–20). [I]

Device. Alternating panels: five blocks of teaching, each sealed by the formula, set inside a narrative that moves Galilee → Jerusalem → Galilee [T]. Three further devices are laid over the panels [T]:

  • Two hinges. ἀπὸ τότε ἤρξατο ὁ Ἰησοῦς (“from that time Jesus began”) stands at 4:17 (to preach) and 16:21 (to show that he must suffer), and nowhere else. ἀπὸ τότε (“from that time”) alone recurs once more, at 26:16, of Judas.
  • An inclusio. 1:23 Ἐμμανουήλ … μεθʼ ἡμῶν ὁ θεός (“Immanuel … God with us”) and 28:20 ἐγὼ μεθʼ ὑμῶν εἰμι (“I am with you”). The book opens and closes on presence.
  • A mountain frame. On a mountain the devil offers πάσας τὰς βασιλείας τοῦ κόσμου (“all the kingdoms of the world”, 4:8). On a mountain the risen Jesus says ἐδόθη μοι πᾶσα ἐξουσία (“all authority has been given to me”, 28:16–18). Between them Jesus goes up a mountain to teach (5:1), to pray (14:23), to heal (15:29), to be transfigured (17:1), and to speak of the end (24:3).

Inside the second movement, the frame 4:23 = 9:35 (διδάσκων ἐν ταῖς συναγωγαῖς αὐτῶν καὶ κηρύσσων τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τῆς βασιλείας καὶ θεραπεύων πᾶσαν νόσον καὶ πᾶσαν μαλακίαν, “teaching in their synagogues and proclaiming the gospel of the kingdom and healing every disease and every sickness”) is verbatim [T]. The phrase πᾶσαν νόσον καὶ πᾶσαν μαλακίαν (“every disease and every sickness”) then appears a third time, and last, as the authority given to the Twelve (10:1) [T]. Christ’s ministry is framed, then handed on.

Contested readings of the shape [S]:

  • A “Pentateuch” of five books, which casts Jesus as a new Moses. The fivefold formula is [T]. As “five books of Moses” the reading is low [S: audit]. The formula itself is not Mosaic: ἐτέλεσεν (“finished”) does not occur in the LXX, and Καὶ ἐγένετο ὅτε (“and it happened when”) occurs twelve times in Swete, never in the Torah. The first instance has a Q precursor (Luke 7:1). The discourses do not map onto Genesis–Deuteronomy, and the count of five depends on how 23–25 are divided [T]. As a Moses frame it is moderate, [I]: Moses-like teaching opened on a Sinai-coloured mountain (5:1) and closed with Deuteronomy’s wording (26:1; see the Intertextual Map, Deut 31:1 / 32:45).
  • A tripartite structure (1:1–4:16; 4:17–16:20; 16:21–28:20), keyed to the two ἀπὸ τότε (“from that time”) hinges (Kingsbury) [S]. The arc above keeps both hinges and divides further.
  • Chiastic schemes for the whole book [S]. None was tested here, and none is adopted.
  • Whether chapter 23 belongs to the fifth discourse. The audience changes at 24:3 and the scene at 24:1 [T]; the formula comes only at 26:1 [T]. This overview treats 23 as the threshold of the discourse, not its first part. Flag moderate.

Microscript

The book of the genesis of Jesus the Messiah, son of David and son of Abraham, who is God-with-us: born to save his people from their sins, he re-lives Israel’s story — Egypt, the waters, the wilderness, the mountain — and fulfils the Law and the Prophets. With an authority no scribe has, he proclaims the kingdom of heaven in word and deed, and teaches a righteousness that must be heard and done. Israel’s leaders reject him; the Son of Man must suffer, and he gives his life as a ransom and pours out his covenant blood for the forgiveness of sins. Raised, and given all authority in heaven and on earth, he sends his disciples from Galilee of the nations to make disciples of all the nations, teaching them to keep all that he commanded, and he is with them to the end of the age.

Keynotes, verified by lemma in the SBLGNT and checked in the NA28:

  • ἡ βασιλεία τῶν οὐρανῶν (“the kingdom of heaven”): 32 occurrences in 31 verses, from 3:2 to 25:1. It is not found in any other New Testament book. Alongside it, ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ (“the kingdom of God”) occurs 4 times in the SBLGNT (12:28; 19:24; 21:31; 21:43), and 5 in the NA28, with [6:33] [T].
  • πληρόω (“to fulfil”): 16 verses. They are 1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 3:15; 4:14; 5:17; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 13:48; 21:4; 23:32; 26:54, 56; 27:9. Ten are the narrator’s formula. Two are Jesus’ own programme: πληρῶσαι πᾶσαν δικαιοσύνην (“to fulfil all righteousness”, 3:15) and οὐκ ἦλθον καταλῦσαι ἀλλὰ πληρῶσαι (“I did not come to abolish but to fulfil”, 5:17). Add ἀναπληροῦται (“is being fulfilled”, 13:14) [T].
  • δικαιοσύνη (“righteousness”): 7 verses. They are 3:15; 5:6, 10, 20; 6:1, 33; 21:32 [T]. Five of the seven stand in chapters 5–6.
  • ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου (“the Son of Man”): 30 occurrences in 28 verses, from 8:20 to 26:64 [T].
  • προσκυνέω (“to worship, bow down”): 13 verses [T]. In 10 of them Jesus is the one worshipped, or is pretended to be (2:2, 8, 11; 8:2; 9:18; 14:33; 15:25; 20:20; 28:9, 17). The hinge is 4:9–10. The devil asks to be worshipped, and Jesus answers, κύριον τὸν θεόν σου προσκυνήσεις (“you shall worship the Lord your God”). After that, Jesus receives worship and never refuses it [T].
  • μαθητεύω (“to make a disciple, to be discipled”): 3 verses, 13:52; 27:57; 28:19 [T]. It is used in no other Gospel [T].

Test against the movements. The prologue gives the genesis, the names (Jesus, Immanuel) and the re-lived exodus (2:15, 20). The proclamation movement is the kingdom of heaven in word and deed (4:17, 23; 5–9), and righteousness that is heard and done (5:20; 7:24). The rejection movement divides those who receive from those who refuse (11–13), and ends at the confession (16:16). The way to Jerusalem is the Son of Man who must suffer, and the ransom (16:21; 20:28). Jerusalem is the King, the temple and the judgement (21–25). The passion is the covenant blood for the forgiveness of sins (26:28), and the commission is the risen Lord’s authority, the nations and his presence (28:18–20). Every movement serves the microscript.

Intertextual Map

Matthew is citation-dense, more than any other Gospel. The narrator’s own fulfilment formula stands ten times (1:22; 2:15; 2:17; 2:23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9). γέγραπται (“it is written”) stands nine times of Scripture. Jesus asks “have you not read?” six times (12:3, 5; 19:4; 21:16, 42; 22:31). The antitheses of 5:21–48 cite the Torah six times as ἐρρέθη (“it was said”) [T]. The quotations cluster in three places: the prologue (1–4, five formulae and four further citations), the controversies and the Jerusalem week (12; 15; 19; 21–22), and the passion (26–27), which is told in the words of Zechariah and the Psalms [T]. Between those clusters, the narrative chapters 8–11 and 13–18 lean on allusion and on the book’s own internal repetitions.

How Matthew uses his Scriptures. This is a text-first finding for the whole sweep, and it needed both Greek editions to state correctly.

  • Where Matthew quotes in Jesus’ mouth, or with γέγραπται (“it is written”), he generally follows the Greek. Isa 6:9–10 at 13:14–15; Isa 29:13 at 15:8–9, including the LXX’s μάτην (“in vain”), where the Hebrew has no such word; Ps 8:3 at 21:16, where αἶνον (“praise”) renders a Hebrew that reads עֹז (“strength”); Ps 118:22–23 at 21:42; Ps 110:1 at 22:44 [T].
  • Where the narrator quotes with his own formula, he several times stands nearer the Hebrew than either Greek text in the corpus. Each case below was checked in Swete and in Rahlfs, and the Hebrew was gated by lemma with find.py [T]:
    • 2:15. ἐξ Αἰγύπτου ἐκάλεσα τὸν υἱόν μου (“out of Egypt I called my son”). The Hebrew of Hos 11:1 reads לִבְנִי (“my son”). Both Swete and Rahlfs read τὰ τέκνα αὐτοῦ (“his children”). The sonship that the citation exists to make depends on the Hebrew.
    • 2:18. οὐκ ἤθελεν παρακληθῆναι (“she would not be comforted”). The Hebrew of Jer 31:15 reads לְהִנָּחֵם (“to be comforted”). Both Greek texts have παύσασθαι (“to cease”; Swete and Rahlfs 38:15).
    • 8:17. τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν ἔλαβεν καὶ τὰς νόσους ἐβάστασεν (“he took our weaknesses and carried our diseases”). The Hebrew of Isa 53:4 reads חֳלָיֵנוּ (“our sicknesses”) and מַכְאֹבֵינוּ (“our pains”). Both Greek texts have τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν φέρει (“he bears our sins”). Matthew cites the verse of a healing summary, so his point needs the Hebrew.
    • 12:18–20. Isa 42:1–3 without the Greek’s Ἰακὼβ … Ἰσραήλ (“Jacob … Israel”). The Hebrew has עַבְדִּי (“my servant”) with no name, and Matthew’s ὁ παῖς μου (“my servant”) likewise has none. But at 42:4b Matthew follows the Greek: τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ ἔθνη ἐλπιοῦσιν (“in his name the nations will hope”), where the Hebrew reads וּלְתוֹרָתוֹ אִיִּים יְיַחֵילוּ (“and the coastlands wait for his torah”). The quotation is mixed. At 61 words it is the longest Old Testament quotation in Matthew [T].
    • 21:5. ἐπὶ ὄνον καὶ ἐπὶ πῶλον υἱὸν ὑποζυγίου (“on a donkey and on a colt, the foal of a beast of burden”). The Hebrew of Zech 9:9 has עַל־חֲמוֹר וְעַל־עַיִר בֶּן־אֲתֹנוֹת (“on a donkey, on a colt, the son of she-asses”). Both Greek texts read ἐπὶ ὑποζύγιον καὶ πῶλον νέον (“on a beast of burden and a young colt”). The two animals of 21:2, 7 follow the Hebrew parallelism.
    • 26:31. πατάξω τὸν ποιμένα (“I will strike the shepherd”), singular. The Hebrew of Zech 13:7 has הַךְ אֶת־הָרֹעֶה (“strike the shepherd”). Both Swete and Rahlfs have πατάξατε τοὺς ποιμένας (“strike the shepherds”). This is the same finding as Mark 14:27.
    • 27:9–10. εἰς τὸν ἀγρὸν τοῦ κεραμέως (“for the potter’s field”). The Hebrew of Zech 11:13 has אֶל־הַיּוֹצֵר (“to the potter”). Both Greek texts have εἰς τὸ χωνευτήριον (“into the furnace”). The potter, and so the field, exist only in the Hebrew.
    • 27:46. ηλι ηλι (“Eli, Eli”) is the Hebrew אֵלִי (“my God”) of Ps 22:2. Mark has the Aramaic ἐλωΐ (“Eloi”, Mark 15:34). λεμα σαβαχθανι (“why have you forsaken me”) is Aramaic in both.
  • Two apparent “Hebrew” cases are really Swete–Rahlfs divergences, and are withdrawn as such [T]:
    • 9:13; 12:7. ἔλεος θέλω καὶ οὐ θυσίαν (“I desire mercy and not sacrifice”). The Hebrew of Hos 6:6 has וְלֹא־זָבַח (“and not sacrifice”). Swete reads ἢ θυσίαν (“rather than sacrifice”), but Rahlfs reads καὶ οὐ θυσίαν (“and not sacrifice”), exactly as Matthew does.
    • 1:23. ἐν γαστρὶ ἕξει (“will be with child”). Swete reads λήμψεται (“will conceive”); Rahlfs reads ἕξει (“will be with child”), as Matthew does.
    A search of Swete alone would have reported both as Matthew departing from the LXX. 4:15 is a third case: Matthew’s ὁδὸν θαλάσσης (“the way of the sea”, Isa 8:23 Hebrew דֶּרֶךְ הַיָּם) is in Rahlfs but missing from Swete. 13:43 is a fourth [S: audit]: Matthew’s ἐκλάμψουσιν (“they will shine out”) is the verb of Theodotion Dan 12:3 in Rahlfs; Swete’s Theodotion reads λάμψουσιν (“they will shine”).
  • Where the Greek names someone else [S: audit]. At 23:35 the murdered Zechariah is identified with 2 Chr 24:20–22 only through the Hebrew, זְכַרְיָה בֶּן־יְהוֹיָדָע (“Zechariah son of Jehoiada”). Swete and Rahlfs both name the priest Ἀζαρίαν (“Azariah”) [T].
  • Matthean moves towards a source [S: audit]. In two places Matthew alters inherited or common wording so that it stands nearer one text [T]:
    • 11:29 (in Jesus’ mouth, and Matthew’s own material). εὑρήσετε ἀνάπαυσιν ταῖς ψυχαῖς ὑμῶν (“you will find rest for your souls”) keeps the frame of Jer 6:16 LXX and corrects its noun from ἁγνισμόν (“purification”) to the Hebrew מַרְגּוֹעַ (“rest”), a word found only there in the Hebrew Bible. Isa 28:12 (Hebrew) is a companion: “this is rest; give rest to the weary”, and “they would not hear”. So the first bullet above has an exception: in Jesus’ mouth, at 11:29, Matthew follows the Hebrew.
    • 9:36. Matthew changes Mark 6:34’s ὡς (“as”) to ὡσεί (“like”), and ὡσεὶ πρόβατα (“like sheep”) occurs in Swete only at Num 27:17 and Ps 79:2.
  • One deliberate departure from every text. At 4:10 Jesus answers, κύριον τὸν θεόν σου προσκυνήσεις (“you shall worship the Lord your God”). The Hebrew of Deut 6:13 has תִּירָא (“you shall fear”), and Swete and Rahlfs both have φοβηθήσῃ (“you shall fear”) [T]. The verb answers the devil’s ἐὰν πεσὼν προσκυνήσῃς μοι (“if you will fall down and worship me”, 4:9). That some LXX manuscripts (Alexandrinus) read προσκυνήσεις (“you shall worship”) is [S] and was not checked in the corpus. Either way, the word becomes the chain that runs to 28:17 (see the Microscript).

Consequence for the digs. In Matthew, Tool 11 Move 2 must read Swete, Rahlfs and the WLC together. A search of one Greek text can misreport a reading in either direction: it hid the Hebrew at 2:15, 2:18, 8:17, 21:5, 26:31 and 27:9, it invented a “departure” at 9:13 and 1:23, and it misreported Theodotion at 13:43 [I].

The Synoptic check [S: audit]. Before an allusion is rated, compare Matthew with Mark (and with Luke, for the sayings they share). The claim audit found that the check changed eight ratings. Four echoes are inherited from Mark, so they belong to the tradition before they belong to Matthew: Isa 50:6 (26:67), the “many” of Isa 53 (20:28 is Mark 10:45 verbatim), Gen 37:20 (21:38) and Deut 18:15 (17:5). Four are Matthew’s own moves towards a source: Num 27:17 (ὡσεί (“like”), 9:36), Isa 61:2 (οἱ πενθοῦντες … παρακληθήσονται, “those who mourn … will be comforted”, 5:4, against Luke 6:21’s “those who weep … will laugh”), Ps 69:22 (χολή (“gall”) for Mark’s myrrh, 27:34) and Zech 12:10–12 (the clause added at 24:30). The pattern of failure in the audit was the same as Mark’s: a single source named where the evidence shows a composite, or Matthean wording that was really Mark’s. Both are invisible from inside one Gospel.

Live sources, in summary [T]. Isaiah (about 20 places, named 6 times) is the spine, from 1:23 to 27:57. Next come the Psalms (2, 8, 22, 110 and 118, each twice or more), Deuteronomy (the temptation, the commandments, divorce, witnesses, blood-guilt, and the last formula) and Daniel (7, 9–12, 3, 2, 4; concentrated in 24–26 and the commission). The rest are Zechariah (9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 2, and 1:1; concentrated in 21–27), Exodus (4:19, 19–24, 34), Genesis (from 1:1 to 24:37), Hosea (11:1; 6:6 twice), Jeremiah (named three times), Micah, Jonah, Malachi with Elijah and Chronicles (first and last). A dig finding a new echo of any of these should give it an elevated prior.

Three concentrations the digs should expect [I]. The prologue (1:1–4:16) packs Genesis, Chronicles and Ruth, Isaiah 7–9, Hosea 11, Jeremiah 31, Micah 5, Exodus 4 and Deuteronomy 6–8 into a hundred verses. The Jerusalem week (21–23) turns on Isaiah 5, 56 and 62, Jeremiah 7, Zechariah 9, and Psalms 8, 110 and 118. The passion (26–27) is told in Zechariah 11 and 13, Psalms 22, 42–43 and 69, Isaiah 50 and 53, Exodus 24, Deuteronomy 21 and 27, and 2 Samuel 17. Between them, the narrative of 8–11 and 13–18 is carried more by internal repetition than by citation, and there Move 4 will do more work than Move 2.

Christological Trajectory

Matthew is a Gospel, so Christ is its subject rather than its horizon. The question for the overview is how the book presents him. It does so in four registers.

  • Ground — God with us. The child is named twice. He is Ἰησοῦς (“Jesus”), “for he will save his people from their sins” (1:21), and he is Ἐμμανουήλ … μεθʼ ἡμῶν ὁ θεός (“Immanuel … God with us”, 1:23) [T]. The book never leaves that claim. Where two or three gather in his name, ἐκεῖ εἰμι ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῶν (“there I am in their midst”, 18:20). The last line is ἐγὼ μεθʼ ὑμῶν εἰμι (“I am with you”, 28:20) [T]. Along the way he is greater than the temple (12:6), than Jonah and than Solomon (12:41–42). He is David’s κύριος (“Lord”, 22:43–45) and walks on the sea saying ἐγώ εἰμι (“I am”, 14:27) [T]. And he receives προσκύνησις (“worship”), never refusing it, after he has told the devil that only the Lord God is to be worshipped (4:10) [T]. Matthew lets the juxtaposition make the claim. [I], high.
  • Israel’s story, re-lived and completed. The Son goes down to Egypt and is called out (2:15, Hosea’s “my son”), and is recalled as Moses was, “for those who sought the child’s life are dead” (2:20; Exod 4:19, with Exod 2:15 behind 2:13–14) [S: audit]. He passes through the waters (3:13–17) and is tested forty days and forty nights in the wilderness, answering from Deuteronomy each time (4:1–11). He goes up the mountain (5:1), in the words Swete uses only of Moses at Sinai (moderate [S: audit]), and gives Torah with ἐγὼ δὲ λέγω ὑμῖν (“but I say to you”, six times in 5:21–48) [T]. The pattern is typological: Matthew’s “fulfilment” includes the fulfilment of a history (Hos 11:1), not only of predictions [T] for the citation’s own context, [I] for the method. Moses and Elijah appear with him and then vanish, leaving αὐτὸν Ἰησοῦν μόνον (“Jesus himself alone”, 17:8) [T].
  • The suffering King and Servant. He is Son of David (8 times of Jesus: 1:1; 9:27; 12:23; 15:22; 20:30, 31; 21:9, 15) and “King of the Jews” from his birth (2:2) to his cross (27:37) [T]. Twice the Voice calls him “my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” (3:17; 17:5). The narrator puts the same words on Isaiah’s Servant (12:18) [T]. The Servant “took our weaknesses” by healing (8:17) and gives his life λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν (“a ransom for many”, 20:28; Mark 10:45 verbatim). His blood of the covenant is poured out περὶ πολλῶν εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν (“for many, for the forgiveness of sins”, 26:28) [T]. That answers the name of 1:21 (see the Echo Table). Isa 53’s “many” is one strand in these two sayings, woven with Exod 24:8, the sin-offering and Dan 9:27, not their single source [S: audit]. The Son of Man must suffer (16:21), and the same Son of Man is the one who comes on the clouds (24:30; 26:64). In the end he is given “all authority in heaven and on earth” (28:18), in the wording of the Old Greek of Dan 7:14 [T].
  • Pattern. “Take up his cross and follow me” (16:24). “It is enough for the disciple to be like his teacher” (10:25). “Whoever would be great among you must be your servant … just as the Son of Man came … to serve” (20:26–28) [T]. The pattern is made visible in two places where Jesus does what he taught [T]. He told disciples that if anyone ῥαπίζει (“slaps”) them, they should turn the other cheek (5:39), and before the council they ἐράπισαν (“slapped”) him and he did not answer (26:63, 67). He taught disciples to pray “your will be done” (6:10), and in Gethsemane he prays γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου (“your will be done”, 26:42). ῥαπίζω (“to slap”) occurs only at 5:39 and 26:67, and γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου (“your will be done”) only at 6:10 and 26:42 [T].

Son of God — and who says it. God says it (3:17; 17:5). The devil says it as a condition: εἰ υἱὸς εἶ τοῦ θεοῦ (“if you are the Son of God”, 4:3, 6). Demons say it (8:29). The disciples say it in the boat, and worship him: ἀληθῶς θεοῦ υἱὸς εἶ (“truly you are God’s Son”, 14:33). Peter says it (16:16). The high priest asks it under oath (26:63). The mockers say the devil’s condition again, εἰ υἱὸς εἶ τοῦ θεοῦ (“if you are the Son of God”, 27:40), and quote his claim (27:43). The centurion and those with him say ἀληθῶς θεοῦ υἱὸς ἦν οὗτος (“truly this was God’s Son”, 27:54) [T]. Unlike Mark, where the disciples’ hearts are “hardened” after the sea-walk (Mark 6:52), Matthew’s disciples confess before the cross [T]. The confession at the cross is then not the first but the echo of the confession in the boat: ἀληθῶς θεοῦ υἱός (“truly God’s Son”) occurs only at 14:33 and 27:54 [T].

Moralism check. Matthew invites three forms of moralism.

  • “Be more righteous than the Pharisees.” This preaches 5:20 as a moral target. It fails because the Sermon begins with blessing, not demand: μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοὶ τῷ πνεύματι (“blessed are the poor in spirit”, 5:3), “for theirs is the kingdom”. And the one who “fulfils all righteousness” is Jesus (3:15) before anyone else is asked for it [T].
  • “Have enough faith” (Peter on the water, 14:28–31). This fails because Matthew’s word for the disciples is ὀλιγόπιστοι (“of little faith”): four times, with ὀλιγοπιστία (“littleness of faith”) once more at 17:20. Each time Jesus acts for them anyway: he stills the storm, he catches Peter, he explains the bread (8:26; 14:31; 16:8–10) [T]. The last scene says οἱ δὲ ἐδίστασαν (“but some doubted”, 28:17), and he still commissions them all [T].
  • “Be like the magi / the centurion / the Canaanite woman.” This fails because each is a recipient before they are an example, and Matthew’s last word is not their faith but his authority and presence (28:18, 20) [T].

The order of the book’s imperatives is the corrective. Blessing comes before the demand (5:3–12 before 5:13–48). Rest comes before the yoke: δεῦτε πρός με … κἀγὼ ἀναπαύσω ὑμᾶς (“come to me … and I will give you rest”, 11:28, before 11:29). Authority comes before the commission (28:18 before 28:19), and presence after it (28:20) [T] for the order, [I] for the reading. Christ is hero, not merely example.

Preaching Traps

  1. The Sermon on the Mount as either a new law or an impossible one. One version preaches 5–7 as rules for a better life; the other as a standard set only to drive hearers to despair. Bites at: 5:17–48, especially 5:20 and 5:48; 7:13–27. Why it is wrong: the Sermon is addressed to the blessed (5:3–12), and it ends by demanding not perfection but hearing and doing (7:24–27). Its τέλειοι (“perfect, complete”, 5:48) is the Father’s undivided love for enemies (5:43–47), and the same word comes again only once, at 19:21 [T]. Corrective: preach it as the righteousness of the kingdom given to disciples, which Jesus “fulfils” (3:15; 5:17) and which the disciples are later sent to teach the nations to keep (28:20). The Sermon’s own last image (7:24) and the commission’s last command (28:20) are the same act.

  2. The formula quotations as proof-texts of prediction. This preaches “Jesus fulfilled 300 prophecies” and treats 2:15, 2:18 and 2:23 as forecasts. Bites at: 1:22–2:23; 8:17; 12:17–21; 13:35; 21:4–5; 27:9–10. Why it is wrong: Hos 11:1 looks back on the exodus. Jer 31:15 mourns the exile. “Nazarene” is “through the prophets” in general (2:23). Ps 78:2 is a psalm of Asaph that Matthew calls “the prophet” [T]. Matthew’s πληρόω (“to fulfil”) includes the completing of a pattern and the filling-full of a story [I]. Corrective: preach each citation in its own context first, then show what Matthew sees completed. Expect the pattern of Israel’s history, the exodus and the exile, more often than a forecast. And note where Matthew reads the Hebrew (see the Intertextual Map): the point of 2:15 depends on it.

  3. 27:25 and chapter 23 turned against “the Jews”. This preaches “his blood be on us and on our children” as a curse on a people, and the woes as an ethnic charge. Bites at: 21:43; 23:1–39; 27:24–25; 28:15. Why it is wrong: the speaker is πᾶς ὁ λαός (“all the people”) in a Jerusalem crowd persuaded by the chief priests (27:20) [T]. The book’s first promise is that Jesus “will save his people (τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ) from their sins” (1:21). The blood they call down is the blood “poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins” (26:28) [T]. Matthew’s narrator, his disciples and his Messiah are Jews, and the woes are addressed to “scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites”, which is a charge any religious reader can earn [T]/[I]. The Torah behind the scene cuts the same way [S: audit]: in Deut 21:6–9 the elders wash and pray that innocent blood may not lie on the people, and in Deut 27:25 “all the people” say Amen to a curse on the bribe for innocent blood (compare Judas’ silver). Matthew’s scene inverts a rite of release; it does not pronounce a verdict on a race [I]. Corrective: preach 27:25 with 1:21 and 26:28, the irony that the text itself sets up. Name the historic misuse from the pulpit, and aim the woes where Matthew aims them, at religion that “says and does not do” (23:3). A pastoral landmine.

  4. Matthew 24 as a timetable, and “one taken, one left” as rapture. Bites at: 24:3–44, especially 24:15, 24:29–31, 24:34 and 24:40–41. Why it is wrong: the discourse turns on βλέπετε μή τις ὑμᾶς πλανήσῃ (“see that no one leads you astray”, 24:4) and γρηγορεῖτε (“keep awake”, 24:42; 25:13). “Of that day and hour no one knows, not even … the Son” (24:36) [T]. In the Noah analogy that governs 24:40–41, those “taken” are the ones the flood ἦρεν ἅπαντας (“took away, all of them”, 24:39) [T]. Corrective: preach 24–25 as the call to watchfulness and faithful service, and read 24:40–41 by 24:37–39.

  5. 16:18 as a confessional battleground, or skipped to avoid one. Bites at: 16:13–20; 18:15–20. Why it is wrong: both the Petrine-office reading and the “confession, not Peter” reading drop the book’s own thread. πέτρα (“rock”) is first the foundation of the house of the one who hears and does (7:24–25). The keys given to Peter (16:19) are given in the same words to the assembly (18:18). And the next scene calls Peter σκάνδαλον (“a stumbling-block”, 16:23) [T]. Corrective: preach the confession, the person and the gift together, as Matthew does, and let 18:18 interpret 16:19.

  6. The Great Commission as “Go”. This makes πορευθέντες (“having gone, go”) the point, as a mandate for foreign travel. Bites at: 28:16–20. Why it is wrong: the only imperative is μαθητεύσατε (“make disciples”). πορευθέντες (“going”), βαπτίζοντες (“baptising”) and διδάσκοντες (“teaching”) are participles [T]. The grammar can be over-pressed both ways, since an aorist participle before an imperative often shares its force [S]. But the command is grounded in the authority of 28:18 (οὖν, “therefore”) and sustained by the presence of 28:20 [T]. Corrective: preach the authority, the discipling, the teaching “to keep all that I commanded” (which points back to the five discourses) and the presence, together.

  7. 25:31–46 as either pure social ethics or pure missions. Bites at: 25:31–46; 10:40–42; 18:5–6. Why it is wrong: the question who “these least of my brothers” are is contested [S]. The text supplies data both ways. Jesus calls his disciples ἀδελφοί μου (“my brothers”, 12:49–50; 28:10), and the “little ones” who receive a cup of water in his name are disciples (10:42) [T]. Yet the judged are πάντα τὰ ἔθνη (“all the nations”), and both groups are surprised (25:37, 44) [T]. Corrective: present the options honestly. Do not build a programme on one of them.

Addressee differentiation. The opening names distinct parties:

  • Abraham and David (1:1);
  • four women, Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and “the wife of Uriah” (1:3–6);
  • Joseph, “a righteous man” (1:19);
  • Gentile magi (2:1);
  • King Herod, and “all Jerusalem” (2:3);
  • “all the chief priests and scribes of the people” (2:4);
  • John, and the Pharisees and Sadducees who come to his baptism (3:7);
  • the devil (4:1).

Most resolve specifically, as follows.

  • Abraham resolves in the nations of 8:11 and 28:19, and in Exod 3:6, “the God of Abraham … of the living” (22:32).
  • David resolves in the cries of the blind and the Canaanite woman, and in “David’s Lord” (22:45).
  • The four women — none of them an Israelite wife of an Israelite, and two of them Canaanite by the narrative of the Tanak (Tamar, Gen 38; Rahab, Josh 2) [T]/[I] — are answered by the Canaanite woman of 15:22. She is called Χαναναία (“Canaanite”) only here in the New Testament [T]. They are answered too by the women at the cross and the tomb, who are the first witnesses (27:55–56, 61; 28:1–10) [T].
  • The magi are answered by the centurion and those with him (27:54), and by “all the nations” (28:19).
  • Herod, who “was troubled” and “mocked”, is answered by Pilate, who “was greatly amazed” (27:14), and by the soldiers’ mockery.
  • The chief priests and scribes, who know the text of Micah and do not go to Bethlehem (2:4–6), resolve in 28:11–15. They know the facts of the empty tomb and pay to bury them [T].
  • The Pharisees and Sadducees called γεννήματα ἐχιδνῶν (“brood of vipers”) by John (3:7) are called the same by Jesus (12:34; 23:33). The phrase occurs only at these three places.
  • The devil, refused on the mountain (4:8–10), is answered by the authority given on the mountain (28:18).

Hold 1:1–4:16 open against 26–28. The book ends on a mountain in Galilee of the nations, with the Son of Abraham and David worshipped, the doubting gathered, and God with them. [I]

Dig-deeper runs. The whole-book sweep of 16 July 2026 and the 1:1–17 dig of July 2026 were opened only at this stage. Both predate the September 2026 method.
