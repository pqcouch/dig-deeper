## 1.1 — Isaiah 2:2 behind Acts 2:17

[**The Logos library does not contain anything by Litwak.**]

**Peter's wording in Acts 2:17.** G. K. Beale identifies Peter's substitution of "in the latter days" for Joel's "after these things" [1], and traces this phrase to Isaiah 2:2–3, noting it is "the only place in the LXX where this exact phrase occurs." [1] Beale explains that Peter appears to interpret the Spirit's coming at Pentecost as fulfilling not only Joel's prophecy but also Isaiah's vision of the end-time temple, toward which the nations would stream. [1] He notes that Joel's own context supports this connection, since Joel 3:18 itself concerns the establishment of the end-time temple. [1] Beale's discussion appears in *A New Testament Biblical Theology* (2011), in the section "Eschatological References in Acts."

Everett Ferguson similarly identifies the substitution as "likely borrowed from Isaiah 2:2 (= Mic. 4:1)." [2] Ferguson suggests the wording change was "intended to bring out an eschatological application of Joel 2:28 to the new age of the Spirit that began at Pentecost," [2] emphasising that early Christians understood themselves as living in the "last days," making the church an eschatological community. [2] This appears in Ferguson's *The Church of Christ* (1996), in the section "People of the End Time."

G. K. Beale's *The Temple and the Church's Mission* (2004) also discusses the substitution, identifying Isaiah 2:2 as its source [3], and explains that Peter interprets the Spirit's coming as fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy of the end-time temple alongside Joel's prophecy. [3]

Jeffrey Niehaus (Niehaus, J.J. (2017) *Biblical Theology: The Special Grace Covenants (New Testament)*. Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press) acknowledges the substitution but does not elaborate on its source or theological rationale.

[1] G. K. Beale, *A New Testament Biblical Theology: The Unfolding of the Old Testament in the New* (Grand Rapids, MI: Baker Academic, 2011), 136.
[2] Everett Ferguson, *The Church of Christ: A Biblical Ecclesiology for Today* (Grand Rapids, MI: Wm. B. Eerdmans Pub. Co., 1996), 67–68.
[3] G. K. Beale, *The Temple and the Church's Mission: A Biblical Theology of the Dwelling Place of God*, ed. D. A. Carson, New Studies in Biblical Theology (Downers Grove, IL; England: InterVarsity Press; Apollos, 2004), 17:209.

**Allison Trites comments:**

> 2:17–21 "In the last days." This wording in the quote from Joel 2:28–32 differs from the LXX. Acts 2:17 has *en tais eschatais hēmerais* ("in the last days") where the LXX (reflecting the MT) has *meta tauta* ("after these things") and then introduces "God says." It also reverses two lines of Joel 2:28. Acts 2:18 adds "and they will prophesy" to Joel 2:29. Acts 2:19 adds "above … signs … below" to Joel 2:30. These changes are appropriate inferences from the immediate context or legitimate expansions for greater clarity and impact (Archer and Chirichigno 1983:149).
>
> The Western text shows a universalizing tendency: it applies Joel to the Gentiles as well as the Jews by making "all flesh" plural, changing "your sons … your daughters" to "their sons … their daughters," and changing "your young men … your old men" simply to "young men … old men." The same motivation may be behind the switch from second person to first person pronouns in verse 39, thus identifying salvation as a gift for spiritual Israel—both Jew and Gentile (Metzger 1994:257). The Western text does not contain "blood and fire and clouds of smoke." Either a copyist saw the details as irrelevant to Peter's points, or he committed a parablepsis—that is, his eye skipped from *katō* (below) to *kapnou* (smoke) so that he failed to copy the line in between (Metzger 1994:258).

Trites, A.A., William J. Larkin (2006) *Cornerstone Biblical Commentary, Vol 12: The Gospel of Luke and Acts*. Carol Stream, IL: Tyndale House Publishers, p. 391.

**Parsons** identifies that the Joel citation has been modified by changing "after these things" (*meta tauta*) in the LXX text to "in the last days," noting that this substitution underscores the eschatological significance of the new community itself [1]. However, the search results do not contain any discussion by Parsons of where the phrase "in the last days" originates or whether he identifies Isaiah 2:2 as its source. The search results include two Greek text handbooks by Parsons (co-authored with Culy and Hall) that offer grammatical analysis of Acts 2:17, and one commentary volume from the Paideia series. While Parsons' commentary explicitly acknowledges the substitution and its theological function, he does not appear to address the textual provenance of the replacement phrase or its connection to Isaiah 2:2.

[1] Mikeal C. Parsons, *Acts*, Paideia Commentaries on the New Testament (Grand Rapids, MI: Baker Academic, 2008), 42.

## 1.2 — Isaiah 53:8 behind Acts 22:22

I. Howard Marshall identifies the phrase "Take him from the earth" (*aire apo tēs gēs*) in Acts 22:22 as reproducing the idiom found in Acts 8:33's citation of Isaiah 53:8, and suggests it may echo that passage to draw a parallel between Jesus's and Paul's fates. [1] Andrew C. Clark similarly notes that the phrase *αἶρε ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* used of Paul in 22:22 recalls the same words in 8:33, part of Isaiah 53:7, which Philip interprets as referring to Jesus. [2] Dennis E. Johnson observes that the crowd's outcry alluded to the Suffering Servant's suffering, with their words echoing "His life is taken away from the earth" from Isaiah 53:8. [3]

The specific wording both commentators emphasise is the phrase **ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς** ("from the earth"), which appears in both contexts.

Regarding whether this represents shared idiom rather than literary allusion, Marshall describes it as reproducing "the idiom in 8:33," [1] suggesting it functions as an established expression. However, the search results do not contain explicit arguments that this is merely a common idiom rather than a deliberate echo. The lexical sources included discuss the verb *αἴρω*'s various meanings in contexts of removal or death, but they do not directly address whether the Acts 22:22/8:33 connection represents formulaic language or intentional Lukan parallelism. Marshall's cautious language — "it may therefore be an echo" — leaves room for both interpretations, though his framing suggests he leans toward recognising a meaningful literary connection.

## 1.3 — Isaiah 60:17 and 1 Clement 42:5 behind Acts 20:28

First Clement 42:4–5 cites a Greek version of Isaiah 60:17 when discussing the apostles' appointment of bishops and deacons [1], making this the primary patristic source linking overseer terminology to that Old Testament passage. The passage describes the apostles' appointment of overseers and deacons through the words "I shall appoint their bishops in righteousness and their deacons in faith," justified through a citation of LXX Isaiah 60:17 [2].

However, the search results do not contain commentary specifically on Acts 20:28 that discusses this Isaiah 60:17 connection or engages with 1 Clement 42:5 in relation to that verse. One search result notes that "the duty of the *episkopos* in the earlier Pauline churches is clearly set out in Acts 20:28" and that the overseers there are "those who, in Acts 20:17, are called 'elders of the church'" [3], but this commentary does not explore Old Testament background or patristic parallels.

One source notes that "*episkopos* does not appear in secular texts before the second Christian century," though it "frequently designates God's powerful manifestations in the LXX" [1]. The LXX uses *episkopos* roughly a dozen times, regularly suggesting "the Levites and their care for the place and apparatus of worship" or "military leadership" [1].

## 2.1 — *ἀκούω* with the genitive and the accusative at Acts 9:7 and 22:9

The standard grammars present a striking internal tension on this question. Robertson argues that the genitive in Acts 9:7 and the accusative in 22:9 reflect a meaningful distinction: the accusative emphasises intellectual comprehension of the sound, while the genitive directs attention to the sound itself without stressing understanding, with *ἀκούω* itself carrying dual senses of "to hear" and "to understand." [1]

However, Wallace directly challenges this solution. He demonstrates that the New Testament contains numerous examples of *ἀκούω* with the genitive indicating understanding (Matt 2:9; John 5:25; 18:37; Acts 3:23; 11:7; Rev 3:20; 6:3, 5; 8:13; 11:12; 14:13; 16:1, 5, 7; 21:3) as well as instances of *ἀκούω* with the accusative where little or no comprehension takes place (Matt 13:19; Mark 13:7; Acts 5:24; 1 Cor 11:18; Eph 3:2; Col 1:4; Phlm 5; Jas 5:11; Rev 14:2). [2] Wallace observes that the exceptions are seemingly more numerous than the rule, concluding that an appeal to different cases probably ought not form any part of the solution to the apparent contradiction. [2]

Robertson himself acknowledges the difficulty: he notes that the genitive can be used where understanding is meant, though not stressed, citing Acts 22:7 (genitive) against Acts 9:4 and 26:14 (accusative) — precisely the inconsistency you identify. [1] BDF similarly notes that the New Testament wavers between genitive and accusative in phrases meaning "to hear a sound," whereas classical Greek uses the genitive. [3]

[1] A. T. Robertson, *A Grammar of the Greek New Testament in the Light of Historical Research* (Logos Bible Software, 2006), 506.
[2] Daniel B. Wallace, *Greek Grammar beyond the Basics* (Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan, 1996), 133–134.
[3] Blass, Debrunner and Funk, *A Greek Grammar of the New Testament and Other Early Christian Literature* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1961), 95.

**Beyond the three standard grammars.** Craig Blomberg notes that classically the genitive with *ἀκούω* meant "to hear without understanding" while the accusative meant "to hear with understanding," so Acts 9:7's genitive could indicate the companions heard the voice without grasping its content, while 22:9's accusative describes their failure to hear with understanding. [1] However, Blomberg acknowledges that this classical distinction was eroding in first-century Greek and doesn't consistently apply throughout the New Testament and Luke, though he argues it is difficult to explain why Luke would vary the case if he didn't intend some distinction. [1]

R. C. H. Lenski observes that the genitive makes one "think more of the speaker, while the accusative would refer more to what is said by the speaker," and concludes that the companions did not hear what the speaker said (accusative in 22:9) but did hear that some speaker was speaking to Paul (genitive in 9:7), finding no contradiction. [2]

Ben Witherington III argues that in classical Greek the genitive refers to hearing sound while the accusative encompasses both hearing and understanding, and suggests Luke may be archaising. [3] Yet Witherington notes R. Bratcher's objection that Luke doesn't consistently observe this distinction elsewhere, and acknowledges that Luke is sporadic in his classicising tendencies. [3] Witherington ultimately favours Polhill's distinction between a voice "which was speaking" (22:9) and the hearing of a sound or noise (9:7), and stresses that 22:9 doesn't deny they heard the sound at all, only that they didn't hear the voice of the one speaking to Paul. [3]

[1] Craig L. Blomberg, *Can We Still Believe in God?* (Grand Rapids, MI: Brazos Press, 2020), 121.
[2] R. C. H. Lenski, *The Interpretation of the Acts of the Apostles* (Minneapolis, MN: Augsburg, 1961), 905.
[3] Ben Witherington III, *The Acts of the Apostles: A Socio-Rhetorical Commentary* (Grand Rapids, MI: Wm. B. Eerdmans, 1998), 312–313.

**Exegetical Guide at Acts 22:9.** *φωνὴν* — noun, accusative, singular, feminine. *ἤκουσαν* — verb, aorist, active, indicative, third person, plural. Lexicon entries: **BDAG is not in the library.**

## 2.2 — The whitewashed wall at Acts 23:3

The *Exegetical Dictionary of the New Testament* identifies Paul's "whitewashed wall" as an invective likely alluding to Ezekiel 13:10–12, characterising the Sanhedrin and high priest as resembling a cracked wall whose fragility is concealed only by whitewash. [1] I. Howard Marshall similarly connects the phrase to Ezekiel 13:8–15. [2] Darrell Bock notes that while "whitewashed wall" is unique in the New Testament, a parallel expression — "whitewashed tomb" — appears in Matthew 23:27, with both drawing on Ezekiel 13:10–16. [3] Craig Keener describes the image as condemning abusive leaders of Israel, citing Ezekiel 13:10–11 and 22:28. [4] Paul Wright observes that Jesus compared Pharisaic actions to whitewashed tombs, while Paul called Ananias a "whitewashed wall" to illustrate hypocrisy, with Ezekiel employing similar imagery of lying prophets as plastered walls. [5]

On whether it is simply an insult: Bock characterises the description as an insult, stating that although the person appears clean, there is no depth to the person. [3] The *Exegetical Dictionary* describes it as "probably a common insult, here likely alluding to Ezek 13:10–12," [1] suggesting the commentators view the expression as operating simultaneously as conventional invective and scriptural echo.

**None of the search results cite Deuteronomy 27:2–4 or Daniel 5:5 in connection with Acts 23:3.**

[2] I. Howard Marshall, "Acts," in *Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament*, 598.
[3] Darrell L. Bock, *Acts*, BECNT (Grand Rapids, MI: Baker Academic, 2007), 669–670.
[4] Craig S. Keener, *The IVP Bible Background Commentary: New Testament* (Downers Grove, IL: IVP Academic, 2014), 398.
[5] Paul H. Wright, "Indicting Hypocrisy with Imagery from the Temple Mount," in *Lexham Geographic Commentary on the Gospels* (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2016), 425.

**A further question was asked:** which commentators on Deuteronomy 27:2–4 discuss the link with "whitewashed wall" in Acts 23:3, and does anyone notice that *κονιάω* in the Pentateuch occurs only at Deuteronomy 27?

The search results do not contain commentaries on Deuteronomy 27:2–4 that discuss a link with Acts 23:3. However, the lexical resources do establish the connection and address the second question. Moses instructed the Israelites to set up stones on Mount Ebal, to "whitewash" them, and to inscribe the Law on them [1]. The Septuagint renders the Hebrew שִׂיד as *κονιάω* at Deuteronomy 27:2 and 27:4 [1].

**The Septuagint version of Proverbs 21:9 gives the only other Old Testament usage** [1]. Lexical references note that *κονιάω* appears in the Septuagint at Deuteronomy 27:2, Deuteronomy 27:4, and Proverbs 21:9 [2]. So yes — *κονιάω* in the Pentateuch occurs exclusively at Deuteronomy 27:2 and 27:4.

However, none of the search results explicitly highlight this lexical rarity as significant for understanding Paul's invective. Paul called the high priest a "whited wall," evidently intending to label him a hypocrite as Jesus had the Pharisees in Matthew 23:27 [1], but the available sources treat the phrase primarily as proverbial invective rather than as a deliberate echo of Deuteronomy 27's specific context of whitewashed stones bearing the Law. **The fact that *κονιάω* appears nowhere else in the Pentateuch remains unexamined in these lexical entries, suggesting an opportunity for deeper intertextual analysis.**

## 2.3 — *ἀκρίβεια* at Acts 22:3 and the preface

John P. Meier observes that Luke echoes the ancestors/fathers theme in Acts 22:3, where Paul describes his Pharisaic instruction at the feet of Gamaliel as being "according to the accuracy of the ancestral law." [1] Meier notes similar language in Josephus, who distinguishes Pharisees from Sadducees by noting that "the Pharisees have handed down to the common people certain laws that come from the tradition of the fathers, laws that have not been written down in the laws of Moses." [1]

On the connection with Luke 1:3: Vine's Dictionary notes that Luke employs the *ἀκριβ-* word family eight times out of thirteen occurrences in the New Testament, with Matthew using it three times and Paul twice. [2] Luke uses *ἀκριβῶς* in Luke 1:3 to describe having "traced the course of all things accurately." [2] **However, none of the search results explicitly connect this Lukan vocabulary preference to Paul's self-description in Acts 22:3**, nor do they argue that Luke deliberately echoes his own methodological claim from the prologue through Paul's description of rigorous Pharisaic training. The lexical resources establish that *ἀκρίβεια* means "exactness" [3], but they do not explore whether Luke's repeated use of this word family signals a thematic connection. This represents an interpretive opportunity that the available commentaries have not yet developed.

[1] John P. Meier, *A Marginal Jew … Volume Three, Companions and Competitors* (New Haven; London: Yale University Press, 2001), 316.
[2] Vine, Unger and White, *Vine's Complete Expository Dictionary* (Nashville, TN: T. Nelson, 1996), 2:10.

## 3.2 — Acts 20:28

**The Lexham Textual Notes, at Acts 20:28, on τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ θεοῦ:**

> Some early manuscripts have "church of God," but other early manuscripts have "church of the Lord." Many later witnesses have "church of the Lord and God." The latter reading is likely a conflation of "church of God" and "church of the Lord." This reading affects the understanding of the verse. Later in the verse, the relative clause "which he obtained through the blood of his own" relies upon "church of God" or "church of the Lord" to resolve the pronoun. Thus this verse, which mentions "his own blood," could be speaking of God's own blood or the Lord's own blood. Note that if the pronoun refers to God, a legitimate translation could also be "the blood of God's own," with "son" implied.

Brannan, R. and Loken, I. (2014) *The Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible*. Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, at Ac 20:28.

**The Assistant's answer.** Acts 20:28a presents three variant readings: "the church of God," "the church of the Lord," and "the church of the Lord and God." [1][2] The first variant has superior manuscript support and is adopted by the Textus Receptus (1551), Westcott and Hort (1881), Nestle-Aland, and the United Bible Society Greek New Testament (1993). [1][2]

The reasoning for preferring "the church of God" rests on two textual-critical principles. The phrase "the church of the Lord" appears nowhere in the New Testament, whereas "the church of God" occurs eleven times, all by Paul and Luke. [1][2] More significantly, the principle that "the more difficult reading is to be preferred" applies here, since scribes characteristically moved toward easier readings. [1] The reading "the church of God" is the most difficult because the following clause would cause any copyist to pause, asking "does God have blood?" Thus the original was altered to "the church of the Lord" because attributing blood to God seemed repugnant. [1]

Regarding Acts 20:28b, two readings exist: "which he purchased with the blood of his own" (understood as "his own Son") and "which he purchased with the own blood." [2] The first variant has superior manuscript evidence and is unquestionably original. [2] Bruce Metzger explains that "his own" (*ho idios*) appears in Greek papyri as a term of endearment for close relatives, and may have functioned as an early Christian title for Jesus comparable to "the Beloved," paralleling Romans 8:32. [2]

One commentator acknowledges the phrase's difficulty — since "his" grammatically attaches to "God" — and notes that early scribes emended it to clarify the meaning, though such emendations are unnecessary; the ancient scribes' theology was correct, as Paul does not assert that the Father's blood was shed, which would constitute the heresy of patripassianism. [3]

[1] Edward D. Andrews, *Reviewing 2013 New World Translation of Jehovah's Witnesses* (Cambridge, OH: Christian Publishing House, 2018).
[2] Edward D. Andrews, *The Challenge of Translating Truth: Bible Translation — No Easy Matter* (Cambridge, OH: Christian Publishing House, 2019).
[3] James M. Hamilton Jr. and Brian J. Vickers, *John–Acts*, ESV Expository Commentary (Wheaton, IL: Crossway, 2019), 9:526–527.

## 3.3 — Acts 11:20

**No note on Acts 11:20 in The Lexham Textual Notes.**

A well-attested alternate reading substitutes "Greeks" (*Hellēnas*) for "Hellenists" (*Hellēnistas*) in Acts 11:20 [1], and multiple commentators argue for this reading based on the contextual contrast with verse 19.

A. T. Robertson identifies "Greeks" as "undoubtedly the correct reading" and argues that **the presence of "also" or "even" (*kai*) makes no sense unless "Greeks" is correct** [2]. Robertson notes that Hellenistic or Grecian Jews as Christians were common enough, as seen in Acts 2 and 6, and that Saul had already preached to Hellenists in Jerusalem (9:29) — so preaching to Hellenists would not have been noteworthy [2]. Robertson suggests these Greeks in Antioch were apparently partly pagan rather than "God-fearers" like Cornelius [2].

The textual evidence emphasises that "Hellenists" in Acts 11:20 clearly contrasts with the word "Jews" in 11:19 [1]. There would have been nothing unusual about preaching to Greek-speaking Jews, since that had already been done in Jerusalem long before (Acts 6) and was being done elsewhere in the same scattering (11:19) [1]. It is possible that those actually evangelised in Antioch were in fact Gentiles — possibly "God-fearers" [1].

However, according to Metzger, "the preponderant weight of the external evidence combines with the strong transcriptional probability in support of Ἑλληνιστάς" (*Textual Commentary*, 6.1) [3], suggesting the printed "Hellenists" reading has stronger manuscript support despite the logical force of the contextual argument for "Greeks."

[1] Thoralf Gilbrant, "Ἑλληνιστής," in *The New Testament Greek-English Dictionary* (WORDsearch, 1991).
[2] A. T. Robertson, *Word Pictures in the New Testament* (Nashville, TN: Broadman Press, 1933).
[3] Matt McMains, "Hellenists," in *The Lexham Bible Dictionary* (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2016).

## 4.1 — The *κατηχέω* and *ἀσφάλεια* thread

The *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament* observes that the idea in Luke 1:4 is the "reliability" of the teachings, while in Acts 21:34, 22:30, and 25:26 the term denotes "the truth" (the facts, the real reason, something definite). — Kittel, Friedrich and Bromiley, *TDNT*, abridged in one volume (Grand Rapids, MI: Eerdmans, 1985), 87.

**The Luke 1:4 question.** Luke's prologue states his purpose as enabling Theophilus to "realise what assurance you have for the instruction you have received," with *ἀσφάλεια* serving as the key term. [1] Commentators debate whether this denotes merely historical reliability or something broader. While some scholars assume *asphaleia* refers to historical assurance, this interpretation proves insufficient, as Luke's historical perspective extends beyond questions of factual accuracy. [1] Luke writes from the church's period to convince Theophilus and similar readers that the church's contemporary teaching and practice derive from Jesus' ministry — the "assurance" being primarily doctrinal or didactic in nature. [1]

O'Toole argues that "truth" poorly translates *asphaleia*, since Luke addresses not merely historical facts but the belief naturally flowing from them — "reliability" offering a superior translation. [2] Thompson similarly emphasises that Luke's audience required "certainty" or "assurance" about their instruction. [3]

Regarding the word's trajectory into Acts, the adjective *asphalēs* appears three times in Luke's account — at Acts 21:34, 22:30, and 25:26 — conveying certain, precise, or exact knowledge. [4] Karris observes that if readers detect the Holy Spirit's reliability and the reliability of those through whom it acts or speaks, they become "assured" (*asphaleia* in Luke 1:4) that God will remain faithful to them. [5]

**Fitzmyer provides the fullest single treatment**, arguing that the Lucan prologue must be understood as introducing both volumes, and that rather than treating *asphaleia* as merely historical assurance, Luke's historical perspective transcends questions of factual accuracy, with the "assurance" being "mainly doctrinal or didactic" in purpose. [1]

By contrast, several commentators address the question only peripherally. Trites and Larkin note that Luke was concerned with unfolding events "carefully" or "accurately" and described his interest in providing "certainty" or "truth" (*asphaleia*), but do not elaborate. [2'] Balch observes that Luke categorises his work as a "narrative" rather than a "Gospel," yet does not develop the implications of *asphaleia*. [3'] Gaffin acknowledges that premodern commentators recognised Acts' selective, theologically motivated accounts, yet maintained it remains "accurate, historically reliable" — but does not examine *asphaleia* specifically. [4'] Johnson notes that Theophilus has already been "instructed" and asks why Luke provides another version, answering that Luke writes to give readers "security" (*asphaleia*) — yet frames this within broader apologetic concerns rather than analysing *asphaleia*'s semantic range. [5']

[2] Robert F. O'Toole, "Theophilus (Person)," in *The Anchor Yale Bible Dictionary* (New York: Doubleday, 1992), 6:512.
[3] Alan J. Thompson, *The Acts of the Risen Lord Jesus*, NSBT (Apollos; InterVarsity Press, 2011), 27:19.

## 4.2 — Acts 23:6, substance or tactics

**The real-issue interpretation.** Paul sees himself on trial because he believes in the doctrine of the resurrection — that there is hope people will live eternally and that God has promised eternal life to those who respond to Him [1]. The charge against him involves the question of the resurrection of the dead in the case of Jesus, which was the point that turned the scales when he became a Christian [2]. Paul felt himself on trial for his whole Christian career, not for any special act, and hence was entirely justified in his statement of the case [2].

**The tactical-manoeuvre interpretation.** Du Toit argues that Paul's strategy was to split the Pharisees from the Sadducees on the doctrine of resurrection [3]. Paul's perception that one part were Sadducees and the other Pharisees was nothing new to him; it was merely that he then saw how he could turn that fact to his advantage [2].

**Reconciling both views.** While it probably was a tactical manoeuvre, based on the present tense in Acts 23:6, it would be presumptuous to say Paul was being deceptive — rather, he could legitimately claim Pharisaic identity in view of his earlier Pharisaic formation and general agreement with core Pharisaic doctrinal beliefs [3]. Paul did not mean he was merely a Pharisee — they knew he was also a Christian — but that, although a Christian, he was still a Pharisee on the essential doctrine of the resurrection, and this doctrine was really at stake in the case against him [2].

[1] Darrell L. Bock, *NT211 Introducing the Gospels and Acts*, Logos Mobile Education (Bellingham, WA: Lexham Press, 2014).

## 4.3 — Acts 21:4, the Spirit's prohibition

**The disobedience interpretation.** Loescher argues that Acts 21:4 represents "the first definite indication that Paul is heading in the wrong direction," noting that **the preposition "through" (rather than "in") suggests the Holy Spirit rather than human spirit, and the imperfect tense indicates sustained prophetic urgency** telling Paul "not to set foot in Jerusalem." [1] If Paul's journey contradicts the Holy Spirit's direction, then positive explanations for ignoring further warnings become unnecessary. [1]

**The reinterpretation approaches.** Robertson distinguishes between duty and warning: Paul interpreted the Spirit's action "as information and warning although the disciples at Tyre gave it the form of a prohibition," with duty calling louder than warning even if both were God's calls. [2] Another explanation suggests that "through the Spirit" indicates attendant circumstances rather than direct command, and that the believers had received a prophecy about Paul's suffering — which Luke condensed into only the exhortation, omitting the underlying prophecy itself. [2] This reading gains support from the parallel at Caesarea, where both prophecy and exhortation are explicitly described. [2]

**The prophecy-interpretation distinction.** Blaylock argues it is unclear whether Acts 21:4 refers to prophecy at all, since the speakers are identified as disciples rather than prophets, and suggests that a prophet among the disciples prophesied that suffering awaited Paul, prompting the disciples' loving plea — meaning these verses do not report Paul disobeying a prophetic word. [3]

**The error theory.** Grudem claims the disciples possessed a revelation about Paul's suffering but coupled it with erroneous interpretation, warning him not to go — characterising this as "an unreliable human response to revelation from the Holy Spirit." [4]

## 5.1 — Claudius Lysias's purchased citizenship

The ancient historian Dio Cassius reported that citizenship could be purchased during Claudius's reign, **though technically individuals could not purchase it directly — rather, they could pay bribes to lesser officials to add their names to candidate lists for citizenship.** [1] The tribune Claudius Lysias obtained his citizenship through this mechanism. [1] Those receiving this status adopted the emperor's name, which explains why the tribune bore the name Claudius Lysias. [2]

Regarding Paul's counter-claim, commentators emphasise the historical plausibility of citizenship by birth in Tarsus. Roman citizenship most commonly was acquired by birth, as was true of Paul. [3] Paul possessed dual citizenship — of both Tarsus and Rome — and since Tarsian citizenship did not automatically confer Roman citizenship, the separation of these two statuses in Acts 21:39 and 22:27 furnishes proof of the narrative's accuracy. [4]

Scholars debate how Paul's family obtained Roman citizenship. One plausible scenario suggests Paul's grandfather or great-grandfather, working as a tentmaker when Pompey, Julius Caesar, or Mark Antony visited Tarsus, could have provided tents for the Roman army and received citizenship as a reward. [5] Another explanation proposes manumission — that Paul's ancestors were freed slaves and received citizenship when emancipated, with evidence suggesting this "seems most likely." [6] A third possibility notes that in 171 BC and during Pompey's time, many residents of Tarsus, including Jews, were granted citizenship. [7] **However, a definitive answer remains impossible, with all arguments remaining speculative.** [6]
