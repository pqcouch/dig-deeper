## 7. Vocabulary

**The question:** Are there words being used in ways that need investigation — big Bible words, familiar words used in a Bible-specific sense, or significant names?

**The principle:** Every field has its own terminology. Bible vocabulary often needs deliberate work. Words take their meaning from how they're used; consult a Bible dictionary and trace the word across Scripture.

### Three sub-categories

**(a) Big Bible words** — technical theological terms uncommon in modern English.

Common candidates: *redemption, propitiation, righteousness, sanctification, glory, atonement, kingdom, covenant, the Word, the Spirit, justification, election, grace, faith, holy, sin, repentance, salvation, gospel, mercy, steadfast love (ḥesed), fear of the LORD, soul, flesh, world.*

Book example: "redemption" in Col 1:13–14. "Deliverance from some evil by payment of a price." Archetype: God's rescue of Israel from Egypt. So Col 1:13–14 — rescued from Satan's dominion into Christ's kingdom, bought to belong to God.

**(b) Familiar words used in a Bible-specific way.** Words we *think* we know.

| Word | Modern English | Bible usage |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| *Hope* | "Would like; might not happen" | Certain expectation (Tit 1:2) |
| *Called* | "Invited" | Often: Christian, chosen (Jude 1); sometimes: invited but not chosen (Matt 22:14) |
| *Fear* | "Terror" | Often: awe, reverence (Ps 33:8) |
| *Love* | "Affection / romance" | Covenant loyalty (*ḥesed*); self-giving (*agapē*) |
| *Heart* | "Emotions" | The whole inner person — mind, will, affections |
| *Soul* | "Disembodied spirit" | The living person; sometimes ≈ life |
| *Flesh* | "Body" | Often: fallen humanity in its weakness (Rom 7–8) |
| *World* | "Earth / globe" | Often: humanity in rebellion (John 3:16's "world" vs 1 John 2:15's "world") |
| *Mercy* | "Pity" | Active covenant kindness; not letting off but doing good |
| *Truth* | "Factual accuracy" | Often: reality, faithfulness, the gospel itself |

**(c) Names.** Biblical names often have meaning.

| Name | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Ichabod | "no glory" (1 Sam 4:21) |
| Maher-Shalal-Hash-Baz | "quick to the plunder" (Isa 8:1) |
| Lo-Ruhamah | "not my loved one" (Hos 2:23) |
| Samuel | "God heard me" |
| Jesus / Yeshua | "the LORD saves" (Matt 1:21 makes this explicit) |
| Immanuel | "God with us" |
| Abraham | "father of a multitude" |
| Israel | "wrestles / strives with God" |
| Peter / Cephas | "rock" |

Place names too: Bethel = "house of God"; Babel = "confusion"; Jericho = "moon city" (?); Golgotha = "skull".

### Canonical history of names, places, and numbers

Etymology (what a name means) is the starting point, not the end. Names — of people, places, and significant numbers — carry their **canonical history** with them: every prior event and association the reader already knows. A later author invoking a name may be invoking an event, not (or not only) an etymology.

Two triggers to check for every significant name, place, or number:

**Trigger 1 — First canonical occurrence.** The first time a name or number appears in the canon establishes its primary meaning for all later uses. Read that passage: it is the root the author is drawing from.

**Trigger 2 — Major event association.** Some names are more closely tied to *what happened there* or *what happened to them* than to their etymology. The name becomes shorthand for the event. Both triggers may fire independently or together.

**The three questions:**
1. Where does this first appear in the canon? (Read it.)
2. What is the most significant event associated with this name? (List 1–2.)
3. Which association is this author most likely invoking — and what does it add to the argument here?

**Question 0 — the proper-noun inventory (run before triaging).** Before deciding which names deserve the check, **list every proper noun in the passage**: place names, personal names, gentilics/peoples, and significant numbers. Then triage: which are routine scenery, and which are candidates for canonical freight? The inventory is mechanical and takes a minute; skipping it is how the one hyperlink that mattered gets missed. Routine names get one line ("inventory: X, Y, Z — no canonical freight detected"); candidates get the full check.

**The load-the-story rule.** For any name-hyperlink rated *moderate* confidence or higher that would be load-bearing for the exegesis, **do not settle for a recalled association — go back and read the source narrative**, exactly as the Citation Triad's Move 1 requires for quotations. Ask of the source story: what actually happens there? What precedes and follows? What specific freight does the event carry (promise, judgement, apostasy, purchase, burial, covenant)? A hyperlink reported from memory alone is tagged `[unverified — source narrative not re-read]` and routed to Open Questions if it matters. *Example of the difference:* "Jericho = conquest city" is an association; reading Joshua 5–6 surfaces that Jericho is the firstfruits of the land, placed under ḥērem, with a curse pronounced on its rebuilder (Josh 6:26) — which is the actual live wire when Jericho reappears later in the canon.

**The outstanding-word check.** Some names carry not just an event but an **unresolved word** — a curse, vow, oath, prophecy, or promise spoken over the name and left hanging in the canon. Ask: *is there an outstanding word attached to this name that this passage activates, fulfils, or violates?* This is the strongest form of name-hyperlink because the earlier text itself sets the fuse. *Paradigm case:* 1 Kgs 16:34 — Hiel rebuilds Jericho "at the cost of his firstborn… according to the word of the LORD spoken by Joshua" (Josh 6:26). Kings runs on such fulfilment-notices; but the check applies even where the narrator does *not* flag the fulfilment — an unflagged activation is a finding, not a coincidence. Other examples: Shiloh under Jer 7's threat; Gibeon and the Saulide bloodguilt (2 Sam 21 ← Josh 9's oath); Bethel under the man of God's word (1 Kgs 13 → 2 Kgs 23:15–18).

**The name-wordplay check.** Distinct from the repetition-triggered paronomasia check below: names themselves can carry or attract sound-play in Hebrew/Greek, even when they appear only once. Three sub-questions:
1. *Does the narrative itself pun on the name?* (Nabal — "as his name is, so is he; fool is his name, and folly is with him", 1 Sam 25:25; Jacob/ʿāqab, Gen 27:36.)
2. *Does a prophet or narrator distort the name polemically?* (Bethel → Beth-aven, "house of wickedness", Hos 4:15; the cascade of place-name puns in Mic 1:10–15.)
3. *Does the name's sound interact with a keyword elsewhere in the passage?* (The N-Ḥ-Š pattern; Babel/bālal, Gen 11:9.)
Also check the **Greek carrier for NT passages**: the LXX/Greek form of a name can itself be the hyperlink — *Iēsous* is both "Jesus" and "Joshua" (Matt 1:21; Heb 4:8), and the identity is exegetically live. Confidence flag every wordplay claim; recommend lexicon verification where reaching.

**Worked examples:**

| Name / Number | First occurrence | Major event(s) | What a later reference may invoke |
|---------------|-----------------|----------------|----------------------------------|
| **Bethel** (place) | Gen 28:10–22 — Jacob's dream; God's house; the stone pillar | 1 Kgs 12:28–30 — Jeroboam's golden calf; Bethel becomes the centre of apostate worship | Amos 3–5: both Jacob's founding encounter *and* the apostasy — the place name holds both together |
| **Shiloh** (place) | Josh 18:1 — tabernacle established; Israel's worship centre | 1 Sam 4 — the ark captured; Hophni and Phinehas killed; glory departs | Jer 7:12–14: "Go to my place at Shiloh… see what I did to it." God invokes the event, not the etymology |
| **Saul** (person) | 1 Sam 9 — tall, chosen, initially humble; made king | 1 Sam 15–31 — disobedience; Spirit departs; rejected by God; tragic end | Acts 7:58–9:1: Paul introduced as *Saulos* — reader hears the rejected king before Damascus. The name change to Paul marks the reversal |
| **Elijah** (person) | 1 Kgs 17 — appears without introduction; confronts Ahab; drought | 1 Kgs 18–19 — Carmel; Sinai; "I alone am left"; taken up (2 Kgs 2) | Matt 11:14; Luke 1:17; Mal 4:5 — the name always invokes confronting Baal-worship, prophetic suffering, and anticipated return |
| **12** (number) | Gen 35:22–26 — the twelve sons of Jacob; the covenant people constituted | — | Matt 10:1–2 (twelve apostles = reconstituted Israel); Rev 21:12 (twelve gates) — twelve is never just a count |
| **40** (number) | Gen 7:4 — forty days of flood; a generation under judgement | Exod 16:35 (forty years wilderness); Deut 9:25 (forty days on the mountain) | Matt 4:2 (Jesus forty days in wilderness) — always invokes testing, probation, divine discipline |

**Confidence flagging applies here too.** A first-occurrence connection backed by a distinctive narrative parallel (like the Saul naming in Acts) is *high*. A looser numerical association with no specific narrative echo is *uncertain*.

> **See also:** Tool 11 (Quotation/Allusion). Name, place, and number allusions are a form of conceptual allusion — any finding here that rises to Tool 11 level should be cross-reported there. Tool 11's confidence scale and the Hays criteria apply.

### Operational guidance for the report

- **Verify before you report.** A recurrence claim is a claim about specific verses, so check those verses. **Search the lemma, not the surface form:** pointing, prefixes and suffixes make surface search unreliable, and that unreliability is exactly how a chain comes to be claimed at a verse that does not contain the word. If a link in a chain rests on sense rather than on the word, say so — a thematic chain and a verbal chain are different findings and must never be presented as one. (SKILL.md Phase 10.5 item (e3) is the hard gate; this is how to satisfy it.) **A *phrase* search needs one step more than a word search.** Maqqef (־) and paseq (׀) are not combining marks, so accent-stripping leaves them standing between the very words your pattern is trying to match: normalise both to spaces before matching. Two searches in the Decalogue run returned nil on texts that contained the phrase for exactly this reason, and **a nil return is indistinguishable from a real absence.** **The third cause is the ketiv.** Where the Masoretes read a word other than the one the consonants spell, the WLC prints the ketiv **unpointed** and leaves the qere to the Masorah — so a pattern spelling the qere, however carefully normalised, does not see the verse. The signal is mechanical: **a word with Hebrew consonants and no combining marks at all.** The lemma index is unaffected and carries the right lemma either way, so **any nil or short return from a phrase search is confirmed by lemma before it is reported as an absence.** This is not rare — 16.8 % of the Hebrew Bible's verses carry an unpointed form, and in Ezra 52.9 % do. Neh 9:17's וְרַב־וחסד cost a correctly-normalised whole-Bible search the book's clearest citation of Exod 34:6.
- **Verify with the corpus's own tool.** `python3 tools/find.py lemma <strongs> [Book]` lists every occurrence; `python3 tools/find.py verify <strongs> <Ref> <Ref> …` tests a claimed chain and **exits non-zero if any reference fails**, so it can gate a script. Use them. A hand-written lemma matcher is a known failure mode, not a hypothetical one: in the Ezra–Nehemiah run of 19 September 2026 one returned **zero** occurrences of חֶסֶד ("covenant-love") and **zero** of קוּם ("to arise") in a book that contains both, because the WLC lemma field carries homograph suffixes (`2617 a`, `6965 b`) that an equality test does not match. `find.py` had the right answer in one line.
- Pick **3–6 words at most** for the report — not every potentially interesting word, just the ones that materially affect the passage's meaning.
- For each: state the original-language term (if confident — see Confidence below), the English range, and what this passage's usage is.
- **Run a paronomasia check on any word repeated three or more times:** Repetition in Hebrew narrative is rarely stylistic filler. When a word recurs, ask: does its root (the three consonants underlying the Hebrew word) share its consonantal pattern with another significant word in the passage's theological register? The same root can appear in words belonging to entirely different semantic domains — the narrator may be exploiting the sound to import meaning from one domain into another. *Example:* four-fold *nĕḥōšet* (bronze) in 1 Sam 17:5–6 resonates with *nāḥāš* (serpent) through the shared N-Ḥ-Š root — the repetition is the signal; the root echo is the content.
- **Check whether key words activate a classification system:** Hebrew words that belong to well-known taxonomies (Levitical clean/unclean, wisdom vocabulary, creation vocabulary, covenant vocabulary) may activate that taxonomy's logic and surrounding concepts even when the classification system isn't explicitly in view. Note when this is happening and what it imports.
- **Confidence flagging is essential** here:
  - *High confidence:* major theological terms with well-established meaning (ḥesed, agapē, dikaiosynē).
  - *Moderate confidence:* less common terms, specific shades of meaning, or paronomasia where canonical precedent exists for the wordplay (e.g. Num 21:9 establishes *nāḥāš/nĕḥōšet* as a canonical word pair).
  - *Uncertain — consider verifying:* anything Claude is reaching for; suggest consulting a lexicon (BDAG, TDOT, NIDOTTE) or a commentary that works the Hebrew.

### Book example (Galatians 4:6)

"Because you are sons God sent the Spirit..." Paul writes "sons" to mixed-gender churches. Why not "children"? In the ancient world, only sons inherited; "full rights of sons" carried legal weight. Paul's theological point about inheritance is partly carried by the word choice "sons". Some translations change to "children" — that's a translation choice that obscures the legal/inheritance vocabulary.

### Pitfall

Assuming a modern English meaning. Assuming a word always means the same thing across the Bible. Overdoing word studies — making lexical observations that don't actually contribute to *this passage's* meaning.

## 8. Translations

**The question:** Where do English translations diverge meaningfully, and does a more literal rendering surface something a dynamic-equivalent obscures?

**The principle:** Translations sit on a spectrum from literal (word-for-word) to dynamic-equivalent (idea-for-idea). For close study, check more than one — especially a literal one if your default is dynamic.

### The spectrum

| More literal | Middle | More dynamic |
|--------------|--------|--------------|
| NASB, ESV, KJV, NKJV, LSB | NIV, CSB, NET | NLT, GNT, The Message |

### Where translations especially matter

- **Linking words** — dynamic translations sometimes drop or smooth over "for" / "therefore" / "so that".
- **Repeated words** — dynamic translations may vary the English word for stylistic reasons, obscuring repetition.
- **Idioms** — literal translations preserve them ("knew his wife"), dynamic translates them ("slept with his wife"). Literal can confuse; dynamic can over-decide.
- **Theological vocabulary** — translation choices on words like "atonement", "propitiation", "expiation", "sons" vs "children" carry weight.
- **Verbal allusions** — a translation choice can obscure or reveal an OT allusion (e.g. "heap" vs "jars" in Ps 33:7 obscures the Exodus 15 / Joshua 3 allusion).
- **Gender language** — choices about "brothers" vs "brothers and sisters", "man" vs "humanity" can either clarify or alter.
- **Tense/aspect** — Greek tenses don't map cleanly onto English; choices can obscure aspectual nuance.

### Book examples

**Romans 1:14–18 (NASB vs NIV).** NASB has a chain of *for...for...for...for...for* — a tight logical argument. NIV drops several. Especially significant: "*for* the wrath of God is revealed" at v.18 — lose the "for", lose the link between salvation and God's anger.

**John 11:5–6 (KJV/ESV vs NIV).** KJV: "Jesus loved Martha...*therefore* he abode two days." NIV: "Jesus loved Martha...*yet* he stayed two more days." The Greek supports the literal "therefore". NIV softened it because it seemed paradoxical — but the paradox is the point.

### Operational guidance for the report

- Default to ESV.
- Compare with NASB and KJV where there's **meaningful divergence** (two literal witnesses); reach for a dynamic version (NIV, NLT) only to show what a smoother rendering obscures.
- For modern translation projects, the NET Bible's translator notes (freely available) are excellent — flag if a question would benefit from consulting them.
- Flag specifically:
  - Dropped or smoothed linking words
  - Idioms where the literal/dynamic gap is significant
  - Theological vocabulary differences
  - Choices that obscure verbal allusions
- N/A is reasonable when translations are substantively identical — **but check before concluding N/A**.

### Ancient versions check

Modern English translations share a scholarly tradition; agreement among them is not independent confirmation. The ancient versions — **LXX** (Old Greek), **Vulgate** (Latin), **Peshitta** (Syriac) — translate from older textual and interpretive traditions, and a split between the ancient and modern traditions is an exegetical finding in its own right. The ancient versions often preserve a reading the modern scholarly consensus has moved away from, typically for grammatical or syntactical reasons.

**Trigger:** Run the check when *both* of these hold:
1. The modern translations compared above (ESV, NASB, and KJV) substantively agree with each other on a rendering, **and**
2. That rendering carries exegetical weight — it affects the passage's argument, a key term, or a disputed clause. (Don't run the check on renderings where nothing turns on the choice.)

**Procedure:**
1. For an OT passage, check the LXX first — it is the version the NT authors most often quote, so an LXX divergence can also explain an NT citation's wording (cross-report to Tool 11 where so). Then Vulgate and Peshitta if known.
2. For a NT passage, check the Vulgate and any patristic-era rendering Claude knows with confidence.
3. Classify the result:
   - *Ancient versions agree with modern translations:* note briefly — one sentence at most.
   - *Ancient versions diverge:* flag as `**Translation-tradition split:**`, state both readings, and say what each would mean for preaching the passage.
4. **Confidence flagging is mandatory here.** Claude's recall of LXX wording is *high* for frequently cited passages (Psalms, Isaiah, Pentateuch citations in the NT) and *moderate to uncertain* elsewhere; Vulgate and Peshitta recall is weaker again. Mark anything uncertain `*Uncertain — consider verifying:*` and suggest checking NETS (LXX) or the NET Bible notes.

**N/A discipline:** N/A only after the trigger has been considered. "No exegetically weighty rendering in this passage where the moderns agree" is a legitimate one-line reason; silence is not.

### Pitfall

Working entirely from one translation. Trusting an English interpretive choice (e.g., a "yet" instead of "therefore") that reflects the translator more than the original. Treating modern-translation agreement as settling a question the ancient versions answer differently.

> **See also:** `extensions/textual-variants.md` for cases where the question isn't translation philosophy but which Greek/Hebrew text the translation is rendering — and, for **any OT passage where the Hebrew and the Greek diverge**, its governing *three-way triage*. Classify the divergence before flagging it: **translation loss** (a Hebrew literary feature no translation carries — report under Tool 7/8, never as a split), **substantive variant** (different *Vorlage* or edition — the only category that is a `**Translation-tradition split:**`, and it has *no* default in favour of the MT), or **the NT's own text** (a Greek rendering the NT argues from — report under Tool 11, and never demote it on the ground that the Hebrew is the original).
