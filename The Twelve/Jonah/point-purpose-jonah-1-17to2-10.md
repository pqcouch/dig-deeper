## Scope decision

**Single sermon.** Jonah 1:17–2:10 is one pericope — the appointed fish framing an embedded thanksgiving psalm (~11 verses) — that hangs together as a single preaching unit, exactly as the book-overview's Preaching Units table has it. `[scope]`

**Overlap to name.** The book's structure pairs chapters 1–2 as *Panel A* (commission → flight → mercy → Jonah's response). If you are preaching Jonah in two sermons rather than four, this unit merges with 1:1–16 into a single "Panel A" sermon, and the Teaching Point would widen to *the God of sea and land who saves the fugitive he pursues*. This backbone assumes the **four-part, chapter-shaped series** (the overview's default), i.e. 1:17–2:10 as its own sermon. If you intend the two-sermon split, say so and I will rebuild at Panel scope.

## Fallen Condition Focus (FCF)

**We are quick to thank God for rescuing *us*, yet our gratitude can leave the heart unreformed — prizing the salvation we have received while staying narrow toward those we would rather God did not save.**

Anchored in the text (not imported): the psalm is liturgically perfect praise for a real deliverance, yet it conspicuously omits any repentance for the flight and even pauses to disparage "idolaters" (2:8) — immediately after a shipful of idolaters turned to Yahweh (1:16), and immediately before the same singer will resent God's mercy to Nineveh (4:2). The shared concern the passage exposes: *a rescued person can sing the right words and remain the wrong shape.* `[T]`/`[I]` (from the report's candidate FCF and Original Audience Reception)

## Teaching Point (TP)

**Salvation belongs to the LORD alone — the God who hears from the belly of Sheol and raises dead men from the pit — a rescue that reaches its depth in the greater Jonah, three days in the grave and raised to bring salvation even to outsiders.**

- **Subject:** *Whose is salvation, and how deep does it reach?*
- **Complement:** *It is wholly the LORD's — the God who raises the drowned from the pit — and it is secured, at its fullest, in the death and resurrection of the greater Jonah (the sign of Jonah, Matt 12:40).*

Built from the convergent Headline Findings — "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (2:9) as the psalm's climactic confession; the deliverance as *resurrection*-shaped (down to Sheol/the pit, 2:2, 2:6, "yet you brought up my life"); and the NT-authorised sign of Jonah. Christ is the actor, not Jonah the example. `[T]`/`[S]`

---

## Purpose Statement (PS)

**To move hearers from a grateful-but-unreformed religion — thanking God for their own rescue while their hearts stay narrow — to receiving his salvation as sheer resurrection-grace in the risen Christ, a mercy that both humbles and widens them.**

Differentiated by audience (from the report's four-audiences So What):

- *The drowning / despairing* — assurance: God hears from the belly of Sheol; no depth is beyond him (2:2, 2:7).
- *The comfortably devout* — a searching word: a thanksgiving that omits repentance and looks down on outsiders is not yet a reformed heart (2:8–9 read toward 4:2).
- *The whole church* — the summons to let gratitude for grace received become generosity with grace toward others.
- *The unbeliever* — the invitation: the God who raised a fugitive from the deep, and raised the greater Jonah from the grave, saves any who call to him from any depth.

No bare imperative: the change is grounded in what God has done (raised from the pit; raised Christ), not in the hearer's willpower. `[T]`

## Candidate Big Question

*(candidate — final wording for the downstream one-sentence-sermon step, or your own pen)*

**"When God saves you from the depths, do you let that mercy reshape your heart — or do you bank the rescue, sing the song, and stay exactly as narrow as before?"**

A shorter pulpit form: **"Has the God who saved you actually changed you?"**

## Proposed Structure (Christ-centred)

Four movements tracing the passage's own shape (the prose frame around the descent-and-ascent psalm), moving from the felt need (rescue) through the truth (salvation is wholly the LORD's, resurrection-shaped) to the response (receive it as a heart-reforming mercy, and rest in the risen Christ):

1. **The rescue you did not engineer** — *1:17; 2:1.* God *appoints* a fish; grace arrives before a word is prayed, and the fugitive is saved from the sea-death his flight earned. (Felt need surfaced: we are the drowning, rescued by a hand not our own.)
2. **The prayer from the pit** — *2:2–7.* Down to Sheol, the roots of the mountains, the barred gates of death — "*yet* you brought up my life from the pit." God hears from the depths; the deliverance is resurrection-shaped. (Truth established: salvation is the LORD's, and it raises the dead.)
3. **The confession that judges the singer** — *2:8–9.* "Salvation belongs to the LORD!" — true, beautiful, and prayed by a man who has not repented of his flight and still looks down on "idolaters." The right words from an unreformed heart. (The FCF pressed: is my gratitude reshaping me, or leaving me narrow?)
4. **Vomited onto dry land — and the greater Jonah** — *2:10 (+ Matt 12:40).* The inglorious deliverance to the "dry land" the Creator commands; three days and three nights as the sign of the One buried and raised, in whom "Salvation belongs to the LORD" is finally secured — for the outsider too. (Response landed: rest in the risen Christ; let received mercy widen you.)

The arc runs felt-need → truth → response, and lands the sermon on the PS: not "pray like Jonah," but *receive the salvation that is wholly the LORD's, in the risen greater Jonah, and be reshaped by it.*

## Keep Honest

Guardrails for *this* sermon, drawn from the report's Preaching Pitfalls and the anti-moralism gate:

1. **Don't preach the psalm as an unironic devotional high point.** Honour it as real thanksgiving — *and* handle its omission of repentance and its swipe at idolaters (2:8). The book frames it ironically (2:9 → 4:2); flattening the irony misreads the author. Let chapter 2 and chapter 4 interpret each other.
2. **Don't let the fish (or the "three days" arithmetic) swallow the sermon.** The species is irrelevant and the genre is narrative reporting a miracle; the fish is God's *rescue*, and the "three days" points to the resurrection sign, not marine biology.
3. **Don't preach it as a lament or a plea.** It is a *thanksgiving* (a *todah*) for a rescue already given — past-tense and confident. Reading it as a desperate cry inverts the tone and the theology.
4. **Don't sentimentalise "you brought up my life from the pit."** The imagery is *Sheol* and the *barred gates of death* — irreversible death reversed by God, which is why the NT reads it toward resurrection. Not "God helped me through a hard patch."
5. **Anti-moralism (the hard gate).** The payoff is *not* "pray like Jonah in your crisis" (imitation of a human figure). The psalm's words are Scripture's own and usable in prayer, but the *character* is a warning, and the *rescue* is grace. Keep Christ the subject — the risen greater Jonah who secured the salvation the psalm confesses — not Jonah the example. ✓ *Gate passed: TP makes God/Christ the actor; PS is grounded in the resurrection; the structure lands on Christ, not imitation.*
