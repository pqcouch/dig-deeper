# Book Overview: Nahum

**Translation:** ESV (Hebrew versification differs slightly — English 1:15 = Hebrew 2:1)
**Genre:** OT prophecy — a single sustained oracle against a nation (Nineveh), opening with a theophany-hymn and building through battle-poetry to a funeral taunt; 7th-century. Seventh in the Book of the Twelve.
**Mode:** Draft (text-first; no dig-deeper runs in hand)
**Date:** 29 July 2026
**Sweep status:** No dig-deeper runs yet — Draft to be tested by them

Warrant tags: `[T]` text-derivable · `[I]` inference from the text · `[S]` supplied by a secondary source, held provisionally.

## Presenting Situation

**Prophet:** Nahum of Elkosh (1:1) — his home town unlocated (Galilee, Judah, and Assyria have all been proposed). His name, *Naḥûm*, means "**comfort / consolation**" (root *n-ḥ-m*), which is precisely what his oracle of Nineveh's doom *is* — comfort to the oppressed. `[T]`/`[I]`

**Date:** framed by two events the book names or assumes — the fall of Thebes/No-Amon (663 BC), spoken of as *past* (3:8–10), and the fall of Nineveh (612 BC), spoken of as *coming*. So Nahum prophesies in the late 7th century, under Assyrian domination, most likely in the decades just before 612. `[T]`/`[S]`

**Audience:** Judah — a small, oppressed vassal living under the shadow of the Assyrian empire, the superpower that had destroyed the Northern Kingdom (722), deported peoples, and terrorised the ancient Near East with calculated cruelty. `[T]`/`[S]`

**Occasion:** the long Assyrian tyranny and the theodicy it forced — will the empire that crushed the nations and mocked their gods go on for ever unpunished? Is the LORD really sovereign over Assyria? `[I]`

**Pressure:** the justice of God against overwhelming, entrenched evil. A people ground down by a brutal empire needs to know that the LORD, not Nineveh, rules — and that the tyrant will fall. `[I]`

**Purpose:** to announce the certain, complete, and *just* fall of Nineveh as the LORD's vengeance on a cruel empire, and thereby to comfort Judah with the assurance that "the LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; he knows those who take refuge in him" (1:7) — the same God who "will by no means clear the guilty" (1:3). `[I]`

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|--------|----------|----------|
| 1:1 | Superscription | "An oracle concerning Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum of Elkosh" |
| 1:2–8 | The theophany-hymn | *Who the LORD is* — jealous and avenging, slow to anger yet not clearing the guilty (1:3), Lord of whirlwind, sea, and mountains, "good, a stronghold" to those who take refuge, but "a complete end" to his adversaries (1:7–8). A partial alphabetic **acrostic** |
| 1:9–2:2 | Doom and comfort, alternating | Address swings between Nineveh ("What do you plot against the LORD? He will make a complete end," 1:9) and Judah ("Behold… the feet of him who brings **good news**… keep your feasts," 1:15; "the LORD is restoring the majesty of Jacob," 2:2) |
| 2:3–13 | The fall of Nineveh (battle-poem) | A cinematic assault — chariots flashing, the river-gates opened, the palace dissolved, plunder, the lions' den emptied — sealed by "Behold, I am against you, declares the LORD of hosts" (2:13) |
| 3:1–19 | Woe to the bloody city | The charges (bloodshed, lies, plunder, sorcery, 3:1–4); shameful exposure (3:5–7); the precedent of Thebes (3:8–11); the futility of Nineveh's defences (3:12–17); the closing taunt — "all who hear… clap their hands over you, for upon whom has not come your unceasing evil?" (3:18–19) |

**Device: from the universal to the particular, then from assault to dirge.** The opening hymn (1:2–8) establishes *who God is* — avenging and refuge together — and everything after is that character applied to Nineveh. `[T]` The first movement (1:9–2:2) **alternates address** — doom for Nineveh, comfort for Judah — so the reader hears the fall of the tyrant *as* the deliverance of the oppressed. The middle (2:3–13) is vivid, present-tense battle-poetry; the last (3:1–19) is a funeral taunt over a city already as good as dead. Two refrains bind it: "**a complete end**" (*kālāh*, 1:8, 9) and "**Behold, I am against you**, declares the LORD of hosts" (2:13; 3:5). The **lion imagery** (2:11–12) turns Assyria's own royal symbol against it. `[T]`

## Microscript

*The LORD — jealous and avenging, slow to anger but by no means clearing the guilty, and yet a stronghold to all who take refuge in him — will make a complete and final end of Nineveh, the bloody, plundering city; so the news of the tyrant's fall is good news to an oppressed people: the God who treads the whirlwind and dries the sea rules Assyria too.* Keywords: **avenging / vengeance** (*nāqam*, 1:2); **"a stronghold… refuge"** (1:7) held against **"a complete end"** (1:8); **"I am against you"** (2:13; 3:5); **"good news"** (1:15). Pulse: God's character → Nineveh's fall → Judah's comfort. `[T]`

Test against the movements: the hymn defines the God (1:2–8); the alternation applies him as doom-and-comfort (1:9–2:2); the battle-poem enacts the fall (2:3–13); the taunt seals it (3:1–19). Each serves the spine — and the spine's twin note (wrath on the cruel, refuge for the trusting) is the book's confession about God.

## Intertextual Map

Nahum is compact but pointed in its canonical connections — above all its deliberate counterpart-relationship with **Jonah** (the same city) and its citation of the **grace formula's justice-clause**.

| Source | Where in Nahum | What the use does | Live? | Confidence |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|-------|------------|
| **Exodus 34:6–7** (the grace formula — the *justice* half) | 1:3 | "The LORD is slow to anger and great in power, and the LORD **will by no means clear the guilty**" — Nahum invokes the clause Exodus attaches to judgment | **live** (Twelve) | high |
| **Jonah** (Nineveh spared) | the whole book (Nineveh judged) | Nahum is the deliberate **counterpart to Jonah** in the scroll — mercy to Nineveh (Jonah 4:2, the formula's *mercy* clause) answered by judgment on Nineveh (Nahum 1:3, its *justice* clause), a century+ later | **live** (Twelve) | high |
| **Isaiah 52:7** (the feet of the herald of good news / peace) | 1:15 (Heb 2:1) | "Behold, upon the mountains, the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace!" — the herald of the tyrant's fall / God's reign | **live** | high |
| **Theophany tradition** (Exod 19; Deut 33; Ps 18; Hab 3) | 1:3–6 | The LORD coming in storm, the mountains quaking, the sea dried — the language of divine advent for judgment | — | moderate–high |
| **Assyrian lion iconography** | 2:11–12 | "Where is the lions' den…?" — Assyria's royal lion-symbol turned into the image of its own destruction | — | moderate–high |
| **Thebes / No-Amon** (663 BC) | 3:8–10 | The recent fall of Egypt's great city cited as precedent: mighty Thebes fell, so shall Nineveh | — | high |

**Forward into the New Testament:** **Nahum 1:15 / Isaiah 52:7 → Romans 10:15** — "How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the good news!" Paul reads the herald-of-good-news motif as the gospel proclamation. `[S]`

**Live sources for Move 2:** the **grace formula** (Exod 34:6, whose justice-half Nahum carries — a thread the Joel, Jonah, and Micah work has been tracking across the Twelve), the **good-news / herald** motif (Isa 52:7), and the **Book of the Twelve itself** (the Jonah↔Nahum Nineveh pairing). `[T]`/`[I]`

**Relation to the Twelve frame.** Nahum completes two of the scroll's cross-book patterns: the **Jonah↔Nahum pairing** (mercy then judgment on Nineveh) the orientation-frame flagged, and the **grace-formula conversation** — Nahum 1:3 is the *justice* voice alongside Joel 2:13 (hopeful mercy), Jonah 4:2 (resentful mercy), and Micah 7:18 (pardon). `[T]`/`[I]`

## Christological Trajectory

Nahum has no messianic figure and no human hero; it points to Christ by **trajectory and contrast**, through the character of God it proclaims.

- **The herald of good news (1:15 / Isa 52:7 → Rom 10:15).** The announcement of the oppressor's fall and God's reign is, in trajectory, the gospel — the good news of the defeat of the ultimate tyranny (sin, death, and the powers) and the proclamation of peace. *Confidence: high (the motif is NT-cited).* `[T]`/`[S]`
- **The refuge and the wrath, met at the cross.** "The LORD is good, a stronghold… he knows those who take refuge in him" (1:7) stands beside "he will by no means clear the guilty" (1:3) and "a complete end" (1:8). At the cross these meet: the wrath Nahum announces is borne by Christ for those who take refuge in him, and poured out on the impenitent at the last (Rev 6:16–17; 19:11–21). *Confidence: high (trajectory).* `[I]`
- **The just Judge of the nations.** The God who rules Assyria and repays "unceasing evil" (3:19) is the one to whom the Father has given all judgment (John 5:22; Acts 17:31). *Confidence: moderate–high.* `[I]`

**Moralism / mishandling check.** Nahum is not a "be like X" moralism magnet (there is no human to imitate) — its characteristic dangers run the other way. One is *embarrassment* — treating divine wrath as sub-Christian and quietly skipping the book. The other is *weaponisation* — reading it as licence for human vengeance, nationalism, or gloating over enemies. The corrective: the vengeance is **God's, not ours** ("Vengeance is mine," Deut 32:35; Rom 12:19), and it is *good news to the oppressed* precisely because it is *his*. Preach the God who is both refuge and avenger, whose justice comforts the crushed and warns the cruel, and who has borne that justice himself at the cross — not a template for our own retaliation. `[I]`

## Preaching Traps

1. **Embarrassment at divine wrath.** *Bites at:* the whole book. *Corrective:* Nahum is the necessary complement to Jonah's mercy — God's justice against entrenched cruelty is *good news* to the oppressed; a God who never judged evil would not be good.

2. **Weaponising it for human vengeance or nationalism.** *Bites at:* chs 2–3. *Corrective:* the vengeance is the LORD's, not ours (Rom 12:19); the oppressed are comforted, not commissioned to retaliate. Guard against gloating and against mapping Nineveh onto a modern enemy.

3. **"Ancient history with no present force."** *Bites at:* the whole book. *Corrective:* its theology — God's sovereignty over empires, his justice, his refuge (1:7) — is perennial; the fall of Nineveh is a case, not the point.

4. **Missing the Jonah pairing.** *Bites at:* 1:2–8. *Corrective:* the same city was spared in Jonah and judged in Nahum — mercy has a limit for the unrepentant, and both books together display the whole character of God (Exod 34:6–7).

5. **Losing 1:7 amid the wrath.** *Bites at:* 1:2–8. *Corrective:* "the LORD is good, a stronghold… he knows those who take refuge in him" sits *inside* the judgment hymn — the refuge and the wrath are one revelation, aimed at opposite responses.

6. **The shameful-exposure imagery (3:5–6).** *Bites at:* 3:5–7. *Corrective:* the metaphor of public stripping is ancient shaming-of-a-city language; handle it with pastoral care for hearers wounded by sexual violence, naming it as judgment-imagery rather than dwelling on it.

## Preaching Units

Three chapters, 47 verses — a short series of three or four. ⭐ HIGH = load-bearing.

| Verses | Working title | Weight |
|--------|---------------|--------|
| 1:1–8 | Who the LORD is: avenger and refuge | ⭐ HIGH |
| 1:9–2:2 | Good news: the tyrant falls, Judah keeps its feasts | ⭐ HIGH (1:15) |
| 2:3–13 | The fall of the bloody city | Standard |
| 3:1–19 | Woe to Nineveh: no easing your hurt | Standard |

A four-part series maps cleanly; a three-part version could combine chs 2–3 as "the fall." The pillars are the character-hymn (1:1–8) and the good-news oracle (1:9–2:2).
