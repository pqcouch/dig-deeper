**2. When did Obadiah prophesy?**

*a. Some possible early dates*

Some scholars place Obadiah's prophecy as early as the ninth century bc, in the reign of King Ahab, following a Jewish tradition found in the Talmud. Others suggest that the background to Obadiah's ministry might be the various attacks on Judah by the Edomites and others in the reign of King Jehoram (852--841 bc).^5^[^1] Jehoram, the eldest son of Jehoshaphat, succeeded his father in 852 bc. He established his position in Judah by putting to death his brothers and other princes of Israel. He reigned in Jerusalem for only eight years, married a daughter of Ahab, and 'did evil in the eyes of the Lord'.^6^[^2]

> However, it was during Jehoram's short reign that the Edomites rebelled against Judah and set up their own rival king. Jehoram attacked the Edomites with his army, and at one stage of the battle found himself surrounded by the Edomites. He managed to escape on that occasion but the chronicler noted, 'To this day Edom has been in rebellion against Judah' ([*2 Chr. 21:10*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21.10)). Could this be the background to Obadiah's ministry?

> It's also possible that Obadiah ministered at the time when the Edomites attacked Judah in the reign of King Ahaz (735--715 bc).^7^[^3] The chronicler comments: 'At that time King Ahaz sent to the King of Assyria for help. The Edomites had again come and attacked Judah and carried away prisoners, while the Philistines had raided towns in the foothills and in the Negev of Judah ... The Lord had humbled Judah because of Ahaz King of Israel, for he had promoted wickedness in Judah and had been most unfaithful to the Lord' ([*2 Chr. 28:16--19*](https://ref.ly/2Ch28.16-19)).

> There is no doubt that the Edomites were a constant thorn in the side of God's people in Judah, and Obadiah would have been aware of that. The prophets lived in the real world, where there is constant conflict and threat. But although it is possible that Obadiah lived and worked in these earlier situations, it seems much more likely that he ministered at a later date.

*b. A probable later date*

The most likely background for Obadiah's ministry was the attack on Jerusalem and its destruction in 586 bc. This was carried out by the Babylonians and their allies, with the Edomites playing a major part. This certainly fits Obadiah's description of the fall of Jerusalem in his prophecy, and his dismay at the role that the Edomites, their own kinsfolk, played. This is how Obadiah described the Edomite involvement: *On the day you stood aloof while strangers carried off his wealth and foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem, you were like one of them*. A little later he says: *You should not march through the gates of my people in the day of their disaster ... nor seize their wealth in the day of their disaster. You should not wait at the cross-roads to cut down their fugitives, nor hand over their survivors in the day of their trouble* ([*11--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob11-14)).

> Those words of Obadiah are so vivid and immediate that some believe that Obadiah prophesied soon after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 bc, while those events were still fresh in his mind. The Psalm of the Exiles ([*Psalm 137*](https://ref.ly/Ps137)), which began with the words 'By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept when we remembered Zion,' goes on to say: 'Remember, O Lord, what the Edomites did on the day Jerusalem fell. "Tear it down," they cried, "tear it down to its foundations." '^8^[^4]

> We don't know whether Obadiah remained in Jerusalem or sat with the exiles in Babylon. But we do know that he shared the grief of those who mourned for Jerusalem and longed to see God's name and reputation honoured and vindicated; and it is clear that the part that the Edomites played was deeply embedded in the collective memory of God's people, and in the heart of Obadiah.

> Obadiah's prophecy foretells the future wiping out of the Edomites as a nation ([*8--10*](https://ref.ly/Ob8-10)). So it is highly likely that Obadiah focused on such a message only after a calamitous event such as the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 bc, and the heavy involvement of the Edomites in siding with the enemies of Judah. The fact that the book of Obadiah comes before Nahum and Zephaniah in the Bible may be no more than a reflection of those traditions that speculated on an earlier, but less probable, date for Obadiah's ministry.

§ 2. THE DATE OF THE BOOK

> The result of the literary criticism of Ob. necessitates the determination of the time of the various component parts of the book. In the absence of all external data, we must rely on internal evidence.

> The two points that have always been used for the fixing of the date are also important for us: the description of the fall of Jerusalem in vv. [*10--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob10-14), and Ob.'s relation to [*Je. 49*](https://ref.ly/Je49).

> Since the date of [*Je. 49:7 ff.*](https://ref.ly/Je49.7-39) was regarded as definitely fixed in the 4th year of Jehoiakim (*cf*. [*25:1 ff.*](https://ref.ly/Je25.1-38)) those who believed in the priority of Ob. connected the capture of Jerusalem, in spite of all difficulties, either with the raid of the Philistines and Arabians under Jehoram ([*2 Ch. 21:16 f.*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21.16-17)), or with the campaign of Joash of Israel against Amaziah ([*2 K. 14:13 f.*](https://ref.ly/2Ki14.13-14), [*2 Ch. 25:23 f.*](https://ref.ly/2Ch25.23-24)). They could not identify it with the capture by the Chaldeans in 586 b.c., though this was, as some acknowledged, the most natural reference. Those who believed in the priority of Je. referred it, of course, to 586 b.c. For them it was merely a question whether Ob. was exilic or postexilic. Also those who believed that both Je. and Ob. had quoted from an older oracle could put Ob. in the exilic or postexilic period. And when the prophecies against the nations (chs. [*46 ff.*](https://ref.ly/Je46)) came to be quite generally regarded as non-Jeremian, it was possible also for those who believed in the priority of Ob. to identify the fall of Jerusalem with that of 586 b.c. and to place Ob. in or after the exile.

> The most natural identification of this capture of Jerusalem is the one in 586 b.c. by the Chaldeans, when acc. to [*La. 4:21 f.*](https://ref.ly/La4.21-22), [*Ez. 25:12--14*](https://ref.ly/Eze25.12-14), [*30:5*](https://ref.ly/Eze30.5), [*35:1 ff.*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.1-15), [*137:7*](https://ref.ly/Ps137.7), [*3 Esd. 4:45*](https://ref.ly/1Esd4.45), Edom acted very cruelly toward the Judeans. It is true that neither the Chaldeans nor the destruction of the temple nor the deportation of the whole people are explicitly mentioned. But the situation is well defined, and critical opinion is more and more agreed on the reference to 586 b.c. Moreover, the fact that a quotation from a Jeremian dirge ([*Je. 38:22*](https://ref.ly/Je38.22)), which dated from the last days of Jerusalem, is incorporated in v. [*7*](https://ref.ly/Ob7) shows that 586 b.c. had passed and that the fall of Jerusalem described here cannot refer to a pre-exilic situation. Nor can a reference to a later conquest be maintained. Winckler connected the verses with a postexilic destruction of Jerusalem about 500 b.c., but such a destruction cannot be proved. And the conquest of Antiochus IV is excluded, because the book of Joel, which was not as late as the 2d cent. b.c., presupposes Ob. (*v. i*.) Our *terminus a quo* is thus 586 b.c.

> The description of the calamity which had befallen Edom is suggestive. The enemies who had driven them to the border of their land and who had plundered them, had formerly been good friends and allies, and it was only by treachery that they had succeeded. We know from [*Diodorus Siculus (19, 94)*](https://ref.ly/logosref/Diodorus$5Fsiculus.D._S.,_Hist._19.94) that in 312 b.c. the Nabateans were in possession of Edom's ancient seats, for it was *vs.* the Nabateans in Petra that Antigonus fought at that time. These Nabateans had therefore taken Mt. Seir before the end of the 4th cent. and had driven Edom northward out of her old territories into the Negeb. How long before 312 b.c. they had succeeded in conquering it, we do not know. But there is every reason to believe that the catastrophe which had befallen Edom in the first half of the 5th cent. and which is described in [*Mal. 1:1 f.*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.1-2) was due to a defeat by the Nabateans. However, [*Mal. 1:l f.*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.1-2) does not imply that Edom had been driven out of its ancient territory. They were still undaunted in Mal.'s time and confidently expected to retrieve their losses. We.[^5], following Vatke, suggests that Ob.'s prophetic interpretation of Edom's fall was connected with [*Mal. 1:1 f.*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.1-2) and that it dealt with one of the stages of the Arabian invasion, though not necessarily with the same as [*Mal. 1*](https://ref.ly/Mal1). We have reason to believe that these invasions began as early as the first half of the 6th cent. and that the occupation of the Negeb by Edom shortly after 586 b.c. (*cf.* [*Ez. 35:10*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.10), [*12*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.12), [*36:5*](https://ref.ly/Eze36.5)) was due in some degree to the pressure exerted upon Edom by the influx of the Arabians from the desert. Of course, if the description of Ob. is regarded as fairly accurate and if the driving *unto the border* is taken seriously, it would seem that Ob. wrote after Malachi rather than before. And so We.[^6] and Now. date him. Marti, Cor., Du. put him before Mal.

> But does not the detailed character of the description of the events connected with the fall of Jerusalem make the impression of having been written by one who had first-hand knowledge, and does not the impassioned manner in which it is given betray an actual participant? If this objection is sustained we cannot go beyond the last quarter of the 6th cent. And we should have to refer the invasion of the enemies in Mt. Seir to one of the earlier phases, perhaps during the exile, and we should also have to assume that the Edomites were able to regain their territory from which they had been driven acc. to [*Ob. 7*](https://ref.ly/Ob7), for in [*Mal. 1:1 f.*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.1-2) they are in Mt. Seir. This is not an easy assumption in view of the character of Mt. Seir, but it is not impossible. The detailed and impassioned character of the description may, however, be accounted for in another way. The story of those unforgettable days, when the holy city fell, must have been told in Jerusalem again and again in all its details. Who knows whether in Ob.'s own, or in a related or befriended family, there was not a tradition of ancestors who had either been robbed or betrayed or killed by the Edomites at that time? As the story was told the vivid oriental imagination of Ob. was filled with all those awful scenes, and made them so real that his heart burned with anger and sorrow. And so when he thought of that day, he seemed to go through those varied scenes himself and to witness Edom's barbarities. It is quite reasonable to account thus for vv. [*12--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob12-14), and to assign Ob.'s date after Mal. in the 5th cent., to which the description in vv. [*5--7*](https://ref.ly/Ob5-7) points so strongly.

> This implies that the older oracle from which Ob. quoted antedates the 5th cent. It represents Edom as still living in the rocky fastnesses of Mt. Seir, on whose impregnable character it relies for safety, and there is no hint that it has already suffered serious reverses. And from the absence of all reference to Edom's behaviour toward Judah in 586 b.c. it would appear that the exile is not yet presupposed. If we knew which nations attacked Edom, we might perhaps be able to determine the time more definitely, but no hint about their identity is given. The older oracle may also have had the Arabians in mind, but we do not know.

> In the appendix, vv. [*15a*](https://ref.ly/Ob15), [*16--18*](https://ref.ly/Ob16-18), no historical situation is indicated which might help us fix its date more exactly. The *terminus a quo* is, of course, the latter part of the 5th cent. after the completion of [*Ob. 1--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob1-14), [*15b*](https://ref.ly/Ob15). And the *terminus ad quem* is fixed by the date of the book Joel, which is dependent on Ob. The likeness of passages in Jo. to Ob. is limited to certain phrases (*cf.* [*Ob. 10*](https://ref.ly/Ob10) with [*Jo. 4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19); [*11*](https://ref.ly/Ob11) with [*4:3*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.3); [*15*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.15) with [*4:14*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.14), [*1:15*](https://ref.ly/Joe1.15), [*4:4*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.4), [*7*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.7); [*17*](https://ref.ly/Ob17) with [*3:5*](https://ref.ly/Joe2.32), [*4:17*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.17)). But the comparison shows that Jo. knew not only the older book but also the appendix. The fact that he states in [*3:5*](https://ref.ly/Joe2.32) that he quotes an older oracle, which occurs literally thus in [*Ob. 17*](https://ref.ly/Ob17), makes it almost certain that he used Ob. And since the phrase מהמם occurs with him also in a passage against Edom ([*4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19)) and the infrequent phrase ידו גורל ([*4:3*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.3)) is found also in Ob., and since Jo. adds to the phrase והיה קדש ([*4:17*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.17)) a comment, the case is decided for the priority of Ob. If the date of Jo. is correctly placed between 400 and 350 b.c. this appendix must have been added some time before or soon after the beginning of the 4th cent.

> Vv. [*19--21*](https://ref.ly/Ob19-21) point in the same direction. If the list in vv. [*19*](https://ref.ly/Ob19), [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) were a description of territory which Judah had already taken, it would be different. The passage would then belong to the time of Alexander Jannæus (103--76 b.c.), where Eich.[^7] actually placed it. For then all this was practically realised. Edom had been definitely conquered by John Hyrcanus, Galilee by Aristobulus I, Philistia, Moab and Gilead by Alexander Jannæus. But if there were no other reasons, the simple fact that vv. [*19*](https://ref.ly/Ob19), [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) are an expression of hope and not a description of fact excludes this. Knowing that these verses must be later than b.c. 400 we might think of the Maccabean time, when patriotism flamed up once more and when the hope of the reconquest of the old dominion seemed not unjustifiable (We.[^8], Marti, Bart.[^9], Hpt.). But [*Ecclus. 49:10*](https://ref.ly/Sir49.10) is not favourable to so late a date. And the mention of the Samaritans and Ammonites, as well as of the Philistines and Edomites, points to the age of Nehemiah or soon after. So does also the manner in which the hope of the reconquest of the ancient territory is expressed.

> As a matter of historical interest Hi.'s interpretation deserves to be recorded. He thought the oracle was written in 312 b.c. in Egypt, when Antigonus ordered the expedition against Petra and Mt. Seir. The author was a Jew who had been carried off to Egypt by Ptolemy Lagi. The difficult החל הזה in v. [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) Hi. referred to the *fortress* in Egypt to which the exiles had been carried. But this date is impossible because in 312 b.c. not the Edomites but the Nabateans occupied Petra and Mt. Seir.

**Historical Background to Obadiah's Prophecies**

> Even then we must ask whether the circumstances apply to the delivery of prophetic oracles or to their transcription and incorporation into the written document before us. For example, although the superscription to the book of Isaiah invites us to read the entire book as a collection of oracles by the prophet who lived at the end of the eighth century BC, it is conceivable that the present literary work, especially chapters [*40--66*](https://ref.ly/Is40-66), was produced in the context of the Judeans' return from exile in the middle of the sixth century BC, when the significance of these earlier prophecies was realized.

> Furthermore, we may need to distinguish between the prophet responsible for the oracles in the book and the person responsible for the book as we have it. Although it may be argued that Ezekiel himself was involved in the transcription, collection, and arrangement of the oracles in the book named after him,[^10] the third person introduction to Obadiah, "the vision of Obadiah," may suggest someone other than the prophet himself produced the book as we have it. We may speculate that the book was produced at a time when Edomites were a problem for those who made up the covenant community in and around Jerusalem. Although Edom as a state seems to have come to an end in mid-sixth century BC, the Edomite people did not disappear until the end of the Persian period (late fourth century BC), when they were replaced by Nabatean Arabs. The reference to the Edomites (as Esau) in Malachi, which reflects conditions in the mid-fifth century BC, suggests that at this time the descendants of Esau were still recognizable, even though the land of Edom had become "a wasteland and ... \[a\] desert for jackals" ([*Mal 1:3*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.3)).

> Indeed, the Edomites' encroachment on Judean territory is reflected in the name Idumea ([*Mark 3:8*](https://ref.ly/Mk3.8)), which referred to a region in the Negev south of Judah.[^11] This suggests that the messages of the prophet would have offered hope to the Judeans long after Obadiah had delivered these oracles. Accordingly, the present written record could have been produced any time from about 550 to 350 BC. Whether or not the book contains all of Obadiah's preaching we will never know. The present text may serve as a representative sampling edited for literary purposes---though the original oral flavor is still strong.

> Whatever the date of the composition of the book, the oracles contained therein come from an earlier time. However, as is common, scholars are not agreed on the date or circumstances of Obadiah's preaching. Six main theories have been proposed.[^12]

> \(1\) Early ninth century BC. In Jewish tradition the present prophet is identified with the steward in the court of Ahab with the same name ([*1 Kgs 18:1--15*](https://ref.ly/1Ki18.1-15)).[^13] [*Second Chronicles 20*](https://ref.ly/2Ch20) links Jehoshaphat, the Judean royal counterpart to Ahab in Samaria, with a conflict against the Transjordanian states, including Mount Seir (= Edom, [*20:10*](https://ref.ly/2Ch20.10)). However, Ahab's steward is never mentioned in this context, nor is there any hint of a threat to Jerusalem.

> \(2\) Mid-ninth century BC.[^14] According to [*2 Kgs 8:20--22*](https://ref.ly/2Ki8.20-22) (cf. [*2 Chr 21:8--20*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21.8-20)), foreigners invaded Judah and carried off the palace treasures and all the members of King Jehoram's family except Jehoahaz ([*2 Chr 21:17*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21.17)). Although the narrator uses the same word for "taking captive" (שׁבה) as we find in [*Obad 11*](https://ref.ly/Ob11), the text is silent not only on the involvement of Edomites (the invaders were Philistines and Arabs), but also on wholesale exile of the population.

> \(3\) Mid-eighth century BC. [*Second Chronicles 28*](https://ref.ly/2Ch28) reports that during the reign of Ahaz Syria (Aram) and the forces of Israel to the north invaded Judah, resulting in a great slaughter and the removal of 200,000 captives and a great deal of booty to Samaria (vv. [*5--8*](https://ref.ly/2Ch28.5-8)), and that Edomites and Philistines invaded Judah from the south, seizing territory in the Negev and the Shephelah and taking many Judeans captive. However, the Edomites seemed to have remained in the south and are never associated with an attack on Jerusalem.

> \(4\) Mid-sixth century BC. Assuming that Obadiah ministered in Judah in the wake of the sacking of Jerusalem in 586 BC and in anticipation of the fall of Edom, most scholars associate the prophecies of Obadiah with those horrific events. This is my view, which I will defend in moment.

> \(5\) Mid-fourth century BC. A few scholars locate the prophecies considerably later to a context when Judah was under the rule of the Persians, perhaps near the time of Malachi or Nehemiah. Some associate the attack on Edom predicted in [*Obad 1--10*](https://ref.ly/Ob1-10) with the Nabatean Arab invasion of Edom. Others dehistoricize the book, interpreting "Edom" as a code word for the enemies of Israel in general. Ehud Ben Zvi argues that the target audience consists of a small group of highly educated literati in the Achaemenid province of Yehud, who could appreciate "puns on words, plays on sounds, ambiguities, indeterminacies and the like," and who looked forward to an ideal future when Israel's enemies would be punished and their status among the nations would be restored. Whether Obadiah was an historical prophet or a literary creation, those who produced and (re)read the book served as brokers of divine knowledge for the illiterate.[^15] While it is possible that "Edom" could serve as a symbol for all Israel's enemies, the prophecies in this book seem to derive from a real person responding to real historical events.[^16]

> \(6\) A combination of ([*4*](https://ref.ly/logosres/exegcomm31ob2ed?pos=INTRO1.1%3AS4)) and ([*5*](https://ref.ly/logosres/exegcomm31ob2ed?pos=INTRO1.1%3AS5)). Some agree that [*Obad 1--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob1-14), [*15b*](https://ref.ly/Ob15) should be dated in the exilic period, but treat vv. [*15a*](https://ref.ly/Ob15) and [*16--21*](https://ref.ly/Ob16-21) as late addition from the Persian or even Hellenistic period. Barton compares this section with [*Joel 3:1--4:21*](https://ref.ly/Joe2.28-3.21)\[[*2:28--3:21*](https://ref.ly/Joe2.28-3.21)\], which also involves "a generalized eschatological prophecy of the 'day of YHWH.' " Here all the nations are forced to drink the cup of divine wrath imposed on Edom, and Israel and Judah are not only restored to their former territories, but their territories are also enlarged.[^17] However, no details of vv. [*15a*](https://ref.ly/Ob15) and [*16--21*](https://ref.ly/Ob16-21) demand such a late date.[^18] Furthermore, that Obadiah should generalize Edom's judgment to that of all Israel's enemies accords with common prophetic practice, viewing specific immediate events as paradigmatic of universal truth.

> As suggested above, the prophecies of Obadiah are best dated to the exilic period, specifically the period between the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC and Edom's demise at the hands of the Babylonians in 553 BC. The terminus a quo (earliest possible date) is established by the image the prophet paints of Jerusalem and her population. Verse [*11*](https://ref.ly/Ob11) speaks of a city that has been invaded by foreigners (נָכְרִים) and its wealth carried off; competing forces were casting lots for the booty. Verses [*12--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob12-14) characterize that event as a day of misfortune (נֵכֶר, [*12b*](https://ref.ly/Ob12)), ruin, perishing (אָבֹד, [*12d*](https://ref.ly/Ob12)), distress (צָרָה, [*12f*](https://ref.ly/Ob12-13), [*14d*](https://ref.ly/Ob14)), doom (אֵד, [*13b*](https://ref.ly/Ob13), [*13f*](https://ref.ly/Ob13-14)), and disaster (רָעָה, [*13c*](https://ref.ly/Ob13)). Verse [*16*](https://ref.ly/Ob16) speaks of that day as "drinking" the cup of divine fury. The image of restoration in v. [*19*](https://ref.ly/Ob19) assumes that the territories of Ephraim, Samaria, and Gilead have been lost (to the Assyrians in 732 and 722 BC). While the "exiles of \[this host\] belonging to the people of Israel" in v. [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) could refer to other invasions, an allusion to the Babylonian exile seems the most natural reading. Obadiah does not refer explicitly to the destruction of the temple, but the reference to Zion as a place of refuge and a holy place alludes to the shattered hopes of the Judeans, who had viewed the temple as a symbol of security and YHWH's unqualified support in 596 BC.

> The image of Edom in Obadiah reinforces the link with the collapse of the Judean state at the hands of the Babylonians. Although the conflict between the descendants of Esau and Jacob has ancient roots and is well documented elsewhere ([*Amos 1:11--12*](https://ref.ly/Am1.11-12); [*Ezek 25:12--14*](https://ref.ly/Eze25.12-14); [*35:5a*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.5)), so is Edom's response to Judah in the face of her deepest crisis.[^19] Ezekiel accuses Edom of handing over the people of Israel to the sword at the time of their calamity (אֵד) and their final punishment (עֲוֹן קֵץ, [*35:5*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.5)), of claiming the emptied lands of Judah and Israel as their own (v. [*10*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.10)), of expressing anger, envy, and hatred toward them (v. [*11*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.11)), and of taunting and celebrating over the desolation of the land (vv. [*12*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.12), [*15*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.15); cf. [*36:2*](https://ref.ly/Eze36.2)).[^20] In [*Joel 4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19)\[[*3:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19)\] the prophet lays specific and serious charges against Edom:

> Egypt shall become a desolation

> and Edom a desolate wilderness,

> because of the violence done to the people of Judah,

> in whose land they have shed innocent blood. (NRSV)

> This picture accords with that of the psalmist, who recalls Edom's role with intense bitterness:

> Remember, O YHWH, against the Edomites

> the day of Jerusalem's fall,

> how they said, "Tear it down! Tear it down!

> Down to its foundations!"

> O daughter Babylon, you devastator!

> Happy shall be they who pay you back

> what you have done to us!

> Happy shall be they who take your little ones

> and dash them against the rock! ([*Ps 137:7--9*](https://ref.ly/Ps137.7-9) NRSV)

> The fourth lament in Lamentations also recalls Edom's involvement in Judah's demise and declares hope in her punishment:

> Rejoice and be glad, O daughter Edom,

> you that live in the land of Uz;

> but to you also the cup shall pass;

> you shall become drunk and strip yourself bare.

> The punishment of your iniquity, O daughter Zion, is accomplished,

> he will keep you in exile no longer;

> but your iniquity, O daughter Edom, he will punish,

> he will uncover your sins. ([*Lam 4:21--22*](https://ref.ly/La4.21-22), NRSV)

> To these poetic and prophetic texts we may add the narrative of the aftermath of the appointment of Gedaliah as governor after the fall of Jerusalem in [*Jer 40:11--12*](https://ref.ly/Je40.11-12):

> Likewise, when all the Judeans who were in Moab and among the Ammonites and in Edom and in other lands heard that the king of Babylon had left a remnant in Judah and had appointed Gedaliah son of Ahikam son of Shaphan as governor over them, then all the Judeans returned from all the places to which they had been scattered and came to the land of Judah, to Gedaliah at Mizpah; and they gathered wine and summer fruits in great abundance. (NRSV)

Apparently, despite the nation of Edom's traditional hostility toward Judah, individual Judeans had sought and found refuge in the Transjordan, which Nebuchadnezzar's forces did not attack in 598--597 BC.

> To these biblical sources we may now add significant supporting archaeological data. First, Arad Ostracon 24, dated early in the sixth century BC, contains an urgent request to Eliashib for troops to be sent to Arad and Ramath-negev to forestall Edomite aggression in the Negev: "lest Edom should go there."[^21] Second, dated about the same time, an ostracon discovered in the fort of Horvat ʿUza, ca. 6 km. southeast of Tel Arad, contains a letter from one Edomite official, Lumalak, to another, Blbl, inscribed with a blessing in the name of Qaus, the patron deity of Edom, and a request for food supplies.[^22] Third, among other inscriptions, a fragmentary ostracon from Ḥorvat Qitmit, an Edomite settlement 10 km. south of Tel Arad, contains the name of the Edomite deity Qaus (קוס).[^23] Fourth, the excavations at Ḥorvat Qitmit provide firm evidence for an Edomite shrine at this place, complete with figurines and other cult objects.^21^[^24] This extrabiblical evidence confirms that the Edomites were indeed poised to mock the Judeans as they fell to the Babylonians and to encroach on their territory.

> However, the Edomites appear to have been opportunists, switching sides whenever it seemed advantageous. According to [*Jer 27:1--11*](https://ref.ly/Je27.1-11), as recently as 594--593 BC the Edomites had been part of an international coalition including Moab, Bene Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon, which sent emissaries to Jerusalem to get Zedekiah to join them in rebelling against Nebuchadnezzar. However, when Tyre, Bene Ammon, and Judah launched the rebellion, Edom apparently listened to Jeremiah's counsel and withdrew from the alliance. By withdrawing, Edom secured its own existence and apparently received permission from Babylon to expand its territory in the Negev.

> These data provide us with the terminus a quo of Obadiah's prophetic ministry. What about the terminus ad quem (latest possible date)? The oracles preserved in this short book obviously assume that Edom still exists as a nation and represents a significant threat to what remains of the Jewish community in Judah. This suggests they were delivered prior to Edom's fall. We lack clear and unequivocal evidence for this event, but a likely scenario is offered by the Chronicles of Nabonidus, a successor to Nebuchadnezzar, who ruled in Babylon from 556--539 BC. The chronicle recording his activities in this region in 553 BC, his third year, reads as follows:

> \[The third (?) year, in the\] month of Ab, \[he marched\] on the Ammanānum \[and ...\] the orchards, fruits, as many as there were, \[...\] among them, \[he brought\] to Babylon. \[?... The king be\]came ill but recovered. In the month of Kislev, the king \[mustered\] his army \[...\] and to Nabû-tattan-usur \[...\] of Amurru to \[...\] they set up their quarters \[facing E\]dom \[...\] and the numerous troops \[... ga\]teway of Sintīni \[...\] he killed him \[... the tr\]oops \[...\].[^25]

> Although the dating is not certain, evidence for destruction prior to the Persian period has been found at Buseira (Bozrah), Tawilan, and Tell el-Kheleifeh.[^26] This region continued to be occupied in the Persian period, but Edom's political and national status had been crushed, opening the doors to Nabatean Arabs to move in and take control. Accordingly, we may see in Nabonidus' campaign the fulfillment of Obadiah's prophecies.[^27]

**Date and Occasion of Writing**

Suggested dates for the book range from the ninth century to the late fourth century bc. Conservative scholars have adopted a wide span of dates, including both preexilic and exilic eras (i.e., from the ninth century to the sixth century bc). Final determination has largely been based on the interpretation of the denunciation of Edom in [*1:10--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob10-14). Some commentators (e.g., Keil, Niehaus) have followed the traditional ninth-century bc date, citing general association with the description of events during the reign of King Jehoram of Judah. Others have defended an eighth-century bc date, either in the time of Amaziah and Uzziah (e.g., Pusey) or Ahaz (e.g., Raven), or the time of Jeremiah (e.g., Young). Most scholars (e.g., Stuart, Raabe) have opted for a sixth-century bc date because they view these crucial verses ([*1:10--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob10-14)) as referring to events concerned with the fall of Jerusalem in 586 bc. Thus Smith opts for a date somewhere between 587 and 500 bc.

> A more precise date could be affixed if the relation of [*Obadiah 1:1--9*](https://ref.ly/Ob1-9) to [*Jeremiah 49:7--16*](https://ref.ly/Je49.7-16) could be determined. Here again, scholars are divided as to whether Jeremiah borrowed from Obadiah (e.g., Keil, Pusey), Obadiah was dependent upon Jeremiah (e.g., Armerding, Bewer), or both drew upon common prophetic material (e.g., Finley). While Obadiah's penchant for drawing upon traditional Hebrew phraseology makes the second option most likely (Raabe 1996:[*22--33*](https://ref.ly/logosres/anchor31ob?ref=Page.pp+22-33)), the data are capable of such diverse evaluation that a final decision as to literary dependency seems unlikely.

> The occasion of Obadiah's prophecy, then, would be variously understood. If a ninth-century bc date is decided upon, the denunciation of Edom would be tied in with the Edomite campaigning in Jehoram's day ([*2 Kgs 8:16--24*](https://ref.ly/2Ki8.16-24)) and the subsequent Arabian-Philistine invasion of Judah and Jerusalem ([*2 Chr 21:16--17*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21.16-17)). If the time period is that of Amaziah/Uzziah, Obadiah's prophecy would parallel the sentiment of Amos, whose general condemnation of Edom's perennial hostility merited a prophetic judgment oracle ([*Amos 1:11--12*](https://ref.ly/Am1.11-12)). If Obadiah is seen as prophesying during the reign of Ahaz, the prophet referred to the defeats by the Edomites and Philistines ([*2 Chr 28:17--18*](https://ref.ly/2Ch28.17-18)). Taking the setting of the book as exilic most naturally views Edom's vile behavior in connection with the fall of Jerusalem in 586 bc.

> Like the date, then, the occasion of Obadiah's prophecy is difficult to determine. Yet the message of Edom's sure judgment for its traditional position of animosity toward God's people remains the same and is in harmony with the words of several other prophets ([*Isa 11:14*](https://ref.ly/Is11.14); [*21:11--12*](https://ref.ly/Is21.11-12); [*34:1--17*](https://ref.ly/Is34.1-17); [*63:1--6*](https://ref.ly/Is63.1-6); [*Jer 25:21*](https://ref.ly/Je25.21); [*49:7--22*](https://ref.ly/Je49.7-22); [*Lam 4:21*](https://ref.ly/La4.21); [*Ezek 25:12--14*](https://ref.ly/Eze25.12-14); [*35:1--15*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.1-15); [*36:1--38*](https://ref.ly/Eze36.1-38); [*Joel 3:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19); [*Amos 1:11--12*](https://ref.ly/Am1.11-12); [*9:12*](https://ref.ly/Am9.12); [*Mal 1:2--5*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.2-5)). In that regard, Edom becomes representative of all of God's foes, who will ultimately be defeated in the great Day of the Lord (see especially [*Isa 34:1--17*](https://ref.ly/Is34.1-17); [*63:1--6*](https://ref.ly/Is63.1-6); [*Ezek 35:1--15*](https://ref.ly/Eze35.1-15); [*36:1--38*](https://ref.ly/Eze36.1-38)).

**The date(s) of Obadiah**

On the basis of the above, it is best, with most commentators, to date [*Obad. 1--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob1-14), [*15b*](https://ref.ly/Ob15b) to the sixth century, and to see it as a response to the Edomite participation in the Babylonian destruction of Judah (Wolff 1986: [*18*](https://ref.ly/logosres/ccs31ob?ref=Page.p+18); Mason 1991: 94; Raabe 1996: 51--5; Barton 2001: [*123*](https://ref.ly/logosres/otl29joe?ref=Page.p+123); Jenson 2008: 4--5). Some attempt greater precision and date the book to the middle of the sixth century on the grounds that it was composed in relation to the campaigns of Nabonidus (Renkema 2003: 32--3; Na'aman 2016: 30--1).

> One piece of information does not fit this dating very well. Verse [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) mentions Sepharad as one of the places where the exiled Jews live. Sepharad is to be identified with Sardes, the capital of Lydia in western Asia Minor (for a discussion see Raabe 1996: 268--70 and Pons 2014). A memorial stele testifies to the presence of Jewish settlers in western Asia Minor from the mid-fifth century BCE (Wolff 1986: [*67--8*](https://ref.ly/logosres/ccs31ob?ref=Page.pp+67-68)), but there is no indication that a Jewish colony existed there as early as the first half of the sixth century. There are several ways in which this difficulty can be resolved. Those who date Obadiah in the sixth century and regard the book as a unity (i) place Sepharad in Mesopotamia (Stuart 1987: [*421*](https://ref.ly/logosres/wbc31?ref=Page.p+421); Block 2017: [*104*](https://ref.ly/logosres/exegcomm31ob2ed?ref=Page.p+104)), (ii) assume that Jewish exiles were settled in Asia Minor already in the sixth century (Allen 1976: [*13*](https://ref.ly/logosres/nicot29joe?ref=Page.p+13); Jenson 2008: 26) or (iii) regard v. [*20*](https://ref.ly/Ob20) as later interpolation (Renkema 2003: 214--15). Alternatively some scholars argue for a fifth-century date for the entire composition (Nogalski 2011: [*367--8*](https://ref.ly/logosres/shcbk12?ref=Page.pp+367-368)). The description of Edom's demise in vv. [*5--7*](https://ref.ly/Ob5-7) is taken not as a prediction but as a past event, connected somehow to the devastation referred to in [*Mal. 1:1--4*](https://ref.ly/Mal1.1-4) and alludes to the Arab pressure on the Edomite territory from the fifth century BCE onwards (Bewer 1911: 7--8; Jeremias 2007: 57--8).

> As shown above, there are reasons to believe that vv. [*15a*](https://ref.ly/Ob15a), [*16--21*](https://ref.ly/Ob16-21) constitute a series of additions to the original prophetic book. The sixth century serves as the historical background only of the first half of Obadiah. The finished text represents a more complex composition which, in its totality, is somewhat removed from the traumatic events that initially gave rise to the prophecy.

**2. Obadiah: the man and his time**

Writing prophets in Israel are regularly identified by a notice of the period in which they prophesied, their hometown (or at least the place where the prophecies took place), and their father, or any combination of these. One is identified simply as a prophet (Habakkuk). Only two prophets are not given any contextual framework and not only are they 'without father and mother', they might even be without proper names of their own. Malachi, 'my messenger', could be a designation of the prophet's role as intermediary, and Obadiah, 'servant/worshipper of Yahweh', could indicate the same role. Prophets were often referred to as 'servant' (e.g. [*1 Kgs 14:18*](https://ref.ly/1Ki14.18); [*2 Kgs 17:23*](https://ref.ly/2Ki17.23); [*Jer. 7:25*](https://ref.ly/Je7.25); [*Zech. 1:6*](https://ref.ly/Zec1.6)). Obadiah is a common Israelite name in the Old Testament, however, so it is probably the name of the prophet.

> Nothing further is known about the prophet; not even his time period is mentioned, so our dating of the book must come from clues within the book itself. Since the prophecy concerns Edom, and especially its treachery during an attack on Jerusalem (vv. [*10--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob10-14)), a synoptic study of the histories of Edom and Judah must be done to determine the periods referred to in the prophecy.

> The outline of Edomite-Judean contacts (pp. [*21--23*](https://ref.ly/logosres/totc31obus?ref=Page.pp+21-23)) shows that there were several periods when Obadiah would have been relevant. If the canonical sequence of Obadiah among the pre-exilic prophets is seen as significant, the background against which the book is set could be that of Jehoshaphat or Jehoram or Ahaz. The first does not fit the biblical evidence, since [*2 Chronicles 20*](https://ref.ly/2Ch20) pictures a victory for Judah through God's intervention rather than the defeat described in [*Obadiah 10--14*](https://ref.ly/Ob10-14). The rebellion against Jehoram mentions nothing of the attack on Jerusalem which is of importance to Obadiah ([*2 Chr. 21*](https://ref.ly/2Ch21); cf. [*Obad. 11*](https://ref.ly/Ob11)). Neither is it explicitly mentioned as suffering during Ahaz's reign ([*2 Chr. 28:16--18*](https://ref.ly/2Ch28.16-18)), although other towns are listed as being taken.

> A suggested date in the fifth century bc, based on Arab raids dispossessing the Edomites during that period (see p. [*22*](https://ref.ly/logosres/totc31obus?ref=Page.p+22)), is not necessary, since the Arab presence in the area was known as early as the ninth century bc.[^28] The most satisfying setting is shortly after the fall of Jerusalem in 587 bc, when refugees were captured in mid-flight ([*2 Kgs 25:4--6*](https://ref.ly/2Ki25.4-6)). Although Edom is not explicitly linked with this catastrophe in the canonical text, its satisfaction at the outcome would have matched that of other nations who had opposed Judah in the past.

1.3 What became of Edom

-   **Ask:** What do my commentators say about the end of Edom as a state: Nabonidus's campaign, Arab or Nabataean pressure, and the Idumeans? Do any of them connect this with the allies and "those who eat your bread" of Obadiah 7? What ancient evidence do they give?
-   **Why:** it bears on the tenses of vv. 5--7 (question 3.2) and on any statement from the pulpit that the oracle "was fulfilled". The dig's only source for Nabonidus is Cook's review.
-   **Watch for:** fulfilment asserted with no ancient evidence, and Nabataeans named without a date.

Hadjiev documents that during his third year (556--539 BCE), the Babylonian king Nabonidus led a campaign against Tema in Arabia, with a fragmentary note in the Nabonidus Chronicle possibly indicating an attack on Edom en route. A rock relief of Nabonidus dated to his fifth year (551 BCE) near the Edomite site of es-Selaʿ confirms such a campaign.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F94948) Na'aman conjectures that Nabonidus campaigned twice, in 553 and 551 BCE.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F95448) It is likely that these campaigns brought the end of the kingdom of Edom.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F95532)

Critically, Hadjiev connects this historical destruction directly to Obadiah's accusations. This reconstruction ties in well with the threats of Obadiah: verse 8 states that Edom's allies will deceive and overpower him, which could be a reference to the Babylonians with whom Edom had allied itself earlier.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F95664) This interpretation transforms the language of verse 7 about allies and "those who eat your bread" from general accusation into specific historical prophecy---Edom's supposed confederates become the very agents of its destruction.

Pusey offers earlier commentary identifying specific allies. Those confederates were probably Moab and Ammon, Tyre and Zidon, with whom they united to resist Nebuchadnezzar, and seduced Zedekiah to rebel, although Moab, Ammon, and Edom turned against him.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES28HO%2F2014-10-03T16%3A31%3A51Z%2F1722078) Pusey suggests these allies, admitted as confederates within Edomite borders, then betrayed the secret of their fastnesses to the enemy.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES28HO%2F2014-10-03T16%3A31%3A51Z%2F1722623)

However, the commentators do not extensively discuss Arab or Nabataean pressure, nor do they address the later emergence of the Idumeans in detail. The focus remains on Nabonidus's campaigns as the immediate cause of Edom's political demise, with the betrayal by allies serving as the fulfillment of Obadiah's warning.

## 2. Direction of Dependence

The audit left direction open at every point. Each of these questions asks for arguments, not dates.

2.1 Obadiah 1--9 and Jeremiah 49:7--22

-   **Ask:** What textual arguments do my commentators give for the direction of dependence between Obadiah 1--9 and Jeremiah 49:7--22? Please set out the arguments on each side, who holds each, and the verses they rest on, with page references.

-   **Why:** the audit rates the shared material *high* and direction open, with evidence running both ways: the odd imperative שִׂים ("set", v. 4); עָלֶיהָ ("against her", v. 1) beside a masculine Edom; Jer 49:7--22 as a compilation (with Jer 50 and Jer 48:41). Your answers feed queue item O27, the Synoptic Mode run.

-   Watch for:

    -   Direction settled by assuming which book is older.
    -   "Most scholars agree". Count the footnotes behind it.
    -   The Greek of Jeremiah cited without its different numbering (Edom stands in Greek Jer 29). The audit found that the Greek translators worked separately on the two books.

The search results reveal substantial disagreement on this question, with commentators divided across three main positions.

Obadiah Dependent on Jeremiah

Some scholars (Armerding, Bewer) argue that Obadiah was dependent upon Jeremiah[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F627265). One older view holds that Obadiah makes use of, or even simply paraphrases, the strikingly similar language of Jeremiah (chapter 49:7 ff.) against Edom[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE31OB%2F2014-10-07T22%3A27%3A39Z%2F11371). However, this view faces a significant objection: Jeremiah's series of announcements concerning foreign lands shows not merely constant use of earlier prophecies, but Jeremiah repeatedly applies earlier prophecies with free reproduction and expansion to present occasions[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE31OB%2F2014-10-07T22%3A27%3A39Z%2F12016). Upon careful comparison of the two predictions, Obadiah appears partly shorter and more rapid, partly heavier and more abrupt, partly more clear and lively than in Jeremiah[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE31OB%2F2014-10-07T22%3A27%3A39Z%2F13016). It is contrary to hermeneutical procedure to suppose that a later writer, regarding a situation meanwhile explained, should have darkened the clear language of the earlier one, while it is a common and explainable occurrence that the obscure prophecy of antiquity should, in the hands of a subsequent seer, become more flowing and more clear[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE31OB%2F2014-10-07T22%3A27%3A39Z%2F13641).

Jeremiah Dependent on Obadiah

Other scholars (Keil, Pusey) contend that Jeremiah borrowed from Obadiah[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F627265). This position faces chronological challenges: Jeremiah's activities, if not the penning of his prophecies, were in the late seventh to early sixth centuries B.C., prior to the date for Obadiah adopted here[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F410117).

Common Source Theory

Most scholars (Finley) favor the view that both drew upon common prophetic material, and Obadiah's penchant for drawing upon traditional Hebrew phraseology makes this option most likely[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F627265). The types of variations between the two texts make it more likely that both used independently an oracle that came to them from a pool of common tradition[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F83430). The fact that the two writers differ from each other in such significant ways in spite of the numerous parallels could indicate that both freely adapt a third source[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F410573).

The data are capable of such diverse evaluation that a final decision as to literary dependency seems unlikely[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F627486).

2.2 A common source

-   **Ask:** Do any of my commentators argue that Obadiah and Jeremiah 49 draw independently on an earlier oracle against Edom? What is their evidence, and does anyone reject the idea?
-   **Why:** this is the third option. The dig's contacts stop at v. 9, with none in vv. 10--21 at the rare-pair level, and the audit found Job 39:27--28 may be common stock behind the eagle lines (O24).
-   **Watch for:** a common source proposed only because the direction cannot be settled. That is a way out, not an argument. Ask what in the wording requires it.

Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke propose that rather than one text borrowing from the other, an independent source could have been used by both Obadiah and [Jeremiah 49](https://ref.ly/Je49)[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F72656). This approach accounts for the variations, especially regarding the order of elements within the two books[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F72943).

Hadjiev cites multiple scholars (Allen, Coggins, Wolff, Stuart, Mason, Ben Zvi, Barton) who argue that the types of variations between the two texts make it more likely that both used independently an oracle that came to them from a pool of common tradition[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F83430). Rather than being a totally original composition, Obadiah used at least some pre-existing materials and gave them a new interpretation and application[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F83918).

Patterson and Hill note that scholars divide on whether Jeremiah borrowed from Obadiah, Obadiah depended on Jeremiah, or both drew upon common prophetic material, with Obadiah's penchant for drawing upon traditional Hebrew phraseology making the third option most likely[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F627265).

However, Block acknowledges that although it is possible that Jeremiah borrowed from Obadiah or that both derive from a common earlier oracle, most scholars who date Obadiah to the exilic period agree that Obadiah borrowed from Jeremiah[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F132990). Block does not explicitly defend the common source theory in the provided excerpts.

The evidence supporting the common source theory includes the variations in wording, order, and emphasis between the parallel passages[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F83430), suggesting independent adaptation rather than direct literary dependence.

2.3 Obadiah 17 and Joel 2:32

-   **Ask:** Which commentators on Obadiah or Joel discuss the link between Obadiah 17 and Joel 2:32 ("there will be those who escape"), including Joel's "as the LORD has said"? What textual arguments are given for the direction, and does anyone argue that Joel is citing something other than Obadiah?
-   **Why:** audit claim A1: *moderate--high*, direction open. The Hebrew phrase תִּהְיֶה פְלֵיטָה ("there will be those who escape") stands only in these two verses (WLC). The July overview said Joel "appears to cite" Obadiah; the November overview leaves the direction open.
-   **Watch for:** "Joel quotes Obadiah" stated as settled, with the date of Joel assumed. Joel's date is itself disputed.

Hadjiev identifies a literary link between [Joel 2:32](https://ref.ly/Joe2.32) and [Obadiah 17](https://ref.ly/Ob17), noting that both contain the phrase "In Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be those who escape, as the Lord has said."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F57678) Smith, Ward, and Bewer argue that Joel's editor makes a literal quotation from [Obadiah 17](https://ref.ly/Ob17), fortifying the promise of deliverance not through the editor's own inspiration but by citing an older prophet, with the phrase "in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be those that escape, as Yahweh has said" explicitly referencing [Obadiah 17](https://ref.ly/Ob17).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F333757)

The direction of dependence is clear: Joel quotes Obadiah rather than the reverse, with the editor appealing to an older oracle's authority.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F333757) Patterson and Hill suggest that when Joel states "just as the LORD has said," he may be referring to God's promise in [Joel 2:27](https://ref.ly/Joe2.27) or to [Obadiah 1:17](https://ref.ly/Ob17).[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F419298)

Regarding whether Joel cites something other than Obadiah, the search results do not present this as a serious alternative. Hadjiev notes that most scholars explain these connections with the hypothesis that Joel was a "learned prophet" who interpreted, applied and actualized written texts for his own time.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F58506) Patterson and Hill observe that a similar play on escape and deliverance occurs between [Joel 2:3](https://ref.ly/Joe2.3) and [Joel 2:32](https://ref.ly/Joe2.32), and suggest that Obadiah's use of the nouns for "escaped one" and "survivors" that also appear in Joel may be deliberate.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F666349) This phrasing suggests Obadiah influenced Joel's vocabulary rather than both drawing from a common source.

2.4 Obadiah 11--17 and Joel 3

-   **Ask:** Which commentators discuss the links between Obadiah and Joel 3 (casting lots for the people, recompense returning on the head, violence against Judah, Edom, the holiness of Jerusalem)? Do they treat these as dependence, as a shared tradition, or as editing within the Book of the Twelve?
-   **Why:** audit claim A2: *moderate--high*, cumulative. Recompense on the head is its strongest point, and Nah 3:10 has the closer frame for the lots.
-   **Watch for:** one verse (Joel 3:19) offered as the whole case.

Hadjiev identifies multiple literary connections between [Joel 3](https://ref.ly/Joe3) and Obadiah, including "they cast lots for my people" ([Joel 3:3--Obadiah 11](https://ref.ly/logosref/BibleNASB95.Joe3.3-Ob11)), "I will turn your deeds back upon your heads" ([Joel 3:4](https://ref.ly/Joe3.4), [7--Obadiah 15](https://ref.ly/logosref/BibleNASB95.Joe3.7-Ob15)), and the culmination with Edom as a symbol of Judah's oppressors ([Joel 3:19](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19))[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F57678).

Smith, Ward, and Bewer note that the phrase "they cast lots" in [Joel 3:3](https://ref.ly/Joe3.3) "reminds us of [Ob. 11](https://ref.ly/Ob11)," and observe that "the editor had used phrases from Obadiah in 4:17, 20," suggesting the editor drew on Obadiah[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F166472). They conclude that these passages "are also part of the editor's work"[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F166625).

Fausset states that "Obadiah here confirms afresh Joel's prophecy ([Joel 3:19](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19)), 'Edom shall be a desolate wilderness, for the violence against the children of Judah'"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2689267), and notes the parallel between [Obadiah 11](https://ref.ly/Ob11) and [Joel 3:3](https://ref.ly/Joe3.3) regarding casting lots upon Jerusalem[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2690830).

The commentators treat these connections as **dependence rather than shared tradition or independent composition**. Hadjiev explains that most scholars view Joel as a "learned prophet" who "interpreted, applied and actualized" written texts, with proponents of the "book of the Twelve" hypothesis arguing that "the primary function of the quotations in Joel was to tie it more closely to the wider context of the Twelve"[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F58506). Block compares Obadiah 15a and 16--21 with [Joel 3:1--4:21](https://ref.ly/Joe2.28-3.21), noting both involve "a generalized eschatological prophecy of the 'day of YHWH'" where "all the nations are forced to drink the cup of divine wrath imposed on Edom"[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F101120). Rather than treating these as editorial additions within a unified Twelve, the commentators present Joel's use of Obadiah as evidence of literary dependence by a later editor drawing on earlier prophetic material.

## 3. Cruxes

For the first three, ask for one commentator at a time if the blended answer is thin. Each of these touches the pulpit text.

3.1 Verses 12--14: past or future?

-   **Ask (once for each owned commentary):** How does \[commentator\] take the series of prohibitions in Obadiah 12--14 ("do not gloat... do not enter...")? Are they warnings before an event, or are they addressed rhetorically back into a past event? How does he relate them to v. 11, and what grammatical reasons does he give?

-   **Why:** the dig leans to the past (*moderate*). The NET note reads them as rhetorical; Collins argues from the same grammar for a real warning and so for the ninth century. The answer decides how the sermon is framed. The audit also found that the chain of eight prohibitions is the densest in the WLC, on its auditor's floor count, and unique to Obadiah.

-   Watch for:

    -   The date deciding the grammar, or the grammar deciding the date. Note which comes first.
    -   An English past sense ("you should not have...", which as far as I recall is the KJV's wording) treated as the Hebrew. Both of your texts read "do not".

**Baker (NIV Application Commentary):** Baker notes that while the prohibitions grammatically express present or future universal commands, the context of verses 10--11 places the perspective after the events have already occurred, and numerous translations incorrectly render them in the past tense.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F461831) He suggests the author uses this form to convey immediacy and horror, imaginatively placing himself back into the time of action and calling out "STOP!"[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F462164) The prohibitions show Edom's involvement escalating from silent complicity in verse 11 to active participation in verses 12--14.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F461601)

**Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke (Tyndale):** These commentators identify the verbal forms as referring to future actions, reflecting the prophet's horror at the deeds described.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F96411) They note that past-tense translations lack grammatical basis.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F96532) Verse 13 resumes verse 11's mention of the enemy entering, foreshadowing Edom's actual position as foe rather than brother.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F97388)

**Hadjiev (T&T Clark Study Guide):** Hadjiev observes that most interpreters treat the prohibitions as a rhetorical device depicting past actions, with Obadiah imaginatively placing himself in the past to enhance dramatic effect.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F90613) The NRSV translates the commands as "you should not have gloated."[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F90832) He connects the crimes to verse 11's reference to the day when foreigners entered Jerusalem.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F90895)

**Patterson and Hill (Cornerstone):** These commentators acknowledge the construction can be understood differently depending on whether the occasion is past, present, or future---either as "do not" (with the prophet vividly reliving events), "you should not have," or as urgent emotive prohibition.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F661462) They note Keil's suggestion that the warning may be all-inclusive, regarding events that have already taken place but will recur.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F662125)

**Mason (T&T Clark):** Mason characterizes the construction as unusual, consisting of a series of prohibitions, and argues that while this might suggest a warning against future judgment, the detail suggests hindsight, making it better understood as a graphic denunciation of attitudes already demonstrated in the past.[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHEFFCL33MIC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHEFFCL33MIC%2F2014-10-04T08%3A49%3A18Z%2F163459)

3.2 Verses 2 and 5--7: the tenses

-   **Ask:** How do my commentators take the perfect verbs in Obadiah 2 and 5--7: as past events that have already befallen Edom, as prophetic perfects describing what is certain, or otherwise? On what grounds?
-   **Why:** this is a pulpit gap. The ESV says "how you have been destroyed", "has been pillaged" and "have driven you"; the NASB95 says "will be ruined", "will be ransacked" and "will send you". Both versions make v. 2 future, though נְתַתִּיךָ ("I have made you") is perfect. The two congregations would hear different events.
-   **Watch for:** "prophetic perfect" as a label with no argument. Also see whether the commentator's date (question 1.1) is doing the work.

**Mason** interprets the perfect verbs in verses 2 and 5--7 as depicting events already completed. He notes that some commentaries understand these as describing a historical assault on Edom, either before or after the exile, possibly reflecting attacks by Arab tribes.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHEFFCL33MIC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHEFFCL33MIC%2F2014-10-04T08%3A49%3A18Z%2F170181) However, Mason acknowledges that most commentators favor the "prophetic perfect" convention, whereby the prophet announces future action with such certainty that he speaks of it as already accomplished.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHEFFCL33MIC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHEFFCL33MIC%2F2014-10-04T08%3A49%3A18Z%2F170375)

**Smith, Ward, and Bewer** (ICC) adopt the historical reading decisively. They assert that "this older oracle has been fulfilled" and "the fall from the height has come,"[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F89259) treating the passage as retrospective description. Their reasoning centers on the vividness of exclamations, the use of past tenses, and detailed description in verse 7, which they argue shows Obadiah describing the fulfillment of an older oracle in Edom's contemporary ruin.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F89716) Crucially, they contend that Obadiah modifies the original prediction into historical retrospect, so that "in Jeremiah the prediction is still preserved, in Obadiah the prediction has become history."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F90757)

**Block** (Zondervan Exegetical Commentary) presents a nuanced middle position. While acknowledging that most commentators interpret the perfect as prophetic perfect---YHWH's future action so certain it appears already accomplished---[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F175518) he argues that since YHWH has already summoned the nations against Esau in verse 1e, Esau's diminished status is a current reality in YHWH's perspective.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F175727) This reading treats the perfects as expressing present divine certainty rather than purely future prediction.

3.3 Verse 16: who drank?

-   **Ask (once for each owned commentary):** In Obadiah 16, "as you drank on my holy mountain", whom does \[commentator\] take "you" to be: Edom, Judah, or someone else? Does he comment on the plural verb after the singular "you" of v. 15? What reasons does he give?

-   **Why:** audit claim B9, *Uncertain*: a slight lean to Judah, low--moderate, on the plural and on Jer 49:12. The NET note rejects Judah on the ground that God's people are addressed nowhere else in the book. The Greek ἔπιες ("you drank") is singular.

-   Watch for:

    -   "The cup" read in as though the text had it. The word is absent.
    -   The plural passed over in silence. Record that as a gap in the commentator's treatment.
    -   Edom's drunken revelling supplied with no text cited.

Baker notes that verse 16 continues addressing an unidentified "you" using second-person forms, which explains why many interpreters maintain Edom as the addressee.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F480261) However, Baker identifies a critical grammatical problem: this verse contains only one of two plural forms among forty second-person forms in the book, and since Edom is consistently addressed in the singular throughout, the plural subject must be someone else---Judah.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F480466) The shift in grammatical number functions rhetorically to signal a change in addressee, surprising the original audience when they realize the people of God themselves are now being addressed.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F480921) Baker interprets the drinking metaphorically: Judah had already suffered God's wrath for disobedience, and the nations will suffer similarly, with the comparative adverb "just as" serving its regular function.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F481991)

Block presents a more detailed grammatical analysis. Block critiques the Edom-as-subject interpretation on three grounds: it overlooks the discourse significance of the shift from singular verbs in verse 15b--c to plural in verse 16a; the Old Testament never explicitly refers to "drinking bouts" celebrating victory; and it requires an awkward shift in the drinking's significance from celebration to divine judgment.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F247691) Block further argues this interpretation fails to account adequately for the singular-to-plural shift and requires an uncomfortable reading of the perfect verb, which typically denotes past completed action.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F248999) Block concludes that verse 16a addresses Judean and Israelite survivors of 586 BC, with the plural verb שְׁתִיתֶם signaling the shift in addressee---Esau having been consistently treated as singular.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F249252) Block treats the perfect verb as simple past ("you drank") or past perfect ("you have drunk").[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F249840)

Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke discuss the structural unity of the book but do not directly address the identity of "you" in verse 16 or comment on the plural-singular shift in their provided excerpts.

3.4 Verse 15: the order and the seam

-   **Ask:** Which of my commentators rearrange or divide Obadiah 15, placing 15b ("as you have done...") with vv. 10--14 and 15a ("the day of the LORD...") with vv. 16--21? What reasons do they give, and does anyone defend the verse as it stands?
-   **Why:** this bears on the outline. The audit's three blocks (1--9, 10--14, 15--21, *moderate--high*) cut between vv. 14 and 15, though יוֹם ("day") crosses the cut. Moving 15b would change the block count and the hinge. The overview has this only from Cook's "hinge line".
-   **Watch for:** a rearrangement offered with no manuscript support. Note that none is recorded in BHS for this.

Some commentators (including Wellhausen, Rudolph, and Wolff) have proposed reversing the two halves of verse 15, placing 15a ("the Day of Yahweh") with the following material and 15b ("as you have done") with the preceding address to Edom.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F73053) The rationale rests on thematic grounds: the more universal message concerning all nations appears in verses 16--21, while verse 15b addresses Edom specifically, the subject of verses 2--14, so reversing the halves would align each portion with its related context.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F73217)

Hadjiev notes that verse 15 fits awkwardly into either section---thematically it belongs with what follows by introducing the Day of the Lord against all nations, yet formally it continues the preceding by addressing Edom in the second person singular. Hadjiev cites Wellhausen's observation that the second half of the verse actually fits well immediately after verses 1--14, suggesting that verse 15b may have been the original conclusion of the first part, separated when verse 15a was inserted.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F87223)

However, Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke defend the verse as it stands. They argue there is no apparent reason why such a dislocation might have been deliberately done, and while scribal error has been suggested, it is unnecessary to suppose any dislocation if the book comes from a single hand.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F73465) They appeal to recent research on transitional techniques showing that common methods for linking paragraphs involve repetitions of key words or linguistic material. The concept "all the nations" appears in both 15a and 16, serving as a deliberate transition between the two sections.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F73668) Additionally, the shift in grammatical person---from the second person singular "you" characteristic of the Edom section to the impersonal third person in 15a, then back to "you" in 15b and 16a---serves as a deliberate structural link binding the paragraphs together.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F74092)

Block similarly opposes rearrangement. While many treat 15b--c as the conclusion to verses 11a--d and attach 15a directly to verse 16, this rearrangement lacks manuscript support and significantly affects the rhetoric.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F233978) Rearranging obscures Esau's fate as paradigmatic for the nations' fate, the shift from retributive to distributive justice, the progression in focus from Esau to nations to Jacob, and the impressive inclusio involving Esau at beginning and end.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F234221)

3.5 Verses 19--20: subjects and objects

-   **Ask:** How do my commentators construe Obadiah 19--20: who possesses what? Which words do they treat as glosses, and what do they make of "this host" (הַחֵל־הַזֶּה) in v. 20? Please quote any translation they offer.
-   **Why:** the ESV and NASB95 draw different maps. The ESV makes the exiles of v. 20 possess "the land of the Canaanites"; the NASB95 has them "among the Canaanites". BHS marks several phrases in v. 19 as probable glosses and v. 20 as corrupt. The dig's land-left-undone reading (audit B7, low--moderate) leans on the syntax.
-   **Watch for:** a confident map resting on a conjecture. Ask which words are the commentator's emendation.

The commentators offer competing interpretations of verses 19--20, with significant disagreement over textual corruption and the identity of possessors.

Verse 19: Basic Agreement

The four territorial expansions reflect the four compass points: north (Ephraim), south (Mount Esau), east (Gilead), and west (the Philistines).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F279799) Those from the Negeb shall possess Mount Esau, with the Jews occupying the south of Judea gaining the adjoining mountainous region of Edom.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2698507) Those of the plain possess the Philistines---Jews occupying the low country along the Mediterranean gaining the land of the Philistines running between hills and sea.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2698751) The rightful owners shall be restored: the Ephraimites to the fields of Ephraim.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2699067)

Verse 20: Textual Corruption and Interpretation

The commentators diverge sharply on textual problems. Block assumes the verb "they will possess" and the direct object marker have dropped out, and that הַחֵל־הַזֶּה ("this company"), which makes little sense, represents a corruption of חָלָה זֶה ("this Halah").[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F280566) Block's translation reads: "And as for the exiles of Halah belonging to the people of Israel \[they will dispossess\] the Canaanites as far as Zarephath; And as for the exiles of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad they will possess the towns of the Negev."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F281004)

Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke similarly read "company" as "Halah" (RSV), an Assyrian site where exiles were located.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F108517) They note the Hebrew has a relative pronoun defining "Israelites who are in Canaan," which is difficult to understand, and suggest a minor textual corruption---one misread letter plus letter reordering---could have produced this from an original meaning Israel would "inherit" Canaanite territory at the far north of their original inheritance.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F108873)

Fausset interprets "the captivity of this host of the children of Israel" as "the captives of this multitude of Israelites" who "shall possess that of the Canaanites, even unto Zarephath."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F2699893)

Bridger presents a straightforward reading: "This company of Israelite exiles who are in Canaan will possess the land as far as Zarephath; the exiles from Jerusalem who are in Sepharad will possess the towns of the Negev," with Israelite exiles from the north (taken by Assyria in 722 BC) and exiles from Jerusalem in Sepharad returning so the entire nation from far south to far north will be restored.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BST31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABST31OB%2F2015-11-05T00%3A11%3A17Z%2F185968)

**3.6 Verse 21: **"**saviours**"** and **"**judge**"

-   **Ask:** In Obadiah 21, how do my commentators take מוֹשִׁעִים ("saviours" or "deliverers"), against the Greek "men who have been saved", and "from Mount Zion" against "on/to Mount Zion"? Who do they take these figures to be, and does לִשְׁפֹּט mean "to judge" or "to rule" here?
-   **Why:** the ESV says "to rule" and the NASB95 "to judge", so the ESV's congregation does not hear the link with Judges. BHS proposes "those saved" and "from the mountain". The audit put the Judges pattern (Judg 2:16, 18; Neh 9:27) nearer than Isa 63.
-   **Watch for:** a messianic identification made without argument from the verse. Record who makes it and on what ground.

The commentators divide sharply on whether מוֹשִׁעִים denotes active deliverers or those who have been delivered, and they offer competing interpretations of the preposition and the identity of these figures.

**On **מוֹשִׁעִים **("deliverers" vs. "those who have been saved")**

Baker treats the term as "deliverers" or saviors, citing parallels in Deuteronomy, Judges, and Isaiah, noting they "first ascend on the mountain to liberate it, and from there they serve as governors or rulers."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F506691) Butler similarly identifies the word as referring to "people like Joshua and the judges from the original conquest period who led God's people to dramatic victories."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5F0$5F3439?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_0_3439%2F2021-11-12T22%3A10%3A58Z%2F528996)

However, Patterson and Hill note that "in agreement with the ancient versions, the NLT has translated this active participle ('those who deliver') as a passive participle ('those who have been delivered'),"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F688990) and Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke mention that "some versions read the last word as a passive, those to whom deliverance or salvation has already come, that is, those who have been released from exile and allowed to return," which "could refer to the returned exiles under Zerubbabel."[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F109570)

On the preposition ("from" vs. "on")

Wiseman, Alexander, and Waltke note that "the LXX reads 'from' Mount Zion, the origin of the deliverers rather than their goal, though this is not necessary, and the present form provides a link with verse 17."[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC31OBUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC31OBUS%2F2016-11-14T15%3A29%3A17Z%2F110012) Bridger renders it literally as "those causing salvation will be on \[Heb. bĕ\] Mount Zion."[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BST31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABST31OB%2F2015-11-05T00%3A11%3A17Z%2F198428)

**On the identity and meaning of **לִשְׁפֹּט

Bridger explains that "the word translated govern (21) is the standard verb 'to judge' (Heb. šāpat) in the basic sense of pronouncing a royal or judicial decision, and 'putting things to right'."[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BST31OB?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABST31OB%2F2015-11-05T00%3A11%3A17Z%2F200049) Butler similarly notes that "'Govern' translates Hebrew shepot, the term used for the judges in the Book of Judges."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5F0$5F3439?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_0_3439%2F2021-11-12T22%3A10%3A58Z%2F529348) Patterson and Hill observe that "the Hebrew verb is used of judging but carries with it an administrative sense,"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F689511) while they note that "Othniel and Ehud were called deliverers ([Judg 2:16](https://ref.ly/Jdg2.16); [3:9](https://ref.ly/Jdg3.9), [15](https://ref.ly/Jdg3.15))."[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F689591)

**3.7 Verse 17: **"**their possessions**"** or **"**their dispossessors**"

-   **Ask:** In Obadiah 17, do my commentators read מוֹרָשֵׁיהֶם as "their possessions" or, with the Murabba'at scroll and the versions, as "their dispossessors" (מוֹרִשֵׁיהֶם)? Which do they adopt, and why? How do they take "and it will be holy"?
-   **Why:** the MT and the older witnesses differ, and BHS says to read the second. The ESV's "their own possessions" is an interpretation, and neither English version shows the variant. The second reading would sharpen the reversal *\[I\]*.
-   **Watch for:** "the versions" with none named. Check any claim against the BHS apparatus; I can do this when you bring the answer back.

The commentators divide on whether to adopt the Masoretic text (מוֹרָשֵׁיהֶם, "their possessions") or the variant reading (מוֹרִשֵׁיהֶם, "their dispossessors").

Reading "their possessions" (MT)

Patterson and Hill maintain the MT reading, which may point to Israel's despoiling its enemies' possessions or the claiming of the age-old promise of a land in perpetuity[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F680813). They note this interpretation aligns with the broader theme of territorial restoration throughout verses 17--21.

Reading "their dispossessors" (Ancient Versions)

Patterson and Hill acknowledge that the ancient versions translate "their possessors," reading the participle and giving the sense that the people of Israel will lay claim to those who once possessed them, envisioning the Edomites or all of Israel's enemies[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F680524). However, they present this as an alternative rather than their preferred reading.

On "and it will be holy"

The commentators interpret this phrase in relation to Mount Zion's restoration. The noun may have been chosen to signify that the Holy God who dwells on Mount Zion and who has brought back a redeemed and purified remnant has rendered the city, people, and environs a total sacred continuum[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F680071). It may also imply the rebuilding of the Temple[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F680385).

Baker notes the clause is problematic since its referent is unclear, as it is difficult for "Mount Zion" to be the subject since it is in a prepositional phrase, and Hebrew has a noun "a holy place/thing" rather than a predicate adjective, suggesting that as deliverance is on Zion, so a holy place, a sanctuary, a rebuilt temple will also be on it in due course[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F484021).

The commentators divide on whether לַחְמְךָ means "your bread" or, reading it as a substantive participle (לֹחֲמֶיךָ), "those who eat your bread."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F646151) Baker notes the term appears odd in context and, since it is poetically parallel to the two previously mentioned phrases indicating positive relationships with Edom, must indicate such a relationship, contrasting with some early versions that revocalized the text to read "those who do battle with you."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F440791) Block acknowledges the expression is difficult and has led to various interpretations and conjectural changes, but suggests the word אַנְשֵׁי ("men of") may have dropped out accidentally, or the elliptical expression may be intentional, heightening rhetorical effect through sequenced shortening of the expressions.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F195274) Pusey translates it as "the men of thy bread," maintaining the MT reading.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES28HO%2F2014-10-03T16%3A31%3A51Z%2F1722978)

On "there is no understanding in him," the commentators offer competing referents. Baker argues that a better rendering is "there does not exist any understanding in it/him," referring either to "in Esau" or "in the place of foreigners," with the self-vaunted wisdom of Edom unknown to the new inhabitants of their land.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NIVAC29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANIVAC29JOE%2F2010-03-22T20%3A26%3A31Z%2F443183) Block notes that the shift to third person reflects the context: Obadiah's hypothetical audience may be Esau, but Obadiah is actually talking about him to his own country folk, either the remnant in Jerusalem or the scattered exiles.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F197845) Patterson and Hill cite a parallel construction in [Deuteronomy 32:28](https://ref.ly/Dt32.28) and present multiple interpretations: if Edom had any sense they would know the awful end to which their covenant-breaking behavior must lead; Edom is so undiscerning they cannot anticipate their allies' treachery; the Edomites are bewildered, not knowing what to do; or with non-Edomites occupying the land, Edom's traditional wisdom will be absent.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM28HO?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM28HO%2F2009-06-10T20%3A48%3A58Z%2F647383) None of the commentators treat the clause as a gloss; rather, they debate its referent and meaning.

## 4. History of Interpretation for Claim Audit 1

4.1 Numbers 24:17--21 (audit B3)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators connect Obadiah with Balaam's oracle in Numbers 24:17--21 (a star from Jacob, Edom a possession, the survivor destroyed by one from Jacob, the nest set in the rock)? What wording do they point to, and does anyone reject the link?
-   **Why:** *moderate--high*. The old connection of Num 24:17--18 with Edom is known; reading Obad 4 and 18 from it is, as far as the auditor could recall, not standard. Your answer would test that.
-   **Watch for:** Num 24:18 alone cited as "Edom's fate", without any point about Obadiah's wording. Record it, but it does not meet the criterion.

Block identifies a connection between [Obadiah 18](https://ref.ly/Ob18) and Balaam's oracle in [Numbers 24:18--19](https://ref.ly/Nu24.18-19), noting that both texts employ the language of possession and survivors. Balaam's prophecy declares: "Edom will become a possession \[יְרֵשָׁה\], Seir a possession of its enemies, while Israel does valiantly. One out of Jacob shall rule, and destroy the survivors \[שָׂרִיד\] of Ir."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F267153) Block observes that Obadiah's statement in verse 18d--f similarly "summarizes the divine mandate for the houses of Jacob and Joseph: burn and consume the house of Esau, leaving no survivor (שָׂרִיד)."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F266174) The shared vocabulary---particularly the term שָׂרִיד ("survivor")---and the parallel motif of Edom's complete destruction establish the intertextual relationship.

Fausset also connects the texts, citing [Numbers 24:21](https://ref.ly/Nu24.21) ("Thou puttest thy nest in a rock") alongside [Obadiah 4](https://ref.ly/Ob4) ("Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down, saith the Lord").[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F3038899) In his commentary on [Jeremiah 49](https://ref.ly/Je49), Fausset again references this imagery, noting that Jeremiah's phrase "though thou shouldest make thy nest as high as the eagle" echoes both [Job 39:27](https://ref.ly/Job39.27) and [Obadiah 3--4](https://ref.ly/Ob3-4), with the eagle building "its nest in the highest craggy eyrie."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACMMNTRYCRTCLEXPRMNTLPRCTLVOL4%2F2018-09-25T01%3A07%3A51Z%2F693195)

Block's comprehensive analysis lists the parallels systematically: "No survivors in Edom/Esau ([Num 24:19](https://ref.ly/Nu24.19))" and "Land of Edom/Esau being dispossessed ([Num 24:18](https://ref.ly/Nu24.18); [Amos 9:12](https://ref.ly/Am9.12))."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F137932) None of the commentators in the collection reject this connection; rather, they treat it as a recognized intertextual echo reinforcing Obadiah's eschatological vision of Edom's complete destruction.

4.2 Micah 7:8--10 (audit A8)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators on Obadiah or Micah discuss a link between Obadiah 10--12 and Micah 7:8--10 ("do not rejoice over me", "shame will cover her", "my eyes will look on her")? Do they treat it as dependence, as shared lament language, or otherwise?

-   **Watch for:** the rejoicing motif alone offered as the link. It is common, and it is the shame clause that carries the claim.

The search results do not directly address a connection between [Obadiah 10--12](https://ref.ly/Ob10-12) and [Micah 7:8--10](https://ref.ly/Mic7.8-10). However, Block does identify a significant intertextual link between these passages.

Block notes that [Obadiah 10](https://ref.ly/Ob10) picks up on notions expressed in [Micah 7:10](https://ref.ly/Mic7.10), where the enemy will see vindication and shame will cover her who said "Where is the LORD your God?" with the speaker's eyes beholding her downfall as she is trodden down like mire in the streets.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F205078) The connection centers on the motif of shame covering the enemy and the vindication of God's people through the enemy's humiliation.

Block explains that when Obadiah declares shame will cover Esau, this shame in the Old Testament relates less to internal guilt than to loss of status and prestige---those once respected have been disgraced.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F204418) Since shame represents loss of honor within the social context rather than felt emotion, Esau could be covered with shame even when gone, as his standing among the nations would be totally destroyed.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F205356)

Block treats this as a shared rhetorical and theological tradition rather than direct literary dependence. Both prophets employ the language of shame and vindication to express how God's people will witness their enemies' reversal of fortune. The parallel language---particularly the imagery of shame covering the enemy and the speaker's eyes beholding the enemy's downfall---suggests both Obadiah and Micah draw on common prophetic vocabulary for expressing divine vindication, though Block does not explicitly argue for textual dependence in either direction.

4.5 Amos 9:12 and the place of Obadiah in the Twelve (audit B2)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators explain the placing of Obadiah straight after Amos by Amos 9:12 ("that they may possess the remnant of Edom")? What wording do they point to? Does anyone argue against reading it as editorial design, and do any discuss the different order in the Greek?

Smith, Ward, and Bewer explain that Obadiah's placement immediately after Amos in the Hebrew canon stems not from historical considerations but from the editor's perception that Obadiah expands upon [Amos 9:12](https://ref.ly/Am9.12), prompting him to position it after Amos.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F72176) Schnurrer demonstrated this connection as early as 1787.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ICC$5FOBJOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3AICC_OBJOE%2F2017-10-26T22%3A47%3A14Z%2F72431) Mason notes that many scholars have observed Obadiah was likely placed where it is because it functions as commentary on [Amos 9:11--12](https://ref.ly/Am9.11-12), which promises that God's people "may possess the remnant of Edom and all the nations who are called by my name."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHEFFCL33MIC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHEFFCL33MIC%2F2014-10-04T08%3A49%3A18Z%2F189563)

Regarding arguments against reading this as editorial design, Block notes that while MT logically places Obadiah after Amos because both concern Edom, the canonical placement invites reading Obadiah in light of Amos, though the separate historical and cultural circumstances of each prophetic book provide a more important control for interpretation than their location in the canon.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F94692) This suggests some caution about over-interpreting the placement as intentional editorial design, though the commentators do not explicitly reject it.

Block observes that in the Hebrew Bible Obadiah is the fourth book between Amos and Jonah, but in the Greek OT (LXX), Obadiah occurs between Joel and Jonah.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM31OB2ED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM31OB2ED%2F2019-03-30T00%3A21%3A34Z%2F93585) This textual variation demonstrates that the ordering was not uniformly fixed in antiquity, complicating any theory of deliberate editorial positioning.

חָמָס *ḥāmās* **violence**

S[^b242] [*2554*](https://ref.ly/logosref/HebrewStrongs.2554); BDB[^b243] [*329a*](https://ref.ly/logosref/BrownDriverBriggs.BDB_329.1); *HALOT*[^b244] 1:[*329a*](https://ref.ly/logosres/hal?ref=Page.p+329); *TDOT*[^b245] [*4:478--87*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TDOT.Vol._4,_Pg._478-487); [*TWOT*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TWOT.TWOT_No._678)[^b246][* 678*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TWOT.TWOT_No._678); *NIDOTTE*[^b247] [*2803*](https://ref.ly/logosres/nidotte?pos=HET.207)

> 1\. The word group *ḥms* qal "to act violently," ni.[^b248] "to suffer violence," and *ḥāmās* "violence" seems to have few direct counterparts outside texts dependent upon the OT; worthy of mention are Yaudi *ḥms* "abominable act" (*KAI*[^b249] no. 214.26 in a fragmentary context) and Imp. Aram.[^b250] *śhd ḥms* "witness who exercises injustice" (Aḥ.[^b251] 140 \[[*ANET*](https://ref.ly/logosref/ANET.ANET_429)[^b252][* 429b*](https://ref.ly/logosref/ANET.ANET_429) "false witness"\] = Hebr.[^b253] *ʿēd ḥāmās*). In the event that *ḥms* should be associated with the root *ḥmṣ* II (Hebr.[^b254] *ḥmṣ* qal "to oppress," [*Psa 71:4*](https://ref.ly/Ps71.4); subst.[^b255] *ḥāmôṣ* "oppressor," [*Isa 1:17*](https://ref.ly/Is1.17) txt?[^b256], read *ḥāmûṣ* "oppressed"; cf. Wildberger, *Isa 1--12*, CC[^b257], [*36*](https://ref.ly/logosres/ccs23is01?ref=Page.p+36)), then Aram.[^b258], Akk.[^b259], and Eth.[^b260] equivalents may be included.

*HAL*[^b261] 316a distinguishes the verb in [*Job 21:27*](https://ref.ly/Job21.27) as *ḥms* II "to devise."

> 2\. The qal occurs 6x ([*Jer 22:3*](https://ref.ly/Je22.3); [*Ezek 22:26*](https://ref.ly/Eze22.26); [*Zeph 3:4*](https://ref.ly/Zep3.4); [*Prov 8:36*](https://ref.ly/Pr8.36); [*Lam 2:6*](https://ref.ly/La2.6); in [*Job 15:33*](https://ref.ly/Job15.33) of dropping fruits; excl.[^b262] [*Job 21:27*](https://ref.ly/Job21.27)), the ni.[^b263] 1x ([*Jer 13:22*](https://ref.ly/Je13.22)). The noun is more frequent: 60x (excl.[^b264] [*Ezek 9:9*](https://ref.ly/Eze9.9), where some MSS have *ḥāmās* instead of *dāmîm*; Psa 14x, Prov 7x, Ezek and Hab 6x each, Gen and Jer 4x each).

> 3\. (a) *ḥamas* is usually sg.[^b265]; the pl.[^b266] forms in [*2 Sam 22:49*](https://ref.ly/2Sa22.49) ([*Psa 18:49*](https://ref.ly/Ps18.48) sg.!), [*Psa 140:2*](https://ref.ly/Ps140.1), [*5*](https://ref.ly/Ps140.4) (v [*12*](https://ref.ly/Ps140.11) sg.[^b267]), and [*Prov 4:17*](https://ref.ly/Pr4.17) form attributive descriptions ("*ḥāmās*-full" man or wine) as an extension of the sg.[^b268] form (*ʾîš ḥāmās*, etc.; cf. [*Prov 3:31*](https://ref.ly/Pr3.31); [*16:29*](https://ref.ly/Pr16.29)). As a rule, gens. accompanying nouns indicate the obj.[^b269] of the violence ([*Judg 9:24*](https://ref.ly/Jdg9.24); [*Joel 4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19); [*Obad 10*](https://ref.ly/Ob10); [*Hab 2:8*](https://ref.ly/Hab2.8), [*17*](https://ref.ly/Hab2.17); with pron.[^b270] suf.[^b271], [*Gen 16:5*](https://ref.ly/Ge16.5); [*Jer 51:35*](https://ref.ly/Je51.35)), rarely the actor ([*Ezek 12:19*](https://ref.ly/Eze12.19); with pron.[^b272] suf.[^b273], [*Psa 7:17*](https://ref.ly/Ps7.16)).

Prophetic diction often uses *šōd* "misdeed, destruction" as a synonym for *ḥāmās* ([*Isa 60:18*](https://ref.ly/Is60.18); [*Jer 6:7*](https://ref.ly/Je6.7); [*20:8*](https://ref.ly/Je20.8); [*Ezek 45:9*](https://ref.ly/Eze45.9); [*Amos 3:10*](https://ref.ly/Am3.10); [*Hab 1:3*](https://ref.ly/Hab1.3); [*2:17*](https://ref.ly/Hab2.17); *šōd* occurs a total of 25x in the OT, with the exceptions of [*Psa 12:6*](https://ref.ly/Ps12.5); [*Job 5:21f.*](https://ref.ly/Job5.21); [*Prov 21:7*](https://ref.ly/Pr21.7); and [*24:2*](https://ref.ly/Pr24.2) only in prophetic books; in addition, *šdd* qal "to destroy, rape" occurs 32x, ni.[^b274] 1x, pi.[^b275] 2x, pu.[^b276] 20x, po.[^b277] 1x, and ho.[^b278] 2x, likewise almost exclusively in the Prophets, rarely in Psa, Job, and Prov, elsewhere only in [*Judg 5:27*](https://ref.ly/Jdg5.27)). The difference may lie in the fact that *šōd* emphasizes the active doing, *ḥāmās* the nature or consequence of the deed.

> \(b\) Religious and a profane usage may not be strictly distinguished because *ḥāmās*, even if interpersonal, violates an order established or guaranteed by God (von Rad, [^b279]*Theol*[^b280].[^b281] 1:157n.34). Nevertheless, various aspects of the concept may be noted.

> The word is anchored first in the legal sphere (R. Knierim, "[*Cht und Chms. Zwei Begriffe für Sünde in Israel und ihr Sitz im Leben*](https://ref.ly/logosref/biblio.at$3DCht$2520und$2520Chms.$2520Zwei$2520Begriffe$2520f$C3$BCr$2520S$C3$BCnde$2520in$2520Israel$2520und$2520ihr$2520Sitz$2520im$2520Leben$7Cau$3DR.$2520Knierim$7Cde$3Ddissertation$7Clbid$3D0000958306$7Cpg$3D125$7Cpl$3DHeidelberg$7Cyr$3D1957)" \[diss.[^b282], Heidelberg, 1957\], 125ff.), but already had various usages there.

In the independent *ḥāmās* cry ([*Hab 1:2*](https://ref.ly/Hab1.2); [*Job 19:7*](https://ref.ly/Job19.7); on [*Jer 20:8*](https://ref.ly/Je20.8), see [*4*](https://ref.ly/logosres/tlot?pos=HETH.15%3aR7166)), von Rad (*Theol*. 1:157n.34), Knierim (op. cit. 129ff.), and H. J. Boecker (*Redeformen des Rechtslebens im AT*[^b283] \[1964\], 60f.) see a cry of the legal community for legal protection (the opening of a hearing). Because Yahweh is addressed in both cases, however, these passages more likely deal with a direct cry for help (similarly, *šmʿ* ni.[^b284] in [*Isa 60:18*](https://ref.ly/Is60.18) and [*Jer 6:7*](https://ref.ly/Je6.7) should also be understood as "to be heard by" \[contra Knierim, op. cit. 131\], because *ḥāmās* in [*Jer 6:7*](https://ref.ly/Je6.7) is combined with *šōd* and [*Isa 60:18*](https://ref.ly/Is60.18) speaks of foreign oppression).

> The expression *ʿēd ḥāmās* ([*Exod 23:1*](https://ref.ly/Ex23.1); [*Deut 19:16*](https://ref.ly/Dt19.16)) probably indicated originally the plaintiff in a case of *ḥāmās* (not the witness who perverts justice; [*Deut 19:18*](https://ref.ly/Dt19.18) indicates explicitly after subsequent investigation that the accusation is false by *šeqer* "deceit"), but then in a shift of meaning generally the "violent, law-breaking witness." *ḥāmās* may indeed have originally meant the misdeed that objectively burdens the land and disturbs its relationship and its inhabitants' relationship to God, so that anyone who knows of it must appear as plaintiff before the legal community in order to divert the consequences (cf. H. J. Stoebe, *WD*[^b285] 3 \[1952\]: 121ff.; on → [*ʿēd*](https://ref.ly/logosres/tlot?pos=AYIN.5) as "plaintiff" see also B. Gemser, *SVT*[^b286] 3 \[1955\]: 130; contra Knierim, op. cit. 127f., although he emphasizes correctly the term's connotation of a concept of an independent sphere of action, op. cit. 135). The law in [*Deut 19:15--19*](https://ref.ly/Dt19.15-19) also lies on the level of regulations concerning murder committed by an unknown hand ([*Deut 21:1--9*](https://ref.ly/Dt21.1-9)), and the series of curses ([*Deut 27:15--26*](https://ref.ly/Dt27.15-26); cf. [*Lev 5:1*](https://ref.ly/Le5.1)) gives evidence of an advanced legal praxis concerned for legal certainty ([*Deut 19:15*](https://ref.ly/Dt19.15)).

> This element is also active where *ḥāmās* is combined with *mlʾ* "to be full" (land, city, and secondarily the temple; [*Gen 6:11*](https://ref.ly/Ge6.11), [*13*](https://ref.ly/Ge6.13); [*Ezek 7:23*](https://ref.ly/Eze7.23); [*8:17*](https://ref.ly/Eze8.17); [*28:16*](https://ref.ly/Eze28.16); [*Mic 6:12*](https://ref.ly/Mic6.12); [*Zeph 1:9*](https://ref.ly/Zep1.9)). If the land is full of *ḥāmās*, then the consequence for its inhabitants is punishment and destruction. This understanding is esp. clear in [*Gen 6:13*](https://ref.ly/Ge6.13); despite later testimony (P[^b287]), it is not a late theologoumenon (contra Knierim, op. cit. 134), but the direct consequence of the original meaning.

> A similar situation pertains where the *ḥāmās* procedure itself overtakes the perpetrator ([*Judg 9:24*](https://ref.ly/Jdg9.24) with *bôʾ* "to come"; [*Psa 7:17*](https://ref.ly/Ps7.16) with *yrd* "to descend"), or *ḥāmās* is otherwise bound to *ʿal* "on" ([*Gen 16:5*](https://ref.ly/Ge16.5) J[^b288]; [*Jer 51:35*](https://ref.ly/Je51.35); [*Mal 2:16*](https://ref.ly/Mal2.16); [*Gen 16:5*](https://ref.ly/Ge16.5) seems ancient: the consequence of the *ḥāmās* that lies upon Sarai is barrenness; she lays it on Abram as cosufferer and thus characterizes the gravity of Hagar's deed; cf. Knierim, op. cit. 134; von Rad, *Gen*, OTL[^b289] \[1972^2^\], 192).

> 4\. The substance of such a *ḥāmās*, even in the Prophets, is the spilling of blood ([*Gen 49:5f.*](https://ref.ly/Ge49.5); [*Judg 9:24*](https://ref.ly/Jdg9.24); [*Isa 59:6*](https://ref.ly/Is59.6); [*Jer 51:35*](https://ref.ly/Je51.35); [*Ezek 7:23*](https://ref.ly/Eze7.23); [*Joel 4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19); in [*Jer 22:3*](https://ref.ly/Je22.3) *ḥms* qal) and presumably moral crimes (reference to this only in [*Jer 13:22*](https://ref.ly/Je13.22) ni.[^b290]) that pollute the land in the same way ([*Lev 18:28*](https://ref.ly/Le18.28); [*20:22*](https://ref.ly/Le20.22)) and also stand under the verdict of capital punishment in the law (e.g., [*Lev 20:11--18*](https://ref.ly/Le20.11-18)).

According to a promising suggestion by J. Berridge, *Prophet, People, and the Word of Yahweh* (1970), 152--54 (cf. also S. Marrow, *VD*[^b291] 43 \[1965\]: 241--55), [*Jer 20:8*](https://ref.ly/Je20.8) could be influenced by the preceding rape metaphor; *ḥāmās* would be the maiden's cry for help (cf. [*Deut 22:24*](https://ref.ly/Dt22.24)), not the cry for legal protection. Whether [*Prov 26:6*](https://ref.ly/Pr26.6) also belongs in this context (cf. Gemser, HAT[^b292] 16, 94) may not be decided given the uncertainty of the text.

> Now, independent spheres of action in the OT are to be delineated theoretically and not precisely, because the concept behind them is a borrowed form that does not itself demonstrate the essence of OT thought but is given new content (cf. N. H. Ridderbos, *GTT*[^b293] 64 \[1964\]: 226ff.). The revelation unique to the OT focuses attention upon human responsibility in the relationship between Yahweh and his people. Thus the emphasis of the word lies on the perpetrator and his individual guilt. This analysis results less from the formulations ([*Isa 59:6*](https://ref.ly/Is59.6); [*Ezek 12:19*](https://ref.ly/Eze12.19); [*Jonah 3:8*](https://ref.ly/Jon3.8); [*Mic 6:12*](https://ref.ly/Mic6.12); [*Mal 2:16*](https://ref.ly/Mal2.16); [*Job 16:17*](https://ref.ly/Job16.17); [*1 Chron 12:18*](https://ref.ly/1Ch12.17), often with *yād/kap* "hand" in which there is *ḥāmās*; these texts could---even if with reservations---be included with the passages discussed earlier) than from the sphere of the acts so categorized. Combined with *bgd* "to act deceitfully" ([*Zeph 3:4*](https://ref.ly/Zep3.4); [*Mal 2:16*](https://ref.ly/Mal2.16); [*Prov 13:2*](https://ref.ly/Pr13.2); similarly [*16:29*](https://ref.ly/Pr16.29) with *pth* pi.[^b294] "to delude") *ḥāmās* characterizes the diminution of another's rights and living space as violation of duty to the neighbor and encompasses the entire range of antisocial behavior ([*Amos 3:10*](https://ref.ly/Am3.10)) in opposition to justice and righteousness ([*Jer 22:3*](https://ref.ly/Je22.3); [*Ezek 45:9*](https://ref.ly/Eze45.9)). Arrogance ([*Psa 73:6*](https://ref.ly/Ps73.6)), deceit ([*Zeph 1:9*](https://ref.ly/Zep1.9)) in speech ([*Mic 6:12*](https://ref.ly/Mic6.12); cf. [*Prov 10:6*](https://ref.ly/Pr10.6), [*11*](https://ref.ly/Pr10.11) and, conversely, the description of the servant's virtue in [*Isa 53:9*](https://ref.ly/Is53.9)), and, finally, improper legal proceedings ([*Psa 55:10*](https://ref.ly/Ps55.9); [*Hab 1:3*](https://ref.ly/Hab1.3) txt?[^b295]) are named.

> [*Ezek 22:26*](https://ref.ly/Eze22.26) and [*Zeph 3:4*](https://ref.ly/Zep3.4) mention attacks on Yahweh's Torah (*ḥms* qal, subj.[^b296], the priests). Yahweh himself turns against the *ḥāmās* that he hates ([*Psa 11:5*](https://ref.ly/Ps11.5)), from which he delivers ([*2 Sam 22:3*](https://ref.ly/2Sa22.3), [*49*](https://ref.ly/2Sa22.49) = [*Psa 18:3*](https://ref.ly/Ps18.2), [*49*](https://ref.ly/Ps18.48); [*Psa 140:2*](https://ref.ly/Ps140.1), [*5*](https://ref.ly/Ps140.4); cf. [*Psa 72:14*](https://ref.ly/Ps72.14), the king under Yahweh's commission), because of which one calls to him ([*Hab 1:2*](https://ref.ly/Hab1.2); [*Psa 25:19*](https://ref.ly/Ps25.19)), and which he avenges ([*Ezek 7:23*](https://ref.ly/Eze7.23); [*8:17f.*](https://ref.ly/Eze8.17); [*12:19*](https://ref.ly/Eze12.19); [*28:16*](https://ref.ly/Eze28.16); [*Zeph 1:9*](https://ref.ly/Zep1.9)). If, paradoxically, *ḥāmās* comes from Yahweh himself ([*Job 19:7*](https://ref.ly/Job19.7)), there is no remedy for it.

> Thus *ḥāmās* becomes an encompassing term for sin per se ([*Ezek 7:11*](https://ref.ly/Eze7.11); [*Jonah 3:8*](https://ref.ly/Jon3.8); cf. also the phrase *ʾîš ḥāmās/ḥ*^*a*^*māsîm*; see [*3a*](https://ref.ly/logosres/tlot?pos=HETH.15%3aR7164)). Characteristically, too, *ḥāmās* is practiced not only in Israel but also by foreign nations against Israel ([*Joel 4:19*](https://ref.ly/Joe3.19); [*Obad 10*](https://ref.ly/Ob10); [*Hab 1:9*](https://ref.ly/Hab1.9); [*2:8*](https://ref.ly/Hab2.8), [*17*](https://ref.ly/Hab2.17)). This development is the context in which psalmic diction equates *ʿēd šeqer* "lying witness" and *ʿēd ḥāmās* ([*Psa 35:11*](https://ref.ly/Ps35.11); cf. [*27:12*](https://ref.ly/Ps27.12), → [*kzb 3a*](https://ref.ly/logosres/tlot?pos=KAPH.4%3aR5542)), an equation that was not original (see [*3b*](https://ref.ly/logosres/tlot?pos=HETH.15%3aR7165)).

> 5\. In the Qumran texts *ḥāmās* finds little usage (Kuhn, [^b297]*Konk*[^b298].[^b299] 73c; also *GCDS*[^b300] 256). On the LXX and the NT, cf. G. Schrenk, "ἄδικος," [*TDNT*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TDNT.Vol._1,_Pg._149-163)[^b301][* 1:149--63*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TDNT.Vol._1,_Pg._149-163); W. Gutbrod, "ἀνομία," [*TDNT*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TDNT.Vol._4,_Pg._1085-1087)[^b302][* 4:1085--87*](https://ref.ly/logosref/TDNT.Vol._4,_Pg._1085-1087).
