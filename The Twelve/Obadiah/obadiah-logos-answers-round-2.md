Plural forms in Ezekiel 35

\> How do commentators on Ezekiel 35 explain the plural forms addressed to Mount Seir in 35:9 (“you will know”) and 35:13 (“you magnified yourselves… against me with your mouth”), in an oracle that otherwise addresses Edom in the singular? Do any compare this with the plural “you drank” in Obadiah 16?

The search results do not directly address the specific grammatical question about plural forms in [Ezekiel 35:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.26.35.9) and 35:13, nor do they compare these with [Obadiah 16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.31.1.16). However, the commentaries provide relevant context about the oracle’s structure and addressee.

The oracle to Mount Seir employs personification, with the prophet addressing the land itself as a meaningful participant in historical events, giving the mountains “personal attributes of speaking, acting, and thinking.”[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM26EZE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM26EZE%2F2011-11-10T21%3A51%3A39Z%2F782192) This rhetorical device—treating a geographical entity as a character capable of response—could explain the shift to plural forms. When the prophet moves from addressing Mount Seir’s identity (singular, referential) to depicting its actions and knowledge (plural, participatory), the plural may reflect the collective population or inhabitants represented by the land itself.

Mount Seir refers to Edom and was chosen as representative of nations seeking to occupy Israel’s land.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EBC06?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEBC06%2F2019-03-16T01%3A51%3A53Z%2F2241402) The plural pronouns in verses 9 and 13 might thus address not merely the geographical feature but the people inhabiting it—shifting from the singular toponym to the plural collective of Edomites who would “know” God’s judgment and who “magnified yourselves… against me.”

The commentaries emphasize Yahweh’s grievances against Mount Seir, citing its “eternal hatred” of Israel and a history of animosity reaching back to their mutual existence as organized lands,[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM26EZE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM26EZE%2F2011-11-10T21%3A51%3A39Z%2F783545) but they do not explicitly analyze the grammatical shift or offer comparative analysis with [Obadiah 16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.31.1.16)’s plural “you drank.” A more detailed grammatical or comparative study would be needed to fully address your question.

**\> Among the reconstructions of Obadiah’s composition that Hadjiev lists, does any treat verses 1–9 (the material shared with Jeremiah 49) as an older oracle, separate from verses 10–14? Who holds that view, and on what grounds?**

Yes, Assis treats verses 1–9 as a late pre-exilic prophecy accusing Edom of arrogance, which was then updated after 587 BCE with an oracle in verses 10–14 and 15b accusing Edom of participating in Jerusalem’s destruction, followed by later additions in verses 15a, 16–21 concerning Edomite appropriation of Judean land[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F84987).

This reconstruction isolates verses 1–9 from the later material by identifying distinct historical occasions for each compositional layer. The shared material with [Jeremiah 49](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.24.49) (verses 1–9) functions as the original unit, addressing Edom’s pride in isolation. Assis dates this initial prophecy to the late pre-exilic period[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F84987), before the catastrophic events that prompted the subsequent accusations. The grounds for this separation rest on recognizing how the text’s accusations shift: the earlier oracle focuses on Edom’s self-confidence and military strength, while the later additions pivot to specific crimes against Judah during and after Jerusalem’s fall.

This approach aligns with the broader pattern Hadjiev documents—that most scholars accept Obadiah as composite, though they envision its evolution differently[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TTCSGOT29JOE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATTCSGOT29JOE%2F2021-06-08T20%3A02%3A46Z%2F84473). Assis’s model is distinctive in explicitly treating verses 1–9 as a pre-existing, temporally distinct piece that was deliberately supplemented rather than integrated into a unified composition.

\> What textual arguments do commentators give for the direction of dependence between Jeremiah 48 and Isaiah 15–16? Do any rely on Isaiah 16:13 (“this is the word the LORD spoke concerning Moab long ago”)?

The search results present two distinct approaches to the relationship between these texts, with limited reliance on [Isaiah 16:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.16.13).

[Jeremiah 48](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.24.48) expands upon [Isaiah 15–16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.15-23.16)[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES20PR?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES20PR%2F2014-10-03T16%3A28%3A03Z%2F614287), according to Barnes’s commentary, which represents the traditional view of Jeremiah’s dependence on Isaiah. However, Barnes offers a nuanced alternative: [Isaiah 16:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.16.13) suggests that Isaiah drew from an ancient prophecy, and Jeremiah may have accessed the same earlier source[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES20PR?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES20PR%2F2014-10-03T16%3A28%3A03Z%2F614867). This argument sidesteps direct literary dependence by positing a shared archival tradition. Given the many prophetical writings mentioned in Chronicles that no longer survive, such a supposition lacks inherent implausibility[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BARNES20PR?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABARNES20PR%2F2014-10-03T16%3A28%3A03Z%2F615001).

A contrasting view emerges from Lalleman, who argues against simple dependence in either direction. The resemblances between [Isaiah 15–16](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.15-23.16) and [Jeremiah 48](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.24.48) do not necessarily indicate dependence; Isaiah reads as an extended lament, while Jeremiah combines laments with explicit judgment pronouncements[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC24JELALLEMAN?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC24JELALLEMAN%2F2017-07-21T01%3A12%3A30Z%2F598740). A much older text in [Numbers 21:27–30](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.4.21.27-4.21.30) demonstrably influenced both prophecies[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TOTC24JELALLEMAN?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATOTC24JELALLEMAN%2F2017-07-21T01%3A12%3A30Z%2F599015), suggesting that both prophets drew independently from earlier material rather than one copying the other.

The Lange commentary takes a middle position, acknowledging Jeremiah’s extensive use of Isaiah while recognizing broader literary sources. Isaiah’s prophecy itself reproduces an older oracle ([Isaiah 16:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.16.13))[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE24JE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE24JE%2F2025-01-24T00%3A22%3A32Z%2F1925488), and from verse 29 onward, Jeremiah makes constant, more or less free use of older utterances, making extensive use of Isaiah particularly in verses 29–38[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LANGE24JE?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALANGE24JE%2F2025-01-24T00%3A22%3A32Z%2F1925402).

Notably, only Barnes explicitly invokes [Isaiah 16:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/bible$2Bnasb95.23.16.13) as textual evidence for the direction of dependence—and paradoxically, he uses it to argue *against* direct Jeremianic dependence on Isaiah, suggesting instead that both prophets accessed a common ancient source.

- **O35, Mal 3:19 (Eng 4:1) with Obadiah:** Which commentators relate Malachi 4:1 (the arrogant as stubble on the coming day) to Obadiah 3, 15 and 18?

Keil–Delitzsch explicitly connects [Malachi 4:1](https://ref.ly/Mal4.1) to [Obadiah 18](https://ref.ly/Ob18), noting that the ungodly will resemble stubble consumed by fire, citing [Isaiah 5:24](https://ref.ly/Is5.24), [Zephaniah 1:18](https://ref.ly/Zep1.18), and [Obadiah 18](https://ref.ly/Ob18) as parallel passages[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.2.11?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.2.11%2F2019-08-01T22%3A56%3A47Z%2F27403164).

Baker’s commentary on Malachi appears in the search results but does not explicitly make these connections.

- **O37, Gen 32:9 (Eng 32:8) at vv. 17–18:** Does any commentator connect “escape” in Obadiah 17 with Jacob’s “the camp which is left will escape” before Esau in Genesis 32:8 (English numbering)?

The connection is thematically apt: Jacob’s strategy of dividing his camp to ensure survival when facing Esau’s threat parallels the promise that Mount Zion will become a place of escape for Jacob’s descendants when facing divine judgment on the nations.
