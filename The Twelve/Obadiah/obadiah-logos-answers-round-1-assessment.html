Summary

  • Do not use the Assistant’s answers to 2.1 and 2.2 on Jeremiah as they stand. It says Patterson–Hill favour a common source, and “most scholars” with them. Your paste of their page says the opposite: “the second option”, Obadiah depending on Jeremiah, citing Raabe. The “most scholars” is the Assistant’s own.
  • Who drank at v. 16: still uncertain, and I have withdrawn my upgrade. Block and Baker argue for Judah from the plural, and Baker’s count holds. But Ezek 35:13, in an oracle to Edom, itself shifts from singular to plural and back (WLC), so a plural need not mean a new addressee. Keil reads Edom carousing. The lean stays low–moderate.
  • Verses 12–14: all the owned commentators returned agree the Hebrew is prohibition, and nearly all that the events are past. They divide on the force: an imaginative return to the event, or a prohibition with ongoing force (Block; in a different form, Keil). I recommend moderate–high for a past referent only, with the force left open. The ESV’s “do not” should stay.
  • Date: Keil is now a direct holder of the Jehoram date, with a real argument: Obadiah nowhere mentions a destruction. Block, Bewer, Hadjiev, Bridger and Baker set vv. 10–14 after 586. Keil’s case overreads 2 Chr 21:17, but it is enough that I withdraw my upgrade: the setting stays at moderate. The charge against Edom in 586 is separately contested (Bartlett), and the histories do not make it.
  • Verse 17: the NET note and Lexham Textual Notes imply that the Greek supports “their possessions”. It does not. Rahlfs and Swete both read τοὺς κατακληρονομήσαντας αὐτούς (“those who dispossessed them”), as BHS and Patterson–Hill indicate.
  • Four new findings came from following commentators’ references:
    • Ezek 35:5, 13 (Block), with the plural noted above.
    • Jer 20:10 (Baker) has friends, deception and “prevail” together, which the audit’s search missed.
    • Deut 32:28–29 (Patterson–Hill) bears on vv. 7–8.
    • 1 Esd 4:45 (Bewer) does not name Edom in Swete. It reads “the Judeans”, while Rahlfs reads “the Edomites”.
  • New Testament use: Jones lists one link, Obad 21 with Rev 11:15, which is the one the overview already has. He gives no argument. Schnittjer lists none (“n/a”), and grades only Jer 49 and Joel as real use within the Old Testament. He treats most other parallels as stock phrases, and Num 24:18 among them. That is a useful sceptical check on the audit’s moderate contacts.
  • Not returned: the other commentators at v. 16; “against her” at v. 1.
  • Monday’s list is now much shorter. Block’s list of parallels and the other pastes answer or remove most of group 4. See section 9.

The early date reaches you through Patterson–Hill only: Keil and Niehaus for the ninth century, Pusey for the eighth. Pusey and Fausset are owned and could be asked directly. Niehaus is not. Keil–Delitzsch is owned and has yet to be consulted on Obadiah (section 1).

What the arguments add:

  • Against Jehoram. Block agrees with the overview: 2 Chr 21:16–17 has Philistines and Arabs, with no Edom and no wholesale exile. He also notes that וַיִּשְׁבּוּ (“they carried off”) is the verb of Obad 11, a real contact (2 Chr 21:17, WLC) that the overview does not mention.
  • Verse 19 (Block). It assumes Ephraim, Samaria and Gilead have been lost, so it comes after the Assyrian conquests. This is [I], and it depends on reading v. 19 as original.
  • Sepharad works against an early sixth-century date (Hadjiev). See section 2.4.
  • The unity of the book is now a live question. Hadjiev says that most accept the book as composite, and sets out five reasons and five reconstructions; Bewer, Wolff, Rudolph and Barton separate vv. 15a and 16–21. Allen, Renkema, Raabe and Block argue for coherence. Block says that “no details… demand such a late date”. The overview mentions the seam but not this consequence. One of Hadjiev’s reasons, the change of addressee at v. 16, depends on the disputed reading in section 4.3. Mount Esau and the twelve uses of “day” span the supposed layers (WLC) [T], as he grants.

Keil’s case for Jehoram [S: paste]. It is the fullest argument for an early date you have:

  • Obadiah never mentions a destruction. Ps 137:7 and Ezek 35 plainly do, and Keil accepts that. Obadiah speaks only of a capture and plunder. The text bears him out on this [T]: no temple, no burning, no fall of the kingdom. Bartlett’s “not specific enough” points the same way, from the opposite side.
  • The epithets do not settle it. נָכְרוֹ (“his misfortune”), אֵידוֹ (“his calamity”) and אָבְדָם (“their destruction”) show a great calamity, not a particular one.
  • Sequence. Obadiah comes before Jeremiah 49, which Keil dates to Jehoiakim’s fourth year, and before Joel. He also uses the book’s place in the Twelve.

Where it is weak:

  • 2 Chr 21:17 does not say what he says. He has the raiders force their way “into Jerusalem”. The verse has Judah and בֵית־הַמֶּלֶךְ (“the king’s house”) and names neither Jerusalem nor Edom (WLC) [T]. He supplies Edom’s part from Joel 3:19 and Amos 1:11.
  • Jer 49:7 carries no date (WLC) [T]. The fourth-year date comes from Jer 46:2, whose heading concerns Egypt, so his sequence rests on an inference. Bewer notes the same assumption.
  • He does not discuss גָּלוּת יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“the exile of Jerusalem”, v. 20) here, which the overview treats as the best terminological evidence. Block’s footnote agrees with the overview: Jer 40:1 and Jer 1:3.
  • The place in the Twelve differs in the Greek.

Assessment. I earlier recommended moderate–high for the setting and now withdraw that. After 586 remains the leading reading, chiefly because Ps 137:7 is the one text that puts Edom and “the day of Jerusalem” together. But Keil’s point about what Obadiah omits is real, and it is the same weakness Bartlett exploits. The setting stays at moderate, with the finished book’s date left open.

2.2 Edom’s part in 586 (question 1.2)

  • The case against is now named. Bartlett argues there is no hard evidence; the charges are not specific to 587; Jeremiah and Kings do not allude to collusion; and Jer 40:11 suggests the opposite. Tebes calls the charge a “stab-in-the-back” myth [S: paste, Hadjiev 45–46]. Neither work is owned.
  • Hadjiev does not side with Bartlett, which the Assistant’s answer hid. Na’aman (2016) answers him from Arad Ostracon 24 and the Negev letters: Edomite pressure was severe, and “the traditional theory… still provides the best explanation” [S: paste]. But Jer 13:19, one of Na’aman’s texts, does not name Edom (WLC) [T].
  • The corpus supports Bartlett on the histories. Edom is not named in 2 Kgs 24–25, Jer 39, Jer 52 or 2 Chr 36. Across Jer 40–43 it appears only at Jer 40:11, as a place of refuge (WLC) [T]. Block himself quotes Jer 40:11–12.
  • The positive case rests on prophecy and poetry, plus indirect archaeology: Ps 137:7; Lam 4:21–22; Ezek 25:12–14; Ezek 35; Joel 4:19. Block adds Arad Ostracon 24 (“lest Edom should go there”), the Ḥorvat ʿUza ostracon and the Qitmit shrine, with publications named [S: paste].
  • These finds show Edom in the Negev. They do not show Edom at Jerusalem. Block’s “confirms that the Edomites were indeed poised to mock” goes beyond them. Bridger’s “the Edomites playing a major part” has only Ps 137 behind it.
  • 1 Esd 4:45 and 4:50 divide the editions. Rahlfs reads οἱ Ιδουμαῖοι (“the Edomites”) at both, so Bewer’s citation stands and my recollection was right. Swete reads οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι (“the Judeans”) at 4:45 and οἱ Χαλδαῖοι (“the Chaldeans”) at 4:50. If cited, name Rahlfs and say that Swete differs. It is late, so it shows the tradition about Edom, not the event.
  • Keil dismisses Lam 4:21–22 as evidence. He says it says only that the cup will reach Edom, which is fair: it names no act against Jerusalem.
  • Bewer’s “Ez. 30:5” does not name Edom (WLC). It may be a slip for 36:5, but that is my guess.

For the pulpit. Say that the Bible’s prophets and poets make this charge, not that history confirms it. The sermon does not need the stronger claim, since Obadiah’s own charge stands in the text.

2.3 What became of Edom (question 1.3)

  • Nabonidus. Block prints the Chronicle: “they set up their quarters [facing E]dom”. The key word is partly restored. He adds “the dating is not certain” for the destruction levels at Buseira, Tawilan and Tell el-Kheleifeh, and then “we may see… the fulfillment” [S: paste]. Hadjiev adds the es-Selaʿ relief and Na’aman’s two campaigns, 553 and 551 [S: summary].
  • Nabataeans. Bewer gives Diodorus 19.94 for 312 BC. Block has the Edomite people lasting to the late fourth century [S: paste].
  • Who are the allies of v. 7? Babylon (Hadjiev); Moab, Ammon, Tyre and Sidon (Pusey); Arabs or Nabataeans (Bewer). Nobody can prove any of these.
  • Slips. “Verse 8” for v. 7 is Hadjiev’s own, not the Assistant’s, as I first said. “Third year (556–539 BCE)” is the Assistant’s garbling of his sentence. The Chronicle’s note reads “[ ]dummu”, which is partly restored. The Assistant also says commentators do not much discuss Arab pressure, which your own pastes of Bewer, Block and Hadjiev contradict.

For the pulpit. Edom’s fall is secure in outline, and Mal 1:3–4 attests it. Do not preach Nabonidus in 553 as the fulfilment.

2.4 Obadiah and Sepharad (questions 1.4, 3.10)

No answers of their own came back, but your 1.1 pastes cover the main points:

  • Obadiah. The Talmud (b. Sanh. 39b) identifies him with Ahab’s steward. Bridger and Block report this and Block rejects it. His footnote notes the different spelling, which the corpus confirms: עֹבַדְיָהוּ (“Obadiah”, 1 Kgs 18:3) against עֹבַדְיָה (“Obadiah”, Obad 1). Block calls the spelling difference “of little consequence”, so it is not an argument either way.
  • Sepharad. Hadjiev identifies it with Sardis (citing Raabe 268–70; Pons 2014), and sets out the problem: no Jewish colony there is attested so early. Solutions are a place in the east (Stuart: Šaparda in Media; Block: Babylon or a cipher for it), early settlers (Allen, Jenson), or v. 20 as an interpolation (Renkema). Block lists Spain (from the Targum), Hesperides, Media and Sardis, and gives the reference for the Lydian-Aramaic inscription, KAI 260:2 [S: paste]. The dig’s “often proposed… verify” can now name these holders. It should still say nothing rests on it.

3. Direction of Dependence

3.1 Jeremiah 49 (questions 2.1, 2.2): direction stays open

The Assistant’s account is unreliable here.

  • It reverses Patterson–Hill. Their page reads: “Obadiah’s penchant for drawing upon traditional Hebrew phraseology makes the second option most likely (Raabe 1996:22–33)” [S: paste]. The second option in their list is Obadiah depending on Jeremiah. The Assistant twice says the third.
  • It invents a majority. “Most scholars (Finley) favor” a common source. Patterson–Hill name Finley as one example.
  • It files Lange under the wrong heading. Lange’s argument favours Obadiah as prior, and it appears only as an objection.

A source error as well. Patterson–Hill list Bewer as holding that Obadiah depends on Jeremiah. Your paste of Bewer shows otherwise: “the older oracle from which Ob. quoted”.

The arguments, sorted:

View Holders Argument offered
Obadiah first Keil (from Caspari), direct; Pusey; Lange Connected in Obadiah, scattered in Jeremiah; Jeremiah’s habit of reusing earlier oracles; obscure to clear
Jeremiah first Raabe, Nogalski, Dicou, Renkema, Jeremias (through Hadjiev); Block; Schnittjer, “mildly” Obadiah’s borrowing habit; his hyperbole (v. 4) as strengthening; otherwise mostly date
Common source Bewer; Mason, Allen, Wolff, Stuart, Coggins, Ben Zvi, Barton; Hadjiev himself Variation in wording and order
No decision Baker (NIVAC); Patterson–Hill, in part “No compelling evidence”

Assessment:

  • Date assumptions are doing much of the work, as expected. Block (p. 38) reasons from an exilic Obadiah to Jeremiah’s priority. Baker (NIVAC, p. 155) uses the same date only to rule out Jeremiah borrowing, says there is “no compelling evidence” for any option, and leaves it there [S: paste], so my earlier “inclines to a third source” overstated him.
  • The claims about a majority conflict. Block says that most who date the book as exilic have Obadiah borrow. Hadjiev lists Allen, Coggins, Wolff, Stuart, Mason, Ben Zvi and Barton for a common tradition. Nobody has counted.
  • Bewer’s best-known contrast fails on the text. He says “in Jeremiah the prediction is still preserved, in Obadiah the prediction has become history”. But Jer 49:9, 10 and 15 also have perfects: הִשְׁחִיתוּ (“they destroyed”), חָשַׂפְתִּי (“I have stripped”) and the identical נְתַתִּיךָ (“I have made you”) (WLC) [T].
  • Keil is the first real textual case for Obadiah first [S: paste].
    • His point that the shared lines run in order in Obadiah but are scattered in Jeremiah holds: Obad 1–4 = Jer 49:14–16, and Obad 5–6 = Jer 49:9–10, as Block’s table shows [T]. That fits the audit’s view of Jer 49:7–22 as a compilation, though it would fit a common source as well.
    • His claim that nothing shared appears elsewhere in Jeremiah is too strong. Jer 6:9 has the gleaning and the grape gatherer, and Jer 51:53 has rising to the heavens, then שֹׁדְדִים (“plunderers”) (WLC) [T].
    • He says the view is held by “every one with the exception of Hitzig”. That is the third conflicting claim of a majority.
  • Block assumes Jeremiah first without arguing it here. “Obadiah modifies the parallelism in Jeremiah’s statement” (on v. 2).
  • Schnittjer [S: paste] calls the direction “sharply contested”. He leans “mildly” to Jeremiah as donor because Obadiah strengthens the imagery (the stars, the “we”), while Keil argues that obscure goes to clear. These are opposite stylistic rules, and neither is decisive. He also notes that Obadiah lacks the mercy of Jer 49:11. His note cites Jer 6:9 at v. 5, as I found. His “Jer 39:8” should be 49:8 (WLC).
  • A small piece of new text evidence. Schnittjer’s note: נֶחְפְּשׂוּ (“ransacked”, Obad 6) inverts two letters of חָשַׂפְתִּי (“I have stripped”, Jer 49:10) (WLC) [T]. He takes that to point to adaptation, or to a common source. Worth testing in O27.
  • Who says “we”? Schnittjer and Block both take שָׁמַעְנוּ (“we have heard”) to include the audience, and Schnittjer reports the Greek’s “I” as assimilation to Jeremiah, citing the BHQ apparatus. Nobody here explains “against her”.
  • The audit’s high, direction open stands. O27, the Synoptic Mode run, is still the right tool. Schnittjer should be read before it.

3.2 Joel 2:32 (question 2.3): I recommend a lean, moderate

  • For Joel using Obadiah. Bewer: “almost certain”, with the cumulative contacts at Joel 4:3, 4:17 and 4:19 [S: paste]. Hadjiev says most scholars see Joel as a “learned prophet” [S: summary].
  • Keil agrees, from the opposite date [S: paste]. He puts Joel early and Bewer late, yet both have Joel using Obadiah, on the same evidence: “as the Lord hath said”, with the line found nowhere else. So this judgement does not depend on dating Joel, which is worth more than either holder alone. Keil also rejects Ewald’s lost older oracle.
  • The other way. Fausset has Obadiah confirming Joel [S: summary], but that seems to rest on a date. The Assistant passes over this. Block’s note says “it is uncertain that Obadiah antedates Joel”, and he writes that Obadiah “seems to assume the fulfillment of Joel 4:17” [S: paste].
  • Schnittjer finds “fairly strong evidence of Obadiah as donor and Joel as receptor”, and grades both Joel contacts B, probable. His note on Joel 2:32 (pp. 377–79) [S: paste] gives three reasons. Joel’s formula כַּאֲשֶׁר אָמַר (“as he has said”) elsewhere points back to something said earlier: he lists seven narrative uses, and 2 Chr 21:7 makes an eighth (WLC). Joel shares a second contact with Obad 17 at 4:17. And Joel universalises: Ezekiel’s Spirit on Israel becomes “all flesh”, and Obadiah’s deliverance for Jacob becomes “all who call”. The last is a judgement about direction, not proof, but it does not rest on a date.
  • Corrections from the text:
    • Bewer’s “occurs literally thus” is not exact. Joel adds וּבִירוּשָׁלִַם (“and in Jerusalem”) (WLC).
    • The sentence Hadjiev is reported to quote is Joel’s wording, not the wording of both.
    • Patterson–Hill’s alternative, Joel 2:27, has no word for escape (WLC). The Assistant’s “does not present this as a serious alternative” sits awkwardly beside its own report of them, but Joel 2:27 does look weak.
    • “This phrasing suggests Obadiah influenced Joel’s vocabulary” is the Assistant’s own sentence.
  • What the text supports. Joel’s lines are the longer ones: he adds “and in Jerusalem” at 3:5, and וְזָרִים לֹא־יַעַבְרוּ־בָהּ עוֹד (“and strangers shall pass through her no more”) at 4:17 (WLC). That fits a later writer, without proving it. Expansion is a weak test. Keil’s list of contacts (his footnote at Joel 3:3, 3:14, 3:19) overlaps Bewer’s closely.

4. The Cruxes

4.1 Verses 12–14 (question 3.1): past referent moderate–high; force open

The Patterson–Hill entry is [S: summary]. Baker, Mason and Hadjiev are now confirmed from your pastes:

  • Baker (NIVAC, p. 181). The forms are present or future commands, but vv. 10–11 place the view after the event. The prophet imaginatively calls “STOP!”. Past-tense translations are wrong.
  • Baker (Tyndale, pp. 40–41). The forms refer to future action, reflecting the prophet’s horror. Past translations (AV, RSV) have “less grammatical basis”, and Edom “is urged to stop”. He holds the grammar constant in both volumes and adds the imaginative setting only in NIVAC. His note confirms that the AV has the past, which I had only recalled.
  • Hadjiev (p. 45). “Most interpreters” see a rhetorical device. He notes the NRSV’s “you should not have gloated”.
  • Mason (p. 94). A series of prohibitions could suggest a future warning, but the detail suggests hindsight. He would “take it with RSV” as a denunciation of past attitudes. So he approves the past-tense rendering that Block, Baker and Keil call ungrammatical.
  • Patterson–Hill (p. 228). Three possibilities; Keil holds a warning about events past that will recur.

Block [S: paste] calls the NRSV’s “you should not have” “grammatically untenable”, notes that Allen and others read imaginative replays, and rejects that too: “prohibitions with ongoing force—not only for Edom, but for everyone else, including the hearers/readers”. He still lists the eight as things Esau did. His note says the form is the strongest argument for a pre-exilic date (Niehaus), but that v. 11’s perfects cut equally the other way.

Keil [S: paste], in his comment and introduction, answers both sides. Caspari, Hengstenberg and Theodoret take the passage as a prediction, but the perfects of v. 11 are past. “Thou shouldest not have” is “opposed to the grammar”: אַל (“not”) with the shortened form is jussive. His own solution: Obadiah “rises from the particular historical event to the idea which it embodied”, and warns Edom of what it has done because it “will take place again”, until the day of the LORD. He does this while dating the event early.

So three readings have holders:

  1. A real warning before the event: Collins, Niehaus; Caspari and Hengstenberg through Keil. Baker’s 1988 wording may belong here.
  2. An imaginative return to the event: Baker (NIVAC), Hadjiev, Mason, Allen, the NET.
  3. A prohibition with lasting force on a past act: Block, for all hearers; Keil, as a type that recurs until the day of the LORD.

Schnittjer quotes Nogalski: vv. 11 and 15 “demonstrate knowledge that Edom has already done what vv. 12–14 say not to do”.

Assessment. The Hebrew is prohibition, and v. 11’s perfects make the referent past. I recommend moderate–high for that. Between 2 and 3 the text does not decide. Block’s numerical scheme (seven plus one, doubling Amos’s “three, even four”) is his own: the count of eight holds (WLC), but the doubling is not in the text, so tag it [S].

For the pulpit. The ESV’s “do not” is right and should stay. Readings 2 and 3 both preach. One has the prophet shouting at Edom in that day, and the other lets the prohibition reach the hearer. Say which you are doing. The NRSV-style “you should not have” is an interpretation, and your congregation will not hear it from the ESV.

4.2 Verses 2 and 5–7 (question 3.2): remains open, now with holders

  • Past event: Bewer, decisively; Jeremias through Hadjiev. Bewer’s argument is the vivid exclamations and the detail of v. 7 [S: summary and paste].
  • Prophetic perfect: “most commentators”, according to both Mason and Block [S: summary].
  • Present reality in the LORD’s view: Block on v. 2, reasoning from the summons in v. 1 [S: summary]. The Assistant extends this to vv. 5–7, but the argument it reports concerns v. 2 only.

Mason [S: paste] confirms that most take these as prophetic perfects and that some see a real assault, perhaps by Arab tribes. Block’s own translation in his table renders vv. 5–6 as past (“how you have been destroyed”, “has been pillaged”) but v. 2 as future, like the ESV [S: paste].

Date shapes each view. If the fall is a fifth-century event, the past reading follows; if 553, it must be prediction. The ESV and NASB95 gap remains. Your congregation hears a thing done. Say that this is a choice in the translation.

4.3 Verse 16 (question 3.3): remains low–moderate, slight lean to Judah

I earlier recommended moderate on the plural. The paste and one further text reverse that.

  • Baker (NIVAC, pp. 189–91). There are forty second-person forms and only two are plural. Edom is singular throughout, so the plural here means someone else: Judah [S: summary].
  • Block (pp. 89–90) [S: paste] sets out three views. (1) Esau revelling: he says this ignores the plural, that “the OT never refers expressly to ‘drinking bouts’ in celebration of victory”, and that the drink changes meaning. (2) Esau’s future judgement: forced by “on my holy mountain”. (3) Judean survivors, his choice, because of the plural, the third-person Esau in v. 18 and a second focusing כִּי (“indeed”). He grants that “both interpretations are possible”. He links Jer 49:12 with Jer 25, as the dig does, but prints it as “Jer 41:12”.
  • Keil [S: paste] argues for Edom directly, naming Ibn Ezra, Ewald and Meier for Judah. He has three reasons. כַּאֲשֶׁר שְׁתִיתֶם (“as you drank”) is parallel to כַּאֲשֶׁר עָשִׂיתָ (“as you have done”), in the verse before. A comparison between Judah’s conduct and the nations’ makes no sense in a word about retribution. And Edom alone is addressed in the book. He accepts that the drinking changes sense, carousing then judgement, and cites Joel 3:3 for the first, which is the text I had set against Block. He never explains the plural. Stuart and Niehaus are with him, through Block.
  • Baker, in his own words [S: paste], adds an argument. If the first “drank” is literal and the next two are metaphorical, the comparison “just as” breaks down. So the first must be metaphorical as well, which means Judah. He cites 1 Sam 30:16 himself for drinking and revelry, and he excludes the other plural (v. 1) because the nations are the other half of the comparison. His notes divide the field: for Edom, the Greek, the Vulgate, Calvin, Keil, Stuart, Finley and Niehaus; for Judah, Armerding, Limburg, Wolff, Ben Zvi and Raabe.
  • Others. Hadjiev reports Judah as the addressee, citing Rudolph and Barton. Mason says that God’s people have tacitly drunk a “cup of judgment”, though the paste reads “v. 1b”, perhaps for v. 16. Schnittjer lists it as “Edom drinks judgment”.

Tests:

  • Baker’s count holds. By hand in the WLC I find 40 second-person forms, counting קוּמוּ (“rise!”, v. 1) as one of the two plurals with שְׁתִיתֶם (“you drank”). That leaves תִּשְׁלַחְנָה (v. 13, “you shall stretch out”) as singular in sense, which BHS and the NET both emend. Other counters could differ by a form or two.
  • But a plural need not mean a new addressee. Ezekiel’s oracle to Mount Seir is singular through 35:12, then plural at 35:13, וַתַּגְדִּילוּ עָלַי בְּפִיכֶם (“you magnified yourselves against me with your mouth”), then singular again at 35:14–15 (WLC) [T]. Block cites this very verse at v. 12 without noting the plural. It is Edom, the same crime, and the nearest parallel. This is the strongest point against the argument from the plural, and no commentator returned meets it, though Block and Baker (Tyndale) both cite the verse.
  • Block’s “never” fails, and now on his exact words. 1 Sam 30:16 has the raiders אֹכְלִים וְשֹׁתִים וְחֹגְגִים (“eating and drinking and dancing”) over the spoil. Joel 4:3 has lots cast over the LORD’s people, then a girl sold for wine וַיִּשְׁתּוּ (“and they drank”) (WLC) [T]. Joel 4:3 uses Obad 11’s own phrase. His note reaches only Ezek 39:17–20 and Lam 2:7. Baker, as noted, has 1 Sam 30:16.
  • “The cup.” כּוֹס (“cup”) is not in Obadiah. Block grants this (“Obadiah does not specify what is drunk”) and still uses the cup as shorthand. BDB and Baker bring it in too. The Judah reading leans on it through Jer 49:12.
  • Greek and Syriac. The Greek’s singular ἔπιες (“you drank”) removes the plural altogether. The Syriac keeps it, ܕܶܐܫܬܺܝܬܽܘܢ (“you [plural] drank”; peshitta.eu), so it supports the MT’s plural against the Greek. The Vulgate at v. 16 has not been checked.

Assessment. The Judah reading keeps real support: Baker’s argument from “just as”, Jer 49:12, and more of the technical commentators than not. Its other main support, the plural, is now weaker. I am keeping the rating at low–moderate rather than moving it a third time, until O30 and the audit have tested it. Keep it open in the pulpit.

4.4 Verse 15 (question 3.4)

  • Rearranged: Wellhausen, Rudolph, Wolff [S: summary]. Hadjiev thinks that 15b ended the first part and 15a was inserted.
  • Kept: Baker (Tyndale), with “all the nations” as the link between 15a and 16 and with the change of person as a binding device. Block says that it “lacks manuscript support” (the BHS apparatus agrees) and would obscure Esau as the paradigm for the nations.

Assessment. The audit’s three blocks stand as a reading of the text as it is. The rearrangement is a composition theory with no witness behind it. It connects with the unity question in section 2.1.

4.6 Verse 21 (question 3.6)

  • מוֹשִׁעִים (“saviours”): Baker and Butler keep the active sense, as “deliverers” like the judges. Patterson–Hill note Othniel and Ehud (Judg 3:9, 15, confirmed in the WLC) [S: summary]. The passive has the Greek and Syriac, and the NLT, behind it.

  • “From” or “on”: Baker (Tyndale) says the LXX’s “from” is not necessary, and “on” links with v. 17.

  • לִשְׁפֹּט (“to judge”): Bridger, Butler and Patterson–Hill all hear the Judges’ verb with an administrative sense.

  • The Syriac has ܦܖ̈ܺܝܩܶܐ (“saved ones”; peshitta.eu), as BHS reports, going up ܠܛܽܘܪܳܐ (“to the mountain”), not from it as in the Greek.

Block [S: paste], who was missing:

  • Saviours. He keeps the active. The present usage “recalls Judg 3:9, 15”. His argument against the Greek is that “neither the hophal nor passive niphals of this verb ever occur”. That is wrong for the niphal: נוֹשָׁע (“saved”) stands at Deut 33:29, Zech 9:9 and Ps 33:16 (WLC) [T]. So BHS’s נוֹשָׁעִים (“those saved”) is a real form. His Judges point survives; this argument does not.
  • To judge or to rule. Because the object is a place, the verb here means “to govern, rule”, against the common judicial sense. So the ESV’s “to rule” has an argument behind it.
  • “Go up” he reads as military, to storm a stronghold.
  • Ps 22:28–29 he calls a “close parallel” (see section 7).

Assessment. The Judges pattern now has holders, and Block’s is the fullest case. No messianic identification came back.

For the pulpit. The ESV’s “to rule” is fair. But your congregation does not hear that it is the Judges’ verb, so tell them.

Rahlfs and Swete both read τοὺς κατακληρονομήσαντας αὐτούς (“those who dispossessed them”) [T]. So does the Clementine Vulgate: eos qui se possederant (“those who had possessed them”). The Peshitta agrees. The Syriac you supplied ends ܠܰܐܝܠܶܝܢ ܕܺܝܪܶܬܘ ܐܶܢܽܘܢ (“those who possessed them”). That is my own reading of the Syriac, not checked against a lexicon. The text comes from peshitta.eu, a web text whose Obadiah page names no edition or manuscript, so it is an observation layer and not the Leiden critical edition, and a finding should not cite it as that. An earlier English text labelled as the Peshitta was the JPS 1917, a translation of the Hebrew, and is set aside. The same verse has ܡܫܰܘܙܒܽܘܬܳܐ (“deliverance”), an abstract noun, as Block and the Greek take פְּלֵיטָה. The versions are not necessarily independent witnesses.

The Assistant’s “commentators divide” rests on one work that keeps the MT and reports the alternative. Block also keeps the MT (“their own possessions”) and, in the pasted pages, does not mention the variant. His “elsewhere only in Isa 14:23” for מוֹרָשׁ (“possession”) overlooks Job 17:11 (WLC). His note that יָרַשׁ with people as object means “dispossess” is useful.

Two points of grammar that the English hides. Baker and Block agree that קֹדֶשׁ (“holiness”) is a noun and Mount Zion is not its subject: Block reads “there shall be holiness”, against Joel 4:17’s feminine verb with Jerusalem. And Block takes פְּלֵיטָה as abstract, “escape”, not “those who escape” as in both your English texts, citing Joel 2:3 and 2 Chr 20:24 [S: paste]. The Greek agrees: σωτηρία (“salvation”, Rahlfs and Swete) [T].

Assessment. I lean to “dispossessors” at moderate–high, up from moderate: Murabba’at’s consonants, the Greek, the Syriac, the Latin, BHS and the NET, against the MT’s pointing alone. The Targum has not been checked. The MT remains what both English texts translate. It sharpens the reversal in the book [I]. Both pulpit and study texts follow the MT, so this is a note for the preacher and not a point to build on.

4.8 Verse 7 (question 3.8): my question was too narrow

I asked about לַחְמְךָ (“your bread”) against לֹחֲמֶיךָ (“those fighting you”). The answers show that the same revocalisation has been read two ways:

  • “Those who eat with you”: Patterson–Hill and the NET.
  • “Those fighting you”: BHS and the versions it cites, with Baker reporting this and rejecting it because the line is parallel to two terms of friendship.
  • Keep the MT as elliptical: Block (perhaps אַנְשֵׁי, “men of”, has dropped out, or the ellipsis is rhetorical); Pusey (“the men of thy bread”).

Rahlfs and Swete have no word for bread at all ([T]).

Consequence for audit A4b. The Ps 41:9 link does not rest only on the MT’s wording. It survives wherever a friend is meant, and “those who eat” is nearer to Ps 41:10 אוֹכֵל לַחְמִי (“who ate my bread”). It fails only on “those fighting you”. The rating stays low–moderate, but the note should change.

“No understanding in him.” Nobody treats this as a gloss, though BHS asks. Patterson–Hill’s parallel, Deut 32:28, is new to us (section 5).

    • It groups violence “by foreign nations against Israel” as Joel 4:19, Obad 10, Hab 1:9, 2:8, 2:17.
    • It treats Ps 7:17 and Judg 9:24 as violence overtaking the doer.
    • It says the substance of the word is commonly bloodshed.
