Two-Framework Analysis: Point and Purpose, Obadiah 1–21

Document analysed: point-purpose-obadiah-1to21 (9 November 2026)

Frameworks: Sailhamer (with Schnittjer and Postell); Proclamation Trust (Cooper, Davies, Styles)

Date: 12 November 2026

Status of new claims: the Hebrew parallels proposed below are made from the text as I read it. They have not yet been checked against the _texts/ corpus. They are listed for verification at the end, and none should enter the plan until they have been checked.

Overview

This is a strong backbone, and both frameworks find less to correct in it than they usually find in a finished sermon. It is text-centred and works from the Hebrew. It holds the “why” until v. 10, it refuses the pride reading, and it keeps the Christological claims at the ratings the audits gave them. Its Anti-Moralism Check is honest.

The two frameworks find the same main weakness from different directions. The plan reads Obadiah as a free-standing oracle and goes to the New Testament directly (Rev 11:15, Mark 3:8, the Bridger inference). It does not read Obadiah as a book of the Twelve, sewn in between Joel–Amos and Jonah. Sailhamer and Postell would call that a missed compositional seam. Styles and Cooper would call it a short-circuit in the canonical step. The seam supplies, from inside the canon, three things the plan currently gets from outside it:

  • a text-level route from v. 17’s escape on Zion to the gospel;
  • a reason for the “door open to Edom” ending;
  • a guard against the gloating the plan rightly fears.

Davies’s chief concern is separate. The Purpose Statement has three aims, and the sermon’s unity depends on how they are ranked.

Davies’ Seven-Second Sermon

Them then Us now
Question? Jerusalem has fallen, and the brother nation stood by, joined in, cut off the fugitives and now holds the land. Has the LORD seen it, and is the day of our ruin the last word? When those who should have stood with us joined in on our worst day, and nothing seems to happen to them, has God seen it, and what will he do?
Answer! The LORD has seen every step. His day is near on all the nations, and it will bring every deed back on the doer’s head. On Mount Zion there will be escape, and the kingdom will be the LORD’s. (the same)
So what… Do not despair, and do not take up Edom’s sword: the day is the LORD’s. Look to Zion, where the remnant is kept and the kingdom is his. Leave justice with the King who has come. Call on his name and escape, because what should come back on us came back on him. And stop being the bystander who enjoys another’s fall, because the door is open even to Edom.

The “them then” column assumes an exilic setting. Ps 137:7 and Lam 4:21–22 both put Edom at Jerusalem’s fall, and the plan’s eight “day” phrases suit that reading. If the dig left the date open, keep this column general, as “the readers of the scroll”. Sailhamer would prefer that anyway (see below).

Sailhamer Analysis

1. Opening summary

The plan is predominantly text-centred, more so than almost any sermon brought to this project. Its question is the right one: why has the prophet written the charge as eight prohibitions over eight day-phrases? It makes almost no event-reconstruction moves. Its weakness is not that it reads behind the text. It reads the text inside too small a frame: one book and not the book of the Twelve.

2. What is working at the text level

  • Narrative braking recognised. The prophet slows to eight prohibitions in vv. 12–14, and the plan slows there with him (“walk all eight steps”). This is Schnittjer’s rule applied correctly: the weight falls where the author lingers.
  • Leading words named. These are אָח (“brother”) at the head of the charge, the eight day-phrases, and the שָׂרִיד/פָּלִיט pair (“survivor”/“fugitive”), which Edom hunted in v. 14 and which returns at vv. 17–18. That last observation is the best single move in the plan. It shows that the book answers itself in its own vocabulary.
  • Reader’s perspective. Movement 1 keeps the hearer asking “why?” until v. 10. That reproduces the author’s own ordering, verdict before reason, and does not explain it away.
  • Synoptic discipline. It notes that “who will bring me down?” is Obadiah’s alone and absent from Jer 49. This is the text/text comparison Sailhamer would want, and it does not speculate about which came first.
  • Refusing the cipher. “Esau is not the flesh”, and no modern map. These are exactly the guards against importing a meaning the text does not encode.

3. Where the plan stops short of the text

(a) “Brother” is treated as a moral category, not a Torah category. The plan calls אָח the “moral centre” (Convergent Finding 1) but does not ask where Obadiah learned the word. The Torah has already defined Edom as Israel’s brother, and has done so in commands:

  • Num 20:14: “Thus says your brother Israel”;
  • Deut 2:4–8: the sons of Esau are “your brothers”, so Israel is not to provoke them or take their land;
  • Deut 23:8 (EVV 23:7): “You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother”.

Israel was commanded to treat Edom as a brother, and Obadiah charges Edom with not doing the same. Amos 1:11, two books earlier, uses the same word: “he pursued his brother with the sword”. The text gives a fact the plan currently has to supply with analogy. The charge is the breach of a relationship that the Torah itself established. Missed opportunity, not harmful.

(b) The Leitwort of vv. 17–20 is set aside as geography. The plan tells the preacher to “keep it light” on vv. 19–20. That is right for the map and wrong for the verb. The root ירשׁ (“possess”) runs through vv. 17–20: the verb and the noun מוֹרָשֵׁיהֶם in v. 17, and the verb twice in v. 19 and once in v. 20 (count to be confirmed against the lemma index). The land list is how the prophet reverses possession: those who were dispossessed possess, and Mount Esau is possessed. That is why the v. 17 crux matters. The Murabbaʿat reading, “those who dispossessed them”, sits directly on the leading word. Recommendation: keep the place-names light, but give the verb one clear sentence.

(c) v. 15 is a janus, and the plan treats it as a start line. Verse 15 faces both ways. “The day of the LORD is near upon all the nations” opens the wider horizon, and “as you have done” closes the case against Edom (it is singular, addressed to Edom). Edom’s eight “days” are answered by one Day. The structure table puts v. 15 at the head of Movement 3, and that is acceptable. The plan should still name the hinge, because it is what allows the sermon to turn from Edom to “all the nations”, and so to us, without a jump.

4. The deepest missed opportunity: the seams of the Twelve

In Postell’s sense, Obadiah sits at a deliberate joint. In the Hebrew order it follows Joel and Amos and comes before Jonah. In the Greek order it follows Joel directly. The joint does theological work at both ends.

Before: Joel and Amos supply Obadiah’s vocabulary. The parallels with Joel 3–4 (EVV 2:32–3:21) are dense, if they are confirmed:

Obadiah Joel (MT; EVV in brackets)
v. 10: מֵחֲמַס (“because of violence”), against Edom 4:19 (3:19): Edom made a desolation מֵחֲמַס בְּנֵי יְהוּדָה (“because of violence to the people of Judah”)
v. 11: they “cast lots” (יַדּוּ גוֹרָל) for Jerusalem 4:3 (3:3): they “cast lots” for my people
v. 14: survivors (שְׂרִידָיו) handed over 3:5 (2:32): among the survivors (שְׂרִידִים) “whom the LORD calls”
v. 15: the day of the LORD is near (קָרוֹב יוֹם־יְהוָה) 4:14 (3:14): the same phrase
v. 15: your deeds will return on your head (גְּמֻלְךָ יָשׁוּב בְּרֹאשֶׁךָ) 4:4, 7 (3:4, 7): I will return your deeds on your head
v. 16: my holy mountain 4:17 (3:17): Zion, my holy mountain
v. 17: in Mount Zion there will be escape (פְלֵיטָה), and it will be holy 3:5 (2:32): in Mount Zion and Jerusalem there will be escape, “as the LORD has said”; 4:17 (3:17): Jerusalem will be holy

Amos then closes on “that they may possess the remnant of Edom” (Amos 9:12, with the root ירשׁ), and Obadiah unfolds that verse. Amos 1:6, 9 also uses Obadiah’s verb for handing over captives (סגר), and Amos 1:9 adds “the covenant of brothers”.

Why this matters for the sermon. Joel 3:5 (EVV 2:32) begins with “everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved”. The New Testament quotes that half-verse twice: Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:21), and Paul in Rom 10:13, where it is preceded by “there is no distinction between Jew and Greek” (10:12). So the escape on Zion in v. 17 is already tied, through the passage Obadiah draws on, to the apostolic call to faith in the Lord Jesus. That gives Movement 3 a gospel turn that is an authorial connection, not a theological inference. It also gives Movement 4 its “even Edom” from the canon itself.

After: Jonah guards the gloating. The next book is about a prophet who wants an enemy nation destroyed. He sits outside the city “till he should see what would become of it” (Jonah 4:5), and God rebukes him for his anger at mercy shown to that nation. Keep Honest 2 warns against the congregation relishing v. 18. The Twelve has already built that warning in. The scroll’s reader goes from Obadiah’s “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” to the LORD’s question in Jonah 4:11. I put the verbal link (the verb רָאָה, “see”, in Obad 12–13 and Jonah 4:5) at low; the thematic link is strong.

A Greek reading to declare. James’s use of Amos 9:12 (Acts 15:17) follows the Greek, which reads “the remnant of men may seek” where the Hebrew has “they may possess the remnant of Edom”. This is Edom read as אָדָם (“man”), and a different verb. The plan’s note “[T for the texts; I for the connection]” is right, but the pulpit should not claim that Amos’s Hebrew says the nations will seek the Lord. The honest sentence is that the apostles read the Greek text of the verse Obadiah unfolds, and found in it the Gentiles coming in. Similarly, the Greek of Obad 21 appears to read “men who have been saved will go up from Mount Zion”, where the Hebrew has “saviours”. This is worth one footnote in the plan, not a pulpit point (to be confirmed in Swete).

5. Summary judgement

The plan was written by someone who already reads the way Sailhamer asks, starting from the author’s choices and verifying in the Hebrew. What the method would add here is a wider frame. The book of the Twelve has its own compositional argument. Read inside that argument, Obadiah’s hope is not left needing a jump to Revelation: Joel has given it a vocabulary, Amos a promise and Jonah a corrective. The plan’s Christological reading would become more text-based, and also more Christological.

PT Framework Analysis

1. Opening summary

The plan is text-centred and handles the triangle well at both ends. Its original-context reading is careful. Its application is drawn from the text, not from a list, especially the bystander turn grounded in מִנֶּגֶד (“aloof”). The weak point is the middle of the rectangle. The step through fulfilment in Christ is made by two jumps (Rev 11:15; Isa 53:6 and 2 Cor 5:21 via Bridger), not by tracing what lies between this text and the cross.

2. What is working well

  • Cooper: authorial intention, not events. Every movement is built on a compositional observation: verdict before reason, the eight steps, the survivor word returning, the kingdom as the last word. A careful reader of the ESV could follow most of it, which is Cooper’s test.
  • Davies: application from the text’s purpose. The FCF’s second half (that we have stood aloof ourselves) comes from v. 11’s מִנֶּגֶד and the escalation of vv. 12–14. It is not “spotted”. The refusal to hurry the wounded into “forgive and forget” is pastorally exact, and it comes from the text: God’s detailed seeing in vv. 12–14 is the comfort.
  • Davies: unity of movement. The four movements form one argument (verdict, reason, outcome, end), and the hinges (vv. 10, 17) are named.
  • Styles: no moralism. The plan explicitly refuses to end on “don’t gloat”. The imperatives are fruit, and God is the subject of the Teaching Point.
  • Styles: no reductionism. Judgement (v. 18), escape, kingdom and response are all present. The gospel is not shrunk to a single transaction.

3. Where the framework is not fully applied

(a) The “them then” is mentioned but not inhabited (Davies). The FCF has a “Shared / Different” line, but the plan never states the original hearers’ pastoral problem. Davies’s structure needs it twice: in the introduction (them then, before us now) and in the conclusion (land on them first). Without it, the Big Question is asked only of us, and the congregation does not see that Judah asked it first. Recommendation: add a “them then” line under the FCF, worded to whatever date the dig settled, or as “the readers of the scroll” if it left the date open. Missed opportunity.

(b) The Purpose Statement has three aims, not one (Davies). Assure the wounded; uncover the Edom in every hearer; lift eyes to the kingdom. Each is warranted, but stated as three equal aims they invite three sermons. The risk is concrete: the wounded and the bystander are being moved in opposite directions, and the sermon could comfort the first and then accuse them in the next breath. The text ranks the aims. Obadiah was heard by Judah, not by Edom, so its primary purpose is assurance and hope. The warning to Judah comes in under v. 15, where “all the nations” includes the hearer. Recommendation: recast the Purpose Statement as one sentence with the bystander turn subordinate, for example:

To assure God’s people that he has seen every step of the wrong done them and will bring it home on his day, so that, leaving justice with the King and repenting of every Edom-like pleasure in another’s fall, they take refuge on the mountain where there is escape, which is open even to Edom’s children.

Potentially harmful to unity if left as it stands.

(c) The cross is brought in, not reached (Styles). In Movement 3, “what should return on us returned on him” is true, and the plan is honest that it is “a theological reading, not Obadiah’s statement”. But Styles’s “little and large” asks the preacher to trace the line from this text to the cross, not to drop the cross in beside v. 15. Two canonical routes run from inside the passage:

  1. v. 17 → Joel 3:5 → Acts 2:21; Rom 10:13 (see the Sailhamer section). The escape on Zion is offered to “everyone who calls on the name”, and the apostles identify that name as the Lord Jesus. This route goes through the cross and resurrection, because that is what Peter preaches in Acts 2.
  2. The brother theme → Heb 12:16–24. Hebrews warns against being “like Esau” (12:16–17), tells believers they “have come to Mount Zion” (12:22), and ends on “the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel” (12:24), the first brother’s blood that cried out from the ground (Gen 4:10). It speaks directly to the FCF. The wounded hearer’s blood cries for justice, as Abel’s did. The blood of Jesus answers that cry with something better. The answer is not less just; it is atonement. Rate this as a canonical reflection, not an allusion: Hebrews does not cite Obadiah. It still brings the cross out of the book’s own brother-and-Zion vocabulary.

The Bridger line can stay as a supporting sentence. It should not be the main route. Missed opportunity; the current route is not wrong, only imported.

(d) Rev 11:15 is the end of the trajectory without the middle (Styles). “The kingdom shall be the LORD’s” goes straight to the consummation. Styles would want the announcement first: the King has come (Mark 1:15), and he reigns by way of the cross. Rev 11:15 can then close the sermon. Without that middle, v. 21 can sound like “wait for the end” and not like “submit to the King now”. Minor.

(e) The hearer table has no speaker (Styles). All four hearers receive, and none is sent. If the door is open to Edom’s children, the obvious corollary is that believers carry the word to those who have wronged them. That is the hardest application in the book, and the gospel makes it possible. One line in Movement 4 would do it. Minor.

4. The deepest missed opportunity

Ground the gospel turn in v. 17 itself. The plan already calls v. 17’s “escape” the hinge that connects judgement to good news. It then leaves the hinge to go to Isaiah 53 and 2 Corinthians 5. If Movement 3 follows the escape word back to Joel and forward to Peter and Paul, the cross arrives as the fulfilment of the passage’s own promise, and the Purpose Statement’s “escape that God, not the hearer, provides” is given its canonical content. This is also where the two frameworks converge most strongly, which makes it the plan’s highest-priority revision.

5. Summary judgement

This is the work of a careful exegete who has already absorbed most of the PT instincts. Application is heard, not invented; God is the subject; moralism is refused. The PT framework would mainly ask for two things: one purpose in place of three, and the patience to walk the canonical road from Zion to the cross, not take a short cut to it.

Where the Frameworks Meet and Part

  • They converge on the seam: v. 17 → Joel 3:5 → Acts 2 / Rom 10. Sailhamer wants it because it is an authorial connection, and PT wants it because it earns the cross. This is the strongest recommendation in the document.
  • They converge on the Torah “brother” texts. They are Sailhamer’s canonical horizon and Cooper’s “original context first”.
  • They part over Rev 11:15. PT is comfortable with a salvation-historical trajectory (“kingdom” → “kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ”). Sailhamer would ask whether that is a textual link or only a thematic one, and would prefer the Twelve’s own seams. This is a real difference, not a mistake on either side. The plan’s own rating (“high on the trajectory; not a quotation”) is exactly right, and both frameworks can use it on those terms.
  • They part, mildly, over Heb 12. PT will welcome it as canonical-context work. Sailhamer would rate it lower than Joel, because no verbal link to Obadiah is claimed.

Recommendations for v1.1 of the Plan

  1. FCF: add a “them then” line. Match the dig’s dating, or use “the readers of the scroll” if the date is open.
  2. Purpose Statement: recast as one sentence, with the bystander turn subordinate (wording above).
  3. Movement 2: add one sentence on the Torah’s brother texts (Num 20:14; Deut 2:4–8; Deut 23:8 MT): Israel was told to treat Edom as a brother.
  4. Movement 3: name v. 15 as the janus. Make v. 17 → Joel 3:5 → Acts 2:21 / Rom 10:13 the main gospel route. Keep Bridger as support. Heb 12:22–24 is optional.
  5. Movement 4: one sentence on ירשׁ (“possess”) as the leading word of vv. 17–20. Insert “the King has come” (Mark 1:15) before Rev 11:15. One line sending hearers to their own Edoms.
  6. Keep Honest: add a fifth point. Acts 15:17 follows the Greek of Amos 9:12 (“men… seek”), not the Hebrew (“possess… Edom”), so say so if it is used. And let Jonah, the next book, carry the warning against relishing v. 18.
  7. Mark 3:8: demote to a passing aside. Idumea in a crowd list is thin beside Amos 9:12 → Acts 15 and Joel 3:5 → Rom 10.
