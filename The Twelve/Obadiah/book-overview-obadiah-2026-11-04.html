Book Overview: Obadiah

Canonical Position

Field Statement
Section Nevi’im, Latter Prophets, fourth of the Twelve in the order BHS prints: Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah [T]. English order agrees. Swete’s Greek differs: Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel, Obadiah, Jonah [T]
Reading implication Obadiah is heard after Amos’s closing promise that Israel will possess “the remnant of Edom”, and before a book in which a prophet is sent to a foreign city. A reader will connect the two, though adjacency in the Twelve has no general lexical effect [I, low–moderate as design]
Presupposes That Esau and Jacob are brothers (Gen 25:26; 27:41–42; Num 20:14; Deut 23:8; Amos 1:11). That Mount Seir was YHWH’s own grant to Esau (Deut 2:5; Josh 24:4), which makes v. 19 a reversal of a gift and not merely a conquest. Joseph, Ephraim, Benjamin and Gilead as tribal land (Josh 16:10; 2 Sam 2:9). Zion as YHWH’s holy mountain (Joel 4:17; Ps 2:6). A fall of Jerusalem with exiles (vv. 11, 20) [T for the references; I for the reader's knowledge]
Handoff Thematic only. The nations are judged here and a nation is spared in Jonah. No lexical handoff was found: Jonah ranks sixteenth by rare shared vocabulary with Obadiah, and Jonah 1 has none at all [T for the measure; I for the theme]
Neighbours Amos before: real and verbal (see the Intertextual Map). Jonah after: thematic. In the Greek order Joel stands immediately before Obadiah; in the Hebrew order Amos stands between them. Joel is first of all books by rate of rare shared vocabulary, so the Greek adjacency is worth noting, though no inference is drawn from it [T]

Canonical and compositional order may diverge here. Obadiah stands among books with eighth-century superscriptions (Hos 1:1; Amos 1:1), but its own superscription has no king. Its content may well be sixth-century. Both facts are reported and neither is used to resolve the other.

Presenting Situation

Author. חֲזוֹן עֹבַדְיָה (“the vision of Obadiah”, v. 1) gives a name and nothing else: no father, no home, no king [T]. עֹבַדְיָה (“Obadiah”, “servant of Yah”) is a common name. Identifying him with Ahab’s steward is a Talmudic tradition (b. Sanh. 39b), which Block rejects [S]. The steward’s name is spelled עֹבַדְיָהוּ (1 Kgs 18:3), but the spelling difference proves nothing either way [T; S: Block].

Voice. The opening שָׁמַעְנוּ (“we have heard”, v. 1) puts the prophet among hearers. Jer 49:14 has the singular שָׁמַעְתִּי (“I have heard”), and so does the Greek of Obadiah, ἤκουσα (“I heard”) [T]. YHWH then speaks in the first person: נְתַתִּיךָ (“I have made you”, v. 2), אוֹרִידְךָ (“I will bring you down”, v. 4), עַמִּי (“my people”, v. 13), קָדְשִׁי (“my holy”, v. 16) [T].

Addressee and audience. Edom is addressed as “you” through v. 15, and is only spoken about from v. 17 on [T]. Nothing suggests Edom was ever meant to hear it. The natural audience is those who suffered what vv. 11–14 describe [I].

Occasion. On the page: strangers took Jerusalem’s wealth, foreigners entered its gates, and lots were cast over it (v. 11). Edom joined in (vv. 11–14). גָלֻת יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“the exiles of Jerusalem”, v. 20) exist [T]. No conqueror is named, and בָּבֶל (“Babylon”) does not occur [T].

Date: contested, and it should stay visible as contested. Three options are live:

  • After Jerusalem’s fall in 586 BC [I, moderate; the leading reading]. Block, Bewer, Hadjiev, Mason, Bridger and Baker (both volumes) set vv. 10–14 after the fall [S]. The noun גָּלוּת (“exile”) with יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“Jerusalem”) stands in three verses, Obad 20, Jer 40:1 and Ezek 33:21, the latter two of that event. Jer 1:3 has the cognate verbal form גְּלוֹת (“going into exile”) of the same event. Edom’s guilt over “the day of Jerusalem” appears at Ps 137:7, and Lam 4:21–22 and Ezek 35 treat it similarly [T for the texts]. Renkema places the composition about three decades after the fall, before Nabonidus’s move against Edom in 554/3 [S, via Cook's review].
  • The reign of Jehoram, mid-ninth century [S, minority among evangelicals but defended]. Edom revolts (2 Kgs 8:20; 2 Chr 21:8), and Philistines and Arabs carry off the property of the king’s house (2 Chr 21:16–17), though that passage names neither Jerusalem nor Edom in the raid. Collins argues this date from the grammar of vv. 12–14 and from the book’s placement [S]. Keil is the fullest owned holder [S: Keil]. His real point is that Obadiah never mentions a destruction: no temple, no burning, no fall of the kingdom, only capture and plunder, and the text bears him out [T]. His case is weaker elsewhere. 2 Chr 21:17 has “the king’s house”, not Jerusalem, and names no Edomites; his date for Jer 49 comes from Jer 46:2, whose heading concerns Egypt; and he does not discuss גָּלוּת יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“the exile of Jerusalem”, v. 20).
  • A later setting, reaching the fourth century, is also proposed [S].

Terminology shows that the 586 reading fits; it does not prove it. Amos 1:6, 9, in a book dated to Uzziah and Jeroboam (Amos 1:1), already use גָּלוּת (“exile”) with Edom [T]. Ps 137:7 is the one text that puts Edom and “the day of Jerusalem” together [T]. The setting stays at moderate, and the date of the finished book is a separate question (see Composition).

Edom’s part in 586 is itself contested. Edom is not named in 2 Kgs 24–25, Jer 39, Jer 52 or 2 Chr 36, and in Jer 40–43 only at Jer 40:11, as a place of refuge [T]. Bartlett argues there is no hard evidence of collusion, and Tebes calls the charge a “stab-in-the-back” myth. Na’aman answers from the Arad ostraca (no. 24: “lest Edom should go there”) and the Negev letters, and Hadjiev sides with him [S: through Hadjiev]. The finds show Edomite pressure in the Negev, not Edom at Jerusalem [I]. The charge rests on the prophets and poets: Ps 137:7; Lam 4:21–22; Ezek 25:12–14; 35; Joel 4:19 [T for the texts]. 1 Esd 4:45, 50 blames “the Edomites” in Rahlfs, but Swete reads “the Judeans” and “the Chaldeans”; it is late, and evidence for the tradition, not the event [T].

Composition. Most critics treat the book as composite, separating vv. 1–14, 15b from vv. 15a, 16–21: Bewer, Wolff, Rudolph, Barton, and Hadjiev, who lists five reasons and five reconstructions [S]. Assis treats vv. 1–9, the lines shared with Jeremiah 49, as a late pre-exilic oracle on Edom’s pride, supplemented after 587 by vv. 10–14, 15b, with vv. 15a, 16–21 later still [S: Assis, through Hadjiev 42–43]. Allen, Renkema, Raabe and Block argue for coherence [S]. Two cautions. One of Hadjiev’s reasons, a change of addressee at v. 16, depends on a disputed reading. And הַר עֵשָׂו (“Mount Esau”, vv. 8, 9, 19, 21, and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible) and the twelve uses of יוֹם (“day”) run across the supposed layers [T], as Hadjiev grants. This overview reads the book as it stands.

Pressure. A people has been destroyed, and its brother helped, and the mountain stands untouched. Does YHWH see what happened on that day, and is it the last word? [I]

Purpose. To tell the survivors that the day of their ruin was not the decisive day: YHWH’s day is, and when it comes the kingdom will be his [I].

Device. Sentence first, charge second, then the day that answers both [I]. Two formal threads run through it [T]:

  • יוֹם (“day”) occurs 12 times: v. 8, ten times in vv. 11–14, and v. 15. Edom’s day-of-Judah phrases are ended by the day of YHWH.
  • הַר (“mountain”) occurs 7 times: הַר עֵשָׂו (“mountain of Esau”) at vv. 8, 9, 19 and 21, the holy mountain at v. 16, and הַר צִיּוֹן (“Mount Zion”) at vv. 17 and 21. The last verse holds both mountains in one line.

Baseline on the three blocks. Verses 1–9, 10–14 and 15–21 are the best division by vocabulary: first of 91 contiguous cuts by distance from each cut’s own shuffle. But shuffling verses is too easy a test, since most stretches pass it. Against the best cuts of arbitrary 21-verse stretches of the Latter Prophets, the realistic odds are about 1 in 20 [T for the measure; I for its meaning]. יוֹם (“day”) crosses 14|15, so v. 15 is a join as well as a turn. The division is moderate–high, supported by content as much as by count.

Contested seams. Whether מִקָּטֶל (“by slaughter”, v. 9) ends v. 9 or opens v. 10: the Greek and Syriac join it to v. 10, as BHS notes. Verse 15a addresses all nations and v. 15b returns to the singular “you”. Wellhausen, Rudolph and Wolff move or split it; Baker (Tyndale) and Block keep it, Block noting that the move “lacks manuscript support” [S]. BHS records no variant [T]. Neither the WLC file nor the BHS export marks a paragraph division inside the book, so no Masoretic break helps here [T, these witnesses only].

Microscript

Because Edom, who sat high and thought no one could bring him down, stood with the strangers on his brother’s day, YHWH will bring him down on the day of YHWH, when what he did returns on his own head. A remnant on Mount Zion will possess the land, and the kingdom will be the LORD’s.

Test against the movements: v. 1 (summons), 2–9 (brought down), 10–14 (brother’s day), 15–16 (returns), 17–18 (remnant on Zion), 19–21 (land and kingdom). All six serve it.

What each use does, with rarity and closest rival:

  • Jer 49:7–22. The same oracle against Edom. Obadiah’s order runs 49:14–16, then back to 49:9, 10, 7. First of 862. קָטֹן נְתַתִּיךָ בַּגּוֹיִם (“small I have made you among the nations”) stands verbatim in both, two verses only. Direction of dependence: open [S, all sides]:
    • Obadiah first (Keil, from Caspari; Pusey): the shared lines run in order in Obadiah and are scattered in Jeremiah, which holds [T]. But Keil’s “nothing elsewhere in Jeremiah” is too strong: Jer 6:9 has the gleaning, and 51:53 the height and the plunderers [T].
    • Jeremiah first (Raabe, Nogalski, Dicou, Renkema; Block; Schnittjer “mildly”): Obadiah’s habit of borrowing, and his strengthening of the imagery (the stars, the “we”). The “we” is MT only; the Greek of Obadiah reads ἤκουσα (“I heard”), as in Jeremiah [T].
    • Common source (Bewer, Wolff, Allen, Stuart, Mason, Ben Zvi, Barton, Hadjiev): variation in wording and order.
    • Keil’s “obscure to clear” and Schnittjer’s “strengthening” are opposite rules of style, and neither decides it. נֶחְפְּשׂוּ (“ransacked”, Obad 6) inverts two letters of חָשַׂפְתִּי (“I have stripped”, Jer 49:10) [T], which Schnittjer reads as adaptation; it cannot show direction.
    • The synoptic run (dig-deeper-edom-oracle-obadiah-1-jeremiah-49, as corrected by claim audit 2):
      • Not one text: no aligned pair is identical even for spelling; 31 of Jeremiah’s 50 words in the shared lines stand in Obadiah in order [T].
      • Jer 49:7–22 is a compilation: nine of its sixteen verses repeat lines found elsewhere in Jeremiah (of Jerusalem, Moab, Hazor, Babylon), and the lines it shares with Obadiah (49:9, 14–16) are the ones it does not repeat. Moderate–high [T; S: audit 2].
      • The lines came into Jeremiah from outside, as Amos 1 does at Jer 49:3, 27 and Isa 15–16 in Jer 48. Jeremiah’s oracles against the nations share lines with other books at 11 of 231 verses, against 4 in Isaiah’s and none in Ezekiel’s. Moderate [S: audit 2].
      • That they are older than Obadiah, or are Obadiah itself: low–moderate. Obadiah first against a common source stays open.
      • Closeness of wording cannot decide whose a line is; neither can the “house vocabulary” of each version, which failed an unchosen-word test [S: audit 2].
  • Joel 3:5. Escape on Mount Zion. פְּלֵיטָה (“those who escape”) with הַר צִיּוֹן (“Mount Zion”): four verses. The ordered תִּהְיֶה פְלֵיטָה (“there will be those who escape”) stands in Joel 3:5 and Obad 17 only. Rivals Isa 37:32 = 2 Kgs 19:31 say “from Mount Zion”. Joel adds כַּאֲשֶׁר אָמַר יְהוָה (“as the LORD has said”), which marks a citation in Joel without naming its source. Slight lean to Joel as the later text, moderate [S: audit 2]. It rests on two things: the formula, which points back to a word Joel itself does not supply, and Joel’s demonstrated habit of reuse (Joel 4:16 = Amos 1:2 verbatim) [T]. Keil (Joel early) and Bewer (Joel late) agree on it from opposite dates; Schnittjer finds “fairly strong evidence”; Hubbard (TOTC), Baker (NIVAC; NBC) and Allen (NBC) take Joel to quote Obad 17, Hubbard allowing a shared oral tradition [S: Logos round 2]. That Joel’s line is the longer is not an argument. Isa 37:32 / 2 Kgs 19:31, the only other texts with escape on Zion, also name Jerusalem, so Joel may be combining two earlier words [T; S: audit 2]. No commentator returned names them as Joel’s referent; Grotius, through Fausset, reads Obad 17 itself with Isa 37:32 [S: Logos round 2] (O40). Block is uncertain [S].
  • Joel 4:3, 4, 7, 12, 14, 17, 19. Live on cumulative grounds: Joel 4 stays between 6th and 12th of 922 chapters when any one shared word is removed. גְּמוּל (“recompense”) with רֹאשׁ (“head”): Joel 4:4, 7 and Obad 15 only, the strongest item, and the Greek agrees. מֵחֲמַס (“because of violence”) with an objective genitive at Joel 4:19 and Obad 10. יַדּוּ גוֹרָל (“cast lots”): three verses, but Nah 3:10 has the closer frame. The near-day line is a formula (five verses verbatim) and adds nothing.
  • Amos 9:12. Amos ends with Edom and Obadiah comes next, so a reader will connect them. But יָרַשׁ (“possess”) with אֱדוֹם (“Edom”) in one verse is what chance would give (expected 0.82), and adjacent books in the Twelve share no more rare vocabulary than non-adjacent ones (mean rank 34.7 of 66; chance 33.5). Do not say Obadiah “unfolds that clause”.
  • Amos 1:6, 9, 11–12; 5:6; 9:2. הִסְגִּיר (“hand over”) with גָּלוּת (“exiles”) and Edom: Amos 1:6, 9 only. A brother pursued (1:11). Fire and Joseph (5:6): two verses. מִשָּׁם אוֹרִידֵם (“from there I will bring them down”, 9:2), near-verbatim. Rival for 9:2 is Jer 49:16, which is exact.
  • Num 24:17–21. Star, Edom a possession, survivor destroyed, “set your nest in the rock” (to the Kenite). שָׂרִיד (“survivor”) with יַעֲקֹב (“Jacob”): Num 24:19 and Obad 18 only. שִׂים (“set”) with קִנֶּךָ (“your nest”), the odd form at Obad 4: Num 24:21 and Obad 4 only; in Swete θῇς… νοσσιάν σου (“you set your nest”) likewise. Star, nest and survivor in one five-verse window: Num 24:17–21 only. The window ranks 13th of about 19,500 by raw count and 95th when weighted by rarity. Both auditors reached it separately, and Delitzsch made the same comparison: “compare Num. 24:21, 18, 19 with Ob. 4, 18, 19” [S: Keil]. Schnittjer, more strictly, files Num 24:18 as a broad allusion only [S]. The senses are transferred boldly: the star is a ruler in Numbers and Edom’s false height here.
  • Gen 25–36; Num 20:14. The family. Jacob is הַקָּטָן (“the younger”, “small”; Gen 27:15, 42), and now Esau is made קָטֹן (“small”, v. 2). תֵּימָן (“Teman”) from Esau’s line (Gen 36:11). אָחִיךָ יִשְׂרָאֵל (“your brother Israel”, Num 20:14), said to Edom’s king, is the nearest frame for אָחִיךָ יַעֲקֹב (“your brother Jacob”).
  • Deut 2:5; Josh 24:4. The grant of Esau’s mountain, reversed. יָרַשׁ (“possess”), עֵשָׂו (“Esau”) and הַר (“mountain”) in one verse: Josh 24:4 and Obad 19 only (Deut 2:5 uses the cognate noun).
  • Lam 2:22; 4:21–22; Lam 2:3. The same catastrophe from two sides. פָּלִיט (“fugitive”) with שָׂרִיד (“survivor”) and יוֹם (“day”): Lam 2:22 and Obad 14 only. Lam 4:21–22 addresses Edom directly, with the cup; “rejoice” with Edom by the verb is Lam 4:21 only, though the noun שִׂמְחָה (“joy”) joins Edom at Ezek 35:15 and 36:5. יַעֲקֹב (“Jacob”) with לֶהָבָה (“flame”): Lam 2:3 and Obad 18 only; but the reversal is a reader’s construal, and Isa 10:17 is closer in shape.
  • Ps 137:7–8. Edom and “the day of Jerusalem”. Edom, יוֹם (“day”) and Jerusalem in one verse: Ps 137:7 only. Obadiah spreads them over vv. 1–12, so the scan sees no rare pair. At v. 15, Ps 137:8’s גְּמוּלֵךְ (“your recompense”) is addressed to Babylon and lacks “return” and “head”; גְּמוּל with שׁוּב and רֹאשׁ stands only at Joel 4:4, 7 and Obad 15, so Joel is the partner there [T; S: audit 2].
  • Ezek 35; 25:12–14. Edom’s calamity-gloating; magnified mouth; “as you… so I will do”. תֵּימָן (“Teman”) with כָּרַת (“cut off”): Ezek 25:13 and Obad 9. גָּדַל (“magnify”) with פֶּה (“mouth”): four verses. Ezek 35:5 has בְּעֵת אֵידָם (“at the time of their calamity”), not v. 13’s form, which is exact only at Jer 18:17 [T; S: audit 2]. Ezek 35:13 וַתַּגְדִּילוּ… בְּפִיכֶם (“you magnified yourselves… with your mouth”) is the one match in sense to v. 12; the pair “magnify” + “mouth” in four verses is at chance level [S: audit 2]. Block and Baker (Tyndale) list both [S]. Ezek 35 addresses Edom in the plural at 35:9 and 35:13, among 31 singular forms [T], which bears on v. 16 (below). No commentator returned remarks on it.
  • Isa 14:13–15; Job 39:27–28; Jer 48:28–29; Amos 9:2; Ezek 31. Height and descent, from two sources that should not be pooled [S: audit 2].
    • The hubris is Isa 14’s: 14:13 אָמַרְתָּ בִלְבָבְךָ (“you said in your heart”) with כּוֹכְבֵי־אֵל (“stars of God”), then 14:15 תּוּרָד (“you will be thrust down”). כּוֹכָב (“star”) with the long form לֵבָב (“heart”): Isa 14:13 only [T]. The best model for the content of Obadiah’s lines against Jer 49.
    • The wording is stock: נֶשֶׁר (“eagle”) with גָּבַהּ (“build high”): Jer 49:16, Obad 4 and Job 39:27 only [T]; with Jer 48:28–29 (Moab’s rock-nest pride) and Num 24:21 (שִׂים בַּסֶּלַע קִנֶּךָ, “set your nest in the rock”).
    • The syntax of v. 4 is Amos 9:2’s: אִם… וְאִם… מִשָּׁם אוֹרִידֵם (“if… and if… from there I will bring them down”), and in the Greek ἐκεῖθεν κατάξω (“from there I will bring down”) only Amos 9:2 and Obad 4 [T; S: audit 2]. Moderate, unaudited (O33). Amos precedes Obadiah.
  • Jer 38:22. הִסִּיתוּךָ וְיָכְלוּ לְךָ אַנְשֵׁי שְׁלֹמֶךָ (“they have misled and overpowered you, the men at peace with you”) against Obad 7 הִשִּׁיאוּךָ יָכְלוּ לְךָ אַנְשֵׁי שְׁלֹמֶךָ (“they have deceived you, they have overpowered you, the men at peace with you”). Three words identical but for a waw, with a deception verb before them; ἄνδρες εἰρηνικοί (“men of peace”) in Swete at those two verses only. Jer 38:22 is a saying, so a shared proverb is possible. Jer 20:10 has the same three ideas, כֹּל אֱנוֹשׁ שְׁלוֹמִי (“every man of my peace”), יְפֻתֶּה (“he will be deceived”) and וְנוּכְלָה לוֹ (“we will prevail over him”) [T], which strengthens the case for idiom. No commentator returned cites it, or calls the line a proverb; Bewer calls Jer 38:22 “not necessarily conscious” quotation [S].
  • Ps 41:10. A motif only. אִישׁ (“man”), שָׁלוֹם (“peace”) and לֶחֶם (“bread”): Ps 41:10 and Obad 7, but לַחְמְךָ (“your bread”) has no equivalent in the Greek. The same consonants are read two ways: “those who eat with you” (Patterson–Hill, the NET), which is nearer the psalm’s אוֹכֵל לַחְמִי (“who ate my bread”), and “those fighting you” (BHS’s לֹחֲמֶיךָ, the versions), which Baker rejects because the line parallels two terms of friendship [S]. So the link survives wherever a friend is meant. Fausset, Baker and Block cite it [S].
  • Deut 32:28–29. וְאֵין בָּהֶם תְּבוּנָה (“and there is no understanding in them”), then לוּ חָכְמוּ (“if they were wise”) [T], beside v. 7’s אֵין תְּבוּנָה בּוֹ (“no understanding in him”) and v. 8’s wise men destroyed. Patterson–Hill and Block cite it [S]. “No understanding” with a pronoun stands only at Deut 32:28 and Obad 7 [T; S: audit 2]. Close but not exact; Prov 21:30 also has אֵין תְּבוּנָה. Low–moderate.
  • Ps 22:29. The closing line. Block calls it a “close parallel” [S]. לַיהוָה הַמְּלוּכָה (“to the LORD the kingdom”), same order, two verses. But it is two words in a formula frame (compare Ps 3:9; Prov 21:31), one in eight of Obadiah’s two-word sequences stands in exactly one other verse, and the psalm shows no other contact. An exact shared acclamation, not an allusion. Isa 34:12, on Edom, is the thematic negative.
  • Judg 2:16, 18; Neh 9:27. Saviours who judge. יָשַׁע (“save”) with שָׁפַט (“judge”): eight verses, led by Judges. Plural מוֹשִׁיעִים (“deliverers”): Neh 9:27 and Obad 21 only. Butler, Block, Baker and Patterson–Hill all hear the judges here, and Patterson–Hill name Othniel and Ehud (Judg 3:9, 15) [S].
  • Lev 24:19; Ezek 35:15; Ps 7:17; Judg 1:7. Measure for measure. Lev 24:19 כַּאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה כֵּן יֵעָשֶׂה לּוֹ (“as he has done, so it shall be done to him”) is the near-formal twin of v. 15a, in the same passive stem. Ezek 35:15 is to Edom. Ps 7:17 יָשׁוּב עֲמָלוֹ בְרֹאשׁוֹ (“his mischief will return upon his own head”) is the best syntax for v. 15b, though שׁוּב (“return”) with רֹאשׁ (“head”) is common. The four-word “only” at Ps 7:17 is an artefact of scattered words; יָרַד (“descend”) there has another sense. Joel 4:4, 7 is nearer in vocabulary.
  • Deut 32:35; Jer 18:17. A plausible background only. Obad 13’s exact בְּיוֹם אֵידָם (“in the day of their disaster”) is at Jer 18:17; Deut 32:35 has כִּי קָרוֹב יוֹם אֵידָם (“for the day of their calamity is near”) without the preposition. The near-day line is a formula. Rom 12:19 quotes this verse’s first colon, so it does not connect to Obad 15 by allusion.
  • Gen 27:41. Low. “Said in his heart” is idiom (30 verses), and Isa 14:13 is closer; “near” differs in form and sense; “brother” belongs to the whole Edom tradition. A reader may hear Esau’s resolve behind v. 10, but the wording does not require this verse.
  • Prov 17:5; 24:17; Job 31:3. A shared word and censure. שָׂמֵחַ לְאֵיד לֹא יִנָּקֶה (“he who rejoices at calamity will not go unpunished”, Prov 17:5). אַל־תִּשְׂמַח (“do not rejoice”, Prov 24:17). נֵכֶר (“misfortune”) with אֵיד (“calamity”): Job 31:3 only. Also prophetic: Mic 7:8; Jer 49:8. Prov 27:10 was tested and discarded: its sense runs the other way.
  • Mic 7:8–10. An enemy gloating over a fallen Zion. אַל־תִּשְׂמְחִי (“do not rejoice”), and בּוּשָׁה (“shame”) as the subject of כָּסָה (“cover”): Mic 7:10 and Obad 10 only (at Ezek 7:18 the subject is “horror”). The sense matches, and it survives because of shape, not vocabulary mass.
  • Hab 2:17. חֲמַס לְבָנוֹן יְכַסֶּךָּ (“the violence done to Lebanon will overwhelm you”) against Obad 10: violence, objective genitive, “cover” with second-person object. In Swete καλύψει σε (“will cover you”) at Hab 2:17 and Obad 10, each with ἀσέβεια (“impiety”); one translator of the Twelve may explain that. Hab 2:9 is a motif only, and 2:16 has been dropped.
  • Isa 51:17–23; Jer 25:28–29; 49:12; Joel 4:3; Amos 4:1. Who drank? See Open Question 2. כּוֹס (“cup”) is not in Obadiah, but all the wrath uses of “drink” elsewhere have a cup, wine or wrath nearby, so its absence argues against neither reading.
  • Judg 1; Josh 16:10; Jer 32:44; 33:13. Mostly the ordinary vocabulary of land. Judg 1 has Negev, Shephelah, Benjamin, Ephraim, Canaanite, Jerusalem and בֵּית יוֹסֵף (“house of Joseph”) with יָרַשׁ (“possess”), which goes modestly beyond that. But nothing in Obadiah marks anything as unfinished. Jeremiah’s restoration lists are the nearer model, with עָרֵי הַנֶּגֶב (“the cities of the Negev”) at Jer 13:19; 32:44; 33:13 and Obad 20.
  • Isa 10:17–20. פְּלֵיטָה (“escape”) with יַעֲקֹב (“Jacob”) in one verse: Isa 10:20 and Obad 17 only [T]. But in Isa 10:17 וְהָיָה אוֹר־יִשְׂרָאֵל לְאֵשׁ (“the light of Israel will become a fire”) the fire is God, not the people. For a people as fire the nearer texts are Zech 12:6 (Judah’s clans as a fire-pot) and, reversed, Amos 5:6 (fire on the house of Joseph) [S: audit 2]. Moderate on the phrase.
  • Isa 29:14 at v. 8. Destroying the wisdom of the wise and the understanding of the discerning; near-verbatim in the Greek, and quoted at 1 Cor 1:19 [S: audit 2]. Nearer than Deut 32:28–29 for v. 8. Moderate, unaudited (O34); relevant for any New Testament reading of v. 8.
  • Isa 63:1–5. Edom judged is the link, not wording. Isa 63:3, 5 denies any helper, so it is a contrast with Obad 21’s plural deliverers. Isa 63 to Rev 19 is firm. Rev 19 names Babylon, not Edom. No commentator returned reads Obad 21 with Isa 63 [S]. Keep Isa 63 for the Edom theme generally, not as v. 21’s partner.
  • Isa 5:24; 47:14. Stubble and fire. אֵשׁ (“fire”), לֶהָבָה (“flame”) and קַשׁ (“stubble”): these three verses.
  • Isa 15:1. Night, destruction, ruin (of Moab). שָׁדַד (“destroy”) with דָּמָה (“ruin”): two verses, these.
  • Mal 1:2–4. Esau and Jacob brothers; Esau’s mountains; the Twelve’s closing Edom text. אָח (“brother”) with Esau and Jacob outside Genesis: Mal 1:2 only. Mal 3:19 adds קַשׁ (“stubble”) with זֵדִים (“arrogant ones”, cognate of זָדוֹן, “arrogance”, v. 3), that pair in Mal 3:19 only. The real link is אָח (“brother”) with יַעֲקֹב (“Jacob”) outside Genesis: Obad 10 and Mal 1:2 only. Mal 3:19 is low as wording. As a field it is stronger: the arrogance root within twenty verses of קַשׁ (“stubble”) occurs only in Obadiah and Malachi (moderate, unaudited, O35); and Greek Malachi’s ἀλλογενεῖς (“foreigners”) for זֵדִים gives foreigners with stubble, a reception datum only (low–moderate) [S: audit 2]. Edom does not frame the Twelve: it clusters in books 2–4, then returns as the only nation named in Malachi.

Top ten of the scan, accounted for (chapters of ten verses or more): Jer 49, Ezek 25, Job 39, Lam 2 and Joel 4 enter the map. Ezek 31 enters as analogue (a tree lifted high and brought down). Jer 40 enters only for background: גָּלוּת יְרוּשָׁלִַם (“the exile of Jerusalem”) at 40:1 and Judeans in Edom at 40:11. Isa 57 is scattered common words, though יִירַשׁ הַר־קָדְשִׁי (“will possess my holy mountain”, 57:13) is worth a look in a dig. Dan 12 is incidental. Josh 16, set aside as incidental in v1.0, now enters the map: its verse is about land not taken.

A scan’s blind spot. Lowering the floor to five verses puts Joel 3 second of 922, a five-verse chapter the ten-verse floor excludes. Isa 15 and Isa 18 also appear (Isa 18’s envoy is incidental). The scan has a second blind spot: it compares verse with verse, so it cannot see words Obadiah spreads over adjoining verses. Scoring five-verse windows finds such clusters (Num 24:17–21). Word sets drawn from scattered verses are candidates only: 14.2 % of the verses of the Hebrew Bible hold a three-word set from Obadiah’s vocabulary found in no other verse.

Book-level finding. Obadiah is not citation-light. It is densely allusive in vv. 1–9, carried there by Jeremiah 49, and in vv. 15–21 by Joel and Lamentations. Verses 10–14 are not short of rare contacts (59 pairs, about 12 per verse, against about 9 and 10 for the other blocks once Jeremiah 49 is set aside), but those contacts are scattered: no chapter concentrates them, and the top of that block’s scan (Dan 12, Ps 54, Nah 3) is mostly incidental. The charge has no dominant outside partner, unlike the sentence before it [T for the measure; I for its meaning], though Mic 7:8–10 is a real partner, and Prov 17:5 and 24:17 share its censure. Passage runs should lean on Move 4 there. This corrects a first-draft claim that vv. 10–14 had the fewest contacts, which the count did not support.

Schnittjer’s stricter grade. Of all these, Schnittjer counts only Jer 49 and Joel as real interpretive use, and treats the rest (lots, Amos 1, Ezek 35, Num 24:18) as stock phrases and shared themes [S]. Block, by contrast, lists about thirty earlier texts for Obadiah’s motifs, and suggests some may be “stock expressions in circulation” [S]. This map’s moderate ratings sit between them.

NT. No quotation. Jones lists one link, Obad 21 with Rev 11:15, without argument; Schnittjer lists none [S]. Treat it as language taken up, not a citation.

Hebrew and Greek Side by Side

Obadiah’s own pluses against Jer 49 [T]. Two items stand in Obadiah and not in Jeremiah’s parallel: the taunt מִי יוֹרִדֵנִי אָרֶץ (“Who will bring me down to earth?”, v. 3), and בֵּין כּוֹכָבִים (“among the stars”, v. 4). Both raise the height from which he falls. Jer 49 also has no יַעֲקֹב (“Jacob”), no צִיּוֹן (“Zion”), and its only אָח (“brother”) at 49:10 is Esau’s own kin. Whichever way the dependence ran, in Obadiah the old oracle has been given a brother.

Christological Trajectory

Type: trajectory, with contrast. No direct prophecy of a messiah. מֶלֶךְ (“king”) does not occur in the book, and no Davidic figure appears [T]. The hope is theocentric: not the saviours but YHWH holds הַמְּלוּכָה (“the kingdom”) [T].

  • The kingdom [I, high; S: Jones, Bridger, Patterson–Hill for Rev 11:15]. “And the kingdom will be the LORD’s” is taken up in the NT’s language: ἐγένετο ἡ βασιλεία… τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν καὶ τοῦ χριστοῦ αὐτοῦ (“the kingdom has become… of our Lord and of his Christ”, Rev 11:15), and Jesus reigns ἐπὶ τὸν οἶκον Ἰακώβ (“over the house of Jacob”, Luke 1:33). The same two words stand at Ps 22:29, as an exact shared acclamation. Neither NT text is marked as citing Obadiah.
  • Saviours on Zion [I, moderate]. The plural belongs to the judges pattern (Judg 2:16; Neh 9:27), and it longs for a final one. Bridger asks whether it finds fulfilment in the Son of David, and Patterson–Hill see a “veiled spiritual hint” of Christ, citing Rev 11:15 and 19:15 [S]; neither argues it from the wording. Zech 9:9 puts the single king coming to Zion as נוֹשָׁע (“saved”, “victorious”), and the Greek of Obad 21 makes those who go up “saved” as well. The move from plural to one is a canonical inference, not Obadiah’s.
  • The brother who does not stand aloof [I, moderate; contrast]. Edom stood מִנֶּגֶד (“aloof”) on his brother’s day. Jesus is made like his brothers in every respect (τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς ὁμοιωθῆναι, “to be made like his brothers”, Heb 2:17).
  • Descent [I, moderate; contrast]. “Who will bring me down?” is answered for Edom by force. Luke 1:52 sings the same reversal, and the Son comes down of his own will.
  • The drinking [I, uncertain]. Verse 16 says all nations will drink, and Jesus asks that τὸ ποτήριον (“the cup”) pass (Mark 14:36). But כּוֹס (“cup”) is not in Obadiah. It comes from Jer 49:12 and Lam 4:21, and the addressee of v. 16 is disputed. This may only be made through those texts.
  • One saviour, from Edom [I, low–moderate from Obad 21; reflection; no commentator links them]. Obadiah’s plural deliverers follow the Judges pattern. Isa 63:1–5 has one who comes from Edom, רַב לְהוֹשִׁיעַ (“mighty to save”), who treads alone with no helper, and Rev 19:13, 15 applies that garment and winepress firmly to the Word of God, though Rev 19 names Babylon. This is a contrast and a canonical reflection, not Obadiah’s direction. Greek Obad 21 has no deliverers at all.
  • The returning deeds [I, moderate; contrast; S: Bridger]. “Your dealings will return on your own head” (v. 15). For those in Christ, what should return on us returned on him (2 Cor 5:21; Isa 53:6). The line comes from the July overview. Bridger (BST, pp. 66–68) makes the same move: the law of v. 15 “was perfectly satisfied, when Jesus became our perfect substitute”. He argues it from God’s justice, not from Obadiah’s words.
  • What became of Edom [T for each text; I for the connection]. Ἰδουμαία (“Idumea”) occurs once in the NT, among the crowds coming to Jesus (Mark 3:8). And at Jerusalem, James quotes Amos 9:12 in a Greek where “possess the remnant of Edom” has become “the remnant of humanity may seek the Lord” (Acts 15:17, οἱ κατάλοιποι τῶν ἀνθρώπων, “the remnant of humanity”). This is Amos, not Obadiah, but it is the NT’s own handling of the verse Obadiah expands, and the possession language is answered by the calling of the nations. A preacher should not skip from v. 19 to a map, or to Edom’s elimination, without passing through this.

Moralism check. The temptation is “don’t be proud, don’t gloat.” True, but Obadiah does not end on an instruction. Its last word is an announcement about who reigns. The gospel grounding is that no one is safe by height, the day comes on all the nations (v. 15), and escape is found on the mountain YHWH calls his own (v. 17), which is given and not achieved.

Preaching Traps

  1. “A sermon on pride.” It sounds right, and vv. 2–4 support it. But the charge in the book is not pride. It is violence to a brother, vv. 10–14. Bites at vv. 2–4, if the sermon never reaches v. 10.
  2. “The sin of standing by.” A good word, but it stops at v. 11. The eight prohibitions escalate from watching, to rejoicing, to entering, to looting, to blocking the road and handing over survivors. Bites at vv. 11–14.
  3. A licence for vengeance. “As you have done, it will be done to you” is YHWH’s act. Prov 24:29 forbids the same words on a human tongue, and Rom 12:19, quoting Deut 32:35, forbids personal revenge. Bites at vv. 15, 18, and in pastoral settings with people who have been deeply wronged. Give the comfort honestly, without turning it into permission.
  4. Mapping modern nations. Vv. 19–20 read as a map of the present Middle East, or Edom as a code for a current people. The text has no such key, and v. 20 is textually damaged. Bites at vv. 17–21.
  5. Edom as a theological cipher. Esau as “the flesh”, Herod as Edom’s heir (Herod’s Idumaean descent is [S], and the NT does not make the argument), or Romans 9 as the book’s key. Obadiah’s charge is historic conduct, not birth, and אָהֵב (“love”) and שָׂנֵא (“hate”) are not here. Bites at vv. 1, 10, 18.
  6. Hearing the tenses as settled. ESV presents vv. 5–7 as past, and NASB95 as future. Both render vv. 12–14 “do not”, though the events appear to be past from v. 11. Is Edom fallen, or falling? At vv. 12–14 the events are past (moderate–high, [S: audit 2]), but the force is open: an imaginative return to the day, or a prohibition with lasting force. The sermon has to decide and say so. Bites at vv. 5–7, 12–14. Likewise the “cup” at v. 16, which the text does not name.
  7. Ducking the judgement. Softening or skipping v. 18 because it is uncomfortable. It sounds kind, but without it the comfort to the wounded is empty. Read it, take the image seriously, and carry it to the NT’s handling. Bites at vv. 15–18.

Pulpit and Study Text

These are notes to the preacher on what the congregation will hear, not verdicts on translation.

  • v. 1. קוּמוּ וְנָקוּמָה (“rise and let us rise”). NASB95: “Arise and let us go”. ESV: “Rise up! Let us rise”. ESV keeps the repetition.
  • v. 2. נְתַתִּיךָ (“I have made you”), perfect. NASB95: “I will make”. ESV: “I will make”. Both future. Neither signals the perfect.
  • vv. 5–7. Perfects. NASB95: “will be ruined”, “will be ransacked”. ESV: “have been destroyed”, “has been pillaged”. ESV: it has happened. NASB95: it will.
  • v. 7. אֵין תְּבוּנָה בּוֹ (“no understanding in him”). NASB95: “(There is no understanding in him.)”. ESV: “you have no understanding”. ESV turns the aside into direct address.
  • v. 11. ketiv “gate” / qere “gates”. NASB95: “gate”. ESV: “gates”. Small; see the apparatus note above.
  • v. 12. וְאַל (“and do not”). NASB95: “Do not”. ESV: “But do not”. ESV adds a contrast that וְ (“and”) may not carry.
  • vv. 13–14. “their” once, then “his”. NASB95: “their” throughout. ESV: “their”, then “his”. ESV keeps the shift to one person.
  • v. 14. תַּסְגֵּר (“hand over”). NASB95: “imprison”. ESV: “hand over”. ESV matches the use in Amos 1:6, 9, HALOT, Keil, Block and Baker; the NASB95 is the outlier.
  • v. 17. מוֹרָשֵׁיהֶם (“their possessions”). NASB95: “their possessions”. ESV: “their own possessions”. ESV’s “own” is an interpretation. A likely text is “those who dispossessed them” (moderate), and neither version shows it. The oldest Hebrew manuscript of Obadiah, the Murabbaʿat scroll, probably reads it. Both also give “those who escape” where Block, the Greek and the Syriac read “escape”, an abstract noun; the audit finds the Zion parallels concrete, so “deliverance / an escaped remnant” holds both [S: audit 2].
  • vv. 19–20. Syntax disputed. NASB95: “those of the Shephelah the Philistine plain”. ESV: “shall possess the land of the Philistines”. Different maps. v. 20’s subjects differ too.
  • v. 21. לִשְׁפֹּט (“to judge”). NASB95: “to judge”. ESV: “to rule”. Block defends “rule”, since the object is a place [S]. Fair, but the ESV’s congregation does not hear the Judges’ verb, so tell them.

Open Questions

  1. Verses 12–14. The Hebrew is prohibition: אַל (“not”) with the jussive. No attested use of אַל with a second-person jussive has a past-tense sense; Jer 20:14 and Job 3:4–7 aim third-person jussives at a past day [T; S: audit 2]. Keil, Block and Baker call “you should not have” ungrammatical, and Mason approves it [S]. The referent is past, from v. 11’s perfects and v. 15, and five words of v. 11 recur in vv. 12–14 (wealth, “you too”, gate, stand, the נכר group) [T]: moderate–high [S: audit 2]. The force is open, with three readings held:
    • a real warning before the event (Collins, Niehaus; Caspari and Hengstenberg, through Keil);
    • an imaginative return to the day (Baker, NIVAC, p. 181; Hadjiev; Allen; the NET). “Most interpreters” take this reading, on Hadjiev’s report (p. 45), a headcount, not an argument [S: Hadjiev];
    • a prohibition with lasting force on a past act (Block, for all hearers; Keil, as a type recurring until the day of the LORD) [S].
    • Patterson–Hill (p. 228) set out the options and quote Finley: “in the final analysis very little difference may exist between the two interpretations” [S]. For a sermon, the second and third readings come to nearly the same thing. No grammar in the library discusses a past-referring prohibition; Davidson cites Obad 12–14 only for אַל (“not”) repeated “in impassioned language” [S: Logos round 2].
  2. Verse 16: who drank? Uncertain; slight lean to Judah, low–moderate.
    • For Judah: the plural שְׁתִיתֶם (“you drank”) after singular “you” [T]; Baker’s count of two plural forms among forty, which holds [T]; Baker’s argument that “just as” fails if the first “drank” is literal and the next two metaphorical; Jer 49:12. Held by Baker, Block, Wolff, Raabe, Ben Zvi, Armerding, Limburg; Rudolph and Barton through Hadjiev [S].
    • For Edom: Keil, from the parallel כַּאֲשֶׁר עָשִׂיתָ (“as you have done”, v. 15), and because Edom alone is addressed; with Calvin, Stuart, Niehaus, the NET, and Robinson (Biblical Expositor), who reads the cup as Edom’s “festive celebration” over Jerusalem’s fall [S]. The Targum reads Edom too: כְמָא דַחדִיתוּן עַל מַחַת טוּרָא דְקוּדשִי (“as you [pl.] rejoiced over the blow on my holy mountain”), replacing the drinking with v. 12’s rejoicing [T: Targum, CAL]. That is an interpretation, not a variant, but it is the oldest Jewish reading of the verse in hand. Joel 4:3 (lots, then “they drank”) and 1 Sam 30:16 show victory drinking, against Block’s “never” [T]. Ezek 35 addresses Edom with 31 singular and 5 plural forms, the plurals at 35:9 and 35:13 [T; S: audit 2], so a single plural need not mean a new addressee: the plural argument is neutralised. No commentator returned meets this. The Greek levels both Ezek 35:13 and Obad 16 to the singular, so its ἔπιες (“you drank”) is a translator’s habit, not a witness; the Syriac keeps the plural [T]. The Judah reading now rests almost wholly on the cup tradition (Jer 49:12; Lam 4:21; Isa 51:17–23). It stays at low–moderate [S: audit 2], but the lean is thin, and a later pass could fairly call the verse balanced.
    • Keil never explains the plural. Block grants “both interpretations are possible” [S].
  3. Direction of dependence with Jer 49. Narrowed by the synoptic run and audit 2: the shared lines came into Jeremiah from outside (moderate). Obadiah first against a common source is open, and no further lever in the text is known.
  4. Verse 20 is corrupt; vv. 19–20 are construed differently. Block and Baker (Tyndale) read Halah (חְלַח, 2 Kgs 17:6) for הַחֵל (“this host”), with a supplied verb [S]. Do not build a map on it.
  5. Sepharad. סְפָרַד (“Sepharad”) only here. Sardis (Hadjiev, after Raabe; the Lydian-Aramaic inscription, KAI 260:2, via Block), a place in the east (Stuart: Media; Block: Babylon), Spain (from the Targum) [S]. Nothing rests on it.
  6. Num 24:17–21 — closed. Audit 1 rated it moderate–high; audit 2’s Auditor A, blind to that verdict, found it again and rated it moderate [S: audits 1–2]. Logos round 2 adds a history row: Delitzsch, “had the prophecies of Balaam as his original”; Robbins (Bibliotheca Sacra, 1846), Obad 3–4 with Num 24:21 and Obad 17–19 with 24:18–19; Schnittjer, a broad allusion at v. 21 only [S]. No rejection was found. Moderate–high kept.
  7. The untested queue (O33–O38, O40), all at moderate or below and marked unaudited. Round 2 found holders for four: Amos 9:2 (Lange); Mal 3:19 at v. 18 (Keil); Jer 48:28 at v. 3 (Fausset); Gen 32:8 (Heb 32:9) at v. 17 (Baker, NIVAC p. 191) [S]. None was found for Isa 29:14. O39 is dropped. אֵיד (“calamity”) occurs in 22 verses (WLC). Jeremiah’s uses are 18:17 and four in the oracles against the nations (Egypt, Moab, Edom, Kedar), and nine are in Job and Proverbs: it is not an Edom word [T].
