Headline Findings

  1. The letter does what it says: it renounces command, and its grammar keeps the renunciation. Paul says he has boldness ἐπιτάσσειν (“to order”, v. 8) and instead παρακαλῶ (“I appeal”, vv. 9, 10); he will do nothing χωρὶς … τῆς σῆς γνώμης (“without your consent”, v. 14) so that Philemon’s good be not κατὰ ἀνάγκην (“by compulsion”) but κατὰ ἑκούσιον (“of your own free will”, v. 14) [T]. And there is no imperative in the letter until v. 17 — all four (vv. 17, 18, 20, 22) stand after the appeal is complete (parsed in the SBLGNT index) [T]. Surfaced by: Tool 3 (structure), Tool 4 (Διό, “therefore”; Εἰ οὖν, “if then”), the mood analysis (Original Language Observations), Tool 7 (the command / compulsion / free-will vocabulary), and Tool 11’s Pauline companions (2 Cor 8:8, “I am not speaking this as a command”; 2 Cor 9:7, “not … under compulsion”). Why it matters: the sermon’s tone and its call must keep the same shape — an appeal to love, not an order.

  2. The thanksgiving is the ground of the request, and the request repays it point by point. Love (ἀγάπη, vv. 5, 7 → 9), fellowship (κοινωνία, v. 6 → κοινωνός, “partner”, v. 17), the good (ἀγαθός, v. 6 → 14), and the refreshing of hearts (vv. 7 → 20) all move from what Philemon has done to what he is now asked to do [T] for the words, [I] for the argument. The hearts are the clearest: σπλάγχνον (“heart”) and ἀναπαύω (“to refresh”) stand in one verse at v. 7 and v. 20 and nowhere else in the New Testament or in Swete’s LXX (SBLGNT by lemma; Swete by word-beginning), and between them Onesimus is τὰ ἐμὰ σπλάγχνα (“my very heart”, v. 12) [T]. To receive Onesimus is to refresh Paul’s heart [I]. v1.2: the claim audit confirms that v. 20 deliberately takes up v. 7 — in Paul’s own idiom of refreshment (2 Cor 7:4–15), but with σπλάγχνα (“heart”) where Paul elsewhere writes πνεῦμα (“spirit”, 1 Cor 16:18; 2 Cor 7:13), and re-addressed with ἀδελφέ (“brother”). The pair ranks first of the letter’s 153 distant verse pairs, a level only about one 25-verse stretch in twenty reaches across Paul [S: audit 1]. It is a recapitulation at two section-endings (vv. 7, 20), not a frame around vv. 8–19, and v. 12’s place between them is not structural — its force is what it says. Surfaced by: Tool 10, Tool 3, Tool 11 Move 4, Tool 7. High confidence on the words; moderate–high on the deliberate recapitulation.

  3. Onesimus is given a new identity in family words before he is given a name, and the master’s titles pass to the slave. He is “my child, whom I have begotten in my imprisonment” (v. 10) — and only then Ὀνήσιμον (“Onesimus”) [T]; “my very heart” (v. 12); “a beloved brother” (v. 16); “as … me” (v. 17) [T]. ἀγαπητός (“beloved”) is Philemon’s title at v. 1 and Onesimus’s at v. 16, and nowhere else in the letter [T]. Philemon is twice called ἀδελφέ (“brother”, vv. 7, 20) — the only singular vocative “brother” in any New Testament letter (the others are in Luke and Acts; SBLGNT, by parse) [T] — a form available only in letters to one person, where Paul’s other singular address is “child” (1 Tim 1:18; 2 Tim 2:1) [S: audit 1] — and he is asked to call his slave what Paul calls him [I]. The audit’s baseline adds that vv. 1 and 16, where “beloved” passes from master to slave, are one of only three verse pairs in the letter sharing an exclusive pair of content words [S: audit 1]. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 4, Original Audience. Why it matters: the letter’s request is relational before it is legal.

  4. Paul puts himself where the slave stands — in identity and in liability — and signs for it. “Accept him as you would me” (v. 17); “if he has wronged you … or owes you anything, charge that to my account (ἐλλόγα, ‘charge to my account’); I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand, I will repay it (ἀποτίσω, ‘I will repay’)” (vv. 18–19) [T]. Then the debt is turned round: Philemon “owes” Paul “even your own self” (v. 19; ὀφείλω, “to owe”, v. 18 → προσοφείλω, “to owe besides”, v. 19, its only NT occurrence) [T]. Surfaced by: Tool 3, Tool 7, Historical Background (the promissory-note form, [S]), Christological Reading. Why it matters: the gospel shape is here in the action, though the letter never says “as Christ did for you” — the preacher may draw the analogy, and should say that he is drawing it [I].

  5. A personal appeal to one householder is made in the hearing of his house-church. The body (vv. 4–21) is addressed to one man — 18 forms of σύ (“you”, singular), with σός (“your”, v. 14) and σεαυτοῦ (“yourself”, v. 19) — and every imperative is singular; the plural “you” stands at v. 3, returns at v. 22 twice, and closes the letter at v. 25 (SBLGNT, by parse) [T]. Philemon decides alone; the church in his house knows he has been asked (v. 2) [I]. Surfaced by: Tool 3, Tool 9, Original Audience, Tool 16. Why it matters: the decision is personal and its consequences are ecclesial — what a believing household does with a slave is the church’s business [I]. v1.2: “private”, used of the letter in v1.0–1.1, is withdrawn — nothing in the text supports it; Philemon is a household letter read before a house-church [S: audit 2].

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level (from the overview, confirmed here). To secure, without command, Philemon’s free and generous reception of Onesimus “no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother” (v. 16), “as you would me” (v. 17), with any debt laid to Paul’s account — so that the κοινωνία (“fellowship”) of Philemon’s faith becomes effective (v. 6) [I]. Confirms the overview’s microscript.

Passage-level. For a 25-verse letter the passage is the book, so the passage-level purpose has to be stated movement by movement:

  • 1–3 set the frame: Paul as δέσμιος (“prisoner”), not apostle; Philemon as “beloved … fellow worker”; a church as witness [T]/[I].
  • 4–7 establish Philemon’s character by thanksgiving, and so set the terms of the request [I].
  • 8–16 make the appeal and redescribe Onesimus — child, heart, useful, brother [T].
  • 17–20 make the request and remove the obstacles to it — identity (“as me”), debt (“charge it to me”), and obligation (“you owe me yourself”) [T].
  • 21–25 add confidence, a visit and the company of witnesses [T].

The passage does one thing — it asks — and everything in it serves the asking [I].

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. For a whole letter there is no preceding passage in the book, so the check is run at two other levels.

(a) Within Paul’s relationship with Philemon — the history the letter presupposes. Philemon owes Paul “even your own self” (v. 19) [T], so Paul is very likely his father in the faith [I]. Philemon has a record of love “toward all the saints” (v. 5) and has refreshed them (v. 7) [T]. Onesimus, Philemon’s slave (v. 16), has been with Paul, has become Paul’s child (v. 10) and has served him (v. 13) [T]. Something has broken between Onesimus and Philemon (vv. 11, 15, 18) [T]. The letter exists because the gospel has created a relationship — Onesimus as Paul’s son and therefore Philemon’s brother — that the old relationship of master and slave cannot now contain without decision [I].

(b) Within the canon’s Pauline letters — the companion letter. Colossians, sent by the same envoys (Col 4:7–9), has already told the church that Onesimus is “our faithful and beloved brother, who is one of your number” (Col 4:9), and has told slaves and masters that they have one Master in heaven (Col 3:22–4:1) [T]. If the two letters arrived together [I] — a well-grounded inference from the eight shared names and the carrier notices, stated by neither letter [S: audit 2] — the letter to the whole church already speaks of Onesimus in the terms Philemon asks one household to accept: the household code becomes a particular household’s decision [I]. v1.2: “faithful and beloved brother” at Col 4:9 is Paul’s commendation formula for trusted envoys (of Tychicus at Col 4:7; Eph 6:21), so the link rests on the shared setting more than on the phrase [S: audit 2].

Necessity answer. Why does this letter exist, as a separate letter, rather than as a paragraph of Colossians? Because the request can only be made to one person and can only be granted freely (v. 14) [T]/[I]. A command in a public letter would be the very compulsion v. 14 rules out [I]. Within the letter, the same logic orders the movements: the appeal (8–16) cannot come before the thanksgiving (4–7), because it is grounded on it (Διό, “therefore”, v. 8); and the request (17–20) cannot come before the appeal, because “if then” (Εἰ οὖν, v. 17) draws on everything said of Onesimus in vv. 10–16 [T].

Implication. The letter is not a slave-ethics treatise with a personal illustration; it is a single act of intercession whose form is part of its content. Read with Colossians, it is the household code made personal [I].

General context

Before (canonically): Titus, whose household instruction to slaves includes μὴ νοσφιζομένους (“not pilfering”, Titus 2:10) [T]. After: Hebrews, with “remember the prisoners” (Heb 13:3) and Timothy “released” (Heb 13:23) [T]. Neither adjacency does interpretive work [I]. The real neighbour is Colossians, especially 3:18–4:1 and 4:7–18 (see Tool 11).

Out-of-context reading to flag. Philemon is rarely quoted out of context — it is more often not read at all, or read only as a source for a reconstruction of Onesimus’s story (Preaching Pitfall 1).

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
1–3 Address and greeting Prisoner (not apostle); four addressees; plural grace
4–7 Thanksgiving and prayer Love, faith, fellowship, the good, hearts refreshed — “brother”
8–16 The appeal “Therefore”: I could order, I appeal; for my child — Onesimus; sent as my heart; free consent; perhaps separated to be received as a brother
17–20 The request “If then”: accept him as me; charge it to me; I will repay; you owe me yourself; “Yes, brother … refresh my heart”
21–22 Confidence and visit “More than what I say”; “prepare me a lodging”; “through your prayers”
23–25 Greetings and grace Fellow prisoner, fellow workers; grace to “your” (plural) spirit

Device. Linear, in two stages — ground (4–16), then request (17–22) — inside a frame of greeting and grace (1–3; 23–25) [T]. Three framing devices hold it, of different strength:

  • The hearts frame, vv. 7 → 20 — σπλάγχνα (“hearts”) and ἀναπαύω (“to refresh”) with the vocative ἀδελφέ (“brother”) in both verses, and σπλάγχνα again at v. 12 (see Headline 2). The words are exclusive to these verses in the whole NT and Swete [T]; whether Paul designed them as a frame is [I], moderate. No chance baseline has been run for a letter this short; it is a claim-audit target. v1.2 — audited. Within the letter, vv. 7 and 20 rank first of 153 verse pairs at least eight verses apart — the only pair sharing three content words; every short letter tested (2 John, 3 John, Jude, Titus) shows some end-to-end exclusive pair, but across 1,716 25-verse stretches of Paul only 4.7 % reach the level of vv. 7 and 20 [S: audit 1]. Restated: v. 20 deliberately takes up v. 7, in Paul’s idiom of refreshment switched from “spirit” to “heart”. On NA28’s paragraphing (1–3 | 4–7 | 8–14 | 15–20 | 21–22 | 23–24 | 25) vv. 7 and 20 are matching section-endings, not a frame outside vv. 8–19, and v. 12’s middle position proves nothing [S: audit 1]. Moderate–high.
  • Prayer, vv. 4 → 22 — προσευχή (“prayer”) only at v. 4 (“my prayers”) and v. 22 (“your prayers”) [T].
  • Grace, vv. 3 → 25 — χάρις (“grace”) only at vv. 3 and 25, with χαρίζομαι (“to give graciously”) at v. 22 [T]. The grace-frame is standard Pauline epistolary form [T], so it shows convention, not design.
  • Fellow-words, vv. 1–2 → 23–24 — συνεργός (“fellow worker”, vv. 1, 24), συστρατιώτης (“fellow soldier”, v. 2), συναιχμάλωτος (“fellow prisoner”, v. 23) [T].

Central assertions, by section.

  • 4–7: “I thank my God” (truth), grounded by “for … the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you” (truth) [T].
  • 8–14: “I appeal to you for my child … Onesimus” (an indicative of appeal — not a command) [T]. Everything else hangs on that verb: three relative clauses on Onesimus, and two purpose clauses on Paul’s reasons.
  • 15–16: “perhaps he was separated … that you would have him back forever … a beloved brother” (truth, offered tentatively) [T].
  • 17–20: “Accept him as you would me” (command), with “charge that to my account” and “refresh my heart” as its two companions (commands) [T].
  • 21–22: “I write to you” (truth) and “prepare me a lodging” (command) [T].

The hand-off to point-purpose: the body has one indicative of appeal (vv. 9–10) and one central command (v. 17), and the command does not appear until the appeal is complete [T].

4. Linking Words

  • v. 5 ἀκούων (“because I hear”) — a participle giving the occasion of the thanksgiving [T].
  • v. 6 ὅπως (“that”) — the content and purpose of the prayer: that the κοινωνία (“fellowship”) become ἐνεργής (“effective”) [T]. Everything the letter later asks is the answer to this ὅπως [I].
  • v. 7 γάρ (“for”) — grounds the thanksgiving in Paul’s own joy; ὅτι (“because”) grounds the joy in the refreshed hearts [T].
  • v. 8 Διό (“therefore”) — →therefore→: because of vv. 4–7, the appeal. The only Διό in the letter, and the hinge into the body [T].
  • v. 9 μᾶλλον (“rather”) — the alternative to ordering; μᾶλλον (“rather, more”) returns at v. 16, πόσῳ δὲ μᾶλλον (“but how much more”) [T].
  • v. 9 νυνὶ δέ (“and now”) and v. 11 ποτέ … νυνὶ δέ (“formerly … but now”) — the two “nows” (see Tool 10) [T].
  • v. 13 ἵνα (“so that”) — the purpose for which Paul would have kept Onesimus; v. 14 δέ (“but”) — the contrast that overrules the wish; ἵνα μή … ἀλλά (“so that … not … but”) — the purpose of the restraint [T].
  • v. 15 τάχα γάρ (“for perhaps”) — ←for←: a tentative ground for sending Onesimus back; ἵνα (“that”) — the providential purpose Paul offers for the separation [T]/[I].
  • v. 16 οὐκέτι … ἀλλά (“no longer … but”) — the overturning of the old category [T].
  • v. 17 Εἰ οὖν (“if then”) — →therefore→ and a first-class condition, assumed true: Paul does not doubt that Philemon counts him a partner [T] (grammar), [I] (force).
  • v. 18 εἰ δέ (“but if”) — the real condition: the wrong is possible, not asserted [T].
  • v. 19 ἵνα μὴ λέγω (“not to mention”) — an idiom of mentioning by not mentioning (paralipsis) [T]/[S].
  • v. 20 ναί (“yes”) — affirmation; it closes the request [T].
  • v. 21 ὅτι … ὑπὲρ ἃ λέγω (“that … more than what I say”) — the open-ended “more” [T].
  • v. 22 ἅμα δέ (“at the same time”); γάρ (“for”) — grounds the lodging request in the hope of release [T].

Translation check on connectives. The NASB95 keeps Διό (“Therefore”) at v. 8 and “If then” at v. 17; the ESV renders Διό “Accordingly” and Εἰ οὖν “So if”. Both are audible; nothing is lost.

5. Parallels

N/A for Hebrew-style poetic parallelism — Philemon is letter prose. Two prose patterns matter:

  • v. 5, a crossed order: ἀγάπην … πίστιν … πρὸς τὸν κύριον Ἰησοῦν … εἰς πάντας τοὺς ἁγίους (“love … faith … toward the Lord Jesus … toward all the saints”) [T]. Read as a chiasm (love → saints; faith → Lord), it makes v. 5 a statement that Philemon’s love runs to “all the saints” — the very love now asked for one more saint [S]/[I]. The variant that reverses “love and faith” (Textual Variants) shows scribes felt the order’s oddness [I]. Moderate confidence.
  • v. 16, an ascending series: “no longer as a slave → more than a slave → a beloved brother → especially to me → how much more to you → both in the flesh and in the Lord” [T]. Each step goes further than the last; the climax is the double sphere “in the flesh and in the Lord” [I].

6. Narrator’s Comment

N/A — an epistle, not narrative. The nearest equivalent is Paul’s autograph statement, “I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand” (v. 19), which steps outside the dictated letter to sign a pledge [T]/[I].

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun inventory. Paul (vv. 1, 9, 19), Christ Jesus / the Lord Jesus Christ, Timothy, Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, Onesimus, Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke [T]. Routine, except for three:

  • Ὀνήσιμος (“Onesimus”) — the name means “useful, beneficial” [S], and the letter plays on it twice: ἄχρηστον … εὔχρηστον (“useless … useful”, v. 11) and ὀναίμην (“let me benefit”, v. 20) [T]/[I]. Ὀνήσιμος occurs only at Col 4:9 and Phlm 10 in the NT [T]. Name-wordplay: moderate–high (the sound is the point: Onēsimos / onaimēn; achrēston / euchrēston). A further pun on χρηστός / Χριστός (“useful” / “Christ”), which were pronounced alike, is proposed [S] — uncertain. v1.2: v. 11’s play is on what the name means, through the stock antithesis ἄχρηστος / εὔχρηστος (from the χρη- root, not the name’s ὀνη- root) — high; v. 20’s ὀναίμην shares the name’s own root but was a stock wish-formula of letters — moderate–high; the Christ-pun is unsignalled (v. 11 does not name Christ) — low–moderate [S: audit 2].
  • Μᾶρκος (“Mark”) — a character dossier: his earlier departure (Acts 13:13) divided Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39) [T]; he is “the cousin of Barnabas”, about whom the Colossians had “instructions” (Col 4:10) [T]; he is a “fellow worker” here (v. 24) [T]; and he is εὔχρηστος (“useful”) to Paul at 2 Tim 4:11 [T]. A formerly unprofitable worker made useful stands among those who greet Philemon [I]. Weight: uncertain — Philemon itself does not invoke Mark’s history.
  • Δημᾶς (“Demas”) — a “fellow worker” here and in Col 4:14; at 2 Tim 4:10 he has deserted Paul, “having loved this present world” [T]. Not live for Philemon; noted for the preacher who reads the names aloud.

Key words (all checked by lemma in the SBLGNT):

  • σπλάγχνα (“heart”, literally “inward parts”), vv. 7, 12, 20. The seat of affection — “the whole inner person” in the emotional register [S]. Three of Paul’s eight uses stand in this letter (2 Cor 6:12; 7:15; Phil 1:8; 2:1; Col 3:12; Phlm 7, 12, 20) [T]. At v. 12 it is an identification: Onesimus is Paul’s σπλάγχνα (“heart”) [T]. High confidence.
  • κοινωνία (“fellowship”, v. 6) / κοινωνός (“partner”, v. 17). The fellowship that belongs to shared faith, and the partnership it creates [T]/[S]. The meaning of ἡ κοινωνία τῆς πίστεώς σου (“the fellowship of your faith”) is the letter’s chief crux: Philemon’s participation in the faith, his generosity that springs from faith, or the partnership with others that his faith creates [S]. The ESV’s “sharing of your faith” can be heard as evangelism, which narrows the range [I]. Moderate confidence on any single reading; v. 17 is the best clue to v. 6 — the κοινωνία becomes effective when Philemon treats Paul as κοινωνός (“partner”) by receiving Onesimus [I]. v1.1: the commentators read the phrase three ways — “the mutual participation which is proper to your faith”, with v. 6 related to the appeal “as premise to conclusion” and the participation enacted in vv. 17–19 (Wright); “the subjective experience of a faith shared in common”, with an active sense possible and “see also v. 17” (Dunn); “generosity” made effective “in the case of Onesimus” (O’Brien) [S: Logos 1]. Brown reports Moo’s four options — participation with Paul, evangelistic sharing, fellowship with believers, generosity [S: Logos 1, second-hand]. The dig’s v. 6 → v. 17 reading, reached from the text, is the one Wright argues and Dunn points to; Hainz calls the letter “a concrete demonstration of what Paul understands by κοινωνία” [S: Logos 1, second-hand]. Dunn’s counts check exactly: κοινωνία (“fellowship”) 19 times in the NT, 13 in the undisputed Paulines, 4 in 1 John 1 (SBLGNT); 3 verses in Swete’s LXX — Lev 6:2 [MT 5:21], Wis 8:18, 3 Macc 4:6. Moderate, now with history; still contested.
  • ἐπιτάσσω (“to order”, v. 8) / ἀνάγκη (“compulsion”, v. 14) / ἑκούσιος (“free will”, v. 14). The vocabulary of how the request is made. ἐπιτάσσω is its only use in Paul (the other nine NT uses are in the Gospels and Acts) [T]; ἑκούσιος occurs only here in the NT [T]. Paul’s other refusal to command is 2 Cor 8:8, Οὐ κατʼ ἐπιταγὴν λέγω (“I am not speaking this as a command”), in the collection appeal, where he is “testing the sincerity of your love” — and 2 Cor 9:7 has ἐξ ἀνάγκης (“under compulsion”) of giving [T]. Both concern generosity: Paul’s habit is to ask for love’s gifts without commanding them [I]. High confidence.
  • ἐλλογέω (“to charge to one’s account”, v. 18). A commercial word [S]; in the NT only here and Rom 5:13 (“sin is not imputed when there is no law”) [T]. High confidence on the sense; uncertain on any theological weight.
  • ἀποτίνω (“to repay”, v. 19). Its only NT occurrence [T]; the legal restitution verb of the LXX (Intertextual, Tool 11) and the verb of the papyrus promissory note [S]. High confidence on the sense.
  • ἀπέχω (“to have back in full”, v. 15). Paul’s other active use is Phil 4:18, ἀπέχω δὲ πάντα (“I have received everything in full”) — the language of a receipt [T] (usage), [S] (the commercial sense). Philemon is to “have Onesimus back in full” — not as a slave, but as a brother [I]. Moderate–high confidence.
  • δοῦλος (“slave”, v. 16, twice). The ordinary Greek word for a slave [T]; see Tool 8 on “bondservant”.

8. Translations

NASB95 against the ESV (pulpit) and the KJV (the KJV quoted from recall [unverified — check wording]; no KJV export is in the corpus):

  • v. 1 “brother”. NASB95 “To Philemon our beloved brother and fellow worker”; ESV “To Philemon our beloved fellow worker”; KJV “unto Philemon our dearly beloved, and fellowlabourer”. The Greek has τῷ ἀγαπητῷ καὶ συνεργῷ (“the beloved and fellow worker”); only D* adds ἀδελφῷ (“brother”) [T: as NA28 prints it]. Here the ESV and KJV are closer to the Greek than the study text. The first “brother” given to Philemon is at v. 7.
  • v. 6. NASB95 “the fellowship of your faith may become effective through the knowledge of every good thing which is in you for Christ’s sake”; ESV “the sharing of your faith may become effective for the full knowledge of every good thing that is in us for the sake of Christ”; KJV “the communication of thy faith may become effectual by the acknowledging of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus”. Three decisions: κοινωνία (“fellowship” / “sharing” / “communication”); ἐν ἐπιγνώσει (“through” / “for” the knowledge); and the variant “in you” / “in us” (Textual Variants). The ESV’s “sharing of your faith” is the most interpretive of the three, and is likely to be heard as personal evangelism [I]. Wright says the same of the NIV’s identical phrase — “a quite extraneous idea” [S: Logos 1]. v1.1 — a fourth decision, εἰς Χριστόν: both the NASB95 (“for Christ’s sake”) and the ESV (“for the sake of Christ”) take it as purpose. Most of the commentators reported do not: Dunn reads it as a variant of ἐν Χριστῷ (“in Christ”), or as “bringing us into (closer) relation to Christ”; O’Brien as union, “in Christ”; Wright as the goal of the body’s growth, “unto Christ” (Eph 4:12–13); only Lohse’s “for the glory of Christ”, which Dunn rejects, comes near the two translations [S: Logos 1]. In Paul the noun πίστις (“faith”) is joined to εἰς Χριστόν only at Col 2:5, as Dunn says (SBLGNT; ten verses in Paul have εἰς Χριστόν at all) [T]. The congregation will hear a purpose (“for Christ”) that most of these commentators do not find in the Greek — a note for the preacher, not a translation verdict.
  • v. 10 ἐγέννησα (“I begot”). NASB95 “whom I have begotten”; ESV “whose father I became”; KJV “whom I have begotten”. The ESV keeps the sense and loses the verb of 1 Cor 4:15.
  • v. 12 ἀνέπεμψα (“I sent back”). NASB95 “I have sent him back”; ESV “I am sending him back”; KJV “Whom I have sent again”. The ESV reads an epistolary aorist (the past from the reader’s point of view, when Onesimus arrives with the letter) [S]/[I].
  • v. 16 δοῦλος. NASB95 “slave”; ESV “bondservant”; KJV “servant”. What the congregation will hear: “bondservant”, a word that softens, and in some ears dignifies, what the Greek states plainly [I]. It is the same word as in Col 3:22–4:1.
  • v. 17 προσλαβοῦ. NASB95 “accept”; ESV “receive”; KJV “receive”. No divergence bearing on a finding.
  • v. 20 ὀναίμην. NASB95 “let me benefit from you”; ESV “I want some benefit from you”; KJV “let me have joy of thee”. The pun on the name is lost in all three.
  • v. 22 χαρισθήσομαι. NASB95 “I will be given to you”; ESV “I will be graciously given to you”; KJV “I shall be given unto you”. The ESV keeps the grace-root audible (Tool 10).

Ancient versions check. The moderns agree on one weighty rendering: v. 16’s “both in the flesh and in the Lord” (καὶ ἐν σαρκὶ καὶ ἐν κυρίῳ). The Vulgate reads et in carne et in Domino [S] (recalled — uncertain, consider verifying), which agrees. No translation-tradition split. The other weighty point, v. 6, is a variant, not a translation split (below). (No Old Testament text is quoted, so the Hebrew / Greek triage does not arise.)

Pulpit divergence note. ESV (Anglicised) declared. Four divergences bear on findings: (1) v. 1, where the ESV is closer to the Greek; (2) v. 6, where the ESV’s “sharing” and “in us” have decided two questions for the congregation; (3) v. 16, where “bondservant” softens δοῦλος (“slave”); (4) v. 22, where “graciously given” serves the grace frame better than the study text. The hearts frame (vv. 7, 12, 20) is fully audible in both.

9. Tone and Feel

Warm, courteous, and pressing. The register is affectionate — “beloved”, “brother”, “my child”, “my very heart”, “refresh my heart” [T] — and it is also argued hard: Paul names what he could do (v. 8), names what Philemon owes him (v. 19), declares his confidence (v. 21) and announces a visit (v. 22) [T].

Devices. Direct address in the singular (Headline 5); the vocative ἀδελφέ (“brother”) twice; self-description that is half humble, half weighty (πρεσβύτης … δέσμιος, “the aged … a prisoner”, v. 9) [T]; wordplay on the name (vv. 11, 20) — wit in a serious matter [I]; and paralipsis, “not to mention” (v. 19) — the saying of a thing by saying one will not say it [T]/[S].

Tonal shift at v. 17. From the gentle accumulations of the appeal to short, direct imperatives (vv. 17, 18, 20). And a lightening at v. 22 — “prepare me a lodging” — almost a smile [I].

Soundtrack. A string quartet: intimate, conversational, with one firm cadence at vv. 17–20. Preach it warmly but not softly — the request is meant to be granted.

Addressee differentiation. The letter opens by naming four parties — Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, the church (vv. 1–2). Only Philemon is addressed in the body; the church returns in the plurals of vv. 22 and 25; Apphia and Archippus are never resolved within Philemon [T]. Archippus’s charge comes in Col 4:17, “Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it” [T]. That the two are connected — that Archippus’s “ministry” bears on Onesimus — is a reconstruction [S], uncertain.

Revisit after Tool 11 (Phase 0.6). The Pauline companions sharpen the positional answer: Paul’s practice elsewhere (2 Cor 8:8; 9:7) is to ask for love’s gifts without command; Philemon is the one letter wholly built on that practice [I].

12. Genre

NT epistle — a personal letter of intercession, with the standard Pauline frame (prescript, thanksgiving, body, greetings, grace) [T]. Reading rules: read for the single request and its flow; hear the letter as read aloud in a house church (v. 2); do not reconstruct a narrative behind it and preach the reconstruction. Sub-genre: the letter of recommendation or intercession for a third party, with Hellenistic parallels (Pliny, Ep. 9.21) [S]. One embedded form: a promissory note, vv. 18–19 — “I, Paul, … with my own hand, I will repay” [S] for the form, [T] for the words.

13. Copycat

The letter is non-narrative, but it is wholly about three people’s actions, so the tool applies in a limited way:

  • Paul’s refusal to command (vv. 8–9, 14) — normative pattern: the apostle models how love asks; it coheres with 2 Cor 8:8 [I]. It is not a rule that church leaders may never command (Paul commands elsewhere, and says he could here, v. 8) [T].
  • Paul’s taking on the debt (vv. 18–19) — pattern, not command: an example of costly intercession [I].
  • Philemon’s reception of Onesimus — direct request, made to one man; the principle (receive the believing brother, whatever his status, as you would receive the apostle) is normative for the church [I].
  • Sending Onesimus back (v. 12) — descriptive, a unique decision in a particular case; not a template for returning fugitives (Difficult Verses) [I].

14. Bible Timeline

This side of the cross and resurrection; the church age; a house church within a few decades of Pentecost [T]/[S]. Slavery is an institution of the world the letter addresses, not one the gospel creates [I]. Where am I? In the same age — awaiting Christ, in a church where social distinctions are still real “in the flesh” but no longer define a believer “in the Lord” (v. 16) [I]. What has happened between? Chiefly, the abolition of legal slavery in much of the world, which changes the application and not the principle: the claims of the new family on every relationship of power, rank or debt [I].

15. Who Am I?

  • Philemon — the hearer most directly addressed: the believer with power, standing and a grievance, asked to receive [I]. The congregation is most often in Philemon’s place.
  • Onesimus — the one received; a recipient of grace, sent back to face the one he wronged (if he did, v. 18) [I].
  • Paul — the intercessor and guarantor. Paul is not the congregation by default, and he is not Christ; he acts in a Christ-shaped way (Christological Reading) [I].
  • The church in the house — the witnesses, and the family into which Onesimus is received [I].

The “Paul-is-me” mistake is to preach the sermon as “be an intercessor like Paul” before it has said “be a Philemon who receives”.

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the author’s intended response. That Philemon freely receive Onesimus as a beloved brother, as he would Paul, forgive or forgo any debt (since Paul has taken it), and do “even more” than is asked [T]/[I].

Stage 2.

  • Worldview. In Christ, the categories that order the world — master and slave, creditor and debtor, wronged and wrongdoer — are not abolished overnight, but they no longer have the last word: “no longer as a slave, but … a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord” (v. 16) [T]/[I].
  • Stop: treating a fellow believer by his category; holding over him a debt that has been paid; demanding what one should be free to give.
  • Start: receiving the brother or sister one has reason to hold at arm’s length; letting one’s reputation for love (vv. 5–7) become an act of love that costs something.
  • Motive. Not command but love (v. 9); the fellowship of faith (v. 6); what one owes for the gospel (v. 19) [T].

Four audiences.

  • For me: whom am I asked to receive “as” someone I honour? What debt am I holding that another has offered to pay?
  • For a Christian friend: the believer estranged from a fellow member over a wrong — the letter shows a way back that neither pretends the wrong away (v. 18) nor lets it rule (v. 17).
  • For the church community: the church in the house is a place where social hierarchy is relativised by family (v. 2, vv. 16–17); a church that sings this and seats people by rank has not heard Philemon.
  • For an unbeliever: a picture, in an ordinary household dispute, of what the gospel does — one who owed nothing standing in for one who owed (vv. 18–19) — offered as an analogy, honestly named.

Prayer. Father, you have received us in your Son as you receive him: give us hearts that refresh your saints, receive one another freely, and do more than we are asked. Through Jesus Christ our Lord.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

  • Time period: the 50s or early 60s AD, during an imprisonment of Paul [S].
  • Location: a house church, very probably at Colossae in the Lycus valley (Col 4:9, 17) [I]; Paul in prison — Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea [S].
  • Political context: Roman provincial Asia; Roman law of slavery in force [S].

What the original audience knew

  1. Slavery in the Roman household was pervasive, varied and largely domestic in a house like Philemon’s: slaves were legally property, and their masters had wide powers over them [S]. Manumission was common, especially for urban domestic slaves, and freedmen often remained bound to their former masters [S]. The letter’s “useful / useless” language is the language of a slave’s value [S]/[I].
  2. A slave’s absence was an injury to the master’s property; the law treated a runaway (fugitivus) severely, and harbouring one was an offence [S]. Roman jurists also discussed a slave who went to a friend of his master (amicus domini) to seek intercession after a quarrel — which on some readings was not flight [S]. Onesimus’s case cannot be settled from the letter (Pitfall 1). v1.1: the runaway reading is ancient — Witherington lists Athanasius, Basil, the Marcionite prologues, Ambrosiaster and the Apostolic Constitutions before Chrysostom, and Theodore of Mopsuestia, Gregory of Nyssa and Jerome beside him, against Callahan’s claim that it begins with Chrysostom; Calvin and Rupprecht hold it, and most interpreters still do [S: Logos 1]. The amicus domini view is held by Lampe, Bartchy, Fitzmyer and Dunn [S: Logos 1, second-hand]; Beavis argues that a runaway would hardly seek help from a man himself in prison [S: Logos 1]; a sending by the church has been argued by Winter, whom Dunn cites [S: Logos 1, second-hand].
  3. The house church met in the home of a householder, whose household — family, slaves, dependants — was present [S]; Rom 16:5; 1 Cor 16:19; Col 4:15; Phlm 2 [T]. The letter would be read aloud among people who knew Philemon, Onesimus and each other [I].
  4. Letters of intercession were a known Hellenistic form; Pliny the Younger’s letter to Sabinianus on behalf of an offending freedman (Ep. 9.21) is the standard comparison [S].
  5. The autograph and the promissory note. A letter dictated to a scribe and signed by the sender was ordinary; a written undertaking in one’s own hand to repay a debt was a legal instrument in the papyri [S]. “I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand, I will repay it” (v. 19) has that form [S]/[T]. v1.1: so O’Brien, Dunn and Barth–Blanke (legal documents as the backdrop) and Robertson (“Paul’s promissory note”) [S: Logos 1]; Dunn adds that Paul had to say what he was doing because the letter was for public reading [S: Logos 1].
  6. Patronage and debt of honour. In an honour culture, a benefactor who has given another his life or standing is owed loyalty [S]; Paul’s “you owe to me even your own self” (v. 19) invokes such a debt — and refuses to collect it in money [I].

What this changes about how we read

  • The request of v. 17 — “accept him as you would me” — asks a householder to receive a slave as he would receive an honoured guest, the apostle himself. In an honour culture that is not sentiment; it is a public inversion of rank [I].
  • The pledge of vv. 18–19 is not a figure of speech: it is a legal undertaking, signed [S]/[I].

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

  • Canonical section: the Pauline corpus, last of the letters to individuals [T].
  • What the canonical reader knows: the Gospels, Acts (including Mark’s departure, Acts 15:37–39), Romans–Titus, and above all Colossians, with its household code and its envoys [T].
  • Canonical register: a canonical reader arrives from Titus 2:9–10 and Col 3:22–4:1; Philemon shows one master being asked to do more than any code requires [I].

The first hearers

  • Who they were: Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and a house church, in which Onesimus was probably now standing, letter in hand [I].
  • Their situation: a household with a broken relationship and a returned slave; a church whose patron had been wronged [I].
  • What they brought: the assumptions of a slave society — that a master’s honour required satisfaction; that a slave’s worth was his use [S]/[I].

Where the passage fits their world

The hearers would have expected an intercessor to plead for leniency for an inferior. They heard instead a plea to receive the slave as an equal in the family and as the apostle’s own self (vv. 16–17) [I].

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: a slave called “my very heart” (v. 12) and “a beloved brother … in the flesh” (v. 16) in the master’s hearing [I].
  • Surprising: an apostle who refuses to command (vv. 8–9), and who offers to pay (vv. 18–19) [T]/[I].
  • Comforting: for Onesimus, a guarantor; for Philemon, an honourable way to receive him [I].
  • Disturbing: for Philemon, the public weight of the request (vv. 2, 21–22); for any slave-owning member, its implications [I].

What we bring that they didn’t

A post-abolition horror at slavery, which leads us either to demand that Paul condemn it outright or to excuse him; an individualism that hears a private note; and a therapeutic instinct that reduces the letter to “forgiveness” in the abstract [I].

Biblical-Theological Themes

1. The family of God (adoption and brotherhood). - Where this passage sits: the church as household, in which believers are brothers and sisters, and apostles beget children through the gospel (1 Cor 4:15; Gal 4:19; Phlm 10) [T]. - Before: Israel as God’s son (Exod 4:22); the brother not to be made a slave (Lev 25:39–43) [T]. - After: the household of God (1 Tim 3:15); the new creation, where the family is complete [I]. - Contribution: it makes the request intelligible — Onesimus is not being “forgiven” by a superior; he is being received by a brother [I]. - Confidence: high.

2. Reconciliation and substitution. - Where: an instance of the gospel’s pattern in human relations — one who stands between offended and offender and takes the debt [I]. - Before: Judah offering himself as surety for Benjamin (Gen 43:9; 44:32–33) is the OT’s nearest narrative shape [I] — uncertain, no shared wording. - After: Christ as the one through whom God reconciles and does not count trespasses (2 Cor 5:19–21); “accept one another, just as Christ also accepted us” (Rom 15:7) [T]. - Contribution: a canonical reader hears the gospel in vv. 17–19; the letter does not state it (Christological Reading) [I]. - Confidence: moderate (as canonical reflection, not textual claim).

Confluence. Mutual illumination: because the family is real (theme 1), the substitution (theme 2) takes the form of a brother standing in for a brother; because Paul stands in Onesimus’s place, the family becomes visible [I].

Christological Reading

Type of Christological connection

NT epistle: Christ is named as ground and sphere. One analogy (not typology — Philemon does not present Paul as a type) is available in vv. 17–19.

How the passage points to Christ

  • Ground: grace and peace from God and the Lord Jesus Christ (v. 3); the closing grace (v. 25); boldness “in Christ” (v. 8); the gospel for which Paul is imprisoned (v. 13) [T].
  • Sphere: Onesimus is a brother “in the Lord” (v. 16); requests are made “in the Lord” and “in Christ” (v. 20); the fellowship of faith is “for Christ” (v. 6) [T].
  • Analogy: Paul identifies with the slave and takes his debt (vv. 17–19) [T]. The NT makes the kind of connection elsewhere — Christ’s accepting (Rom 15:7), God’s not counting trespasses (2 Cor 5:19) [T] — but not of this text [I].

Trajectory

  • What the passage anticipates: a family in which the categories of the world are subordinated to the relation “in the Lord” [I].
  • What Christ fulfils: he is the Lord in whom the new relation exists, and by canonical analogy the one who paid the debt [I].
  • What still remains: “in the flesh” (v. 16) — the brotherhood has to be lived in the old world’s structures until they pass [I].

Moralism check

  • The “be like X” temptation: “Be a Paul — intercede; be a Philemon — forgive” [I].
  • The gospel grounding: the fellowship of faith “toward the Lord Jesus” (vv. 5–6); the brotherhood “in the Lord” (v. 16); the debt each believer owes for the gospel (v. 19) [T].
  • Christ as hero: Christ is the Lord in whose household master and slave are brothers; the sermon that offers only good relationships without that Lord has preached Pliny, not Paul [I].

Confidence

High on ground and sphere; moderate on the analogy, which must be named as such.

Difficult / Contested Verses

v. 12 with v. 16 — Paul sends a slave back to his master

  • Category: ethical; apologetic; pastoral.
  • The difficulty: Paul returns Onesimus to Philemon and does not, in so many words, condemn slavery or demand manumission [T].
  • Common mishandlings: “Paul endorsed slavery”; or “Paul secretly abolished it” [I].
  • Rhetorical function in context: v. 12 is part of the appeal — sending Onesimus is sending Paul’s own heart, and it is done so that Philemon’s response be free (v. 14) [T].
  • The main interpretive options: (a) Paul seeks reconciliation within the existing relationship, with the brotherhood transforming it from inside; (b) Paul asks, by implication, for manumission (vv. 16, 21, “even more”); (c) Paul asks for Onesimus to be released back to Paul’s service (v. 13) [S].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the text asks for reception as a brother “in the flesh and in the Lord” and hopes for “more” than it says; it does not specify the more [T]. Option (b) and (c) are possible readings of v. 21, neither stated [I]. Moderate.

v. 19 — pressure or love?

  • Category: ethical (rhetoric); pastoral.
  • The difficulty: “not to mention … you owe me even your own self” can sound manipulative [I].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the letter is open about its means; the pressure is applied in the light, before the church, and the decision is left free (v. 14) [T]/[I]. Moderate.
  • v1.1 — the readings, named. Seesengood: “too much protest”, the reminder of authority an “inherent … threat”, aiming at affective as well as actual compliance; Pao: Paul “expects nothing short of the absolute ‘obedience’ of Philemon”, on apostolic authority; Copenhaver–Arthurs: ὑπακοή (“obedience”) as the “obedience of faith”, a life shaped by the gospel rather than compliance with a command; Nordling: authority wielded “with extreme delicacy” [S: Logos 1]. Dunn: ὑπακοή was little known outside Christian Greek, and ὑπακούω (“to obey”) renders שָׁמַע (“to hear”) in the LXX, so the word carries “heedful hearing” [S: Logos 1]. The corpus bears Dunn out: ὑπακοή occurs in one verse of Swete (2 Sam 22:36), and ὑπακούω renders שָׁמַע at, for example, Gen 22:18 and Lev 26:14 (WLC; Swete) [T]. It also adds a Pauline datum none of them reports: γράφω (“to write”) with the obedience family in one verse occurs only at 2 Cor 2:9 and Phlm 21 — “for this purpose also I wrote, so that I might put you to the test, whether you are obedient in all things” — and 2 Thess 3:14 speaks of obeying “our instruction in this letter” (SBLGNT) [T]. In Paul “obedience” is often the readers’ response to his own letter [I]. So the tension of v. 8 → v. 21 resolves as obedience to an apostolic word that is itself an appeal (vv. 9–10): Pao is right that it is Paul’s word that is heeded; Dunn and Copenhaver–Arthurs are right about the kind of heeding [I]. Moderate.
  • Suggested handling: acknowledge; preach both the appeal and its weight (Pitfall 3).

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Preaching the runaway story as the text

  • What it looks like: “Onesimus stole from Philemon, fled to Rome, by providence landed in Paul’s cell, and was converted.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the letter says “separated” (v. 15) and “if he has wronged you” (v. 18) [T]; theft, flight, Rome and the cell are reconstruction [S].
  • The corrective: preach the broken relationship, the possible wrong, the conversion and the return; name the details once as the traditional reconstruction. v1.1: the reconstruction is old — fourth-century at least — and still the majority view; it remains a reconstruction (Historical Background, item 2) [S: Logos 1].

Pitfall: Making the sermon about slavery — or avoiding slavery

  • What it looks like: a lecture on Paul and abolition; or a sermon that never says “slave” at all, helped by the ESV’s “bondservant”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the letter’s own subject is the reception of one believing slave as a brother (v. 16) [T]; it neither legislates on the institution nor hides it [I].
  • The corrective: say “slave” once, plainly (it is δοῦλος, “slave”, in v. 16); say what the letter asks and what it leaves open (v. 21); then preach the request.

Pitfall: “Sharing your faith” (v. 6, ESV) as a text on evangelism

  • What it looks like: “Paul prays that Philemon’s personal witness will become effective.”
  • Why it’s wrong: κοινωνία here is the fellowship that faith creates, cashed out at v. 17 as partnership [T]/[I]; the ESV’s “sharing” is one possible rendering, narrowed by modern idiom [I].
  • The corrective: read v. 6 with v. 17: the fellowship becomes effective when Philemon treats Paul as partner by receiving Onesimus. v1.1: Wright makes the same objection to the NIV’s identical phrase; Dunn allows an active “sharing of faith”, but as testimony within the gathered church, not evangelism [S: Logos 1].

Pitfall: Calling Paul manipulative — or pretending he applies no pressure

  • What it looks like: “Paul guilt-trips Philemon”; or “Paul just gently suggests”.
  • Why it’s wrong: the letter names its weight openly (vv. 8, 19, 21–22) and keeps the decision free (v. 14) [T].
  • The corrective: love that asks hard, in the light, and leaves the other free.

Pitfall: “God planned the wrong” (v. 15)

  • What it looks like: “Onesimus’s running away was all part of God’s plan.”
  • Why it’s wrong: Paul says “perhaps” (τάχα) [T]; the line consoles the wronged, it does not justify the wrongdoer [I].
  • The corrective: keep the “perhaps”; hope in providence without explaining evil away.
