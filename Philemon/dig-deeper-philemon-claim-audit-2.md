## Claims Audited

### Claim P3: the name-play on Onesimus

**Source:** overview, Echo Table 18 (moderate); dig v1.1, Tool 7 and Original Language Observations (raised to high after Logos round 1; the χρηστός / Χριστός pun moderate–uncertain).
**Claim:** (a) ἄχρηστον … εὔχρηστον ("useless … useful", v. 11) plays on Ὀνήσιμος ("Onesimus", "useful, profitable"); (b) ὀναίμην ("let me benefit", v. 20), from ὀνίνημι, its only NT occurrence, plays on the name again; (c) v. 11 also puns on χρηστός ("useful") / Χριστός ("Christ").

**Checks.** Ὀνήσιμος occurs only at Col 4:9 and Phlm 10; ἄχρηστος only at Phlm 11; εὔχρηστος at 2 Tim 2:21; 4:11; Phlm 11; ὀνίνημι only at Phlm 20 (SBLGNT) `[T]`. Swete has ἀχρηστ- in ten verses (Hos 8:8, σκεῦος ἄχρηστον, "a useless vessel"; Wisdom, Sirach, Maccabees) and εὐχρηστ- in four; Ὀνήσιμος none; the ὀνη- family at Sir 30:2, Tob 3:8, Zech 8:10 and 1 En 14:6 `[T]`. No verse of the NT has a χρηστ- word in a recognisable pun with Χριστός, and **v. 11 has no Χριστός in it** — the nearest stand at vv. 8–9 `[T]`.

**Findings.**
- **(a)** The play is real but **semantic**: ἄχρηστος / εὔχρηστος come from the χρη- root, not the name's ὀνη- root, so v. 11 plays on what the name *means*, through a stock antithesis of ordinary Greek (with the "useless vessel" image at Hos 8:8 and the "useful vessel" at 2 Tim 2:21) `[T]`/`[I]`.
- **(b)** This is the **stronger** play, because ὀναίμην shares the name's own root `[T]`. But ὀναίμην is a stock wish-formula of letters — Ignatius uses it repeatedly, once beside the bishop Onesimus (*Eph.* 1:3; 2:2) *(recalled)* — so the formula alone does not prove a pun. Its place inside the plea for Onesimus, and its wording ἐγώ σου ("I … of you"), make the play likely `[I]`. (A noted that v. 20 is one verse after ἀποτίσω, "I will repay"; B replied that Paul's offer begins at v. 18. Both are right; the dig's "two verses after the offer to pay" stands.)
- **(c)** The sound-merger that would make χρηστός and Χριστός sound alike was under way in Koine, and later Christians made the pun explicitly (Justin, *1 Apol.* 4; Tertullian, *Apol.* 3; the *Chrestus* of Suetonius) *(recalled)*. But nothing in v. 11 signals it, and Christ is not named there `[T]`. The commentators who allow it (Logos round 1: Wright, Dunn, Davids) give no textual signal either `[S: Logos 1]`.

**Verdict:** (a) **Confirmed with nuance** — *high*; (b) **Confirmed with nuance** — *moderate–high* (lowered from high: the formula is idiomatic); (c) **Uncertain** — *low–moderate* (lowered from moderate–uncertain: possible, with history of interpretation, but without signal in the text).

**Restated:** *Onesimus ("Beneficial") was "useless" and is now "useful" — a play on the name's meaning through a common antithesis (v. 11). At v. 20 Paul plays on the name's own root with ὀναίμην ("let me benefit"), the only NT use of the verb, though it was a stock wish-formula in letters. A further play on Χριστός ("Christ") is possible but unsignalled.*

**Book-overview action required:** Echo Table 18 — restate as above; the Christ-pun stays "proposed", low–moderate.

### Claim P4: the Torah's restitution law behind vv. 18–19

**Source:** overview, Intertextual Map row 1 (uncertain–moderate); dig v1.1, Tool 11.
**Claim:** ἐγὼ ἀποτίσω ("I will repay") after "if he has wronged you … or owes you anything" echoes the Covenant Code's restitution law (Exod 21:19–22:17, Swete), where ἀποτίνω is the key verb — claimed 30 verses in Swete, 15 in Exod 21:19–22:17; with a wrongdoing word in the same verse, three.

**Corrections to the counts — both errors are the proposing run's.**
- ἀποτίνω stands in **33 verses of Swete, not 30**. The first search missed the present-stem forms ἀπετίννυον ("I was paying back", Gen 31:39; Ps 68:5) and ἀποτιννύων (Sir 20:12) `[T]`. Fifteen are in Exod 21:19–22:17, as claimed `[T]`.
- With an ἀδικ- word ("wrong") in the same verse: **four verses, not three** — Exod 22:9; Lev 6:5; Ezek 33:15; and Ps 68:5, ἀδίκως … ἀπετίννυον ("wrongfully … I was paying back") `[T]`.
- In Exodus ἀποτίσει renders the piel of שׁלם ("make good") in thirteen of the fifteen verses, נתן ("give") at 21:19 and שׁקל ("weigh out") at Swete 22:17 (Hebrew 22:16) `[T]`.
- The slave law that opens the chapter (Exod 21:2–6) shares no wording with Phlm 18–19: the link proposed was structural, not verbal `[T]`.

**Rivals — closer than the claimed source.**
1. **Lev 6:1–7 (Swete; Hebrew 5:20–26).** 6:2 ἢ ἠδίκησέν τι τὸν πλησίον ("or has wronged his neighbour in anything") is **the only verse in Swete with ἠδίκησεν + τι** ("has wronged … anything") — Phlm 18's words, in reversed order (τι ἠδίκησεν) — and the same verse has κοινωνία ("partnership"; Phlm 6, with κοινωνόν, "partner", at v. 17); 6:5 has ἀποτίσει ("he shall repay") `[T]`. One paragraph holds "wrong" + "anything" + "partnership" + "repay", as Phlm 17–19 holds κοινωνόν, τι ἠδίκησεν and ἀποτίσω `[T]`. Auditor B reproduced it and called it A's best point.
2. **Gen 31:36–39 (Jacob to Laban).** 31:39 ἐγὼ ἀπετίννυον ("I myself made it good") is the only verse in Swete with ἐγώ ("I") as the subject of the verb — a servant making good his master's losses from his own pocket `[T]`.
3. **The papyrus promissory note** *(recalled; Deissmann)*: ἀποτίνω is the standard contract verb for damages, and "I … have written with my own hand" is the formula of a handwritten IOU — the reading the commentators in Logos round 1 take `[S: Logos 1]`.

**Weight.** A verb in 33 verses is idiom and cannot carry an allusion by itself `[I]`.

**Verdict:** **Needs reframing** — *uncertain–moderate*. The commercial IOU stays the leading explanation; if any Torah text stands behind vv. 17–19, it is Lev 6:2–5, not the Covenant Code.

**Restated:** *ἐγὼ ἀποτίσω uses the ordinary legal and commercial verb of repayment (33 verses in Swete). If a Torah text is heard behind vv. 17–19, Lev 6:2–5 (Swete) is the closest — it alone has ἠδίκησέν τι ("has wronged … anything"), beside κοινωνία ("partnership") and ἀποτίσει ("he shall repay") — and Gen 31:39 is the nearest picture of a servant saying "I will make it good myself".*

**Book-overview action required:** Intertextual Map row 1 — replace the Covenant Code with Lev 6:1–7 as the best Torah candidate; correct the counts (33; four); keep the IOU as the leading reading.

### Claim P5: the "forever slave" contrast behind vv. 15–16

**Source:** overview, Intertextual Map row 2 (uncertain); dig v1.1, Tool 11.
**Claim:** ἵνα αἰώνιον αὐτὸν ἀπέχῃς, οὐκέτι ὡς δοῦλον ("that you would have him back forever, no longer as a slave") reverses the Torah's permanent slave (Exod 21:6; Deut 15:17) and recalls the brother not to be made a slave (Lev 25:39); a δουλ- word with αἰών in one verse, 18 verses of Swete; ἀδελφός with a δουλ- word in the Torah, only Lev 25:39 and Deut 15:12; rival in wording, Job 40:23.

**Corrections — both the proposing run's.**
- The 18 is right, but **Deut 15:17 is not among them**: its word is οἰκέτης ("household servant"), not a δουλ- word (an οἰκετ- word with αἰών: Exod 32:13; Deut 15:17 only) `[T]`.
- ἀδελφ- with a δουλ- word in the Torah: **five verses, not two** — Gen 27:29; 27:40; 29:15 ("because you are my brother, should you serve me for nothing?"); Lev 25:39; Deut 15:12 `[T]`. The first search was restricted, without saying so, to Exodus–Deuteronomy. The claim holds only for the law codes.

**Findings.**
- **The Hebrew behind the texts** `[T]`: Exod 21:6 וַעֲבָדוֹ לְעֹלָם ("he shall serve him permanently"); Deut 15:17 עֶבֶד עוֹלָם ("a servant forever"); Lev 25:39 לֹא־תַעֲבֹד בּוֹ עֲבֹדַת עָבֶד ("you shall not subject him to a slave's service"); Job 40:28 [Swete 40:23] לְעֶבֶד עוֹלָם, which Swete renders δοῦλον αἰώνιον — **the only pairing in Swete of δοῦλος with the adjective αἰώνιος**, the pairing Phlm 15–16 has, and the Hebrew phrase of Deut 15:17.
- **The fullest Torah form of the contrast is Lev 25:39–46**: brothers are not to be made slaves, while foreign slaves are a possession εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα ("forever", 25:46) `[T]`.
- **1 Sam 27:12** ἔσται μοι δοῦλος εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα ("he shall be my servant forever") is as close in wording as Deut 15:17 `[T]`.
- **The closest NT rival in thought is John 8:35**: "the slave does not remain in the house forever; the son does" — δοῦλος with αἰών in one verse in the NT only at John 8:35 and Rev 15:3 `[T]`.
- The NT has its own vocabulary of "brother" and "slave" across the master–slave line (1 Tim 6:2) and of kinship "according to the flesh" (Rom 9:3) `[T]` (auditor B): a Torah background is not needed to explain v. 16 `[I]`.

**Verdict:** **Confirmed with nuance** — *uncertain*. A reader's contrast, not shown to be intended.

**Restated:** *αἰώνιον … οὐκέτι ὡς δοῦλον ("forever … no longer as a slave") reverses the Torah's permanent servant (Exod 21:6; Deut 15:17, עֶבֶד עוֹלָם). Its fullest Torah form is Lev 25:39–46; its closest Greek wording is Job 40:23, δοῦλον αἰώνιον, which renders the Hebrew of Deut 15:17; its closest NT parallel in thought is John 8:35. It is a reader's contrast.*

**Book-overview action required:** Intertextual Map row 2 — correct the two counts; name Lev 25:39–46 and John 8:35.

### Claim P6: Col 4:9 as the public counterpart of Phlm 16

**Source:** overview, Canonical Position (Handoff) and Pauline companions table (moderate on reading together); dig v1.1, Tool 2 (Positional Necessity Check: "Colossians states in public what Philemon asks in private") and Tool 11.
**Claim:** Col 4:9's τῷ πιστῷ καὶ ἀγαπητῷ ἀδελφῷ ("the faithful and beloved brother") is the status Phlm 16 asks for, ἀδελφὸν ἀγαπητόν ("a beloved brother"); if the letters went together, Colossians states in public what Philemon asks in private.

**Tests and results.**
- **The phrase is a commendation formula.** ἀγαπητός ("beloved") and ἀδελφός ("brother") stand together in 13 NT verses: five are the vocative address "my beloved brothers" to a church; three have the words unjoined (Phlm 1; 1 Tim 6:2; 3 John 5); **five are a title for one named person** — Tychicus (Eph 6:21; Col 4:7), Onesimus (Col 4:9), Paul, so called by Peter (2 Pet 3:15), and Onesimus as the status asked for (Phlm 16) `[T]`. With πιστός ("faithful"), all three are applied to one person only of Tychicus (Eph 6:21; Col 4:7) and Onesimus (Col 4:9) `[T]`; πιστός with ἀδελφός also names Silvanus, the letter-carrier (1 Pet 5:12) `[T]`. **Col 4:9 gives Onesimus, two verses after Col 4:7, the words it gives Tychicus**: it matches Col 4:7 before it answers anything in Philemon `[T]`/`[I]`. And Col 4:7–8 is repeated almost word for word in Eph 6:21–22 `[T]`.
- **One real difference.** In Philemon "beloved brother" is *asked for*, set against "no longer as a slave"; in Colossians it is a *commendation* of a man already counted as a co-worker `[T]`/`[I]`.
- **Joint delivery is a well-grounded inference, stated by neither letter.** Eight of Philemon's names recur in Colossians (Timothy, Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke, Archippus, Onesimus); no other Pauline letter shares more than four (2 Timothy) `[T]`. Col 4:7–9 sends Tychicus "with Onesimus" (ἔπεμψα, "I have sent"); Phlm 12 has ἀνέπεμψα ("I have sent back") of Onesimus `[T]`. Against: Philemon never names Tychicus; Colossians never names Philemon or Apphia; "fellow prisoner" is Aristarchus's title at Col 4:10 and Epaphras's at Phlm 23 `[T]`. *Moderate–high as an inference.*
- **"Public / private" is not supported.** Colossians is public (Col 4:16, read aloud and exchanged with Laodicea) `[T]`; but Philemon is addressed to three people "and the church in your house" (v. 2), with the plural "you" at vv. 3, 22 and 25 `[T]`. The texts support a different contrast: **a letter to a city's church** against **a letter to one household, read before its house-church** `[I]`.

**Verdict:** **Needs reframing.** The words: *high*. A deliberate answer between the letters: *low–moderate* (a formula). Joint delivery: *moderate–high* as inference. "Public / private": **discarded as worded**.

**Restated:** *Col 4:9 gives Onesimus the commendation Paul uses for trusted envoys ("faithful and beloved brother", as of Tychicus at Col 4:7). If the two letters travelled together — as the eight shared names and the carrier notices suggest — the letter to the whole church already speaks of Onesimus in the terms Phlm 16 asks Philemon's household to accept. The link rests on the shared setting more than on the phrase, and Philemon is a household letter read before a house-church, not a private one.*

**Book-overview action required:** Canonical Position (Handoff) and the Pauline companions row for Col 4:7–17 — restate as above; mark the phrase as a commendation formula (Col 4:7; Eph 6:21); remove "public / private" wording.

### Claim P7: the freewill offering behind v. 14

**Source:** dig v1.1, Tool 11 (new; uncertain–moderate); not in the overview.
**Claim:** κατὰ ἑκούσιον ("of your own free will") resonates with τὸ ἑκούσιον, the LXX freewill offering; καθʼ ἑκούσιον once in Swete (Num 15:3); a ἑκουσ- word with an ἀγαθ- word once (Ps 53:8). Rival: ordinary Greek idiom.

**Checks.**
- Both "once" counts are **confirmed** `[T]`. καθʼ ἑκούσιον at Num 15:3 renders בִנְדָבָה ("as a freewill offering", WLC) `[T]`. At Ps 53:8 the word is the adverb ἑκουσίως, and ἀγαθόν ("good") refers to the Name `[T]`. *Positive control for the phrase pattern:* the same search found κατὰ ἀνάγκην ("under compulsion") at 2 Macc 15:2.
- **τὸ ἑκούσιον is one rendering of the freewill offering among several**, not a fixed term: of the thirteen Torah verses with נְדָבָה ("freewill offering", WLC), Swete has ἑκούσιον only at Lev 7:6 (Swete numbering), 23:38, Num 15:3 and 29:39, and elsewhere ἀφαίρεμα ("contribution"), κατὰ αἵρεσιν ("by choice") or ὁμολογία ("vowed offering") `[T]`.
- **The antithesis is idiom.** A compulsion word with a willing word in one verse occurs in the NT only at Phlm 14 and 1 Pet 5:2 (μὴ ἀναγκαστῶς ἀλλὰ ἑκουσίως, "not under compulsion, but voluntarily") `[T]`; Paul's own 2 Cor 9:7 has μὴ … ἐξ ἀνάγκης ("not … under compulsion") of a gift `[T]`; Job 36:19 (Swete) joins ἑκών ("willing") and ἀνάγκη ("compulsion") `[T]`; and the willing / unwilling pair is stock in ordinary Greek *(recalled)*. Nothing else in v. 14 is cultic `[T]`.

**Verdict:** **Needs reframing** — *uncertain (low–moderate)*.

**Restated:** *κατὰ ἀνάγκην … κατὰ ἑκούσιον is the ordinary antithesis of compulsion and willingness (compare 2 Cor 9:7; 1 Pet 5:2). A reader of the LXX may hear the freewill offering — καθʼ ἑκούσιον occurs once in Swete, at Num 15:3 (בִנְדָבָה) — but nothing else in v. 14 is cultic.*

**Book-overview action required:** if the row is added at the Finalise, add it at low–moderate, with the idiom as the leading reading.

### Claim P8: "in the flesh and in the Lord" against "masters according to the flesh"

**Source:** dig v1.1, Tool 11 (new; words moderate–high, contrast moderate); not yet in the overview.
**Claim:** σάρξ ("flesh") with κύριος ("Lord, master") in one verse, in Colossians, Ephesians and Philemon, only at Col 3:22, Eph 6:5 and Phlm 16; the codes' slave has a master "according to the flesh", Philemon's slave is a brother "in the flesh and in the Lord".

**Tests and results.**
- **The "only" holds** within the three letters `[T]`. Across Paul the pair occurs in six verses (adding Rom 7:25; 13:14; 1 Cor 5:5), across the NT in seven (adding Mark 13:20) `[T]`. Only Col 3:22, Eph 6:5 and Phlm 16 share the senses "human sphere" + "Lord or master" `[T]`/`[I]`.
- **Baseline: the bare pairing means nothing.** In Paul's letters σάρξ stands in 77 verses and κύριος in 247, of 2,029; independent placement would give about 9.4 verses with both, and six are observed — **below chance** `[T]`.
- **The sharper datum: δοῦλος ("slave") + σάρξ + κύριος in one verse — exactly Eph 6:5, Col 3:22 and Phlm 16 in the whole NT** `[T]`. This is stronger than the claim proposed.
- **The phrase is not the same.** κατὰ σάρκα ("according to the flesh") stands in 19 verses, all Pauline; nine concern descent, kinship, nation, birth or (Eph 6:5; Col 3:22) masters `[T]`. ἐν σαρκί ("in the flesh") stands 25 times, and **Phlm 16 is its only NT use for a relationship between two people** `[T]`. 2 Cor 10:3 shows Paul setting the two phrases apart — walking *in* the flesh, not warring *according to* the flesh `[T]`. Phlm 16 does not repeat the codes' phrase; it uses a close neighbour `[I]`.
- **The double sphere is already in the codes.** Col 3:22 sets "masters according to the flesh" against "fearing the Lord", and Col 4:1 gives the masters "a Master in heaven" (Eph 6:5–9 likewise) `[T]`. What Philemon changes is the **axis**: in the codes the two spheres run along the master–slave line (obey the flesh-master as serving the Lord); in Phlm 16 both are applied to the slave *as a brother* `[I]`.
- **Rom 9:3** ("my brothers, my kinsmen according to the flesh") is the only other verse that ties ἀδελφός to a "flesh" phrase in a human-relations sense `[T]` — so "brother … in the flesh" at Phlm 16 can naturally mean a real human or social bond, not only "as a man" `[I]`. **1 Tim 6:2** applies brotherhood across the slave–master line in the other direction: slaves are not to despise believing masters "because they are brothers" `[T]`.

**Verdict:** **Confirmed with nuance.** The words: *high*. The contrast as Paul's intention: *moderate*.

**Restated:** *Only Eph 6:5, Col 3:22 and Phlm 16 in the New Testament combine "slave", "flesh" and "Lord/master" in one verse. The codes already set "masters according to the flesh" against "the Lord"; Phlm 16 keeps the two spheres and changes their axis — with ἐν σαρκί, not κατὰ σάρκα, the slave becomes a brother in both the human sphere and the Lord, the only NT use of ἐν σαρκί for a relationship between two people. The bare pairing of "flesh" and "Lord" is not significant (below chance in Paul).*

**Book-overview action required:** add to the Pauline companions (Col 3:22 / Eph 6:5) as restated; *moderate* on the intended contrast.
