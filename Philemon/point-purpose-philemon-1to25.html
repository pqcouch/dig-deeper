Point and Purpose: Philemon 1–25

Passage: Philemon 1–25 (the whole letter)
Exegesis consumed: dig-deeper-philemon-1to25.md v1.2 (with claim audits 1–2 and Logos round 1 applied); book-overview-philemon.md v1.0 (Finalise)
Study text: NASB95
Pulpit text: ESV (Anglicised), declared by Patrick for this book
Date: 22 October 2026

Scope Decision

One sermon on the whole letter. Philemon is 25 verses and a single appeal built in two stages, the ground (vv. 1–16) and the request (vv. 17–25). Its argument depends on holding the thanksgiving against the request: love (vv. 5, 7 → 9), fellowship (v. 6 → v. 17), the good (v. 6 → v. 14) and the refreshed hearts (v. 7 → v. 20). Divide the letter and those links are lost. The overview’s Option A (“Receive him as you would me”) is followed. Its Option B, three sermons split at v. 8 and v. 17, remains available, but this plan does not use it.

Fallen Condition Focus

We keep fellow believers at the distance our grievances, our rank or their debts allow, and our reputation for love outruns our willingness to pay what love costs.

Anchored in the text. Philemon has a name for love “toward all the saints” (v. 5) and has refreshed their hearts (v. 7). He is now asked to receive, as a brother and as Paul himself, a slave who was “useless” to him (v. 11), who was “separated” from him (v. 15) and who may have wronged him or owe him something (v. 18). He is asked this in the hearing of the church in his house (v. 2). The institution of slavery is not ours. The instinct to keep a wrongdoer, or an inferior, in his category is ours (dig, Candidate FCF).

Teaching Point

In the Lord, the gospel has made the slave a beloved brother; so the fellowship of faith receives him freely, as it would receive the apostle himself, with his debt laid on another’s account.

  • Subject: How does fellowship in Christ receive a brother whom grievance, rank or debt would keep at a distance?
  • Complement: As family made by the gospel. It receives him freely, not under command; it receives him “as me”; and his debt is carried by another.

Built from the convergent Headline Findings:

  • Headline 3: the new identity in family words. Onesimus is “my child, whom I have begotten” (v. 10), “my very heart” (v. 12), “a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord” (v. 16).
  • Headline 4: Paul puts himself in the slave’s place and signs for his debt (vv. 17–19).
  • Headline 1: appeal, not command (vv. 8–9, 14).
  • Headline 2: the thanksgiving as the ground (vv. 5–7 → 9, 14, 17, 20).

The subject of the indicative is the gospel and the Lord in whom the new relation exists. Philemon’s act answers to it. It is not the point.

Purpose Statement

To move hearers from holding fellow believers at the distance of category, grievance or debt, to receiving them freely as family in the Lord. The motive is not command but love (v. 9): the gospel has made them our brothers and sisters, and our own debt to that gospel is greater than anything they owe us (v. 19).

  • For the wronged believer (Philemon’s place): to receive the one who wronged you, neither pretending the wrong away (v. 18) nor letting it rule (v. 17). Then let your reputation for love become an act of love that costs you something.
  • For the one who has done wrong or been estranged (Onesimus’s place): the way back runs through another who stands for you and signs for your debt. The letter will not let you be defined by “formerly” (v. 11).
  • For the church: the church in the house is the place where rank is subordinated to family (vv. 2, 16–17). A church that sings this but still keeps people at a distance by their rank has not heard Philemon.
  • For the unbeliever: a picture, in an ordinary household dispute, of the shape of the gospel: one who owed nothing stands in for one who owed (vv. 18–19). Offer it as an analogy, and name it as one.

Candidate Big Question

When a fellow believer has wronged you, or stands beneath you, will you keep him in his place — or receive him as you would receive the one who brought you to Christ?

Shorter alternative, for the pulpit: “Whom are you keeping at arm’s length — and what would it take to receive them as family?”

This is a candidate. The one-sentence-sermon step, or the preacher, settles the final wording.

Proposed Structure

The letter’s own seams are Διό (“therefore”, v. 8) and Εἰ οὖν (“if then”, v. 17). There is no imperative until v. 17. NA28 paragraphs the letter 1–3 | 4–7 | 8–14 | 15–20 | 21–22 | 23–24 | 25. The four movements follow the Greek connectives, so the third begins inside NA28’s 15–20.

  1. vv. 1–7 — A love already known. The prisoner writes, not the apostle (v. 1). The church in the house is named and listening (v. 2). Philemon’s love and faith are known, and his fellowship (κοινωνία, “fellowship, partnership”, v. 6) is prayed into action. “The hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you” (v. 7). Function: surface the FCF. The thanksgiving is not flattery: it sets the terms by which Philemon will keep faith with his own reputation. Close the movement on v. 7’s “hearts … refreshed”, because v. 20 will return to it.

  2. vv. 8–16 — A brother made by the gospel. Paul could command and will not; he appeals “for love’s sake” (vv. 8–9). He names Onesimus only after calling him “my child, whose father I became in my imprisonment” (v. 10). Once “useless”, Onesimus is now “useful” — the name means “Beneficial” (v. 11). Paul calls him “my very heart” (v. 12) and kept him only with Philemon’s consent, so that the good be free (vv. 13–14). “Perhaps” he was parted for a while to be had back forever, “no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord” (vv. 15–16). Function: the subject of the Teaching Point, the indicative. What the gospel has already made of Onesimus comes before anything is asked of Philemon. “Beloved” passes from master (v. 1) to slave (v. 16).

  3. vv. 17–20 — Receive him as me; charge it to me. The first imperative of the letter: “receive him as you would receive me” (v. 17). Then the liability: “charge that to my account … I will repay it”, signed in Paul’s own hand (vv. 18–19). Then the debt is turned round: “your owing me even your own self” (v. 19). Then “refresh my heart in Christ” (v. 20), taking up v. 7. Function: the complement of the Teaching Point. Fellowship is cashed out as partnership (κοινωνία, v. 6 → κοινωνός, “partner”, v. 17). Here, and only here, the preacher names the analogy the letter does not draw: one who owed nothing standing in for one who owed (compare Rom 15:7; 2 Cor 5:19). Say plainly that it is an analogy.

  4. vv. 21–25 — More than I say, in the light of grace. Paul is “confident of your obedience” — obedience to a word that is itself an appeal (v. 21). “You will do even more than I say”: “more than a slave” (v. 16) and “more than I say” (v. 21) are a deliberate pair, and the “more” is left open. “Prepare a guest room for me”: Paul is coming to see the reception for himself (v. 22). The plural returns: “your prayers”, “graciously given to you”, grace “with your spirit” (vv. 22, 25). The fellow workers stand behind the request (vv. 23–24). Function: the Purpose Statement, a free, costly, open-ended response, made before the church and under grace.

Hinge to watch. Movement 2 must be the longest and must be indicative. If it becomes advice, the sermon becomes “be a Philemon”.

Keep Honest

  1. Do not preach the reconstruction as the text, and keep the “perhaps”. “He stole, ran to Rome and met Paul in prison” is the traditional reconstruction. It is ancient and may be right, but the letter says only “parted” (passive, v. 15) and “if he has wronged you” (v. 18). Name it once as tradition and move on. At v. 15 Paul’s τάχα (“perhaps”) consoles the wronged; it does not justify the wrong. Do not turn it into “God planned it all”.

  2. Say “slave” once, plainly, and leave v. 21 open. The congregation hears “bondservant” (ESV). Tell them it is δοῦλος (“slave”), the same word as in Col 3:22. The letter neither legislates on the institution nor hides it. What v. 21’s “more” asks — manumission, release to Paul’s service, or a fuller reception — is genuinely contested, and the text does not decide. “Both in the flesh and in the Lord” (v. 16) means the brotherhood is to be real in the ordinary human relationship, not only spiritual. Do not let the modern slavery question replace the letter’s own request, and do not dodge it.

  3. Preach the request first and name the analogy as an analogy. Paul never says “as Christ did for you”. The reading “we are all his Onesimi” (Luther) is legitimate, but it belongs to the whole canon, not to this letter. If the analogy becomes the point, the sermon loses what the letter asks of a believing householder. If the sermon offers only good relationships, it has preached Pliny, not Paul. Christ is the Lord in whom master and slave are brothers (vv. 16, 20).

  4. Keep the letter’s tone: appeal, not command — and do not pretend there is no weight. Paul argues hard and in the open (vv. 8, 19, 21–22) and still leaves the decision free (v. 14). The sermon’s own call should have the same shape: urgent, honest about cost, and free. That rules out guilt-tripping, and it equally rules out a gentle suggestion.

Christ-Centred Check

  • “Be like X” or “trust the Christ whom X points to”? The payoff is not “be a Paul, be a Philemon”. It is receiving the one whom the Lord has already made your brother. Pass.
  • Is Christ the subject? The indicative is the gospel that begot Onesimus (v. 10), the Lord in whom he is a brother (v. 16), and the grace that frames the letter (vv. 3, 25). Philemon’s act is the response. Pass.
  • Is the good news grounded in Christ’s saving work? It is grounded in the fellowship of faith “toward the Lord Jesus” (vv. 5–6), and in the debt each believer owes for the gospel (v. 19). The substitutionary shape of vv. 18–19 is offered as a named analogy, not as the letter’s claim. Pass, with the analogy flagged.

Pulpit-Text Note (ESV against NASB95)

Where the ESV and NASB95 part company on a point the plan rests on:

  • v. 16 — δοῦλος (“slave”). ESV “bondservant”; NASB95 “slave”. This bears on Movement 2 and Keep Honest 2: say “slave” once, plainly.
  • v. 6 / v. 17 — κοινωνία / κοινωνός (“fellowship” / “partner”). ESV “sharing … partner”; NASB95 “fellowship … partner”. The congregation cannot hear the link that Movement 3 cashes out. The ESV’s “sharing of your faith” also sounds like evangelism. Read v. 6 and v. 17 together aloud, and say that “sharing” here is the fellowship that faith creates.
  • v. 6 — εἰς Χριστόν. ESV “for the sake of Christ”; NASB95 “for Christ’s sake”. Both read purpose. Most commentators reported read it as relation or goal (“into Christ”, “in Christ”). This is a minor point for the sermon; mention it only if v. 6 is expounded.
  • v. 11 and v. 20 — the name. ESV “useless … useful” and “I want some benefit from you”; NASB95 “useless … useful” and “let me benefit from you”. Neither tells the congregation that “Onesimus” means “Beneficial”. Movement 2 needs it, so tell them.
  • v. 10 — ἐγέννησα (“I begot”). ESV “whose father I became”; NASB95 “whom I have begotten”. The ESV keeps the family relation but loses the verb Paul uses of the Corinthians in 1 Cor 4:15. This does not affect the plan.
  • v. 22 — χαρισθήσομαι (“I will be graciously given”). ESV “graciously given”; NASB95 “given”. Here the ESV serves Movement 4 better than the study text: the grace frame (vv. 3, 22, 25) is audible.

Where the two agree, and the plan can lean on the ESV:

  • “receive him as you would receive me” (v. 17);
  • “more than a bondservant … even more than I say” (vv. 16, 21), so the “beyond … beyond” pair is audible;
  • “hearts … refreshed … my very heart … refresh my heart” (vv. 7, 12, 20), so the return of v. 20 to v. 7 is audible, though the ESV moves “my brother” earlier in v. 7;
  • “command … appeal” (vv. 8–9);
  • “of your own accord” (v. 14).

Caveat. The ESV in the corpus is the 2025 text, not explicitly the Anglicised edition. The wording above has not been checked against an Anglicised copy. Nothing here turns on spelling.

Hand-off

The candidate Big Question and the four-movement structure are ready for the one-sentence-sermon step and the manuscript. In the manuscript, the order inverts: English first, the Greek in brackets.
