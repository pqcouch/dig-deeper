Sermon Review: Philemon — “Refreshed Hearts”

Draft reviewed: “Sermon – Philemon” (first draft, about 1,175 words, stopping at the heading of Part 3)
Also read: “Philemon – Exposition” (the earlier PT-framework reading); “The Contribution of Philemon” (Carson, Moo and Morris, An Introduction to the New Testament, 1992, p. 390)
Tested against: the 1–25 dig (v1.2), the book overview (v1.0), the point-purpose plan, and the ESV and NASB95 texts in the corpus
Pulpit text: ESV (Anglicised), the version declared for Philemon, and the one the draft already quotes. If you will preach this away from your own church, say so; the Pulpit-text Notes below would change
Date: 26 October 2026

This review is Stages 1 and 2 of the sermon-review process: a review of the draft, then proposed rewrites and drafts of the missing material. The drafted passages are offered as material for you to rework, not as a finished sermon. Take what sounds like you, change what doesn’t, and throw away the rest. The final manuscript waits until you have decided.

What Is Working

  • The big question is already in place, and it is the right one. “Whom are we keeping at arm’s length, and what would it take to receive them as family?” comes straight from the text’s own terms (vv. 5–7, 16–18). The point-purpose plan arrived at the same question independently. Keep it.
  • The three hearts are a sound, memorable spine. The word “heart” at vv. 7, 12 and 20, with “refreshed” at vv. 7 and 20, is the letter’s own repetition. The ESV keeps it audible: “hearts … refreshed”, “my very heart”, “refresh my heart”. Building the sermon on it is expository, and the congregation can follow it with their Bibles open. The audit found that v. 20 deliberately takes up v. 7. Your sentence “to take back Onesimus is to refresh Paul’s heart” says exactly that, without overclaiming.
  • You have refused to speculate about the runaway story (“we’d be wise not to speculate!”). That is the single most important exegetical decision in a Philemon sermon. Most published sermons get it wrong.
  • “What the gospel has already made of Onesimus comes before Paul asks anything of Philemon.” This is the theological hinge of the whole letter, and you have it. It keeps the sermon from turning into “be a nicer Philemon”.
  • “Beloved” passing from master (v. 1) to slave (v. 16). This is a good, checkable observation that the ESV lets the congregation see.
  • The house church listening (v. 2). This is correctly placed, and it will matter at the end.
  • The tone. Conversational, direct, and pointing the congregation to the text (“Look at verse 7”). That is your voice; the rewrites below try to keep it.

What Needs Attention

1. Structure — the sermon stops before its centre

The draft ends at the heading of Part 3. What is missing is the part of the letter the other two parts lead up to:

  • vv. 17–19, “receive him as you would receive me … charge that to my account … I will repay it”;
    1. 20, the return to v. 7;
  • vv. 21–25, the open “more”, the visit, and the plural “you”.

Also missing are the application, the answer to the Carson question, and a closing. The structure itself is sound: Philemon (vv. 1–7), Onesimus (vv. 8–16), Paul (vv. 17–25). It follows the letter’s own seams, “Accordingly” at v. 8 and “So if” at v. 17.

2. Opening — the two strongest things are not yet at the front

The draft opens with the big question, then two long quotations (John 13:34–35 and 1 John 4:7–11), and only then reaches Philemon. Two problems:

  • The Carson question is not in it. “What difference would not having this letter make?” is the hook you want, and it is a better opening than the big question. It is concrete (a missing page), it is the congregation’s own suspicion (“isn’t this just a private note?”), and the sermon can answer it at the end. Ask it first; let the big question follow from it.
  • 1 John 4:7–11 is in the wrong place. It is a fine text, but at the start it delays the passage by a full paragraph of scripture before we reach Philemon. Its real work is later: “not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son … if God so loved us, we also ought to love one another” is exactly the motive of vv. 17–19. Move it to Part 3, where the gospel shape of “charge that to my account” needs a text that states it. Keep John 13:34–35 in the opening.

3. Biblical argument — three points to tighten, one gap to fill

  • “The word translated ‘sharing’ reappears in verse 17.” It does not quite: v. 6 has κοινωνία (“fellowship, sharing”) and v. 17 has κοινωνός (“partner”), a related word from the same family. The ESV’s “sharing … partner” hides the link, so the congregation needs to be told. Say “a word from the same family — ‘partner’”.
  • “Paul tells us that the name ‘Onesimus’ … literally means ‘profitable’.” Paul does not tell us; the play is there for those who know Greek. And “Paul uses the word in verse 20” is not quite right either: ὀναίμην (“may I benefit”) is from the same root as the name, not the same word. The draft also puts the Greek where it must be read aloud (“I want some benefit – ὀναίμην – from you”). Move it into brackets at the end, or drop it.
  • “‘My very heart.’ What does Paul mean?” is asked and not answered. The draft goes straight on to vv. 13–14. This is the gap in Part 2, and a proposed paragraph is below.
  • “Both in the flesh and in the Lord” (v. 16) is quoted but not opened. This is the phrase that answers the Carson question (see below). It is also where the sermon must say “slave” once, plainly.

4. Illustrations — the draft has none yet

Every point so far is made from the text, which is a strength. But two places need a concrete picture, and the letter almost supplies them:

  • The missing page (opening). This one costs nothing, because the congregation is holding the Bible it describes.
  • “Charge that to my account” (vv. 18–19). A guarantor’s signature is the obvious picture: a parent who co-signs a son’s or daughter’s first tenancy or loan. The landlord looks at the young person’s record, sees not enough, then sees the parent’s name, and the door opens. The debt is not wished away; it is covered by someone else’s signature. Paul literally signs: “I, Paul, write this with my own hand.”

The earlier Exposition’s advice still holds: don’t import what the letter already gives more powerfully. These two pictures stay inside the letter’s own images, the page and the account-book.

5. Application — not yet written

The plan (point-purpose) names four hearers, and the sermon should speak to each briefly:

  • Philemon’s place: the one who has been wronged.
  • Onesimus’s place: the one who did wrong, or who stands outside.
  • The church: the house that is listening.
  • The unbeliever: the one who needs someone to say “charge it to me”.

The drafts below do this inside the conclusion.

6. Transitions

Those between Parts 1 and 2 are clean (“Philemon was known for refreshing … Then Paul turns to Onesimus”). Part 2 lurches once: from “What does Paul mean?” to vv. 13–14. Part 3 will need a transition built on the letter’s own turn: the first command of the letter comes at v. 17.

7. Closing — none yet

The closing should be a declaration, not a question. Tie it back to the opening (the missing page) and to v. 7 (the hearts of the saints refreshed).

8. Timing

About 1,175 words is roughly 9 minutes. Your 20–25 minutes needs about 2,800–3,500 words. The drafts below add about 1,900, which would bring a lightly edited sermon to about 3,000 words, or 21–22 minutes.

9. Language and small corrections

  • “verses 6 and7” — a missing space.
  • “It’s almost just an afterthought” — “almost an afterthought” is cleaner aloud, and the proposed opening reuses it.
  • “who Paul says is his very heart” — “whom Paul calls his very heart”, or simply “Paul’s very heart”.
  • “Paul, the prisoner, has heard …” — good; keep the title “prisoner” in front of the congregation, since it is the title Paul chooses (v. 1).
  • “Nowhere in the letter is an explanation given as a reason for this separation” — “The letter never tells us why they were parted” says it in half the words.

10. The pulpit text

This is set out in full under Pulpit-Text Notes below. In short, three points your argument rests on are not carried by the ESV, and the congregation must be told:

  • “sharing … partner” (vv. 6, 17);
  • “bondservant” (v. 16);
  • the meaning of the name (vv. 11, 20).

The Supporting Files — What to Trust

“The Contribution of Philemon” (Carson, Moo and Morris)

This is the right question, and the extract gives two good answers: Philemon shows in practice what “neither slave nor free” means (Gal 3:28; Col 3:11), and it shows Paul’s character. Four cautions before you quote it:

  • “Pleading for a runaway slave.” This assumes the reconstruction your draft wisely refuses. If you quote the paragraph, stop before that phrase, or say “pleading for a slave”.
  • “He makes slavery meaningless.” This is a strong claim the letter does not quite make. Paul does not abolish the institution, and what v. 21’s “even more” asks is genuinely open: release, return to Paul, or a fuller reception. What the letter does is take away slavery’s power to define how one believer treats another. “No longer as a bondservant … as a beloved brother” is the text; say that.
  • The quotations are from the NIV84 (“better than a slave, as a dear brother … both as a man and as a brother in the Lord”). Your congregation will have the ESV open, so quote the ESV of v. 16, not Carson’s wording.
  • Rollins’s “shaped early Christian attitudes toward slavery for four centuries” is a secondary claim I cannot check from the corpus. The history cuts both ways: recalled, not verified, Philemon was also cited in the nineteenth century by those defending the return of fugitive slaves. I would leave the footnote out of the sermon.

“Philemon – Exposition” (the earlier PT reading)

Much of it survives the dig: the puns, the free-not-compelled theme, “receive him as me”, and the warnings against moralism and against a sermon on slavery. But the dig and its audits have corrected four things since. Don’t carry them into the sermon:

  • “Theme: a runaway slave returns to his master.” This is the reconstruction again. The letter says only “parted” (v. 15) and “if he has wronged you” (v. 18).
  • “An inclusio across vv. 7, 12, 20.” Withdrawn. The words are real, and v. 20 deliberately takes up v. 7. But there is no designed frame with v. 12 at its centre. Your sermon’s “three hearts” device is fine because it doesn’t claim one.
  • “Paul has written his appeal in the exact vocabulary of the gospel … imputation.” The verb in v. 18, ἐλλογέω (“charge to an account”), is a business word. Paul’s own word for reckoning righteousness is a different one, λογίζομαι (“to reckon”). And Paul never says “as Christ did for you.” The gospel shape is there to see, but the preacher draws the line, and should say he is drawing it. The draft below does that.
  • Colossians 2:14 (the record of debt cancelled) was never tested by the dig. It may be a fair cross-reference, but it is your call, not an established link.

Answering the Carson question from the text

The strongest answer the dig supports is one Carson’s extract only gestures at. Without Philemon, the New Testament’s only word to a Christian master about his Christian slave would be the household rules.

  • In Colossians (very probably the letter that travelled with Philemon, to the same town, on the same journey) Paul tells slaves to obey “your earthly masters”, and masters to treat their slaves “justly and fairly” (Col 3:22; 4:1).
  • In Philemon he writes to one of those masters about one of those slaves, and asks for something no household rule asks: “no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord” (v. 16).
  • In the Greek, “earthly masters” (Col 3:22) is “masters according to the flesh”. The words “slave”, “flesh” and “Lord” stand together in only three verses of the New Testament: Eph 6:5, Col 3:22 and Phlm 16.
  • In the household rules, the “flesh” side is the master’s authority. In Philemon it is a brotherhood: not just at the Lord’s Table on Sunday but in the house on Monday.

That is what we would lose: the principle would be there without its practice. Carson’s second answer, Paul’s character, can stand beside it: the apostle who could command, and asks, and pays.

Proposed Rewrites and New Sections

Four pieces: the opening (rewritten), the gap in Part 2 (new), Part 3 (new), and the conclusion (new). Your Part 1 stands as it is, with the small corrections above.

Rewrite 1 — The opening

Replaces: from “Here’s my question for us this morning …” to the end of the 1 John 4 quotation.

Imagine opening your Bible this morning and finding that one page had gone missing. Not a long page — just the one between Titus and Hebrews. Twenty-five verses. Would you notice? Would the church be any the poorer?

That page is Paul’s letter to Philemon. It’s the shortest of Paul’s letters, and placed at the end of them it can look almost like an afterthought — a personal note about one man and his slave. There’s no great chapter on justification here, no hymn to Christ, nothing about the end of the age. So why is it in our Bibles? What difference would not having this letter make — to the church, and to you and me?

I want us to hold on to that question this morning, because by the end I think we’ll see that we would lose something we cannot do without. And it has to do with a question every one of us faces sooner or later. When a fellow believer has wronged us, or is in some way beneath us, will we keep him in his place? Or, to put it another way: whom are we keeping at arm’s length — and what would it take to receive them as family?

Jesus left us in no doubt about what he expects. After the Passover meal, once Judas Iscariot had gone out, he said to the eleven:

“A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.” (John 13:34–35)

Our problem, you see, is that we are prone to keeping fellow believers at the distance our grievances, our rank or their debts allow. …

Then continue with your own paragraph, unchanged, to “… whom grievance, rank or debt would keep at a distance?”, and add one sentence:

The New Testament tells us a great deal about that love. In Philemon, we watch it happen — in one house, between two men, over one real and awkward wrong.

What it achieves: the concrete hook comes first and the Carson question is set up to be answered at the end. 1 John 4 is saved for the point where it does the most work.

New 1 — Filling the gap in Part 2 (“My very heart”)

Insert after: “‘My very heart.’ What does Paul mean?” and before “He had kept him only with Philemon’s consent”.

The word Paul uses means the inward parts — the place where we feel things most deeply. We’d say “the heart”. Paul is telling Philemon: this man is not a piece of property I am returning to its owner. He is part of me. You cannot receive him without receiving me, and you cannot turn him away without turning me away.

And notice how Paul is going about this. He’s an apostle. He tells Philemon in verse 8 that he could simply command him to do what is right. He doesn’t. “For love’s sake,” verse 9, “I prefer to appeal to you.” He could have kept Onesimus with him — he’d have been glad to, verse 13. He doesn’t do that either, because he will not take Philemon’s decision away from him. Listen:

Continue with vv. 13–14 and vv. 15–16 as drafted. Then, after “we’d be wise not to speculate!”, replace the sentence with:

The letter never tells us why they were parted. From very early on, Christians have told the story of a slave who ran away, and perhaps that is what happened. But Paul doesn’t say so. He says Onesimus “was parted” from Philemon — and he says “perhaps”. Paul isn’t explaining the wrong away. He’s daring to hope that God might bring good out of it.

And we mustn’t be coy about the word “bondservant” in verse 16. The word Paul uses is the ordinary word for a slave — the same word he uses in Colossians, the letter that very probably travelled to the same town on the same journey. There, Paul tells slaves to obey “your earthly masters”, and masters to treat their slaves “justly and fairly” (Colossians 3:22; 4:1). Those are the household rules. But here, writing to one of those masters about one of those slaves, Paul asks for something no household rule asks for. “No longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord.” Not just “in the Lord” — brothers at the Lord’s Table on a Sunday. “In the flesh” as well — brothers in the house on a Monday.

Then continue with your paragraph “However, we are expected to notice …” to the end of Part 2.

What it achieves: it answers the question the draft asks; it says “slave” once, plainly; and it plants the Carson answer in the text.

New 2 — Part 3: Paul, in anticipation of a heart refreshed (vv. 17–25)

Up to this point, Paul hasn’t told Philemon to do anything. He’s appealed. But now, at verse 17, for the first time in the whole letter, comes a command:

So if you consider me your partner, receive him as you would receive me. (v. 17)

Do you remember the “sharing” of Philemon’s faith in verse 6 — the fellowship that Paul prayed would become effective? “Partner” in verse 17 is a word from the same family. Here is how that fellowship becomes effective: receive Onesimus as you would receive me. If Paul himself knocked at Philemon’s door, he would get the best room and the best of the table. That is how Onesimus is to be met.

Then Paul goes further.

If he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything, charge that to my account. I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it — to say nothing of your owing me even your own self. (vv. 18–19)

Notice that Paul doesn’t pretend nothing has happened. “If he has wronged you … or owes you anything.” He doesn’t ask Philemon to pretend either. But he won’t let the wrong have the last word. He moves it. “Charge that to my account.” And he signs it, in his own hand, like an IOU.

Some of you will have signed something like that for a son or daughter — the guarantee on a first tenancy, or a first loan. The landlord looks at the young person’s record and it isn’t enough. Then he sees your name on the form, and the door opens. The debt hasn’t been wished away. It has been covered by someone else’s signature.

And then the turn: “to say nothing of your owing me even your own self.” Whatever Onesimus owes you, Philemon, you owe me more. Most likely Philemon had come to faith through Paul. The creditor is gently reminded that he is himself a debtor.

Now, Christians reading this letter have always heard an echo in it. Martin Luther said, “We are all his Onesimi.” Let’s be honest: Paul doesn’t draw that line. He never writes “just as Christ did for you”. But anyone who knows the gospel can’t miss its shape here — one who owes nothing standing in for one who owes, and saying, “Receive him as me. Put it on my account.” John put it like this:

In this is love, not that we have loved God but that he loved us and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. Beloved, if God so loved us, we also ought to love one another. (1 John 4:10–11)

And Paul himself wrote to the church in Rome, “welcome one another as Christ has welcomed you” (Romans 15:7) — and the word for “welcome” there is the very word he uses here for “receive”. That is why Philemon can do what Paul asks. Not because Paul has ordered him to, but because Philemon has himself been received, and his own debt has been carried.

Then verse 20:

Yes, brother, I want some benefit from you in the Lord. Refresh my heart in Christ. (v. 20)

There’s the play on the name again — “benefit” is what “Onesimus” means. And do you see where Paul has brought us? Right back to verse 7. The man who refreshed the hearts of the saints is asked to refresh one more heart: Paul’s. And Paul’s heart — verse 12 — is Onesimus. Three hearts, one request.

Confident of your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say. (v. 21)

What is the “more”? Paul doesn’t say. Some think he hoped Philemon would set Onesimus free; some that he’d send him back to help Paul; others simply that he’d do it gladly and generously. We don’t know, and Paul leaves it open. But notice the pattern: “more than a bondservant” in verse 16, “more than I say” in verse 21. Love doesn’t look for the minimum.

And Paul isn’t finished: “prepare a guest room for me” (v. 22). He’s coming to see for himself. And the letter that has been speaking to “you” — one man, singular — all the way through, turns back to the whole church at the end: “your prayers”, “the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit” — and in Greek both of those are plural. Philemon decides. But what happens to Onesimus in Philemon’s house is the church’s business.

What it achieves: it supplies the centre of the letter and names the gospel analogy honestly. It lands the three hearts, and it leaves v. 21 open, as the text does.

New 3 — Conclusion: what we would lose

So — back to that missing page. What difference would not having this letter make?

We would still have the great statements: “there is neither slave nor free … for you are all one in Christ Jesus” (Galatians 3:28). We would still have the household rules. But we would never see it. We would have the principle without the practice. Philemon is “neither slave nor free” with a name and an address — the gospel walking into one house, and standing between one master and the slave who may have wronged him.

We would lose a picture of how a Christian leader asks. Paul could command, and he appeals. A church run on command produces compliance. Paul wants something better: love that is free, “not by compulsion but of your own accord” (v. 14).

And we would lose a mirror, because every one of us is somewhere in this letter.

Some of us are Philemon. Someone has wronged you, or someone sits a long way beneath you — at work, in the family, here in church. You have a good name for love, and you have kept that person at arm’s length. Paul doesn’t ask you to pretend the wrong never happened. He asks you to receive that person as you would receive Christ — because that is how Christ has received you.

Some of us are Onesimus. You’re the one who walked away, or let people down, or don’t feel you belong. Hear what the gospel has made of you: no longer “formerly useless”, but useful; a child, a heart, a beloved brother or sister.

All of us are the church in the house, listening in. How we treat the person nobody rates is our business too.

And if you are here this morning and you’re not yet a Christian, this little letter is a picture of what you need. Not someone to tell you that your debt doesn’t matter — but someone to say, “Charge that to my account.” That is what Jesus came to do. He has signed for it, in his own blood.

Placed at the end of Paul’s letters, Philemon looks like an afterthought. It isn’t. It is the gospel with its sleeves rolled up. So receive one another, as Christ has received you. And let it be said of this church, as it was said of Philemon: the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you.

Amen.

What it achieves: it answers the opening question from the text, speaks to all four hearers, and ends on a declaration that ties back to v. 7.

Pulpit-Text Notes (ESV against NASB95)

Where the sermon’s argument turns on a word, here is what the ESV congregation will hear:

  • vv. 6 and 17 — “sharing … partner”. NASB95: “fellowship … partner”. In Greek they are κοινωνία and κοινωνός, words of one family, but neither version lets the link be heard. The ESV’s “sharing of your faith” also sounds like evangelism. Tell the congregation the two words belong together (done in New 2).
  • v. 16 — “bondservant”. NASB95: “slave”. The congregation must be told it is the ordinary word for a slave (done in New 1).
  • vv. 11 and 20 — the name. Neither version shows that “Onesimus” means “beneficial”, or that “benefit” in v. 20 plays on it. Tell them (your draft already does; New 2 picks it up again).
  • Col 3:22 — “earthly masters”. The Greek is “masters according to the flesh”. The ESV’s “earthly” hides the link with “in the flesh” at Phlm 16. Your congregation will not hear it, so the sermon makes the point without depending on it (New 1).
  • Rom 15:7 — “welcome”. The same Greek verb as Phlm 17, “receive”. The ESV uses two different English words, so the sermon says so (New 2).
  • v. 22 — “graciously given”. NASB95: “given”. Here the ESV serves you better.
  • The corpus ESV is the 2025 text, not the Anglicised edition. The wording quoted here has not been checked against an Anglicised copy; nothing turns on spelling.
