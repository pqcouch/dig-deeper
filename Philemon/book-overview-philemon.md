## Canonical Position

The NT equivalent of the Tanak block — position in the collection, what it presupposes, what it hands on.

| Field | |
|---|---|
| **Section** | Pauline corpus, last of the thirteen letters that bear Paul's name; after Titus and before Hebrews in both NA28 and the English Bibles `[T]`. It closes the group of letters to individuals (1–2 Timothy, Titus, Philemon). The ordering of the corpus broadly by length, letters to churches before letters to persons, is a description of the arrangement `[S]`, not a claim the text makes. Philemon is the shortest of the thirteen: 25 verses, 334 words in the SBLGNT, against 659 for Titus, the next shortest (measured) `[T]`. |
| **Reading implication** | Shelved among the letters to individuals, Philemon is easily read as a private note. **Its own address forbids that**: it greets Philemon, Apphia, Archippus "and the church in your house" (v. 2), and its grace is to "you" in the plural at the opening and the close (vv. 3, 25) `[T]`. Read it as a household letter — a personal appeal to one householder, read before his house-church `[I]` — neither a private note nor, like Colossians, a letter to a city's church read aloud and passed on (Col 4:16) `[T]`/`[I]`. |
| **Presupposes** | (1) A prior bond between Paul and Philemon, close enough that Philemon "owe[s] to me even your own self" (v. 19) `[T]`; that Philemon was converted through Paul is the natural reading of that clause `[I]`. (2) Paul's imprisonment (vv. 1, 9, 10, 13; Epaphras "my fellow prisoner", v. 23) `[T]`; its place is not stated `[S]`. (3) Onesimus's prior relation to Philemon — he was "formerly … useless to you" and "separated from you for a while" (vv. 11, 15), and is a δοῦλος ("slave", v. 16) `[T]`. (4) **Colossians**, as a companion letter: eight of the ten names in Philemon besides Paul's recur in Colossians — Timothy, Archippus, Onesimus, Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke (verified against the SBLGNT); no other Pauline letter shares more than four (2 Timothy) `[T]`. That the two letters went to Colossae together is `[I]`, moderate–high as an inference `[S: audit 2]`: Col 4:7–9 sends Tychicus "with Onesimus … who is one of your number" (ἔπεμψα, "I have sent"), Phlm 12 has ἀνέπεμψα ("I have sent back") of Onesimus, and Col 4:17 charges Archippus `[T]`. Neither letter says so: Philemon never names Tychicus, Colossians never names Philemon or Apphia, and "fellow prisoner" is Aristarchus's title at Col 4:10 but Epaphras's at Phlm 23 `[T]`. (5) The gospel by which Paul "begot" Onesimus (v. 10; cf. 1 Cor 4:15, the only other verse in the NT where Paul is the subject of γεννάω, "to beget") `[T]`. No Old Testament text is presupposed by citation (see the Intertextual Map). |
| **Handoff** | Thematic, not lexical: a slave received as "a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16). The letter hands on no quoted formula. Within the corpus it hands on one detail worth holding: εὔχρηστος ("useful") occurs only three times in the NT — of Onesimus here (v. 11), and of Mark at 2 Tim 4:11, where Paul calls him "useful to me for service" (and at 2 Tim 2:21, of a vessel) `[T]`. Mark is greeted at v. 24. Whether a canonical reader should hear the two "useful" men together is `[I]`, uncertain. |
| **Neighbours** | **Titus before:** its household instruction to slaves, μὴ νοσφιζομένους ("not pilfering", Titus 2:10), stands a page before "if he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything" (v. 18) `[T]`; the adjacency does no interpretive work, but the canonical reader arrives at Philemon from it `[I]`. **Hebrews after:** "remember the prisoners (δεσμίων) as though in prison with them" (Heb 13:3) and Timothy "released" (Heb 13:23) `[T]` — again adjacency only. **The real neighbour is Colossians**, four letters back (see Presupposes), and especially Col 3:22–4:1 (the slaves and masters) and 4:7–17 (the envoys and greetings). |

## Presenting Situation

**Author:** Paul names himself three times — in the address (v. 1), in the appeal (v. 9, "such a person as Paul, the aged"), and in the pledge (v. 19, "I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand") `[T]`. Timothy is co-sender (v. 1), but from v. 4 the voice is Paul's alone, in the first person singular `[T]`. **Philemon is the only one of the thirteen letters in which Paul introduces himself as δέσμιος ("prisoner")**: every other opening has ἀπόστολος ("apostle"), δοῦλος ("slave, servant"), or the name alone (checked against the first verse of all thirteen, SBLGNT) `[T]`. The NA28 apparatus shows that one Western witness (D\*) "corrected" it to ἀπόστολος ("apostle"), and others to δοῦλος ("slave") or ἀπόστολος δέσμιος ("apostle, prisoner") `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. The choice of title fits the letter: an apostle could command; a prisoner appeals (vv. 8–9) `[I]`. Pauline authorship of Philemon is not seriously disputed in modern scholarship `[S]`.

**Circumstance of the writer:** in chains (δέσμιος, "prisoner", vv. 1, 9; δεσμοί, "bonds, imprisonment", vv. 10, 13) `[T]`, yet expecting release — "prepare me a lodging, for I hope that through your prayers I will be given to you" (v. 22) `[T]`. The place is not stated `[T]`. The proposals are Ephesus, about 52–55 (Still, Keown); Rome, about 60–61, the traditional view, sometimes supported from v. 9's "old man"; and Caesarea, about 57–59, held by few `[S: Logos 1]`. The lodging request is the text's only datum on distance, and it is often used to favour a nearer prison such as Ephesus `[S]`; the text itself settles nothing.

**Audience:** four parties, named in order (vv. 1–2) `[T]`:

- **Philemon**, "our beloved … and fellow worker" (v. 1). The church meets "in your house" (κατ' οἶκόν σου, v. 2, singular "your"), so he is a householder `[T]`, and on the natural reading the house is Philemon's `[I]`. He has a reputation for love "toward all the saints" (v. 5) and has "refreshed" their hearts (v. 7) `[T]`.
- **Apphia**, "our sister" (v. 2) `[T]`. That she is Philemon's wife is a common inference (Everett, Beet) `[S: Logos 1]`; the text says only "sister".
- **Archippus**, "our fellow soldier" (v. 2) `[T]`. In Colossians he receives a pointed charge to fulfil "the ministry which you have received in the Lord" (Col 4:17) `[T]`. Whose son, and whose house, are not stated `[T]`. He has been read as Philemon's son (Beet) and as the householder and owner himself (Gillman; Knox) `[S: Logos 1]`. The σου ("your") of v. 2 stands directly after his name, which is Knox's datum; but Philemon is named first and with the fuller titles `[T]`, so the house remains Philemon's on the natural reading, at moderate confidence `[I]`.
- **"The church in your house"** (v. 2) `[T]`.

**The singular and the plural.** After v. 3 the body of the letter is addressed to one person. From v. 4 to v. 21 every second-person form is singular — eighteen forms of σύ ("you": σου, σοι, σε), with σός ("your", v. 14) and σεαυτοῦ ("yourself", v. 19) besides (SBLGNT, by parse) — and so is every imperative (vv. 17, 18, 20, 22) `[T]`. The plural returns at v. 22 ("your prayers … given to you") and v. 25 ("your spirit"), as it stood at v. 3 `[T]`. Within the singular body Paul calls Philemon ἀδελφέ ("brother") twice, at v. 7 and v. 20 `[T]`. These are the only singular vocatives of ἀδελφός ("brother") in the New Testament letters (elsewhere only Luke 6:42; Acts 9:17; 21:20; 22:13) `[T]`; but the form is available only in letters to one person, so the rarity is largely genre, and what carries information is the choice — Paul calls Timothy τέκνον ("child", 1 Tim 1:18; 2 Tim 2:1), the Elder calls Gaius ἀγαπητέ ("beloved", 3 John 2, 5, 11), and Paul calls Philemon "brother" `[T]`/`[I]` `[S: audit 1]`. **The letter is therefore a personal appeal to one householder, read before his house-church** `[I]`: Philemon is to decide, and the church will know he has been asked. "Private" is the wrong word for it `[S: audit 2]`; the contrast the texts support is between a letter to a city's church (Colossians, read aloud and exchanged, Col 4:16) and a letter to one household `[T]`/`[I]`.

**Occasion:** Onesimus, Philemon's slave (vv. 15–16) `[T]`, has been with Paul in prison, has become Paul's "child, whom I have begotten in my imprisonment" (v. 10) `[T]`, and has served Paul (v. 13) `[T]`. Paul is sending him back (ἀνέπεμψα, "I have sent back", v. 12 — an epistolary aorist, "I am sending", on the usual reading `[S]`), with this letter `[I]`. Something has gone wrong between Onesimus and Philemon: Onesimus was formerly "useless" to him (v. 11); he was "separated from you for a while" (v. 15); and "if he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything, charge that to my account" (v. 18) `[T]`. **The text does not say that Onesimus ran away.** It says he was separated, in the passive (ἐχωρίσθη, "he was separated", v. 15), and frames a possible wrong conditionally (εἰ, "if", v. 18) `[T]`. The runaway reading is the traditional one, and it is ancient: it is usually traced to Chrysostom, Witherington cites witnesses earlier still, and it is held from Calvin to Pao `[S: Logos 1]`. The rival reconstructions are serious: a slave who sought out Paul, his master's friend, to intercede after a quarrel (the *amicus domini* reading — Lampe, Bartchy, Fitzmyer, Dunn); a slave sent by the church to serve Paul (Winter); a brother rather than a slave (Callahan) `[S: Logos 1]`. The overview works from the features, not from any one reconstruction (see Preaching Trap 1).

**Pressure:** *What does the gospel do to the bond between a master and his slave when both are now brothers "in the Lord"?* More sharply, as Paul frames it: *will Philemon's love, so well spoken of (vv. 5, 7), act when it costs him* `[I]`? Paul says plainly that he could command (v. 8) and will not (vv. 9, 14) `[T]`, so the pressure on Philemon is the pressure of a free decision made in the open `[I]`.

**Purpose:** to secure, without command, Philemon's free and generous reception of Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother" (v. 16) — "as you would me" (v. 17) — with any debt laid to Paul's account, so that the κοινωνία ("fellowship, partnership") of Philemon's faith becomes effective (v. 6) `[I]`.

**Genre note.** Philemon has the shape of a letter of intercession, and is regularly compared with Pliny the Younger's letters to Sabinianus on behalf of an offending freedman (*Ep.* 9.21, 9.24) `[S]`. The comparison is useful for what it shows Philemon does *not* do: Paul does not ask Philemon to forgive a penitent inferior; he asks him to receive a brother as he would receive Paul `[I]`. The comparison has not yet been checked against Pliny's text; it is open for a second Logos round.

## Structural Arc Map

| Verses | Movement | Function |
|--------|----------|----------|
| 1–3 | Address and greeting | Paul "prisoner" (not "apostle"), with Timothy, to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and the house church; grace "to you" (plural) |
| 4–7 | Thanksgiving and prayer | Philemon's love and faith; the prayer that the κοινωνία ("fellowship") of his faith become ἐνεργής ("effective", v. 6); "the hearts (σπλάγχνα) of the saints have been refreshed (ἀναπέπαυται) through you, brother" (v. 7) |
| 8–16 | The appeal | "Therefore" (Διό, v. 8): I could command, but I appeal (παρακαλῶ, vv. 9, 10); for Onesimus — named only now (v. 10); once useless, now useful; sent back as "my very heart" (σπλάγχνα, v. 12); kept back only so that Philemon's goodness be free (vv. 13–14); perhaps separated "for a while" to be received "forever … a beloved brother" (vv. 15–16) |
| 17–20 | The request | "If then (Εἰ οὖν) you regard me a partner, accept him as you would me" (v. 17); charge any debt to me — I will repay (vv. 18–19); "refresh my heart (σπλάγχνα) in Christ" (v. 20) |
| 21–22 | Confidence and a visit | "You will do even more than what I say" (v. 21); "prepare me a lodging" (v. 22) |
| 23–25 | Greetings and grace | Epaphras, "my fellow prisoner"; Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke, "my fellow workers"; grace with "your" (plural) spirit |

**Device and shape** `[T]` unless tagged. The letter is linear, a single appeal built in two stages — first the ground, then the request.

- **No imperative until v. 17.** The letter's four imperatives all stand in vv. 17–22: προσλαβοῦ ("accept", v. 17), ἐλλόγα ("charge to my account", v. 18), ἀνάπαυσον ("refresh", v. 20), ἑτοίμαζε ("prepare", v. 22) — verified by parsing in the SBLGNT index. Before v. 17 Paul only "appeals" (παρακαλῶ, "I appeal", vv. 9, 10), having said that he could "order" (ἐπιτάσσειν, "to order", v. 8). The form of the letter does what v. 8–9 says: it asks before it requests, and it requests rather than commands `[I]`.
- **The name delayed.** Onesimus is not named until v. 10, and then as the last word of his clause, after "my child, whom I have begotten in my imprisonment" — Ὀνήσιμον is the 146th of the letter's 334 words (SBLGNT). Philemon meets him first as Paul's child and only then by his name `[I]`.
- **The hearts: v. 20 takes up v. 7.** σπλάγχνον ("heart", literally "inward parts") stands three times — v. 7 (the saints' hearts refreshed), v. 12 (Onesimus is "my very heart"), v. 20 ("refresh my heart") — and ἀναπαύω ("to refresh") twice, at v. 7 and v. 20 (SBLGNT, by lemma). **σπλάγχνον and ἀναπαύω stand together in one verse at Phlm 7 and Phlm 20 and nowhere else in the New Testament**, and in no verse of Swete's LXX (searched by lemma in the SBLGNT, by word-beginning in Swete). The Swete absence weighs little: σπλάγχνον is almost absent from the Greek of the Hebrew-canon books (twice, both in Proverbs) and is chiefly a word of 2 and 4 Maccabees, Wisdom and Sirach; the New Testament result is the meaningful one `[S: audit 1]`. Both verses turn on the vocative ἀδελφέ ("brother"). The words are Paul's idiom for joy in a refreshed co-worker (2 Cor 7:4–15; see the Pauline companions); what is Philemon's own is their placement — v. 7 closes the thanksgiving and v. 20 the appeal — and their object: elsewhere ἀναπαύω takes πνεῦμα ("spirit", 1 Cor 16:18; 2 Cor 7:13), here σπλάγχνα, which ties both verses to v. 12's τὰ ἐμὰ σπλάγχνα ("my very heart") `[T]`/`[I]`. **v. 20 deliberately takes up v. 7, so that the thanksgiving and the appeal end on the same note; v. 12 supplies the identification that gives v. 20 its point** `[I]`. *Confidence:* moderate–high `[S: audit 1]`. *Baselines (audit 1):* of the letter's 153 pairs of verses at least eight verses apart, vv. 7/20 is the only pair sharing three words that stand together in no other verse, and it ranks first on every measure; across Paul's letters about one 25-verse stretch in twenty (4.7 %) has a pair at that level `[T]`. **Withdrawn:** v0.1.0's frame around vv. 8–19 with v. 12 at its centre. NA28 paragraphs the letter 1–3 | 4–7 | 8–14 | 15–20 | 21–22 | 23–24 | 25 `[T: as NA28 prints it]`; on that layout vv. 7 and 20 are matching section-endings, and v. 20 lies inside the body, not outside it. And the middle one of three occurrences must fall between the other two, so v. 12's position proves nothing; its force is in what it says `[S: audit 1]`.
- **"Now" twice.** νυνί ("now") stands only at v. 9 ("now also a prisoner") and v. 11 ("now … useful"): Paul's "now" in chains and Onesimus's "now" of usefulness (SBLGNT) `[T]`; read together `[I]`.
- **Prayer at both ends of the body.** προσευχή ("prayer") stands at v. 4 ("my prayers", Paul's for Philemon) and v. 22 ("your prayers", the church's for Paul), and nowhere else in the letter `[T]`: the prayer has turned round `[I]`.
- **Grace at both ends of the letter.** χάρις ("grace") at v. 3 and v. 25 (the standard Pauline frame), with χαρισθήσομαι ("I will be given [as a gift]", v. 22) between `[T]`.

**Rhetorical analysis.** Philemon is often analysed with the categories of ancient rhetoric — *exordium* (vv. 4–7), *probatio* (vv. 8–16), *peroratio* (vv. 17–22) `[S]`. The seams above coincide with that analysis, but the overview rests them on the text's own markers (Διό, "therefore", at v. 8; Εἰ οὖν, "if then", at v. 17; the move from appeal to imperative), not on the classification.

## Microscript

*Paul, the prisoner who could command, appeals in love that Philemon, whose love has refreshed the saints, receive back Onesimus — once "useless", now "useful", Paul's own child and heart — no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother, as he would receive Paul himself, with any debt laid to Paul's account; so the fellowship of Philemon's faith becomes effective, and Paul's heart is refreshed in Christ.*

The keynote is **receiving the brother as Paul**. The engine is the κοινωνία family: Paul prays that ἡ κοινωνία τῆς πίστεώς σου ("the fellowship of your faith") become ἐνεργής ("effective", v. 6), and the request turns on it: Εἰ οὖν με ἔχεις κοινωνόν ("if then you regard me a partner", v. 17) `[T]`. κοινωνία ("fellowship") and κοινωνός ("partner") are cognate, not one lemma (SBLGNT), and each occurs once in the letter `[T]`. The family word ἀδελφός ("brother") carries the relationship: Timothy (v. 1), Philemon (vv. 7, 20) and Onesimus (v. 16), with ἀδελφή ("sister") for Apphia (v. 2) — **ἀδελφός at vv. 1, 7, 16, 20, four verses** (SBLGNT, by lemma) `[T]`. The word that changes owner is ἀγαπητός ("beloved"): it is Philemon's title at v. 1 and Onesimus's at v. 16, "a beloved brother", and nowhere else in the letter `[T]`.

**Test against the movements.** 1–3: Philemon "beloved"; the church named as witness. 4–7: the love and the κοινωνία ("fellowship") prayed into action; the saints' hearts refreshed. 8–16: the appeal for "my child … my very heart … a beloved brother". 17–20: "accept him as you would me"; the debt laid on Paul; "refresh my heart". 21–22: "more than what I say"; Paul himself coming to the house. 23–25: the fellow prisoner and fellow workers who stand behind the request; grace for the whole church. Every movement serves the microscript `[I]`.

## Intertextual Map

| # | Source | Philemon | Live? | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lev 6:1–7 (Swete; Hebrew 5:20–26) — wrong, partnership and repayment; with Gen 31:39 and the Covenant Code's restitution verb (Exod 21:19–22:17) | 17–19 | — | uncertain–moderate (the commercial IOU leads) |
| 2 | Lev 25:39–46; Exod 21:2–6; Deut 15:12–17 — the brother not to be made a slave; the servant "forever" | 15–16 | — | uncertain (a reader's contrast) |
| 3 | Deut 23:15–16 [LXX 23:16–17] — the runaway slave not to be handed back | 12, 15 | — | uncertain (background only; no shared wording) |

Swete's LXX unless stated. The notes give, for each row, what the use would do, the rarity count and the closest rival.

**1. Wrong, partnership and repayment → vv. 17–19 εἰ δέ τι ἠδίκησέν σε ἢ ὀφείλει … ἐγὼ ἀποτίσω ("if he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything … I will repay").** Paul takes on himself the liability that the Torah lays on the one who did the damage `[I]`. *Rarity and rivals:* ἀποτίνω ("to repay, make restitution") stands in **33 verses of Swete** (present and aorist stems together), **fifteen of them in Exod 21:19–22:17** — the Covenant Code's restitution verb, rendering the piel of שׁלם ("make good") in thirteen of the fifteen `[T]` (audit 2). With a word of wrongdoing (ἀδικ-) in the same verse it stands in **four**: Exod 22:9; Lev 6:5 (Hebrew 5:24); Ps 68:5 (ἀδίκως … ἀπετίννυον, "wrongfully … I was paying back"); Ezek 33:15 `[T]`. In the NT ἀποτίνω occurs only here (SBLGNT) `[T]`. The slave law that opens Exod 21 shares no wording with vv. 18–19 `[T]`. **The closest Torah text is Lev 6:1–7 (Swete).** Lev 6:2, ἢ ἠδίκησέν τι τὸν πλησίον ("or has wronged his neighbour in anything"), is the only verse in Swete with ἠδίκησεν + τι ("has wronged … anything") — Phlm 18's words in reversed order — and it has κοινωνία ("partnership") in the same verse; Lev 6:5 has ἀποτίσει ("he shall repay") `[T]`. One paragraph thus holds "wrong", "anything", "partnership" and "repay", as Phlm 17–19 holds κοινωνόν ("partner"), τι ἠδίκησεν and ἀποτίσω `[T]`. The nearest picture is Gen 31:39, Jacob to Laban: ἐγὼ ἀπετίννυον ("I myself made it good"), the only verse in Swete with ἐγώ ("I") as the verb's subject — a servant making good his master's losses from his own pocket `[T]`. **The leading reading is still the commercial one:** ἀποτίνω is the standard contract verb for damages in the papyri (Deissmann, recalled), and "I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand" (v. 19) is the form of a handwritten IOU (O'Brien, Dunn, Barth–Blanke) `[S: Logos 1]`. A verb in 33 verses is idiom and cannot carry an allusion by itself `[I]`. *Confidence:* uncertain–moderate `[S: audit 2]`; if any Torah text is heard behind vv. 17–19, it is Lev 6:2–5, not the Covenant Code.

**2. The servant "forever" → vv. 15–16 ἵνα αἰώνιον αὐτὸν ἀπέχῃς, οὐκέτι ὡς δοῦλον ("that you would have him back forever, no longer as a slave").** In the Torah a Hebrew slave who chooses to stay "shall serve him forever" (Exod 21:6, δουλεύσει αὐτῷ εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, for וַעֲבָדוֹ לְעֹלָם, "he shall serve him permanently"); "he shall be your servant forever" (Deut 15:17, ἔσται σοι οἰκέτης εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, for עֶבֶד עוֹלָם, "a servant forever"); and "your brother" who is sold to you is not to be subjected to a slave's service (Lev 25:39) `[T]`. **The fullest Torah form of the contrast is Lev 25:39–46**: brothers are not to be made slaves, while foreign slaves are a possession εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα ("forever", 25:46) `[T]`. Philemon is to have Onesimus "forever" — but not as a slave, as a brother `[I]`. *Rarity and rivals:* a δουλ- word with αἰών in one verse: 18 verses in Swete, most of them "your servant(s) forever" in prayer (2 Sam 7:25, 29 and the like); **Deut 15:17 is not among them** — its word is οἰκέτης ("household servant") `[T]` (audit 2). 1 Sam 27:12, ἔσται μοι δοῦλος εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα ("he shall be my servant forever"), is as close in wording as Deut 15:17 `[T]`. ἀδελφ- ("brother") with a δουλ- word in the Torah: **five verses** — Gen 27:29; 27:40; 29:15 ("because you are my brother, should you serve me for nothing?"); Lev 25:39; Deut 15:12 — two of them in the law codes `[T]` (audit 2). **The closest wording of all is Job 40:23 [Hebrew 40:28; Eng 41:4]**, λήμψῃ δὲ αὐτὸν δοῦλον αἰώνιον ("will you take him for a slave forever?"), of Leviathan: the only pairing in Swete of δοῦλος ("slave") with the adjective αἰώνιος ("eternal"), the pairing Phlm 15–16 has, and a rendering of the Hebrew phrase of Deut 15:17 (לְעֶבֶד עוֹלָם, "for a servant forever") `[T]`. **The closest New Testament parallel in thought is John 8:35**: "the slave does not remain in the house forever; the son does" — δοῦλος with αἰών in one NT verse only there and at Rev 15:3 `[T]`. The NT has its own vocabulary of "brother" across the slave–master line (1 Tim 6:2) and of kinship "according to the flesh" (Rom 9:3) `[T]`, so no Torah background is needed to explain v. 16 `[I]`. Philemon's "forever" is αἰώνιος ("eternal"), the word Paul uses for eternal life, against the law's εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα ("for life") `[T]`/`[I]`. *Confidence:* uncertain — a reader's contrast, not shown to be intended `[S: audit 2]`.

**3. Deut 23:15–16 [LXX 23:16–17] — "You shall not hand over to his master a slave who has escaped from his master to you."** Proposed as background: Paul does send Onesimus back `[I]`. *Rarity and rival:* the Greek has παῖς ("slave") and κύριος ("master"); Philemon shares no word of it (κύριος in Philemon is always the Lord). The row depends on the runaway reconstruction, which the text does not state (Presenting Situation). *Confidence:* uncertain — background only; flagged because a sermon may reach for it (Preaching Trap 1).

**4. Joseph — Gen 45:5–8 ("God sent me before you"); 50:20 ("you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good") → v. 15 τάχα γὰρ διὰ τοῦτο ἐχωρίσθη ("for perhaps he was for this reason separated").** A separation in which a purpose of God is discerned after the event `[I]`. *Rarity and rival:* no shared wording; χωρίζω ("to separate") is not in the Joseph story. Paul's τάχα ("perhaps") is itself a check on over-reading providence `[T]`/`[I]`. *Confidence:* uncertain (conceptual only).

**Tested and not entered — the freewill offering at v. 14.** κατὰ ἀνάγκην … κατὰ ἑκούσιον ("by compulsion … of your own free will") might recall τὸ ἑκούσιον, one LXX rendering of the freewill offering: καθʼ ἑκούσιον ("as a freewill offering") occurs once in Swete, at Num 15:3, for בִנְדָבָה ("as a freewill offering", WLC) `[T]`. But ἑκούσιον is one rendering of נְדָבָה ("freewill offering") among several — of the thirteen Torah verses with the noun, Swete uses it in four (Lev 7:6 Swete; 23:38; Num 15:3; 29:39) `[T]` — nothing else in v. 14 is cultic, and the antithesis is stock: a compulsion word and a willing word stand in one NT verse only at Phlm 14 and 1 Pet 5:2 ("not under compulsion, but voluntarily"), and Paul's 2 Cor 9:7 has "not … under compulsion" (ἐξ ἀνάγκης) of a gift `[T]`. *Confidence:* low–moderate `[S: audit 2]`; not entered as a row.

**The density scan.** The New Testament scan was run on the whole letter against Swete's LXX, in all six settings (whole chapters, 8- and 16-verse windows; all words and rarer words). **No Old Testament passage stands out.** Every chapter in the top ten of the stability table is a Greek-composed letter or a book of the Greek period — Esther 8 (the royal decree of the Greek additions), 3 Maccabees 7, 5, 2 and 4, 2 Maccabees 7, 12 and 9, Wisdom 13, 4 Maccabees 15 — and each is accounted for in one line:

- **Esth 8, 3 Macc 7, 2 Macc 9 and 2 Macc 1 (just outside the ten)** share the vocabulary of the Hellenistic letter — writing, greeting, thanks, peace, "what is proper" (ἀνήκω), "to order" (ἐπιτάσσω), boldness (παρρησία). Genre, not source.
- **3 Macc 5, 2 and 4, 2 Macc 12** are carried by common words and by stem faults in the scan: "Demas" (δημ-) catches δημόσιος ("public") and δήμιος ("executioner"); γενν- ("beget") catches γενναῖος ("noble"); ἀπεχ- catches ἀπεχθής ("hateful"); ἀνηκ- catches ἀνήκεστος ("incurable"). Read word by word, they share nothing distinctive.
- **2 Macc 7 and 4 Macc 15** (the mother and her seven sons) share the language of a parent's love — σπλάγχνα ("inward parts"), τέκνον ("child"), παρακαλέω ("to encourage") — and, in 2 Macc 7:5, ἄχρηστος ("useless"). A common field of family affection; no allusion.
- **Wisdom 13** has ἄχρηστος ("useless", 13:10) and εὔχρηστος ("useful", 13:13) of the woodcarver's timber, the only chapter of the LXX with both words. A coincidence of the pair, not a source: Wisdom 13 is about idols.

**Citation-light — the key finding for the sweep.** Philemon has **no quotation of the Old Testament, no formula of citation, and no allusion with exclusive wording** `[T]`. The four rows above are all uncertain or idiom. The letter's argument is carried entirely by **internal repetition** — the hearts (σπλάγχνα), the refreshing (ἀναπαύω), "brother" and "beloved", the fellowship (κοινωνία / κοινωνός), "useless / useful" — and by its relation to **Colossians**. The whole-letter dig bore this out: **Move 2 (book-usage tracking) had nothing to track**, and Tool 11's yield came from **Move 4 (internal echo)** and the Pauline companions below; the two Torah rows and the freewill candidate it tested all stayed at uncertain–moderate or below `[S: dig v1.2]` `[S: audit 2]`. Do not manufacture Old Testament allusions to fill this map; the sparseness is the finding `[T]`/`[I]`.

## Christological Trajectory

Philemon names Christ often for so short a letter — Χριστός ("Christ") in eight of its twenty-five verses (vv. 1, 3, 6, 8, 9, 20, 23, 25), κύριος ("Lord") in five (vv. 3, 5, 16, 20, 25), always of Jesus (SBLGNT) `[T]`. Christ is the **ground** and the **sphere** of everything Paul asks. He is **not**, in the letter's own words, the pattern of the request.

- **Ground.** Grace and peace are "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 3), and the closing grace is "of the Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 25) `[T]`. Paul's boldness to command is ἐν Χριστῷ ("in Christ", v. 8) `[T]`. Onesimus was "begotten" in Paul's imprisonment (v. 10), and the imprisonment is "for the gospel" (v. 13) `[T]`: the gospel is what made a slave Paul's child `[I]`. Philemon's faith is "toward the Lord Jesus" (v. 5) `[T]`.
- **Sphere.** The new relation exists "in the Lord": Onesimus is a beloved brother "both in the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16) `[T]`. Paul's requests are made "in the Lord" and "in Christ" (v. 20) `[T]`. The κοινωνία ("fellowship") of faith is to become effective εἰς Χριστόν (v. 6) `[T]` — "for Christ's sake" in both translations, "in relation to Christ" or "in Christ" in Dunn and O'Brien `[S: Logos 1]` (Pulpit Notes). Epaphras is a fellow prisoner "in Christ Jesus" (v. 23) `[T]`. Union with Christ is the reason a master and a slave are now brothers `[I]`.
- **Pattern — the analogy the letter does not draw.** Paul stands between the offended master and the offending slave; he identifies himself with the slave ("accept him as you would me", v. 17); he takes the slave's debt on his own account ("charge that to my account … I will repay", vv. 18–19) `[T]`. Christian readers have heard the gospel in this since the Reformation — Luther: "we are all his Onesimi" `[S]`. The shape is real and the analogy is apt `[I]`. **But Philemon never draws it.** Paul does not say "as Christ did for you". The analogy belongs to the reader of the whole canon (2 Cor 5:19–21; Rom 15:7), and a sermon should say so `[I]`. The ἐλλογέω ("to charge to an account") link to Rom 5:13 is lexical; it is not Paul's doctrine of imputation, which uses λογίζομαι ("to reckon") `[T]`.

**Moralism check.** The temptation is to preach Philemon as a lesson in forgiveness or reconciliation — "be a Paul; be a Philemon" — and to stop there. The letter itself refuses to make its request free-standing. It grounds Philemon's action in his faith and love "toward the Lord Jesus" (v. 5), in the κοινωνία ("fellowship") of that faith (v. 6), in what he owes Paul for the gospel (v. 19), and in the brotherhood that exists "in the Lord" (v. 16) `[T]`/`[I]`. **The indicative is a new family made by the gospel; the imperative is to act as family.** Christ is the Lord in whom the new relation exists, and only by canonical analogy the substitute who pays the debt; preach the first from Philemon, and the second honestly, as a reading the letter invites but does not state `[I]`.

## Preaching Traps

1. **Preaching a reconstruction as the text.** *Bites at:* vv. 10–19, and every introduction to the letter. It sounds like a vivid story — "Onesimus stole from his master and ran away to Rome, where by providence he met Paul in prison". The letter states none of this: it says "separated" (passive, v. 15) and "*if* he has wronged you" (v. 18) `[T]`. The runaway reading is ancient and may be right; the rival reconstructions — Onesimus seeking out his master's friend to intercede (*amicus domini*), Onesimus sent by the church, Onesimus a brother — are serious (Presenting Situation) `[S: Logos 1]`. **Preach what the text gives** — a broken relation, a possible wrong, a conversion, a return — and say once, briefly, that the details are reconstruction. The opposite trap is to spend the sermon on the debate.

2. **Making the letter answer the modern slavery question in either direction.** *Bites at:* vv. 15–16, 21. One version: "Paul accepted slavery; he sent the slave back." The other: "Paul demanded Onesimus's freedom." The text says "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother … both in the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16), and "you will do even more than what I say" (v. 21) `[T]`. What v. 21's "more" is, is genuinely contested, and the readers fall into three camps: manumission (Wright, Houlden, Caird, Bellen, Petersen; Harrill cautiously), release to Paul's service (Knox, Harrison, O'Brien, Bruce, Marshall, Pao), or better treatment in the household (Lohse, Barth–Blanke, Davids); Dunn holds it open `[S: Logos 1]`. The text does not decide: the "beyond … beyond" pair (vv. 16, 21) is marked but does not name its content, and vv. 13–14 keep release to Paul's service one live option among several `[S: audit 1]`. "In the flesh" (ἐν σαρκί, "in the flesh" — its only NT use for a relationship between two people) means the brotherhood is to be real in the ordinary, human relationship and not only "spiritually" `[I]`. This is a pastoral landmine. The congregation hears "bondservant" (ESV) where the study text says "slave" (NASB95); tell them it is the same word, δοῦλος ("slave"), as in Col 3:22–4:1, and do not let either the modern question or its evasion replace the letter's own.

3. **Calling Paul manipulative — or pretending he is not applying pressure.** *Bites at:* vv. 8–9, 14, 17–22. Modern readers hear vv. 19 ("not to mention … you owe to me even your own self") and 21–22 (the confidence; the coming visit) as pressure, and some call it manipulation `[S]`. The library's readers divide: Seesengood presses the pressure; Pao hears absolute, apostolic obedience; Copenhaver and Arthurs the obedience of faith; Nordling a pastor's delicacy `[S: Logos 1]`. The other trap is to sentimentalise the letter as gentle. The text is open about what it is doing: Paul says he could command (v. 8), refuses to (vv. 9, 14), names the debt between them (v. 19), expects ὑπακοή ("obedience", v. 21) to what he writes — 2 Cor 2:9 is the only other NT verse joining γράφω ("to write") to the obedience family — and announces his visit (v. 22), all in a letter the church will hear (vv. 2, 22, 25) `[T]`. It is love that argues hard and in public `[I]`. Preach both the appeal and its weight.

4. **Allegorising the letter into the gospel.** *Bites at:* vv. 17–19. "Onesimus is the sinner, Philemon is God the Father, Paul is Christ" — the Reformation reading `[S]`. The shape is there (Christological Trajectory), and the analogy is legitimate as an analogy. The trap is to make the analogy the *point* of the text and lose what the letter actually asks of a believing householder. Preach the letter's request first; offer the analogy second, and name it as an analogy.

5. **Treating "perhaps" as a certainty about providence.** *Bites at:* v. 15. "God planned the whole thing" turns Paul's τάχα ("perhaps") into a doctrine, and can sound, to anyone who has been wronged, as if the wrong were justified `[I]`. Paul offers a hopeful reading of a separation, tentatively, and the passive ἐχωρίσθη ("he was separated") leaves the agent unnamed `[T]`. Keep the "perhaps".

6. **The private note.** *Bites at:* vv. 1–3, 22–25. Preaching Philemon as a private matter between two men misses the church in the address and in the closing plurals `[T]`, and with it the letter's implication that the way a household treats its slave is the church's business `[I]`. Equally, do not build a doctrine of church discipline on the plurals.

**2.** v. 7 τὰ σπλάγχνα τῶν ἁγίων ("the hearts of the saints") → v. 12 τοῦτ' ἔστιν τὰ ἐμὰ σπλάγχνα ("that is, my very heart") — of Onesimus. **Develops** — Onesimus *is* the heart Philemon is now to refresh (with v. 20). σπλάγχνον stands only at vv. 7, 12, 20. v. 12's force is in what it says, not in where it stands `[S: audit 1]`. *Confidence:* high.

At v. 1 the words are unjoined — ἀδελφός is Timothy's, ἀγαπητός Philemon's; at v. 16 both are Onesimus's `[T]`. *Confidence:* high.

**9.** v. 8 ἐπιτάσσειν σοι τὸ ἀνῆκον ("to order you to do what is proper") → v. 21 πεποιθὼς τῇ ὑπακοῇ σου ("having confidence in your obedience"). **Resolves** — the command Paul declined to give returns as an "obedience" he is sure of. What is Philemon obeying, if Paul has not commanded? The letter's own idiom gives the answer: obedience to an apostolic word that is itself an appeal `[I]` — γράφω ("to write") with the obedience family stands in one NT verse only at 2 Cor 2:9 and Phlm 21 `[T]`. How much authority to hear in it divides the commentators (Preaching Trap 3) `[S: Logos 1]`. *Confidence:* moderate.

**10.** v. 9 νυνὶ δὲ καὶ δέσμιος ("and now also a prisoner") → v. 11 νυνὶ δὲ … εὔχρηστον ("but now is useful"). **Replants** — νυνί ("now") only at vv. 9 and 11, both introduced by δέ ("but, and"): Paul's "now" is chains; Onesimus's "now" is usefulness — made so in those chains (v. 10) `[I]`. *Confidence:* moderate.

**11.** v. 10 ὃν ἐγέννησα ("whom I have begotten") → v. 19 σεαυτόν μοι προσοφείλεις ("you owe to me even your own self"). **Develops** — Onesimus owes Paul his new life; so, Paul hints, does Philemon. The hint is `[I]`, the natural reading. *Confidence:* moderate.

**12.** v. 18 ἢ ὀφείλει ("or owes") → v. 19 σεαυτόν μοι προσοφείλεις ("you owe to me even your own self"). **Reverses** — Onesimus's possible debt to Philemon is set against Philemon's real debt to Paul. ὀφείλω ("to owe") only at v. 18; προσοφείλω ("to owe besides") only at v. 19, its only NT occurrence. *Confidence:* high.

**13.** v. 16 ὑπὲρ δοῦλον ("more than a slave") → v. 21 ὑπὲρ ἃ λέγω ("more than what I say"). **Pairs** — Onesimus is to be "beyond a slave", and Philemon will do "beyond what I say". Both are the comparative accusative, which is rare in Paul (ten tokens in his other twelve letters); two in 334 words would occur by chance less than one time in a hundred, and only one other place in Paul has two within 25 verses (2 Cor 11:23 – 12:13) `[T]` (audit 1). v. 13's ὑπὲρ σοῦ ("on your behalf") is the ordinary genitive, Paul's dominant use, and not part of the pair; v0.1.0's three-step "escalation" was an artefact of English `[S: audit 1]`. v. 21's phrasing is stock (1 Cor 4:6; 10:13; 2 Cor 12:6) `[T]`, and its nearer link is to v. 19: γράφω ("to write") and λέγω ("to say") stand together only at vv. 19 and 21 in the letter `[T]`, so "what I say" most naturally picks up the requests of vv. 17–20 `[I]`. The pair does not decide what the "more" is (Preaching Trap 2). *Confidence:* moderate–high (the pairing) `[S: audit 1]`.

**14.** vv. 1, 9 δέσμιος Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ ("prisoner of Christ Jesus") → v. 10 ἐν τοῖς δεσμοῖς ("in my imprisonment"), v. 13 ἐν τοῖς δεσμοῖς τοῦ εὐαγγελίου ("in my imprisonment for the gospel") → v. 23 ὁ συναιχμάλωτός μου ("my fellow prisoner"). **Develops** — the chains are where Onesimus was begotten and where he served; the imprisonment is "for the gospel". *Confidence:* high.

**15.** vv. 1–2 the four addressees and "the church in your house" → v. 22 "your prayers … given to you" (plural); v. 25 "your spirit" (plural). **Frames** — the singular body is enclosed by the church. **Addressee differentiation:** Apphia and Archippus are not mentioned again in Philemon; Archippus receives his own charge only in Col 4:17 `[T]`. Why he is named here is not known `[S]`. *Confidence:* high (the pronouns); the reason `[I]`.

**16.** v. 1 συνεργῷ ἡμῶν ("our fellow worker", Philemon) → v. 24 οἱ συνεργοί μου ("my fellow workers", Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke). **Frames** — συνεργός ("fellow worker") only at vv. 1 and 24; with συστρατιώτης ("fellow soldier", v. 2) and συναιχμάλωτος ("fellow prisoner", v. 23), the letter's σύν-compounds ("fellow-") stand at its two ends. Philemon is enrolled in the company that greets him `[I]`. *Confidence:* moderate.

**17.** v. 3 χάρις ὑμῖν ("grace to you") → v. 22 χαρισθήσομαι ὑμῖν ("I will be given to you") → v. 25 ἡ χάρις … μετὰ τοῦ πνεύματος ὑμῶν ("the grace … be with your spirit"). **Frames** — the grace-greeting is standard; the χαρίζομαι ("to give graciously") between is not. Paul's coming is itself a gift of grace. The ESV ("graciously given") lets the congregation hear it; the NASB95 ("given") does not. *Confidence:* high (words); moderate as design.

**18.** v. 11 τόν ποτέ σοι ἄχρηστον νυνὶ δὲ … εὔχρηστον ("formerly useless to you, but now … useful") → v. 20 ἐγώ σου ὀναίμην ("let me benefit from you"). **Replants (wordplay).** Ὀνήσιμος means "beneficial, useful" `[S]`, formed on the root of ὄνησις ("benefit"). At v. 11 the play is on the name's *meaning*, through a stock antithesis of ordinary Greek: ἄχρηστος / εὔχρηστος ("useless / useful") come from the χρη- root, not the name's (compare the "useless vessel" of Hos 8:8 and the "useful vessel" of 2 Tim 2:21) `[T]`/`[I]`. At v. 20 ὀναίμην ("may I benefit", from ὀνίνημι, its only NT occurrence) plays on the name's own root, two verses after Paul's offer to pay `[T]`/`[I]`. But ὀναίμην is a stock wish-formula of letters — Ignatius uses it repeatedly, once beside the bishop Onesimus (*Eph.* 1:3; 2:2, recalled) — so it is its place inside the plea for Onesimus, and its wording ἐγώ σου ("I … of you"), that make the play likely `[I]`. A further pun on χρηστός / Χριστός ("useful" / "Christ"), which were coming to sound alike in Koine, has been proposed, and Wright, Dunn and Davids allow it `[S: Logos 1]`; but v. 11 has no Χριστός in it, and nothing in the verse signals the pun `[T]`. Neither translation lets the congregation hear any of it. *Confidence:* high (v. 11, a play on the name's meaning); moderate–high (v. 20); low–moderate (the Christ-pun) `[S: audit 2]`.

**19.** v. 17 προσλαβοῦ αὐτὸν ὡς ἐμέ ("accept him as you would me") → v. 22 ἑτοίμαζέ μοι ξενίαν ("prepare me a lodging"). **Develops** — Philemon is to receive Onesimus as Paul, and then to receive Paul, who will see the reception for himself `[I]`. ξενία ("lodging, hospitality") occurs elsewhere in the NT only at Acts 28:23, of Paul's lodging in Rome `[T]`. *Confidence:* moderate.

**Hold vv. 4–7 open against vv. 17–22.** The letter asks Philemon to do, for one man, exactly what the thanksgiving said he had done for "all the saints": love (v. 5 → v. 9), fellowship (v. 6 → v. 17), the good (v. 6 → v. 14), the refreshing of hearts (v. 7 → v. 20). The thanksgiving is not flattery; it is the ground of the request, and it sets the terms by which Philemon will be seen to keep faith with his own reputation `[I]`.

## The Text: NA28 Apparatus and Pulpit Notes

### Where the NA28 and the SBLGNT differ in Philemon

A word-by-word comparison of the two editions, verse by verse, finds **one difference of wording**, with one of spelling. Neither changes a count above.

| Verse | NA28 | SBLGNT |
|---|---|---|
| 11 | νυνὶ δὲ [καὶ] σοὶ καὶ ἐμοί ("but now [both] to you and to me") | νυνὶ δὲ σοὶ καὶ ἐμοί |
| 16 | ἀλλ' (elided) | ἀλλά |

- **v. 11.** NA28 brackets καί ("both"); it is read by the corrected Sinaiticus, A C D and the majority, and omitted by the original hand of Sinaiticus, F G 33 104 and the Vulgate `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. The NASB95 ("both to you and to me") and the ESV ("indeed useful to you and to me") both reflect it.

### Variants that bear on findings (NA28 apparatus)

- **v. 6 — "in us" or "in you".** NA28 prints ἐν ἡμῖν ("in us"), with A C D K L Ψ and the majority; ἐν ὑμῖν ("in you") is read by 𝔓⁶¹ א F G P 33 1739 1881 and others `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. **The ESV follows NA28 ("every good thing that is in us"); the NASB95 follows the other reading ("which is in you").** The verse is the letter's hardest (see Preaching Units), and the variant changes whose "good" is in view: the believers' in common, or Philemon's own. The scribes also added Ἰησοῦν ("Jesus") after Χριστόν ("Christ") and, in some witnesses, ἔργου ("work") after ἀγαθοῦ ("good"). The manuscripts slightly favour ὑμῖν, but the committee preferred ἡμῖν as the more expressive reading, a change to the second person being the likelier assimilation (Omanson–Metzger); Dunn reads "among us" `[S: Logos 1]`. *Moderate for ἡμῖν.*
- **v. 5 — the order of "love and faith".** NA28 prints τὴν ἀγάπην καὶ τὴν πίστιν ("love and faith"); 𝔓⁶¹ (probably), D 1739 1881 and others reverse it to "faith and love" `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. The printed order puts love first, and makes the verse a crossed pattern — love … faith … toward the Lord Jesus … toward all the saints — in which love goes to the saints and faith to the Lord `[S]`/`[I]`. The reversal is a scribe normalising Paul's usual order `[I]`. Both translations keep the printed order.
- **v. 12 — the missing "receive him".** NA28 reads ὃν ἀνέπεμψά σοι, αὐτόν, τοῦτ' ἔστιν τὰ ἐμὰ σπλάγχνα ("whom I have sent back to you, him, that is, my very heart"), with the original hand of Sinaiticus, A and 33. The majority and others supply προσλαβοῦ ("receive") — the KJV's "thou therefore receive him" — anticipating v. 17 `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. The broken sentence is the harder reading, and the addition explains it `[I]`. **Both translations follow NA28**, so the first imperative is heard at v. 17, as the structural finding requires.
- **v. 1 — "prisoner" or "apostle".** D\* reads ἀπόστολος ("apostle"); 629 ἀπόστολος δέσμιος ("apostle, prisoner"); 323 945 δοῦλος ("slave") `[T: as NA28 prints it]`. Every one is a scribe conforming Philemon to Paul's other openings `[I]`.
- **v. 2 — "Apphia the sister" or "the beloved".** The majority reads τῇ ἀγαπητῇ ("the beloved"; KJV "beloved Apphia"); NA28 τῇ ἀδελφῇ ("the sister"), with א A D\* 33 1739 and others. Both translations read "sister".
- **v. 20 — "in Christ" or "in the Lord".** NA28 ἐν Χριστῷ ("in Christ"); the majority ἐν κυρίῳ ("in the Lord"). With NA28, v. 20 has both phrases ("in the Lord … in Christ"); both translations follow it.
- **v. 9 — πρεσβύτης ("old man") or πρεσβευτής ("ambassador").** **No manuscript in the NA28 apparatus reads "ambassador"**; it is a conjecture (Bentley; adopted by Lightfoot and the RSV) `[S]`, supported by Eph 6:20, "I am an ambassador (πρεσβεύω) in chains" `[T]`. The ESV and the NASB95 both print the manuscripts' reading ("an old man"; "the aged"). A sermon that preaches "ambassador" is preaching a conjecture.
- **v. 25 — "our" Lord; "Amen".** The majority adds ἡμῶν ("our") and ἀμήν ("Amen"); NA28 omits both, with א and others.

### Pulpit Notes — where the ESV and the NASB95 diverge in a way that bears on a finding

These are notes for the preacher about what the congregation will hear, not translation verdicts. **The ESV export in the corpus is the ESV (2025) text, not explicitly the Anglicised edition**; the Anglicised edition differs chiefly in spelling and punctuation, and nothing below turns on spelling, but its wording has not been checked against an Anglicised copy `[unverified]`.

- **v. 16 — δοῦλος.** ESV "bondservant"; NASB95 "slave". It is one word, and the same word as in Col 3:22–4:1 (Preaching Trap 2).
- **v. 1 — "brother".** The NASB95 supplies "brother" ("our beloved brother and fellow worker"); the ESV does not. The Greek first calls Philemon "brother" at v. 7 (Echo Table 7).
- **vv. 7, 12, 20 — σπλάγχνα / ἀναπαύω.** ESV "hearts … my very heart … my heart", "refreshed … refresh"; NASB95 the same. **Both translations let the congregation hear v. 20 take up v. 7** (Structural Arc Map; Echo Table 1–2). Read v. 7, v. 12 and v. 20 aloud together.
- **v. 6 / v. 17 — κοινωνία / κοινωνός.** ESV "sharing … partner"; NASB95 "fellowship … partner". The link is lost in both (Echo Table 4); the ESV's "sharing" also makes v. 6 sound like evangelism ("sharing your faith"), a reading the Greek allows but does not require `[I]` — a trap for the 4–7 unit.
- **v. 6 — "in us" / "in you".** Textual, not translational (above): the ESV follows NA28's ἡμῖν ("us"), moderate.
- **v. 6 — εἰς Χριστόν.** ESV "for the sake of Christ"; NASB95 "for Christ's sake". Both read purpose, and the congregation will hear "for Christ's sake". Most of the commentators reported read relation or goal — "into relation with Christ", a variant of ἐν Χριστῷ ("in Christ") (Dunn), "in Christ" (O'Brien), the goal of growth into Christ as in Eph 4:12–13 (Wright) `[S: Logos 1]`; faith with εἰς Χριστόν stands elsewhere in Paul only at Col 2:5 ("your faith in Christ") `[T]`. A preacher on the ESV may want to say what the phrase can mean.
- **v. 11 — the name.** ESV "(Formerly he was useless to you, but now he is indeed useful …)"; NASB95 "who formerly was useless … but now is useful". Neither tells the congregation that "Onesimus" means "useful". Tell them; the pun is the verse's point (Echo Table 18).
- **v. 20 — ὀναίμην.** ESV "I want some benefit from you"; NASB95 "let me benefit from you". The second pun on the name is inaudible in both.
- **v. 10 — ἐγέννησα.** ESV "whose father I became"; NASB95 "whom I have begotten". The NASB95 keeps the verb of 1 Cor 4:15 audible as "begotten"; the ESV paraphrases with "father".
- **v. 22 — χαρισθήσομαι.** ESV "graciously given"; NASB95 "given". **The ESV pulpit text serves the grace frame better than the study text does** (Echo Table 17).
- **v. 1, v. 9 — δέσμιος Χριστοῦ Ἰησοῦ.** ESV "a prisoner for Christ Jesus"; NASB95 "a prisoner of Christ Jesus". The genitive is open; the ESV has decided for cause ("for"), the NASB95 keeps the ambiguity ("of" — perhaps Christ's own prisoner) `[I]`.
- **v. 9 — πρεσβύτης.** ESV "an old man"; NASB95 "the aged". Both follow the manuscripts against the "ambassador" conjecture (above).
- **v. 12 — ἀνέπεμψα.** ESV "I am sending him back"; NASB95 "I have sent him back". The ESV renders the epistolary aorist from the reader's point of view: Onesimus arrives with the letter `[I]`.
- **v. 15 — ἐχωρίσθη.** ESV "was parted from you"; NASB95 "was separated from you". Both keep the passive; neither names an agent (Preaching Trap 5).

## Preaching Units

Philemon will usually be preached in one sermon. Both options are given.

**Option A — a single sermon**

| Verses | Working title | Weight |
|--------|---------------|--------|
| 1–25 | Receive him as you would me | ⭐ HIGH (the whole letter; the slavery landmine; the reconstruction; v. 6) |

**Option B — three sermons**

| Verses | Working title | Weight |
|--------|---------------|--------|
| 1–7 | A faith that refreshes hearts | ⭐ HIGH (v. 6 — the letter's hardest verse, with a variant and a translation question) |
| 8–16 | No longer as a slave, but a beloved brother | ⭐ HIGH (pastoral landmine; "perhaps" and providence; the reconstruction) |
| 17–25 | Charge that to my account | ⭐ HIGH (the Christological analogy and its limit; vv. 19, 21) |

**A companion option.** Philemon can also be preached alongside Colossians, after Col 3:18–4:1 and 4:7–18, since the letters share their envoys and their household. If so, the Colossians household-code sermon should not anticipate the Philemon sermon's conclusions.

The seams at v. 8 (Διό, "therefore") and v. 17 (Εἰ οὖν, "if then") are the text's own `[T]`. NA28 paragraphs 8–14 and 15–20 `[T: as NA28 prints it]`, so the three-sermon split at v. 17 follows Εἰ οὖν but divides NA28's 15–20, and leaves v. 20's return to v. 7 to the third sermon: read v. 7 aloud there `[I]`.
