Book-Level Orientation: The Acts of the Apostles

Purpose of this document: to orient passage-level exegesis of Acts — not to replace it. This is a planning document. It sketches the whole so that when you run dig-deeper on a single pericope, you already know what the book is doing, which Old Testament sources are live, where the passage sits on the arc, and which traps lie in wait. It is a book-overview, not a full sixteen-tool run.

Translation cited: ESV, with the NIV 2011 (Anglicised) as the pulpit text. Confidence flags (high / moderate / uncertain) are used on contested historical and critical judgements. British English throughout.

1. Author, Date, Audience, Occasion

Author. Acts is formally anonymous, but three things are near-certain. First, it is the second volume of a single two-part work: both books are addressed to Theophilus, and Acts 1:1 explicitly refers back to “the first book” about “all that Jesus began to do and teach” (cf. Luke 1:1–4). High confidence: Luke and Acts share one author. Second, the author was at some points a travelling companion of Paul — the first-person “we” passages (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16) slip into eyewitness narration without explanation. Third, church tradition from the second century (the Muratorian Canon, Irenaeus) names that companion as Luke, the physician of Colossians 4:14, Philemon 24, 2 Timothy 4:11. Moderate-to-high confidence: Lukan authorship. The “we” passages are the strongest internal support; the main critical alternatives (a later author using a travel-source, or a literary convention) are possible but less economical.

Date. Genuinely contested; flag as moderate/uncertain and do not build a sermon on a date.

  • Early (~62 AD): Acts ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome, with no mention of his release, trial outcome, death (mid-60s), the Neronian persecution (64), or the fall of Jerusalem (70). The simplest explanation is that Luke wrote up to the present.
  • Middle (~75–85 AD): the majority critical view. If Luke’s Gospel post-dates 70 (many read Luke 19:43–44; 21:20 as reflecting the siege), Acts is later still. Luke’s use of sources and his settled retrospective tone are cited.
  • Late (post-90): a minority; harder to sustain given the apparent independence from Paul’s letters.

The exegetical takeaway: the ending’s silence is a real datum whichever date you hold, and it is better read as theological design (see §4) than merely as a dating clue.

Audience. Named: “most excellent Theophilus” (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1), probably a real patron, possibly a God-fearer or recent convert; the honorific may indicate rank. Luke’s stated aim is that he “may have certainty (asphaleia) concerning the things you have been taught” (Luke 1:4). Beyond Theophilus, the implied readership is a mixed Jewish-and-Gentile Christian community needing assurance — that this movement is the true continuation of Israel’s story, that the Gentile mission is God-authorised, and that the gospel’s collision with the Roman world is not sedition.

Occasion / presenting situation. Read the whole book as answering an anxiety: is this Jewish-messianic movement, now overwhelmingly Gentile, opposed by the synagogue and repeatedly on trial before Rome, actually the fulfilment** of God’s ancient promises — and is it safe to belong to it?** Luke’s answer is a sustained yes, argued historically. Moderate confidence on the precise reconstruction; the assurance-motif itself is high confidence because Luke states it (Luke 1:4).

2. Genre

Acts is ancient historiography — a historical monograph in the Hellenistic tradition, but written by a man soaked in the Greek Old Testament, so its narrative texture constantly echoes Septuagintal historical narrative (the birth stories, Stephen’s speech, the “and it came to pass” rhythms). Reading rules that follow:

  • Speeches are a defining feature — roughly a third of the book. Ancient historians composed speeches appropriate to speaker and occasion; Luke’s are theologically shaped summaries, not verbatim transcripts, yet they are rooted in tradition and are the primary vehicle of the book’s theology (Peter at Pentecost, Stephen, Paul at Athens and Miletus, the defence speeches). Preach them in their narrative setting.
  • The title is later. “The Acts of the Apostles” is a second-century heading. Most of the twelve apostles never appear; the book is really the acts of the risen Christ by his Spirit through chosen witnesses (1:1–2, 8). Let that correct the title.
  • Selective, not exhaustive. Luke narrates representative episodes to carry a thesis, not a complete chronicle.

High confidence on genre; the historiography-plus-Septuagintal-narrative blend is widely recognised.

3. Purpose and Thesis

Three interlocking statements of purpose:

1. Continuation. “In the first book… I have dealt with all that Jesus began to do and teach” (1:1). The implication — Acts is what Jesus continues to do and teach, now from heaven, by the Spirit, through the church. Jesus is the acting subject throughout.

2. The programmatic verse — Acts 1:8. “You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth.” This is the book’s table of contents (see §4). High confidence — the structural payoff is explicit.

3. The unstoppable word under the sovereign plan of God. The gospel advances through, not around, opposition; every setback (persecution, prison, shipwreck, trial) becomes an engine of expansion. God’s boulē (plan/purpose) and the recurring divine dei (“it is necessary”) govern the whole (2:23; 4:28; 5:39; 19:21; 23:11; 27:24). The book closes on the word going out in Rome “without hindrance” — the last word, akōlytōs.

4. Structure and Arc Map

The 1:8 geography is the spine: Jerusalem → Judea and Samaria → the end of the earth.

  • 1–7 — Jerusalem. Ascension and Pentecost; apostolic preaching and the name of Jesus; the Spirit-formed community; mounting opposition; Stephen’s speech and martyrdom (the pivot).
  • 8–12 — Judea, Samaria, and the transitions outward. Philip in Samaria and with the Ethiopian; the conversion of Saul; Peter and Cornelius (the Gentile Pentecost); Antioch; Herod’s death.
  • 13–28 — to the ends of the earth. Paul’s missionary journeys (13–14, 16–20), the Jerusalem Council as the theological hinge (15), the return to Jerusalem, arrest, the defence speeches, the voyage, and Rome.

Pao1 acknowledges the geographical movement, but also shows the theological progress:

Not to be missed, however, is the significance of the language used in this verse. The phrase “when the Holy Spirit comes on you” reflects the language of Isaiah 32:15 (cf. Luke 24:49), a passage that points to the eschatological restoration of Israel. The call to be Jesus’s “witnesses” also finds its closest parallels in the prophecies of Isaiah (43:10, 12) where the eschatological people of God will witness the powerful work of God at the end of time. In light of these references to Isaiah, the references to “Jerusalem,” “all Judea and Samaria,” and “to the ends of the earth” take on added significance. According to the programmatic statement in Isa. 40:1–11, the eschatological era consists of a three-part program: **

(1) the arrival of the salvation of God in Jerusalem (Isa. 40:1–2),

(2) the restoration and reunification of Israel (Isa. 40:9–11), and

(3) the mission to the Gentiles (Isa. 40:3–5).

Here in Acts, “Jerusalem” likewise points to the first step of this program as God fulfils his promises to Israel. The emphasis on “all” Judea and Samaria points to the reunification of Israel, as “Judea” becomes a symbol for the southern kingdom, and “Samaria” for the northern kingdom. When both “Judea and Samaria” accept the gospel message, one witnesses the fulfilment of God’s promises (Isa. 11:13).

Finally, the phrase “to the ends of the earth” appears only four times in the Old Testament (Septuagint), in Isaiah (8:9; 48:20; 49:6; 62:11), where it points to the Gentiles. Therefore, this phrase most likely refers to this ethnic group rather than a precise geographical locale such as Spain or Rome. In the context of Isaiah, this phrase points to yet another stage of God’s work, when he rebuilds his people in the messianic age.

From this discussion, it becomes clear that 1:8 not only provides the geographical ground plan of Acts, but also points to the three stages of the fulfilment of God’s promise to Israel. In this sense, then, Luke is not merely tracing the geographical expansion of the early Christian movement; he is also describing how the events that follow the ascension of Jesus are significant events in God’s redemptive history.

Luke’s own structural seams are the six summary/growth statements that punctuate the book, each roughly closing a panel: 6:7; 9:31; 12:24; 16:5; 19:20; 28:31 — typically “the word of God increased/spread and multiplied.” High confidence: these are deliberate hinges, widely recognised, and they make “the word” (not the apostles) the growing subject.

Peter–Paul parallelism. The book falls into a Peter half (1–12) and a Paul half (13–28), and Luke draws them in parallel with each other and with Jesus in the Gospel: each preaches a programmatic sermon, heals a congenitally lame man (3:1–10; 14:8–10), raises the dead (9:36–42; 20:9–12), confronts a magician (8:18–24; 13:6–11), and is miraculously delivered from prison. This is a literary argument for the unity and legitimacy of the one mission. Moderate-to-high confidence.

The Jerusalem Council (ch. 15) sits at the centre as the theological hinge: the question of whether Gentiles must be circumcised is settled by appeal to God’s action (Peter/Cornelius), the mission’s fruit (Paul and Barnabas), and Scripture (James on Amos 9). Everything before drives towards it; everything after flows from it.

The ending (28:30–31). Deliberately open — Paul preaching the kingdom and teaching about the Lord Jesus “with all boldness and without hindrance.” No verdict, no death, no closure. The mission is not finished; the reader is now inside the story. Read the abruptness as design, not accident.

5. Microscript (the running spine)

The risen Jesus commissions his witnesses and ascends, promising the Spirit and defining the mission geographically (1). At Pentecost the Spirit is poured out and Peter announces that the crucified Jesus has been raised and enthroned as Lord and Christ; thousands repent (2). The name of Jesus heals and provokes, and the apostles preach through mounting opposition while the community shares life (3–5). Conflict over the Hellenist widows produces the Seven; Stephen indicts the nation’s history of resisting God and is killed, and the scattering that follows carries the word outward (6–7). Philip evangelises Samaria and an African official; the arch-persecutor Saul is confronted by the risen Christ and commissioned to the Gentiles; Peter is shown by God that the Gentile Cornelius receives the same Spirit — so the mission crosses the decisive boundary (8–11). Herod’s persecution fails and dies with him; the word keeps growing (12). From Antioch, Paul and Barnabas are sent; the gospel goes to Jew and Gentile across Asia Minor, and the Council settles that Gentiles are saved by grace apart from the law (13–15). Paul crosses to Europe, plants churches, reasons in synagogue and marketplace from Philippi to Athens to Corinth to Ephesus, always through opposition (16–20). Set on Jerusalem, he is arrested, defends the resurrection hope before Jewish and Roman authorities, appeals to Caesar, survives storm and shipwreck, and reaches Rome — where, a prisoner, he proclaims the kingdom unhindered (21–28).

6. Intertextual / Old Testament Map

Acts is saturated with the Greek Old Testament. For dig-deeper Tool 11 (Move 2, book-usage tracking), these are the sources already live in the book, so a later citation must be read against earlier ones:

  • Joel 2:28–32 → Acts 2:17–21 (Pentecost): the “last days,” the Spirit poured out on all flesh, “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved.” Sets the eschatological frame for the whole book. High confidence.
  • Psalm 16:8–11 → Acts 2:25–28 (and echoed 13:35): “you will not let your Holy One see corruption” — read as pointing beyond David to the resurrection. High confidence.
  • Psalm 110:1 → Acts 2:34–35: “Sit at my right hand” — the ascension/enthronement proof-text. High confidence.
  • 2 Samuel 7 / Psalm 132 (Davidic covenant) → Acts 2:30; 13:22–23, 34: God swore to set David’s seed on the throne. High confidence.
  • Psalm 2 → Acts 4:25–26 (nations rage) and 13:33 (“you are my Son, today I have begotten you”). High confidence.
  • Psalm 118:22 → Acts 4:11: the stone the builders rejected. High confidence.
  • Deuteronomy 18:15–19 (the prophet like Moses) → Acts 3:22–23; 7:37. High confidence — links Jesus and the Mosaic-deliverer typology that structures Stephen’s speech.
  • Genesis 12/22 Abrahamic promise (“in your offspring all the families/nations shall be blessed”) → Acts 3:25; and the whole of ch. 7. Undergirds the Gentile mission. High confidence.
  • Isaiah 53:7–8 → Acts 8:32–33 (the Ethiopian reads the Servant song). High confidence.
  • Isaiah 49:6 (“a light for the nations… to the end of the earth”) → Acts 13:47; and it stands behind 1:8 itself. High/moderate confidence on the 1:8 echo.
  • Amos 9:11–12 (LXX) → Acts 15:16–18 (James at the Council): the rebuilt tent of David “that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord.” The Septuagint form is doing load-bearing work here — worth its own Move 1/Move 3 in any run on ch. 15. High confidence that the LXX form matters.
  • Habakkuk 1:5 → Acts 13:41 (Pisidian Antioch warning). High confidence.
  • Isaiah 6:9–10 → Acts 28:26–27: the closing citation of judicial hardening — Luke ends the book on the same text that frames Jesus’ parables. Structurally weighty. High confidence.
  • Stephen’s speech (ch. 7) is a compressed retelling of Genesis–Exodus–wilderness–tabernacle/temple, arguing that Israel has always resisted God’s appointed deliverers and misplaced its confidence in land and temple. Treat the whole chapter as an intertextual argument, not a history lesson.

7. Biblical-Theological Themes

  • The Holy Spirit. The dominant actor. The promise of the Father (1:4–5), the power for witness (1:8), poured out at Pentecost and at each frontier (Samaria 8, Gentiles 10, Ephesus 19), directing and even redirecting the mission (8:29; 10:19; 13:2; 16:6–7). Acts is, rightly, “the Acts of the Holy Spirit.”
  • The Word of God that grows. The recurring subject of verbs of increase (6:7; 12:24; 19:20). The church is built by proclamation; “the word” almost becomes a character.
  • The plan and necessity of God. Boulē (2:23; 4:28; 20:27) and the divine dei (1:16; 3:21; 9:16; 17:3; 23:11; 27:24). History, the cross, and the mission unfold under God’s sovereign purpose.
  • Witness to the resurrection. Martys language throughout; apostolic testimony to the risen Christ is the engine of every sermon.
  • The kingdom of God. An inclusio: Jesus teaches it for forty days (1:3) and Paul preaches it in the last verse (28:31). The kingdom is present in the reign of the enthroned Christ.
  • Salvation and the name. “There is salvation in no one else” (4:12); repentance, forgiveness, baptism, and the gift of the Spirit; the movement called “the Way.”
  • Israel and the nations. Continuity and fulfilment, not simple replacement: the restoration hope (1:6; 3:19–21) and the ingathering of the Gentiles as the promised blessing to the nations; God shows no partiality (10:34–35).
  • The church. Koinōnia, shared possessions, prayer, the breaking of bread, teaching, generosity, suffering, and developing leadership (the Twelve, the Seven, elders).
  • Suffering and opposition as the road of advance. Persecution scatters the word (8:1–4); prison becomes pulpit; the pattern of Jewish rejection and Gentile reception recurs.

8. Christological Trajectory

Acts preaches a living, reigning Christ. Jesus is the risen and ascended Lord who pours out the Spirit and acts through his witnesses — so the book is the continuing “acts of Jesus” (1:1). He is Lord (*kyrios*) and Christ enthroned at God’s right hand (2:36), the Davidic king whose throne is now heaven, the prophet like Moses (3:22), the suffering Servant (8:32–35), the Author/Prince of life (3:15), the appointed Judge of the living and the dead (10:42; 17:31), and the one name under heaven for salvation (4:12). He is so united to his people that persecuting them is persecuting him (“Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” 9:4). The resurrection is the hinge of every apostolic sermon. For preaching Acts: the hero is never Peter or Paul but the ascended Lord working by his Spirit — keep the sermons Christ-centred, not apostle-centred.

9. Preaching Traps

  • The template fallacy (the biggest one). Reading narrative episodes as normative blueprints for church practice — tongues at conversion, community of goods, apostolic sign-gifts, casting lots, “tarrying” for the Spirit. Apply Tool 13 (Copycat): distinguish descriptive from prescriptive, and watch for once-for-all salvation-historical events that are not meant to repeat.
  • Making the apostles the heroes to imitate. Moralising (“be bold like Peter,” “be a missionary like Paul”) displaces the risen Christ as the agent and turns grace-narrative into exemplarism.
  • Flattening the Spirit-transitions into an *ordo salutis*. Pentecost (2), Samaria (8), Cornelius (10), and the Ephesian disciples (19) mark distinct frontier moments in salvation history; harmonising them into a single repeatable pattern of “how to receive the Spirit” misreads Luke’s design.
  • Triumphalism / prosperity. The advance runs through prison, riot, shipwreck, and trial. Any reading that promises unbroken success has not read to the end of the chapter.
  • Detaching the speeches from their settings. Mining a sermon for doctrine while ignoring who is speaking, to whom, and why, loses Luke’s rhetorical point (e.g. Athens is not a template for “natural theology” abstracted from its idol-filled setting).
  • Mining incidental detail for lessons. Eutychus, the viper on Malta, the sailors’ tackle — narrative colour, not allegory.
  • Supersessionism vs. Luke’s nuance. The book neither writes off Israel nor collapses the church into a law-keeping remnant; hold the tension the Council holds.

10. Weighted Pericope Map

A suggested preaching division with weight. ⭐ HIGH = run a full solo dig-deeper before the pulpit; Standard = proportional depth. Divisions are indicative; adjust to your series length.

Jerusalem (1–7)

  • ⭐ 1:1–11 — Ascension and commission; the programmatic 1:8.
  • 1:12–26 — Waiting and the replacement of Judas (Ps 69/109; use of lots).
  • ⭐ 2:1–41 — Pentecost and Peter’s sermon (Joel, Ps 16, Ps 110). Foundational.
  • 2:42–47 — The Spirit-formed community (first summary).
  • 3:1–26 — The lame man healed; the prophet-like-Moses sermon.
  • 4:1–31 — First arrest; the boldness prayer (Ps 2).
  • 4:32–5:11 — Barnabas, and Ananias and Sapphira.
  • 5:12–42 — Apostles before the Sanhedrin; Gamaliel.
  • 6:1–7 — The Seven; the word spreads (summary seam).
  • ⭐ 6:8–7:60 — Stephen’s speech and martyrdom (the pivot outward).

Judea, Samaria, transitions (8–12)

  • 8:1–25 — Philip in Samaria; Simon Magus; the Samaritan Spirit-transition.
  • ⭐ 8:26–40 — The Ethiopian eunuch (Isaiah 53).
  • ⭐ 9:1–31 — The conversion/commission of Saul.
  • 9:32–43 — Aeneas and Dorcas (Peter’s Elijah/Elisha echoes).
  • ⭐ 10:1–11:18 — Cornelius: the Gentile Pentecost and God’s impartiality. Decisive.
  • 11:19–30 — Antioch; “Christians”; famine relief.
  • 12:1–25 — Herod, Peter’s deliverance, and the word that grows (summary seam).

To the ends of the earth (13–28)

  • ⭐ 13:1–52 — Antioch commissioning; Paul’s Pisidian sermon (Ps 2, Isa 49, Hab 1).
  • 14:1–28 — Iconium, Lystra (mistaken for gods), Derbe; strengthening the churches.
  • ⭐ 15:1–35 — The Jerusalem Council (Amos 9 LXX). Theological hinge.
  • 15:36–16:15 — The split with Barnabas; the Macedonian call; Lydia.
  • 16:16–40 — Philippi: exorcism, prison, the jailer, Roman citizenship.
  • ⭐ 17:16–34 — Paul at the Areopagus (creation, idolatry, resurrection).
  • 18:1–17 — Corinth; Gallio; the assurance vision.
  • 18:18–19:41 — Ephesus: the disciples of John, the sons of Sceva, the riot; the word prevails (summary seam at 19:20).
  • ⭐ 20:17–38 — The Miletus farewell to the Ephesian elders (the one sustained address to Christians).
  • 21:1–36 — Arrival at Jerusalem and arrest.
  • 21:37–23:11 — Defence to the crowd and Sanhedrin; the Lord’s “take courage.”
  • 23:12–26:32 — Before Felix, Festus, and Agrippa; the appeal to Caesar; the conversion retold.
  • ⭐ 27:1–28:16 — The voyage and shipwreck; providence and mission.
  • ⭐ 28:17–31 — Rome: Isaiah 6 and the unhindered word (the open ending).

11. How to Use This With dig-deeper

When you run dig-deeper on any Acts pericope, this document is the book-overview to hold in peripheral vision (its Phase 0.5 “active layer”):

  • Christological trajectory — §8.
  • Intertextual map — §6 (this is your Tool 11 Move 2 input: check which OT sources are already live before you reach your passage).
  • Preaching traps — §9 (watch for the passage-level manifestation, above all the template fallacy).
  • Presenting situation — §1 (assurance; the legitimacy of the Gentile mission; the movement before Rome).

Start each run text-first, then let the passage confirm, sharpen, or correct this overview — and flag it where it does. For any ⭐ HIGH passage, run a full solo dig-deeper before pulpit preparation rather than relying on sweep-level depth.

12. Confidence and Limitations

This is a book-level orientation produced from the text of Acts and standard reference knowledge, not a sixteen-tool run and not a substitute for one. Authorship is moderate-to-high confidence (Lukan); date is contested — moderate/uncertain, and no exegetical weight should rest on it. The structural seams (§4), the 1:8 programme, and the major OT citations (§6) are high confidence. The Peter–Paul parallelism and the precise reconstruction of the presenting situation are moderate. Verify any wording-dependent point against the actual text of the passage before it reaches the pulpit, and check the pulpit NIV 2011 (Anglicised) where a finding hangs on ESV wording.
