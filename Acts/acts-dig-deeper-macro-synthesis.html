Acts — Dig-Deeper Macro-Synthesis (Acts 18:23–26:32)

What the six full solo /dig-deeper runs over Acts 18:23–26:32, taken together, demonstrate about the last third of the book — the cross-pericope arguments invisible at the level of a single passage but unmistakable once the runs are laid side by side. Companion to the Acts Book Overview (v2.5) and the Acts dig-deeper reports. Text-first; confidence-flagged.

Date: 19 November 2026

Scope, and why it is not the whole book

The book-wide gate fails, decisively. Acts has 33 preaching units, 14 of them ⭐ HIGH. Eight ⭐ HIGH units still have no solo run — Stephen (6:8–7:60), the Ethiopian eunuch (8:26–40), Cornelius (10:1–11:18), the Pisidian sermon (13:1–52), the Jerusalem Council (15:1–35, which has a claim audit but no run), the Areopagus (17:16–34), the voyage (27:1–28:16) and Rome (28:17–31). The mode’s prerequisite — all or nearly all pericopes in hand, and no missing HIGH-weight unit — is nowhere near met for the book.

Scoped instead to Acts 18:23–26:32, where the gate is met on the scope’s own terms:

§ Unit Weight Run
§1 18:23–19:20 — Ephesus: disciples, Sceva, the scrolls Standard ✓
§2 19:21–20:1 — “I must see Rome”; the riot Standard ✓
— 20:2–16 — Macedonia, Troas, Eutychus Standard gap
§3 20:17–38 — the Miletus farewell ⭐ HIGH ✓
§4 21:1–36 — arrival at Jerusalem and arrest ⭐ HIGH ✓
§5 21:37–23:11 — defence to the crowd and the Sanhedrin ⭐ HIGH ✓
§6 23:12–26:32 — Felix, Festus, Agrippa, the appeal Standard ✓

Six runs, one missing Standard-weight unit, and all three ⭐ HIGH units inside the scope covered — which is exactly the tolerance the mode allows. This is a wider scope than the 18:23–21:36 the overview recommended in September, because two further runs have landed since.

Two further resources are consulted but do not count toward the gate: the runs on 1:1–11 and 2:1–41, where a thread reaches back into them, and the authorial-intent study of the three Saul-conversion accounts (9; 22; 26) filed 19 November, which is a cross-passage study rather than a solo dig.

Everything below is therefore a claim about the last third of Acts, not about Acts. Where a thread plainly runs the length of the book, it is marked as reaching outside the scope and its early anchors are named — but the synthesis cannot and does not establish book-wide patterns, and should be re-run when the missing ⭐ units are worked.

How to read this document

Each macro-argument recurs across at least three of the six runs; threads resting on one or two are listed at the end as candidates, not yet macro. These are emergent — they are what the runs found, clustered afterwards, not a grid laid over them. The runs’ own warrant tags are carried forward unchanged.

Confidence key: NT-authorised = high · converging text-first findings reached independently in several runs = strong · looser trajectory = moderate.

1. Paul turns from binder into bound, and the Spirit binds him first

Claim. The last third of Acts is organised around a reversal in a single verb: the man who went to Damascus to bind becomes the man who is bound, and Luke gives the first binding to the Holy Spirit before any human hand touches him.

Converging evidence.

  • δέω in the sense “bind” stands twelve times in Acts — 9:2, 9:14, 9:21, 12:6, 20:22, 21:11 ×2, 21:13, 21:33, 22:5, 22:29, 24:27 — separated from the 24 occurrences of δεῖ, which MorphGNT files under the same lemma (§3) [T]
  • 20:22 is the pivot: δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι, the Spirit binding him before Jerusalem does (§3) [T]
  • The chain runs on through Agabus’s belt and Paul’s answer (21:11, 21:13), the arrest (21:33) (§4) [T]
  • 22:29–30 is the only place in Acts where Paul is unbound — and it happens because he is a Roman, three verses after he was bound because nobody could say who he was (§5) [T]
  • 24:27 leaves him bound for two years; 26:29 ends the last speech παρεκτὸς τῶν δεσμῶν τούτων (§6) [T]
  • Luke lets Paul recite his own earlier role at 22:5 while he is in chains (§5) [T]

Confidence: strong (every distribution [T] and lemma-verified; the design is [I] at moderate–high in the runs that found it, and four runs found it independently).

Payoff. The chain is the strongest single answer to the “be bold like Paul” trap, because it makes Paul the object of the book’s main verb through the whole last third. And it reaches back outside the scope: the Saul study found that eight of Acts’ nine occurrences of διώκω stand in the three conversion accounts, the ninth being Stephen’s charge at 7:52 — the same reversal in a different word, and planted thirteen chapters earlier.

2. The Rome-ward δεῖ takes over as the structural device, in consecutive verses

Claim. Luke’s summary-notices are exhausted at 19:20 and he changes marking device rather than stopping; three Rome-ward δεῖ statements carry the panels from there to the end.

Converging evidence.

  • Every summary-notice device in Acts — αὐξάνω, πληθύνω, προστίθημι, στερεόω, περισσεύω — is exhausted by 19:20, which §1 reaches as the climax of its own unit and treats as the panel’s close [T]
  • 19:21 — δεῖ με καὶ Ῥώμην ἰδεῖν — is §2’s second headline finding, reached independently as “the programmatic hinge of the whole second half”, planted here and executed in ch. 28 [T]
  • 23:11 is the second, and §5 shows it is a janus: ὡς … διεμαρτύρω … εἰς Ἰερουσαλήμ closes the preaching unit, οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην opens the movement (§5) [I], high
  • §6 adds that 23:11 is the only divine action narrated in the whole of M8 — the narrator does not name God again until 27:35, nor the Lord until 28:31, nor the Spirit from 21:11 to 28:25 [T], moderate (absence cap)
  • The contest is explicit: the Lord’s δεῖ stands against the crowd’s μὴ δεῖν αὐτὸν ζῆν μηκέτι (25:24) and against the persecutor’s old self-generated ἔδοξα ἐμαυτῷ … δεῖν (26:9) (§6) [T]

Confidence: strong (three runs, each reaching the device from inside its own unit; the handover at 19:20/19:21 is [T] on both verses).

Payoff. The device decides how the series is divided. It also supplies the one thing a preacher most needs in chs. 21–28: the reason the narrative does not sag when Paul stops travelling and starts standing trial. Nothing is happening to Paul that the book has not already said must happen.

3. The officials cannot obtain the certainty the reader was promised — and they misreport, while Paul’s accounts check out

Claim. Luke runs his preface’s own vocabulary through the trial narrative and lets it fail in the officials’ hands, while the reader holds what they cannot get.

Converging evidence.

  • Luke 1:4 promises ἀσφάλεια about things κατηχήθης. Both words recur at 21:21 and 21:24 (the report about Paul is false) and 21:34 (the tribune cannot reach τὸ ἀσφαλές for the uproar) (§4) [T]
  • §5 shows the tribune’s three failed attempts in seven verses — ἵνα ἐπιγνῷ (22:24), ἐπιγνούς (22:29), γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλές (22:30) — and that the one fact he obtains is Paul’s citizenship, not the charge [T]
  • Festus ends the sequence: περὶ οὗ ἀσφαλές τι γράψαι τῷ κυρίῳ οὐκ ἔχω (25:26) (§6) [T]
  • The officials’ own accounts are shown to be wrong: Lysias writes that he rescued Paul “having learned he was a Roman” (23:27) when the narrative showed otherwise; Festus tells Agrippa he was “at a loss” (25:20) when the narrator said he wanted to do the Jews a favour (§6) [T]
  • Paul’s accounts are shown to be right: 24:17–18 recites the temple scene in three lemmata whose only other Acts occurrences are in 21:24–34 (§4, §6) [T]
  • κράτιστος stands four times in the New Testament — Luke 1:3 of Theophilus, then Felix twice and Festus (§6) [T]. The governors hold the reader’s title and lack the reader’s certainty.

Confidence: strong on the distributions, moderate–high on the irony being designed.

Payoff, and an honesty note. This is the thread that most repays preaching the trials as a unit. But two Logos rounds have now failed to corroborate the design reading: commentators note what ἀσφάλεια means in the preface and group the Acts occurrences lexically, and one reference work assigns them different senses. No treatment of the thread as one designed irony has been located, and none has been shown not to exist. It stands on its distributions as the project’s own observation, and should be offered as such.

4. Paul’s passion is narrated in the vocabulary of Jesus’ — as trajectory and contrast, never as typology

Claim. Luke tells the arrest, the hearings and the acquittals in wording drawn from his own Gospel’s passion, and the runs are unanimous that the relation is parallel-and-contrast, not type-and-antitype.

Converging evidence.

  • §4: ἀκολουθέω + τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ stands in two New Testament verses, Luke 23:27 and Acts 21:36 — “and the parallel exists in order to invert”; Agabus’s oracle fuses Luke’s two passion-prediction forms [T]
  • §5: the crowd’s Αἶρε ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς (22:22) is the Servant’s own phrase from the Isaiah 53:8 citation at 8:33 — the only two in the New Testament; and 23:9’s acquittal is Pilate’s formula from Luke 23:4, 14 [T]
  • §6: Luke 23 is the template of the whole trial narrative — ἀναπέμπω (Luke 23:7, 11, 15 and Acts 25:21), ἄξιος + θάνατος + πράσσω, ἄτοπος, ἀνακρίνω, a Herodian king and a governor sitting together to acquit [T], high
  • The contrast is the point, and §6 states it: Pilate παρέδωκεν τῷ θελήματι αὐτῶν; Festus does not, because οὐκ ἔστιν ἔθος Ῥωμαίοις χαρίζεσθαί τινα ἄνθρωπον (25:16) [I], high

Confidence: strong overall; high on §6’s distributions.

Payoff. The runs supply their own guard-rail, and it should go into the series unchanged: the servant’s road runs where the master’s ran, and the servant does not do the master’s work. Every parallel here fails the escalation test deliberately — Paul does not exceed the Servant, and the four acquittals exist to show that what happened to Jesus did not happen to Paul.

5. The Servant’s commission is transferred to Paul — and it runs through the prophetic call as well

Claim. The Isaianic Servant material moves outward through the book onto Paul, and the runs show the transfer carries the prophets’ commissioning language with it.

Converging evidence.

  • §3: Acts 20:28 sits inside a cluster of Isaianic redemption texts, not chiefly Ezekiel 34 — Isa 43:21 (the Greek Old Testament’s only middle-aorist περιποιέομαι with God acquiring his people, high), Isa 63:9–11 supplying φείδομαι, τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον twice, the shepherd, and τίθημι governing the Spirit (moderate–high) [T] on the data
  • §3 also corrects the map: Ezekiel 34 LXX contains no wolf; the wolves come from Ezek 22:27 / Zeph 3:3 / Jer 5:6 [T]
  • §6: 26:16–18 recites Ezekiel 2:1 (στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου, once in Swete), Jeremiah 1:7–8 (ἐξαιρούμενός σε … εἰς οὓς ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε), and Isaiah 42:7, 16 (ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμούς and τὸ σκότος εἰς φῶς, each once in Swete) — with the scribal τυφλῶν at 26:18 restoring Isaiah’s own word [T]
  • The Saul study adds the chain’s end: φῶς stands in ten verses of Acts and seven are in the three conversion accounts; of the remaining three, the only other theological use is 13:47, Isaiah 49:6 — which 26:23 joins to the Damascus light in one sentence [T]

Confidence: strong, with high on Isaiah 42 and Ezekiel 2 and moderate on Isaiah 60:17.

Payoff, and a correction carried forward. §6’s proposal stands: the overview’s “transferred commission” should be nuanced — the transfer runs through the prophetic office as well as the Servant figure. And the Isaiah 60:17 link behind 20:28 has now been put to the library twice, in October and November, and corroborated neither time; it should be labelled an uncorroborated allusion wherever it appears.

6. The late units are composed out of the book’s own earlier material

Claim. From Miletus onward Luke builds his text mainly by reaching back into Acts and his Gospel, not by citing the Old Testament — and the density of that reaching is the chief literary fact about the last third.

Converging evidence.

  • §3: the Miletus speech is assembled out of Acts 13–14 vocabulary — seven links, five of them lemmata with two or three occurrences in the whole book, including ὁ λόγος τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ verbatim at 14:3 and 20:32 [T]
  • §4: 26 Move-4 rows, the densest internal-echo load in the book at the time, with six Gospel echoes that are verbally unique pairs [T]
  • §5: 29 Move-4 rows, 24 new, of which eleven reach into the Stephen complex of chapters 6–8 — ἀνατρέφω, the three-verb résumé of 7:20–22, παρὰ τοὺς πόδας (7:58), συνευδοκέω (8:1), ὁ δίκαιος (7:52) [T]
  • §6: 37 rows, 29 new, including Felix as an inverted Cornelius (Caesarea, μεταπέμπομαι, ἔμφοβος γενόμενος — twice in Acts only, 10:4 and 24:25 — alms against money) [T]
  • The Saul study is the same method at book scale: the three tellings of one event, with the invariant core and everything else moving [T]

Confidence: strong (four runs, each measuring it independently).

Payoff, with a finding that cuts against an earlier recommendation. The overview advised planning solo runs in contiguous pairs, because contiguity makes near-range echo visible. §5 tested that and it failed: though contiguous with §4, it produced almost no within-movement echoes — its echoes run long, to M1, M2, M3, M4, M5, M6 and the Gospel. §6 then found five within-movement rows, all between narrative and speech. The refined rule the runs actually support is §6’s: near-range echo needs narrative on at least one end — which is a statement about genre, not about contiguity. Depth is what matters; contiguity is incidental.

7. Absence of a citation formula is not absence of a source

Claim. The last third of Acts quotes almost nothing and alludes constantly, and five runs independently found substantial Old Testament substrata where no formula marks them.

Converging evidence.

  • §1: the major seam at 19:20 carries no OT quotation at all; the unit’s intertextual weight is entirely internal [T]
  • §3: the Isaianic cluster behind 20:28 was found only by searching the LXX, not by following formulae [T]
  • §4: Acts 21:23–27 is written in the technical vocabulary of Numbers 6 LXX — six items including the NT hapax ἁγνισμός and Num 6:5’s ἡμέραι τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ verbatim — with no formula, and Numbers otherwise unused in Acts [T], high on the function
  • §5: the Isaiah 53:8 echo at 22:22 is a phrase, not a citation; and 23:5 is the last γέγραπται in Acts [T]
  • §6: there is no formal citation anywhere in 23:12–26:32, and the unit nevertheless yields Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Isaiah 42, Isaiah 49, Isaiah 60 and Daniel 12 candidates [T]

Confidence: strong — five of six runs, and it is the methodological finding with the widest base in the scope.

Payoff. This is the sweep correcting the overview rather than confirming it. The overview’s rule of thumb — that the late Pauline material is citation-light and should lean on Move 4 rather than Move 2 — is right about citations and wrong about sources. A run on this material that skips the LXX work because it sees no formula will miss the best evidence in the unit. The honesty note is already in the overview; this synthesis is the demonstration that it earns its place.

8. Where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied

Claim. Repeatedly in this stretch, the feature the exegesis turns on is precisely the feature scribes smoothed — so scribal discomfort is external evidence that a reading is marked.

Converging evidence.

  • §3: the Byzantine tradition normalises τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου at 20:28 toward the commoner idiom [T]
  • §4: D removes λαός at 21:35–36, which is the very alternation the exegesis identified as load-bearing [T]
  • §5: three of five significant variants harmonise toward another part of Acts (22:7 ← 26:14; 22:20 ← 8:1; 21:39 ← 22:3) and a fourth completes an implicit echo (23:9 ← 5:39) — and three of those four places were identified from the lemma index before the apparatus was opened [T]
  • §6: seven further instances, two of a new kind — the tradition supplying what Luke withholds (a closure at 26:32; the Holy Spirit at 26:1) [T]
  • Outside the scope, the Acts 2 run adds a fifth class: B, C, 076 and the Sahidic correcting Luke back to the Septuagint at 2:17 [T]

Confidence: strong, and now externally corroborated — the November Logos round found the inference stated in a commentary: the Byzantine normalisation at 20:28 “functions as indirect evidence that the unusual word order in earlier witnesses was intentional rather than accidental” [S: Trites and Larkin, *Cornerstone* 12:574].

Payoff. The rule can be promoted from the project’s working observation to corroborated method. Its most useful form for a preacher: a variant is not only a question about what Luke wrote, it is early evidence about what his first readers found strange.

9. The acting subject is the word, the Lord or the Spirit — and in the last movement Luke achieves it by silence

Claim. The runs are unanimous that the missionaries are not the agents of the narrative, and §6 shows Luke securing the point in the trials by a device the earlier units did not need.

Converging evidence.

  • §1: the seam’s subject is the word; the ἰσχύω catchword binds the demoniac’s overpowering of the exorcists (19:16) to the word’s prevailing (19:20); κατὰ κράτος is conquest language [T]
  • §2: the word prevails without Paul lifting a finger — he is not even in the theatre — and the Way is vindicated by the mouth of a pagan town clerk [T]
  • §3: everything the speech says about the future belongs to God or the flock; everything about the past belongs to Paul. The Spirit made them overseers (20:28) [T], high — “this is [T] from the grammar”
  • §4: the Spirit’s information is consistent and every human inference from it is partial or wrong — the Tyrians’, the companions’, James’s, the crowd’s, the tribune’s [T]
  • §6: the narrator falls silent about God. God is not named from 23:11 to 27:35, the Lord not until 28:31, the Spirit not from 21:11 to 28:25 — and 23:11 is the only divine action narrated in M8 [T], moderate (absence cap)

Confidence: strong on the positive evidence; moderate on §6’s silence, which is capped as an absence claim.

Payoff. This is the series’ guard against its biggest trap, and the shape is better than the usual corrective. It is not merely that Luke declines to make Paul a hero; it is that as Paul loses his freedom, Luke progressively withdraws the narrator’s own reports of divine action, so that the last movement rests on a single word spoken in a barracks at night. The preacher who supplies God’s activity in chs. 24–26 where Luke withholds it is undoing the design.

Candidate threads — not yet macro

Each rests on one or two runs and fails the ≥3 gate. Listed so they are not lost, and so a later run can promote them.

  • The last speech speaks in the words of the first mission (§3 only). Striking, and the evidence is [T] throughout, but it is a fact about one speech until another unit shows the same reach-back.
  • Judas and Paul as Acts’ two men who “received a ministry” (§3 only) — five lexical pairs, each with two occurrences in Acts.
  • Three citizenships in three scenes, the third invisible in English (§5 only) — πολίτης 21:39, πολιτεία 22:28, πολιτεύομαι 23:1.
  • Felix as an inverted Cornelius (§6 only) — high on its data, but a single pairing.
  • Why ἀπόστολος stops at 16:4 (§4, partial). §4 established the omission is systematic — 21:18 has James and all the elders and no apostle, as 20:17–28 has elders and overseers and none — without explaining why. This is the most promising candidate, and the units that would settle it (15:1–35, 16:1–15) are outside the scope and unrun.

What this means for the series as a whole

Preached as a unit, Acts 18:23–26:32 is not a long anticlimax in which the mission stops and the legal process takes over. It is the book’s argument changing instrument. The word that grew and prevailed through six panels stops being counted in summary-notices and starts being carried by a divine must; the apostle who bound others is bound, by the Spirit before anyone else; the officials who must decide his case are shown reaching repeatedly for a certainty they never obtain, while the reader — who was promised exactly that certainty in the Gospel’s first sentence — watches them fail and watches Paul’s own account check out every time. Luke writes the whole of it out of his own earlier pages rather than out of proof-texts, and as the trials close in he withdraws even his narrator’s reports of God’s activity, so that the movement rests on one sentence spoken at night in a barracks. The cumulative effect is an apologetic that never argues: four acquittals, no verdict, a man in chains and a word that is not. A series over this stretch should let the instrument-change be audible, and should resist, at every unit, the sermon that makes Paul the subject of the sentence.
