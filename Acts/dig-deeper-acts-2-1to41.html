Headline Findings

1. Luke replaces Joel’s opening phrase with a phrase that occurs once in the whole Greek Old Testament — and it is Isaiah’s oracle about the nations coming to Jerusalem. Joel 2:28 LXX (Swete) begins Καὶ ἔσται μετὰ ταῦτα; Acts 2:17 begins Καὶ ἔσται ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις. A search of the full Swete corpus for that exact phrase returns exactly one hit: Isaiah 2:2 — ἔσται ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις ἐμφανὲς τὸ ὄρος Κυρίου … καὶ ἥξουσιν ἐπ’ αὐτὸ πάντα τὰ ἔθνη, with 2:3 adding ἐκ Σειὼν ἐξελεύσεται νόμος, καὶ λόγος Κυρίου ἐξ Ἰερουσαλήμ. [T] on the uniqueness. Luke alters a text he is quoting, and the replacement carries the one Old Testament phrase whose home oracle is “all the nations come to Jerusalem and the word goes out from it” — in a scene where Jews from every nation under heaven are in Jerusalem hearing God’s mighty works, at the head of a book whose programme is the word going out from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth. [I], moderate–high, and raised by the overview’s finding that Isaiah is the book’s most-used source (v2.3: ten uses across seven texts). Flagged for Logos — the phrase-change is universally noticed; whether the Isaiah 2:2 provenance is, I do not know. Corroborated from the apparatus after this run was first written: B, C, 076 and the Sahidic read μετὰ ταῦτα at 2:17, correcting Luke back to the Septuagint [T]. The tradition felt the substitution and undid it, which settles that it is Luke’s own and that it was conspicuous. See Textual Variants (c).

2. Peter cuts the Joel citation one clause early, and deploys the cut clause eighteen verses later with its limit removed. The quotation stops at Joel 2:32a (πᾶς ὃς ἂν ἐπικαλέσηται τὸ ὄνομα κυρίου σωθήσεται). Joel 2:32b continues: ὅτι ἐν τῷ ὄρει Σειὼν καὶ ἐν Ἰερουσαλὴμ ἔσται ἀνασωζόμενος … καὶ εὐαγγελιζόμενοι οὓς Κύριος προσκέκληται. At 2:39 Peter says πᾶσι τοῖς εἰς μακρὰν ὅσους ἂν προσκαλέσηται κύριος ὁ θεὸς ἡμῶν — Joel’s uncited verb, with Joel’s geographical limit (Zion and Jerusalem) replaced by τοῖς εἰς μακράν, the phrase of Isaiah 57:19. [T] on the verb and the omission. And προσκαλέομαι then carries the Gentile mission: 13:2 (εἰς τὸ ἔργον ὃ προσκέκλημαι αὐτούς) and 16:10 (προσκέκληται ἡμᾶς ὁ θεὸς εὐαγγελίσασθαι αὐτούς) [T]. The word by which Acts describes God’s call to the nations is a word Peter takes from the part of Joel he did not read out.

3. The Babel reading passes a real test, and the test is worth showing. συγχέω occurs five times in the NT, all in Acts, and 2:6 is the first [T]. In the LXX the verb-group appears in sixteen lines, and Genesis 11 is the only place it is used of language [T], full-corpus search; Wisdom 10:5 (ἐθνῶν συγχυθέντων) shows the verb had become shorthand for Babel [T]. The overlap is not one word but four: Gen 11:7 συγχέωμεν … ἵνα μὴ ἀκούσωσιν ἕκαστος τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ πλησίον against Acts 2:6 γενομένης τῆς φωνῆς ταύτης … συνεχύθη, ὅτι ἤκουον εἷς ἕκαστος. Genesis 11:7’s negated purpose is Acts 2:6’s realised fact. And the inversion is exact: at Babel God confuses so that they cannot hear; at Pentecost the crowd is confused because they do hear. High confidence.

4. But Joel 2:1 is a live rival for the same verb, and both should be preached. συγχυθήτωσαν πάντες οἱ κατοικοῦντες τὴν γῆν, διότι πάρεστιν ἡμέρα Κυρίου (Joel 2:1); συγχυθήσεται ἡ γῆ (Joel 2:10). Acts 2:5 has ἐν Ἰερουσαλὴμ κατοικοῦντες, 2:6 συνεχύθη [T] — and Peter quotes Joel eleven verses later. So the crowd’s confusion may be Babel’s, or the day-of-the-Lord alarm Joel commands, or both. The honest reading is both, and it is better than either alone: the languages come from Genesis 11, the alarm from Joel 2, and Peter’s citation confirms Joel is in the room. [I], moderate on the Joel 2:1 strand.

5. Acts 2:36 is where Luke’s two-volume purpose statement is delivered — and the previous run showed where it is denied. Luke 1:4: ἵνα ἐπιγνῷς … τὴν ἀσφάλειαν. Acts 2:36: ἀσφαλῶς οὖν γινωσκέτω πᾶς οἶκος Ἰσραήλ. The root then vanishes from the preaching and reappears only in Roman hands, where it is never obtained — 21:34, 22:30, 25:26 [T]. The certainty Luke promised his reader is delivered in the first sermon and withheld from every magistrate who later tries to establish it. This is the pay-off of the 21:1–36 run read from the other end.

6. ἀποφθέγγομαι occurs three times in the New Testament, all in Acts, and in two of the three the hearers say the speaker is out of his senses. 2:4 (the Spirit gives them ἀποφθέγγεσθαι), 2:14 (Peter ἀπεφθέγξατο), 26:25 (Paul: ἀληθείας καὶ σωφροσύνης ῥήματα ἀποφθέγγομαι) [T]. At 2:13 the charge is Γλεύκους μεμεστωμένοι εἰσίν and Peter denies it at 2:15; at 26:24 it is Μαίνῃ, Παῦλε and Paul denies it at 26:25. Twice, a rare word for inspired utterance is met with an accusation of derangement and answered in the next verse. [T] on the distribution; [I], moderate–high, on the pattern. A Peter–Paul parallel the overview does not have.

7. Acts’ word for “language” is used of a language other than Hebrew exactly twice, and both are in this chapter — against a dark third use fourteen verses earlier. διάλεκτος occurs six times in the NT, all in Acts: 1:19, 2:6, 2:8, 21:40, 22:2, 26:14 [T]. Three are τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ. The other three are τῇ ἰδίᾳ διαλέκτῳ — and 1:19 is the Jerusalemites’ own language naming a field Ἁκελδαμάχ, τοῦτ’ ἔστιν Χωρίον Αἵματος. The same phrase at 2:6 and 2:8 is every nation’s own language declaring τὰ μεγαλεῖα τοῦ θεοῦ. [T] on the distribution; [I], moderate, on the contrast.

8. Peter’s last word is the Song of Moses’ verdict on the wilderness generation. γενεὰ σκολιά occurs in Swete at Deuteronomy 32:5 (γενεὰ σκολιὰ καὶ διεστραμμένη), Psalm 77:8, and Odes 2:5 (Deuteronomy 32 again) [T], full-corpus search. Acts 2:40: Σώθητε ἀπὸ τῆς γενεᾶς τῆς σκολιᾶς ταύτης. Luke has used the Deuteronomy 32:5 pair once already, at Luke 9:41 (γενεὰ ἄπιστος καὶ διεστραμμένη) [T]. The generation addressed at Pentecost is put in the position of the generation that died in the wilderness — and offered rescue from it. Not in the sweep.

Phase 0.5 — Book-Overview Active Layer

Presenting situation. The book exists to give a mixed church ἀσφάλεια that the risen Jesus is still acting and that the Gentile mission is God’s purpose. Bearing: 2:36 is the word itself (Headline 5), and 2:39’s τοῖς εἰς μακράν is the Gentile horizon planted in the first sermon — a horizon the overview notes is announced early and reached late (echo row 2:39 → 22:21).

Structural arc (v2.3). M1, 1:1–2:47, “Commission and outpouring”. This pericope is the bulk of it. The movement closes at 2:47 with προσετίθει — which v2.3 added to the summary-notices table as the M1/M2 boundary marker. So this run sits inside the one movement whose closing device was itself corrected two versions ago, and it can now test it: see §3.

Intertextual map (v2.3). Joel 3:1–5 LXX at 2:17–21 (high, one use); Psalm 16:8–11 at 2:25–28 and 13:35 (high, live, 2 uses); Psalm 110:1 at 2:34–35 (high, one use). Isaiah is the most-used source, live across seven texts; Luke’s Gospel was given its own row at v2.2 and marked live. All four bear on this passage, and the first three are its explicit citations.

Christological trajectory. κύριος and χριστός by God’s act (2:36) — the overview’s ground-statement is this verse. Enthroned at the right hand (2:33–35). Bearing: the sermon Luke puts first is the one the overview’s Christology is quarried from, so this run is checking the overview’s foundation, not merely applying it.

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Q1. What has to have happened before this passage for it to make sense?

  1. 1:4–5 — the promise of the Father and the baptism in the Spirit “not many days from now”; 2:1–4 is that sentence coming true, and 2:33 names it τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν … λαβὼν παρὰ τοῦ πατρός, the same word as 1:4 [T]. (b) 1:8 — the programme; the nations-list of 2:9–11 is its first instalment, gathered rather than gone to. (c) 1:9–11 — the ascension, without which 2:33–34’s ὑψωθείς and οὐ γὰρ Δαυὶδ ἀνέβη εἰς τοὺς οὐρανούς have no referent. (d) 1:15–26 — the Twelve reconstituted, without which 2:14’s σὺν τοῖς ἕνδεκα would be eleven, and 1:15’s hundred and twenty without which 2:41’s three thousand is not a number but a quantity [T].

Q2. Why does this passage exist here rather than anywhere else?

Because a promise made in chapter 1 must be kept before anything else can be narrated, and because the content of the witness has to be preached once, at length, before it can be assumed for twenty-six chapters. Every later sermon in Acts is shorter and presupposes this one: 3:12–26 assumes the resurrection argument, 4:8–12 assumes the enthronement, 13:16–41 re-runs the Psalm 16 argument for a different audience and can therefore compress everything around it.

There is a second answer the position makes visible. Luke puts the nations in Jerusalem before he sends the church out of it. The list of 2:9–11 is not a mission itinerary; it is an audience. The book’s movement out from Jerusalem (1:8) is prefaced by a scene in which the world has already come in — which is Isaiah 2:2–3’s shape exactly (Headline 1), and which is why the Gentile mission in Acts is never presented as a change of plan.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Luke’s purpose is visible in the proportions. The event occupies four verses; the crowd’s reaction nine; the sermon twenty-three; the response five. [T] The phenomenon is the shortest part of the passage. A writer interested chiefly in the experience would not allocate like this.

Within the sermon the proportions are equally telling: Joel occupies five verses of citation and no exposition; Psalm 16 occupies four verses of citation and seven of argument (2:29–32); Psalm 110 occupies two verses of citation and one of conclusion. Luke’s weight falls on the resurrection argument, not on the Spirit. The Spirit is what raises the question (2:12, Τί θέλει τοῦτο εἶναι;) and Jesus is the answer.

A second purpose is definitional and easy to miss. 2:38 gives the only full statement in Acts of what a person is to do — repent, be baptised in the name of Jesus Christ, receive the gift — and every later conversion in the book is measured against it, sometimes by conforming and sometimes by not (8:14–17; 10:44–48; 19:1–7). Luke states the norm once and then narrates the variations, which is why the passage is so easily misused (see §13).

2. Context

Immediately before (1:12–26). Supplies the hundred and twenty, the eleven, and — in 1:19 — the ἰδία διάλεκτος that Headline 7 turns on. It also supplies ἐκχέω: at 1:18 Judas’s entrails ἐξεχύθη, and at 2:17, 2:18, 2:33 the Spirit is poured out [T]. The sweep flagged this juxtaposition at moderate confidence; I would put it lower. Fourteen verses apart with no other shared vocabulary and a wholly different register, this is more likely the ordinary reach of a common verb than a design. Recorded as tested and down-rated.

Immediately after (2:42–47). The summary that closes M1. Two threads run straight on: προσκαρτεροῦντες (1:14 → 2:42, 2:46) and προστίθημι (2:41 → 2:47) [T]. And 2:43’s τέρατα καὶ σημεῖα picks up the pair Luke inserted into the Joel citation at 2:19 (see §11) — so the citation’s alteration is cashed within six verses of the pericope’s end.

Boundaries. 2:41 is the right end and 2:47 is not: v.41 closes the response to the sermon (ἀποδεξάμενοι … ἐβαπτίσθησαν … προσετέθησαν), while 2:42 begins a durative summary in the imperfect. But the two are stitched: the same verb closes both (προσετέθησαν, 2:41; προσετίθει, 2:47), the second time with the Lord as its subject [T]. A preacher taking 2:1–41 should read 2:47 aloud anyway, for that reason.

The binding device is a chain of three citations, each doing a different job. Joel identifies what is happening; Psalm 16 proves what happened to Jesus; Psalm 110 states where he now is. Each is introduced differently — τὸ εἰρημένον διὰ τοῦ προφήτου Ἰωήλ (16), Δαυὶδ γὰρ λέγει εἰς αὐτόν (25), λέγει δὲ αὐτός (34) — and the introductions get shorter as the argument gathers speed [T].

The sermon is held together by a two-word hinge repeated three times. τοῦτον at 2:23 (τοῦτον … ἀνείλατε), τοῦτον at 2:32 (τοῦτον τὸν Ἰησοῦν ἀνέστησεν ὁ θεός), τοῦτον at 2:36 (τοῦτον τὸν Ἰησοῦν ὃν ὑμεῖς ἐσταυρώσατε) [T]. You killed this man; God raised this man; God made this man Lord — and you crucified him. The demonstrative does the whole christological work, and the sermon ends where it began, on the hearers’ act.

Not a chiasm, and worth saying so. The three-citation shape invites a symmetrical reading with Psalm 16 at the centre. It does not hold: the three citations are not of the same kind (one identifies, one argues, one enthrones), and the movement is linear and cumulative, ending outside itself in 2:37’s question. A build, not a ring.

4. Linking Words

  • ἀλλά (2:16) — the sermon’s pivot. Not drunk … but this is what was spoken. The whole argument turns on a single adversative; Peter does not explain the tongues, he re-labels the moment.
  • οὖν ×3 (2:30, 2:33, 2:36) — the argument’s three joints: being therefore a prophet (from David’s death to David’s foresight), therefore exalted (from resurrection to enthronement), therefore let all Israel know (from enthronement to verdict). [T] Three inferential steps and no fourth: the sermon claims to be a deduction.
  • γάρ ×5 (2:15 ×2, 2:25, 2:34, 2:39) — every one supplies evidence, and the last (ὑμῖν γάρ ἐστιν ἡ ἐπαγγελία) grounds the command of v.38 in a promise, not in a duty.
  • καθότι (2:24) — a rare causal, “inasmuch as”: it was not possible for him to be held. Luke uses it four times, all in Luke–Acts. The impossibility, not the power, is the claim.
  • καί γε (2:18) — not in Joel. Luke’s addition (see §11), and an emphatic: yes, even on my slaves.
  • μὲν οὖν (2:41) — Luke’s resumptive, closing the scene.

5. Parallels

Within Acts — the four Spirit-frontiers. 2:1–4 is the first of four, and the overview’s trap-list turns on their differences. [T]: γλῶσσα of speech recurs at 10:46 and 19:6 and nowhere else in Acts; δωρεά at 2:38, 8:20, 10:45, 11:17; ἐκχέω of the Spirit at 2:17, 2:18, 2:33, 10:45. The vocabulary of Pentecost is re-used in full only at Cornelius — 10:45–46 has δωρεά, ἐκχέω, γλώσσαις, and ἐξέστησαν (cf. 2:7, 2:12) — which is exactly why 11:15 can say ὥσπερ καὶ ἐφ’ ἡμᾶς ἐν ἀρχῇ. [T] on all four lemmata; [I], high, that Luke built 10:44–46 out of chapter 2’s words on purpose.

Within Acts — the amazement of the crowd and the amazement at magic. ἐξίστημι occurs eight times in Acts: 2:7, 2:12, then 8:9, 8:11, 8:13 (Simon ἐξιστάνων Samaria; Simon himself ἐξίστατο at signs), 9:21, 10:45, 12:16 [T]. Luke uses one verb for the effect of the Spirit and the effect of μαγεία, and lets Simon Magus stand on both sides of it. The passage does not comment; chapter 8 does.

Within Acts — Peter’s two “whom you crucified”. σταυρόω occurs twice in Acts: 2:36 and 4:10, both Peter, both ὃν ὑμεῖς ἐσταυρώσατε [T]. The formula is his, and it is not repeated by anyone else in the book.

Within Luke–Acts. 2:38’s μετανοήσατε … εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν executes Luke 24:47 (κηρυχθῆναι ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ μετάνοιαν εἰς ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν εἰς πάντα τὰ ἔθνη, ἀρξάμενοι ἀπὸ Ἰερουσαλήμ) — repentance, forgiveness, in his name, beginning from Jerusalem, four elements in order [T]. And εὐλαβής occurs four times in the NT: Luke 2:25 (Simeon), Acts 2:5, 8:2, 22:12 [T] — all four of devout Jews, and the first is the man who saw τὸ σωτήριον.

6. Narrator’s Comment

Luke comments sparingly and every comment is load-bearing.

  • v.5 ἄνδρες εὐλαβεῖς ἀπὸ παντὸς ἔθνους τῶν ὑπὸ τὸν οὐρανόν. The narrator establishes that the audience is Jewish and international before a word is spoken. Without this the scene reads as a Gentile Pentecost, which it is not — 2:14 Ἄνδρες Ἰουδαῖοι, 2:22 Ἄνδρες Ἰσραηλῖται, 2:36 πᾶς οἶκος Ἰσραήλ.
  • v.6 ὅτι ἤκουον εἷς ἕκαστος τῇ ἰδίᾳ διαλέκτῳ. The narrator states the cause of the bewilderment, and it is comprehension, not noise.
  • v.13 ἕτεροι δὲ διαχλευάζοντες. Luke records the mockery. He does not have to; that he does is the same instinct that reports the apostles’ quarrel and their fear.
  • v.15 ἔστιν γὰρ ὥρα τρίτη τῆς ἡμέρας — nine in the morning, before the first meal. Peter’s answer to the charge is chronological, not theological, and it is faintly funny.
  • v.41 ψυχαὶ ὡσεὶ τρισχίλιαι. The count, hedged with ὡσεί exactly as 1:15’s hundred and twenty is hedged with ὡς [T]. Luke rounds both numbers and rounds them the same way.

What the narrator does not do: he never says the tongues were understood by everyone, never explains the mechanism, never says the hundred and twenty spoke or only the Twelve, and never returns to the nations-list. Four silences in a passage the tradition has filled in confidently.

7. Vocabulary

Counts against SBLGNT via the MorphGNT lemma index; LXX searches accent-insensitive over NFD-stripped text.

Six words in this pericope occur nowhere else in the New Testament [T]: μεγαλεῖος (2:11), ἐνωτίζομαι (2:14), προσπήγνυμι (2:23), κατανύσσομαι (2:37), and — with one other NT occurrence apiece — πρόγνωσις (2:23; 1 Pet 1:2) and γλεῦκος (2:13). Note where they cluster: the crowd’s report of what it hears, Peter’s opening address, the crucifixion, and the hearers’ response. The rare vocabulary is not on the Spirit; it is on the human transactions around him.

διάλεκτος (2:6, 2:8). Six in the NT, all in Acts. See Headline 7. [T]

γλῶσσα (2:3, 2:4, 2:11). Six in Acts — and 2:26 is the fourth, inside the Psalm 16 citation (ἠγαλλιάσατο ἡ γλῶσσά μου), which is a lemma-collision and not part of the chain. The three that are the chain move the word across three senses in eight verses: tongues as of fire (image) → other tongues (faculty) → our own tongues (language) [T]. The remaining two are 10:46 and 19:6.

συγχέω (2:6). Five in the NT, all Acts: 2:6, 9:22, 19:32, 21:27, 21:31 [T]. See Headlines 3–4, and note the arc across the book — Pentecost’s confusion is everyone hearing his own language; Jerusalem’s, at 21:31, is nobody able to hear anything (21:34).

ἀποφθέγγομαι (2:4, 2:14). Three in the NT, all Acts. See Headline 6. [T]

ἐπικαλέω (2:21). Twenty in Acts. Most are “surnamed” or “appeal to Caesar”, but the theological thread is real and starts in Joel’s line: 2:21 → 9:14, 9:21 (τοὺς ἐπικαλουμένους τὸ ὄνομα τοῦτο — Luke’s description of Christians is Joel’s phrase) → 22:16 (βάπτισαι … ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα) → 7:59 (Stephen dying). [T] The citation supplies the book’s name for believers.

βουλή (2:23). Seven in Acts. The sweep’s division is confirmed and is worth repeating because it is exact: four are God’s purpose and stand (2:23, 4:28, 13:36, 20:27); three are human plans that fail or are overruled (5:38, 27:12, 27:42) [T]. 2:23 is the first, and it is the one that makes the crucifixion an act of God’s ὡρισμένη βουλή without ceasing to be the hearers’ crime — the sentence holds both in one clause.

ὁρίζω (2:23). Five in Acts: 2:23, 10:42, 11:29, 17:26, 17:31 [T]. Three of the five are God determining something — the delivering-up, the Judge, the day — and 17:26 the nations’ boundaries. A quiet Lukan word for divine determination.

διαφθορά (2:27, 2:31). Six in the New Testament, all in Acts, and all in two sermons — 2:27, 2:31 (Peter) and 13:34, 13:35, 13:36, 13:37 (Paul) [T]. So is ὅσιος in Acts (2:27; 13:34, 13:35). The overview calls Psalm 16 “the only OT text Luke gives to both Peter and Paul in the same role”; the lemma data makes that count exact — two words exist in the New Testament only inside this shared argument.

ἐπαγγελία (2:33, 2:39). Eight in Acts, of which 23:21 is a collision (the plotters’ undertaking). The chain is 1:4 → 2:33 → 2:39 → 13:23, 13:32 → 26:6 [T]: the promise of the Father, received and poured out, extended to those far off, identified as made to the fathers, and finally the thing Paul is on trial for.

προστίθημι (2:41). Six in Acts; the church-growth uses are 2:41, 2:47, 5:14, 11:24 [T]. All four are passive or have God as subject — προσετέθησαν (2:41), ὁ δὲ κύριος προσετίθει (2:47), προσετίθεντο (5:14), προσετέθη ὄχλος ἱκανὸς τῷ κυρίῳ (11:24). Nobody in Acts ever adds anyone to the church. That is a [T] observation with immediate homiletical force, and it is the same grammatical point the overview makes about the growth-notices. And the point is sharper than the English suggests: at 2:47 the text reads only ὁ δὲ κύριος προσετίθει τοὺς σῳζομένους — τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ is a Byzantine addition that NA28 and SBLGNT do not print [T]. The Lord adds those being saved; the object was supplied later.

κατανύσσομαι (2:37). NT hapax. In the LXX the verb group is used of being struck dumb, stunned, pierced — Gen 34:7 of Jacob’s sons, Ps 4:5, Ps 108:16. Not a gentle word: the hearers are not moved, they are stabbed.

τέρας and σημεῖον. The pair occurs at 2:19 (inside the citation, where Luke has added σημεῖα — see §11), then 2:22 and 2:43, and nine times in Acts altogether [T]. Luke inserts a word into a quotation and then makes it a refrain.

(b) προσπήγνυμι is “fix, fasten” (NT hapax) and neither the cross nor the verb σταυρόω is in v.23 — both versions supply the cross. 33 τῇ δεξιᾷ … ὑψωθείς “exalted to the right hand” “exalted at the right hand” A genuine ambiguity: the dative may be locative (“to/at the right hand”, supported by 2:34’s ἐκ δεξιῶν) or instrumental (“by the right hand of God”, good Greek and the LXX’s idiom at Exod 15:6). Name it; do not settle it.
38 εἰς ἄφεσιν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν “for the forgiveness of your sins” “for the forgiveness of your sins” Identical, and the identity conceals the oldest dispute in the verse. What the text gives: the same preposition governs the same noun at Luke 3:3 of John’s baptism [T], so whatever εἰς means here it meant there. That is the honest control and it is rarely offered.
40 Σώθητε ἀπὸ τῆς γενεᾶς τῆς σκολιᾶς “Be saved from this perverse generation!” “Save yourselves from this crooked generation.” The sharpest divergence in the passage. Σώθητε is aorist passive imperative. NASB95 is right; ESV’s reflexive “save yourselves” makes the hearers the agents of their own rescue, which is precisely what the passive denies. Verdict. Neither version is adequate alone here. NASB95 wins vv.8 and 40; ESV wins vv.23 and 40’s adjective. Both fail at v.6, and that failure is the one a sermon must repair.

9. Tone and Feel

Vv. 1–4 are flat, fast and almost clinical. Four verbs of event (ἐγένετο, ἐπλήρωσεν, ὤφθησαν, ἐκάθισεν), two similes (ὥσπερ … πνοῆς, ὡσεὶ πυρός), and no adjectives of wonder. Luke describes the most extraordinary event in the book in the register of a report, and gives the emotional vocabulary to the crowd instead (ἐξίσταντο, ἐθαύμαζον, διηπόρουν).

Vv. 5–13 are noisy and disordered, and the syntax enacts it: a long nominal list with no main verb across three verses (9–11), then a question with no answer (Τί θέλει τοῦτο εἶναι;), then a sneer.

Vv. 14–36 are controlled, forensic and rising. τοῦτο ὑμῖν γνωστὸν ἔστω, ἀκούσατε τοὺς λόγους τούτους, ἐξὸν εἰπεῖν μετὰ παρρησίας, ἀσφαλῶς οὖν γινωσκέτω. Peter is arguing a case, and the last sentence is a verdict delivered to πᾶς οἶκος Ἰσραήλ.

Vv. 37–41 turn on one violent verb. κατενύγησαν τὴν καρδίαν — stabbed. The sermon that began by denying drunkenness ends by wounding.

One tonal fact worth the pulpit. Peter is not conciliatory. He ends on ὃν ὑμεῖς ἐσταυρώσατε and answers the resulting question with μετανοήσατε. There is no softening between the accusation and the invitation — they are eleven words apart.

10. Repetition

  • πᾶς ×11 (2:1 πάντες, 2:4 πάντες, 2:5 παντὸς ἔθνους, 2:7 πάντες, 2:12 πάντες, 2:14 πάντες, 2:17 πᾶσαν σάρκα, 2:21 πᾶς ὃς ἐάν, 2:32 πάντες ἡμεῖς, 2:36 πᾶς οἶκος Ἰσραήλ, 2:39 πᾶσι τοῖς εἰς μακράν) [T]. The leading word of the pericope, and it moves outward as it goes: all of them in one place → every nation → all flesh → everyone who calls → all the house of Israel → all who are far off. The word does the theology.
  • ἀκούω ×5 (2:6, 2:8, 2:11, 2:22, 2:37) plus ἀκούετε at 2:33 [T] — the crowd hears the tongues, hears the summons, hears the sermon, and the hearing at 2:37 is what pierces. Hearing is the passage’s organising sense, which is why the ἵνα μὴ ἀκούσωσιν of Genesis 11:7 matters (Headline 3).
  • τοῦτον ×3 (23, 32, 36). See §3.
  • οὗτος family — τοῦτό ἐστιν (2:16), Τί θέλει τοῦτο εἶναι; (2:12), τοῦτο … βλέπετε καὶ ἀκούετε (2:33) [T]. The crowd asks what this is; Peter says this is that; and at 2:33 this is what they are watching. Three uses, one referent, and the sermon’s entire claim.
  • ἐκχεῶ ×2 in the citation (17, 18) and ἐξέχεεν at 2:33 [T] — Joel’s future twice, then the aorist. The tense change is the sermon: what Joel said God would do, Peter says Jesus has done.
  • ὄνομα (2:21 τὸ ὄνομα κυρίου; 2:38 ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ) [T] — the name called upon in the citation and the name baptised into in the command, seventeen verses apart, and Peter never argues the identification.

11. Quotation / Allusion

Three named citations, all with formulae — the densest citation-load in Acts. Plus one unmarked LXX allusion at 2:6 and another at 2:40.

Move 1 — the sources in their own context

Joel 2:28–32 LXX (Swete numbering; see the corpus note in the Text-First Declaration). Its setting: after the locust devastation and the call to return, and immediately after the promise that the years the locusts ate will be restored and ἐπιγνώσεσθε ὅτι ἐν μέσῳ τοῦ Ἰσραὴλ ἐγώ εἰμι (2:27). The outpouring is the sign that the LORD is in Israel’s midst; it runs into the day of the LORD and ends in deliverance in Zion and Jerusalem.

Psalm 15:8–11 LXX (Eng. 16). A psalm of confidence ending in the presence of God and fullness of joy; the quoted verses are its climax. Swete reads οὐκ ἐνκαταλείψεις … οὐδὲ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν — verbatim what Acts 2:27 reads apart from orthography [T].

Psalm 109:1 LXX (Eng. 110). Swete: Εἶπεν ὁ κύριος τῷ κυρίῳ μου Κάθου ἐκ δεξιῶν μου ἕως ἂν θῶ τοὺς ἐχθρούς σου ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν σου — verbatim identical to Acts 2:34–35 [T].

Genesis 11:1–9 LXX. The tower; God comes down, confuses the γλῶσσα so that they cannot hear one another, scatters them, and the place is named Σύγχυσις.

Deuteronomy 32:5 LXX. The Song of Moses: γενεὰ σκολιὰ καὶ διεστραμμένη, of the wilderness generation.

Isaiah 2:2–3 LXX. ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις the mountain of the LORD is manifest, all the nations come to it, and law and word go out from Zion and Jerusalem.

Move 3 — OT to OT

Joel’s πᾶσα σάρξ already stands against Numbers 11:29 (τίς δῴη πάντα τὸν λαὸν Κυρίου προφήτας), so the democratising of prophecy is Joel’s own reach back into the Torah, not Luke’s innovation. Psalm 16 and Psalm 110 were a Davidic-covenant cluster with 2 Samuel 7 before Luke reached them — which is why 2:30 can allude to the oath (ὅρκῳ ὤμοσεν … ἐκ καρποῦ τῆς ὀσφύος αὐτοῦ, cf. Psalm 131:11 LXX ἐκ καρποῦ τῆς κοιλίας σου θήσομαι ἐπὶ τὸν θρόνον σου) without a formula [T]. And Isaiah 2:2–3 and Joel 2:32 are already in conversation: both end at Zion and Jerusalem. Luke cuts Joel’s Zion and imports Isaiah’s last days — trading one Zion-text’s limit for another Zion-text’s nations.

12. Genre

Narrative with an embedded missionary sermon — the first of the type Acts will repeat at 3:12–26, 4:8–12, 10:34–43 and 13:16–41. Its conventions are visible and consistent: an address to “men of Israel/brothers”, a this-is-that correlation of scripture to the moment, a kerygmatic core (you killed him / God raised him / we are witnesses), an OT proof, and a call to repentance.

Two things follow. First, the shape is a template Luke will vary rather than repeat — 10:34–43 has the same core without the Psalm argument, 17:22–31 has none of the Jewish scaffolding at all. So the sermon is normative in content and not in form. Second, the citation-density here is the highest in the book, and it drops sharply afterwards, because this sermon establishes the exegetical basis the rest can assume.

A genre warning worth naming. Ancient historiography composed speeches appropriate to speaker and occasion, and this is regularly used either to discount the sermon as Lukan or to defend it as verbatim [S]. The text cannot settle it, and preaching does not need it settled: the sermon is in Acts, Luke presents it as Peter’s, and Acts is what is being preached.

13. Copycat

This is the most-copied passage in Acts and therefore the one where the discipline matters most.

Normative:

  • 2:38’s content — repent, be baptised in the name of Jesus Christ, receive the gift of the Spirit. Luke states it once, plainly, in answer to a direct question, and 2:39 grounds it in a promise.
  • 2:39’s scope — you, your children, and all who are far off, and the ones God calls. The extension is stated, not inferred.
  • 2:23’s double claim — God’s determined purpose and human guilt in one clause, neither cancelling the other.
  • 2:36’s conclusion — Jesus is Lord and Christ by God’s act.
  • 2:32’s warrant — the apostles are witnesses to the resurrection. That is what they claim to be and what makes the sermon evidence rather than opinion.

Descriptive — do not transfer:

  • The wind, the fire, the tongues. The overview names this as the book’s chief trap and it bites hardest here. Acts has four Spirit-frontiers with four different sequences [T], and only Cornelius reproduces this one’s vocabulary — which Luke notes is what made it recognisable (11:15), i.e. it was not the norm but the repeat of a beginning.
  • The three thousand. A number, not a promise.
  • The nations-list. An audience Luke assembled, not a missions strategy.
  • The third hour. Peter’s answer to a specific sneer.
  • Speaking in known foreign languages. What Luke narrates at 2:6–11 is comprehension in διάλεκτοι; whether 10:46 and 19:6 are the same phenomenon he does not say. Do not settle from here what Luke leaves open there.

The specific trap. Because 2:38 is normative, preachers slide from it into treating 2:1–4 as normative too, and the phenomenon goes across with the command. Ask of each clause: is this Peter telling them what to do, or Luke telling us what happened? The first transfers; the second is the occasion for it.

14. Bible Timeline

Fifty days after Passover, seven weeks from the resurrection, ten days from the ascension (1:3 ἡμερῶν τεσσεράκοντα) — the feast of Weeks, at which the first-fruits of the harvest were brought. Luke does not press the harvest symbolism and neither should a sermon that wants to stay with him.

In redemptive-historical terms the passage sits at a specific joint: the point at which the promised Spirit becomes the possession of the whole people rather than of individuals raised up for a task. That is Joel’s own point (ἐπὶ πᾶσαν σάρκα), Numbers 11:29’s wish, and the reason the citation’s added clause (καὶ προφητεύσουσιν, Move 2 alteration 6) is the one Luke thought worth inserting.

15. Who Am I?

The hundred and twenty are the passive party throughout: they are sitting (2:2), the sound comes to them, the tongues appear to them and sit on them, they are filled, and the Spirit gives them to speak. Every verb in 2:1–4 has them as object or as recipient. [T] The one active thing they do is begin to speak, and even that is καθὼς τὸ πνεῦμα ἐδίδου.

The crowd is devout, international, and Jewish. It hears, is bewildered, asks a real question (2:12), and part of it sneers. Luke gives it both responses without deciding between them — and then has three thousand of it converted.

The modern hearer is not in the upper room. If the congregation maps itself anywhere it is onto the crowd: people who have heard something they cannot account for and must decide what it means. The passage’s question is theirs — Τί θέλει τοῦτο εἶναι; — and the sermon answers it.

God’s part. God pours out (2:17–18, 33), God attested Jesus (2:22), God’s determined purpose delivered him up (2:23), God raised him (2:24, 32), God made him Lord and Christ (2:36), God calls (2:39), and the Lord adds (2:47). The passage has no human agent of anything decisive, which is exactly the overview’s Christological thread and the reason §7’s προστίθημι observation is more than a grammatical curiosity.

16. So What?

First: the strange thing is a dated thing. Peter’s whole answer to what does this mean? is this is that — an event with a text, a date and a place in a purpose. The passage does not defend the experience; it locates it. A church that can only say what it feels has not learned Pentecost’s first lesson.

Second: the sermon’s weight is not on the Spirit but on the man the Spirit is testifying about. Four verses on the phenomenon, twenty-three on Jesus. The Spirit’s arrival raises a question Peter answers with a crucified and risen man, and the outpouring is itself offered as evidence of where that man now is (2:33). The Spirit points away from himself, and the sermon’s proportions are the doctrine.

Third: the last word is not “you crucified him” but “the promise is for you”. Peter does not soften the accusation and does not withhold the promise; they are eleven words apart, and the promise is wider than the accusation — you, your children, and all who are far off. The generation called σκολιά in the Song of Moses is the generation offered rescue.

And the shape of the whole: God took a text about a coming day, told the church it had arrived, and then added the people himself.

Extensions

Original Language Observations

ἐν τῷ συμπληροῦσθαι (2:1). Articular infinitive with ἐν τῷ — a Lukan favourite — and the verb is “to be filled up completely”. Luke uses it of the days of the ascension being fulfilled (Luke 9:51 ἐν τῷ συμπληροῦσθαι τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ἀναλήμψεως αὐτοῦ) [T]. The same construction and verb open the journey to Jerusalem and the day of Pentecost.

ὥσπερ … ὡσεί (2:2–3). Two comparatives in consecutive verses: a sound as of a rushing wind, tongues as of fire. Luke does not say wind or fire. He says what the sound and the sight were like. The precision is deliberate and most preaching removes it.

ἐκάθισεν (2:3). Singular verb with plural subject (γλῶσσαι) — either a distributive singular (“it sat on each one”) or the subject has shifted to the fire. NA28 marks the clause. Either way the emphasis falls on ἐφ’ ἕνα ἕκαστον: individually, not corporately.

ἑτέραις γλώσσαις (2:4) against ταῖς ἡμετέραις γλώσσαις (2:11). ἕτερος is “other, different”; the crowd’s word is “our own”. The narrator describes the phenomenon from the speakers’ side and the crowd from theirs, and the two descriptions are not identical. [T]

τῇ ἰδίᾳ διαλέκτῳ … ἐν ᾗ ἐγεννήθημεν (2:8). The relative clause is redundant and therefore emphatic: the language in which we were born. Not learned, not acquired — native. This is what makes the Genesis 11 reversal precise: Babel gave each group a language it had to be born into, and that is the barrier crossed.

διὰ χειρὸς ἀνόμων προσπήξαντες ἀνείλατε (2:23). Two participial-plus-finite constructions with you as subject throughout, and ἀνόμων in an instrumental phrase between them. Grammatically the hearers are the agents and the lawless are the instrument — which is what makes the verse a charge rather than an observation, and why its careful distribution of guilt should not be flattened (see §8).

Σώθητε (2:40). Aorist passive imperative. English has no idiom for a passive command, which is why versions drift to the reflexive. The nearest honest rendering is “let yourselves be saved” or “be rescued”. [T]

ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι (2:38) against ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι (10:48) and εἰς τὸ ὄνομα (8:16; 19:5). Acts uses three prepositions for baptism “in the name” [T]. No doctrine should rest on which one appears where, and 2:38’s ἐπί is the rarest of the three.

Historical and Cultural Background

Pentecost. The feast of Weeks: fifty days from Passover, a pilgrim feast, which is why Jerusalem holds Jews ἀπὸ παντὸς ἔθνους. Luke’s κατοικοῦντες (2:5) suggests residents rather than pilgrims, and 2:9’s οἱ κατοικοῦντες τὴν Μεσοποταμίαν uses the same participle of people in Mesopotamia — a mild tension the text does not resolve [T].

The nations-list (2:9–11). Roughly east-to-west, beginning with the Parthian empire and ending at Rome, Crete and Arabia. Two observations the text supports: Judea is in the list, which is odd in a list of foreign parts and has generated much discussion [S]; and the list ends with Rome [T], which is where the book ends.

The third hour (2:15). About 9 a.m., before the morning meal. The charge of drunkenness is answered as absurd on its face.

γλεῦκος (2:13). New or sweet wine — and at Pentecost, in early summer, the previous vintage would be some months old. The mockery may be deliberately silly.

Original Audience Reception

In the narrative world, devout Jews from across the diaspora hear Galileans declaring God’s mighty works in the languages they were born into, and are told by a Galilean fisherman that a man they knew of as executed is now Lord and Christ. Three thousand accept it that day.

For Theophilus and the book’s first readers, four things land. (1) The certainty promised at Luke 1:4 is delivered here in a single adverb (Headline 5). (2) The movement’s origin is Jewish, in Jerusalem, at a feast, from Scripture — which matters to a reader wondering whether a Gentile-majority church has drifted from Israel. (3) The Gentile horizon is present from the first sermon (2:39), so the later mission is not a change of policy. (4) Prophecy is now common property — sons and daughters, young and old, male and female slaves — which is Luke’s added clause doing its work.

One thing they would have heard that we do not. The Joel citation’s alterations. A reader who knew the Greek Joel would notice ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις where his text read μετὰ ταῦτα, and would notice the citation stopping one clause short of Zion. Both are audible only to someone who knows the source, which is a reason to read Joel 2:28–32 aloud before the sermon text [I].

Biblical-Theological Themes

  • The Spirit given to all God’s people. Numbers 11:29 → Joel 2:28–29 → Acts 2:17–18, with Luke’s added καὶ προφητεύσουσιν making the point twice.
  • Babel and the nations. Genesis 11 → the table of nations → Isaiah 2:2–3 → Acts 2:5–11. The scattering is not undone; it is made no longer a barrier. Nobody at Pentecost stops speaking his own language.
  • The Davidic covenant. 2 Samuel 7 / Psalm 131:11 → Psalms 16 and 110 → 2:29–36 → 13:32–37. Luke’s argument is that the covenant’s terms are unfulfillable by a dead David and therefore point past him.
  • The name. Joel 2:32’s ὄνομα κυρίου → 2:21 → 2:38’s baptism ἐπὶ τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ → 4:12. The identification is made by juxtaposition and never argued.
  • The last days begun. ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις — and the book then runs for twenty-six chapters inside them.

Schnittjer Pass

Three observations in the later-uses-earlier key.

  1. Luke alters a text he is quoting, and every alteration widens it. Eight changes, none of which narrows the promise; two of which (the μου on δούλας, the added καὶ προφητεύσουσιν) extend it to people the source left implicit. The direction of the transformation is itself the argument. [I], high.
  2. The most consequential use of Joel is of a clause Luke does not quote. Cutting at 2:32a and redeploying προσκαλέομαι at 2:39 is a use of scripture by omission and delay. [I], moderate–high, and a category worth naming: a citation’s silence can be load-bearing.
  3. Genesis 11 is used without being named, and used by inversion. The verb is retained and its cause reversed. That is not fulfilment and not typology; it is a deliberate re-run of a scene with the outcome changed. [I], moderate–high.

Christological Reading

Ground. The sermon’s Christology is stated in three deductions (§4’s three οὖν): David was a prophet and spoke of another (2:30–31); the risen Jesus is exalted and has poured out what you see (2:33); therefore God has made him Lord and Christ (2:36). κύριος is the sermon’s own word for God (2:34’s first κύριος) and for Jesus (2:34’s second, and 2:36) in the same sentence, on the authority of Psalm 110 [T]. That is as high a Christology as Acts states anywhere, and it is reached by exegesis rather than assertion.

The Spirit as evidence. 2:33 is the hinge: having received the promise … he has poured out this which you both see and hear. The Spirit’s outpouring is offered as proof of the ascension. What the crowd can observe is made evidence for what it cannot.

Moralism check. This passage resists moralism better than most — nobody in it is exemplary — but it invites two substitutes. (a) Experientialism: making the sermon’s point the phenomenon rather than the person. The proportions refute it (§16). (b) Technique: turning 2:38 into a method. The passive of 2:41 and 2:47 refutes that one — nobody in Acts ever adds anyone to the church [T]. Christ is the sermon’s subject and God is its agent; the hearers’ part is to be pierced and to repent.

Difficult / Contested Verses

2:4 — what were the tongues? Luke says ἑτέραις γλώσσαις and the crowd says τῇ ἰδίᾳ διαλέκτῳ. Within this passage they are intelligible human languages. Whether 10:46 and 19:6 are the same is not stated. Preach what is here; do not import or export.

2:17 — “in the last days”. See Headline 1. If asked why the versions’ footnote does not match Joel, say plainly that Luke changed it, and say what the change does.

2:23 — divine purpose and human guilt. The clause holds both without explanation and Acts repeats the structure at 4:27–28 [T]. Any sermon that resolves the tension has said more than Luke.

2:33 — “by” or “at” the right hand? See §8. Genuinely open.

2:38 — what does εἰς ἄφεσιν mean, and is baptism required? The oldest dispute in the verse and the text will not settle it here. The available control is Luke 3:3, where the identical phrase describes John’s baptism [T] — so whatever it means at 2:38 it meant before Pentecost. Offer that, name the two readings, and move to 2:39, which grounds the whole in a promise.

2:39 — who are “those far off”? Within the sermon, diaspora Jews are the natural referent, and 2:5 supports it. But τοῖς εἰς μακράν is Isaiah 57:19’s phrase for those outside, and Acts 22:21 uses μακράν of the Gentiles [T]. Luke has written a phrase that will grow. Say both; the ambiguity is doing work.

Convergent Findings

  1. Luke’s handling of Joel is a single coherent strategy, not eight separate tweaks. Tool 7 (the τέρας/σημεῖον refrain), Tool 11 (the collation and the Move 2 table), the Schnittjer pass and the Original Audience section converge: every alteration widens the promise, the one added clause names what is happening in the room, the substituted phrase imports Isaiah’s nations-oracle, and the cut clause is redeployed to remove Joel’s geographical limit. High on the data; moderate–high on the strategy.

  2. The passage’s decisive verbs have God as subject and the church as object. Tool 7 (προστίθημι — all four growth-notices passive or divine), Tool 15 (every verb in 2:1–4 has the hundred and twenty as recipient), Tool 16, and the Christological reading converge from four directions. High — this is [T] from the grammar throughout.

  3. Hearing is the organising sense, and that is why Genesis 11 is in the room. Tool 10 (ἀκούω ×6), Tool 11 Move 1 (Gen 11:7’s ἵνα μὴ ἀκούσωσιν), Tool 8 (the loss at 2:6) and the Original Language note on 2:8 converge. High.

  4. This is the book’s foundation deposit, and the plant-count proves it. Tool 11 Move 4 lists eleven forward threads, more than any other pericope run so far (20:17–38 had eight, 21:1–36 nine). Tool 1’s proportions, Tool 12’s genre note and Phase 0.6’s Q2 agree. High — the count is [T].

  5. Two words exist in the New Testament only inside the Peter–Paul shared argument. διαφθορά (6×) and ὅσιος in Acts (3×) occur only at 2:27–31 and 13:34–37 [T]. Tool 5, Tool 7 and Move 2 converge, and this is the hardest evidence in the book for the overview’s Peter–Paul parallelism claim. High.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Preaching the phenomenon and skipping the argument

How it sounds: forty minutes on wind, fire and tongues; five on Jesus. Why it is wrong: Luke gives four verses to the phenomenon and twenty-three to the sermon, and the sermon’s own weight is on the resurrection argument, not the Spirit. 2:33 makes the outpouring evidence for something else. Instead: preach the proportions. Let the strange thing raise the question (2:12) and let Peter answer it.

Pitfall 2: “Babel reversed” as a slogan

How it sounds: “At Babel God divided the languages; at Pentecost he reversed it.” Why it is wrong: it is nearly right and the “nearly” matters. Nobody’s language is taken away at Pentecost; the hearers hear the language they were born into (2:8). The scattering is not undone — it is made no longer a barrier. And the confusion is not removed either: the crowd is still συνεχύθη, now because it understands. Instead: show the verb, show Genesis 11:7’s so that they may not hear, and let the inversion be precise.

Pitfall 3: Making 2:1–4 the template for Christian experience

How it sounds: “Have you had your Pentecost?” Why it is wrong: the overview names this as the book’s chief trap. Acts has four Spirit-frontiers with four different sequences, and only Cornelius reproduces this vocabulary — which 11:15 treats as remarkable precisely because it was a repeat of the beginning. Instead: preach 2:38–39, which is the part addressed to hearers, and let 2:1–4 be what happened once.

Pitfall 4: Softening “whom you crucified”

How it sounds: moving quickly from 2:36 to 2:38 so the accusation does not land. Why it is wrong: Luke reports that the accusation is what pierced them (2:37, κατενύγησαν), and the question that opens the way to the promise is caused by it. The wound is the door. Instead: let 2:36 stand, and let the hearers’ question come from it rather than from the preacher.

Pitfall 5: “Save yourselves” (ESV) preached as an exhortation to self-rescue

How it sounds: a call to moral extraction from a corrupt culture. Why it is wrong: Σώθητε is passive. And γενεὰ σκολιά is the Song of Moses’ phrase for the wilderness generation — a verdict, not a sociological description. Instead: “let yourselves be rescued”, and name Deuteronomy 32:5.

Pitfall 6: Using 2:44–47 to interpret 2:41

How it sounds: the sermon’s success measured by the community of goods that follows. Why it is wrong: 2:42–47 is a separate summary in the imperfect, and the overview’s trap-list warns about it specifically. What 2:41 says is that they received the word, were baptised, and were added. Instead: if 2:47 is read, read it for its verb and its subject — the Lord added — not for its economics.
