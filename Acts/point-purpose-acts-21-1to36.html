Point & Purpose: Acts 21:1–36

Study text: NASB95 Pulpit text: ESV (Anglicised) — declared for this engagement, Patrick’s own church Date: 12 September 2026 Exegesis consumed: dig-deeper-acts-21-1to36 (solo run, 9 Sep 2026, revised 10 Sep from the full NA28 apparatus) — its Headline Findings, Convergent Findings, So What, Who Am I, Christological Reading and Preaching Pitfalls; oriented by book-overview-acts v2.3 Working titles: The will of the Lord be done · The doors were shut

Scope decision

A two-part series. Thirty-six verses, seven structural movements, and two distinct concerns that each want a pulpit hour. One sermon over the whole would compress the Agabus material and the James material to about a third of what each carries, and would flatten the register change the exegesis identifies as structural — vv.1–17 are a slow domestic travelogue in the first person, vv.18–26 are the minutes of a meeting, vv.27–36 are eleven finite verbs of action in ten verses. [scope]

The seam, adjusted. When we settled the scope I named the split as vv.1–16 / 17–36. Luke’s own seam falls one verse later. The structure table in the dig report puts 15–17 together as a single movement (Μετὰ δὲ τὰς ἡμέρας ταύτας — the ascent and the welcome), and v.18 opens a new scene with Luke’s standard marker τῇ δὲ ἐπιούσῃ. So:

  • Sermon One — 21:1–17. Tyre, Caesarea, Agabus, the ascent. Ends on ἀσμένως ἀπεδέξαντο ἡμᾶς οἱ ἀδελφοί — “the brethren received us gladly.” A sermon about obedience in the dark that ends with a welcome is a better sermon than one that ends on a boat.
  • Sermon Two — 21:18–36. James, the rite, the riot, the arrest. Ends on Αἶρε αὐτόν.

Overlaps to name. (a) This unit executes the Miletus farewell (20:17–38), which has its own completed dig. If you are preaching 20:17–38 in the same series, the δέω chain belongs to that sermon’s ending and must not be spent twice: at Miletus Paul is δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι (20:22); here he is bound in fact (21:33). Preach the chain forward, not twice. (b) Sermon Two’s final verse is one verse short of the steps becoming Paul’s pulpit (21:37–40): ἀναβαθμός occurs twice in the NT and both times here. If you intend to preach 21:37–22:29 next, end Sermon Two by pointing at those steps rather than by resolving anything.

Governing Fallen Condition Focus

We cannot get at the certainty we most want — about what God is doing, about what we should therefore do, about what is actually true of someone — and we keep trying to secure it by our own means; and the people who love us most are often the ones who draw the wrong conclusion.

Anchored in the text, not imported. The dig’s fourth Convergent Finding puts it as the pericope’s governing shape: the passage’s certainties belong to God and its uncertainties to everybody else. The Spirit’s information is consistent throughout — bonds await (20:23; 21:4, 11). Every human inference from it is partial or wrong: the Tyrian disciples’ (v.4), the companions’ and Luke’s own (v.12), the Jerusalem believers’ about Paul (v.21), the Asian Jews’ about Trophimus (v.29), the tribune’s (v.34). And Luke’s own preface word for the thing the reader was promised — ἀσφάλεια, Luke 1:4 — is precisely what the tribune cannot obtain: οὐ δυνάμενος γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλὲς διὰ τὸν θόρυβον (21:34), and Festus cannot obtain it either (25:26). [T] on the distributions; [I], moderate–high, on the design.

Who the hearer is. Not Paul. The congregation stands with the friends at Tyre and Caesarea — people who love a man, hear something true from God, draw a reasonable conclusion, are wrong, and end by praying rightly. That is where most people in a church actually stand, and it is the single most useful thing the exegesis offers the preacher.

Governing Teaching Point

The certainty the gospel gives belongs to the Lord and not to us: his will was being done in Jerusalem exactly while his servant was being dragged out of the temple, and the road that servant walked is the road his Master walked first.

  • Subject: When nobody in the story can get at the truth — not the friends, not the elders, not the crowd, not the magistrate — whose certainty is left standing?
  • Complement: The Lord’s. The same Lord whose will was done in Gethsemane is having his will done here, unvindicated and unexplained, and he has already walked this road himself.

Built from convergent findings, not from one tool: Headline 1 (the κατηχέω/ἀσφαλής thread, Luke 1:4 → 21:21 → 21:34 → 25:26), Convergent Finding 4 (the certainties/uncertainties shape), the So What’s third movement, and the Christological Reading. Christ is the actor. Paul is the object of nearly every verb after v.27 — seen, seized, dragged, beaten, arrested, bound, carried, followed — and says nothing at all between v.13 and v.37.

Governing Purpose Statement

To move hearers from trying to secure their own certainty — about guidance, about their good name, about the outcome — to resting on the Lord whose will is done whether or not they can see it; and, in the same movement, to make them slower to draw conclusions about people they love and people they suspect.

Differentiated by audience:

  • The one agonising over a decision — relief: God has not promised you certainty about the course. He has given you true information and a Lord to surrender to, and the friends’ prayer is the right end of the argument, not a failure of faith.
  • The one whose name has been taken — the deepest comfort the passage has: v.29 is the only place in Acts where Luke explicitly labels an accusation against Paul as factually wrong, and he does it in a passing aside that the crowd never hears. The narrator knows. That is a picture of a vindication that is real without being public.
  • The one who is sure about someone else — the sharp edge: sincere religious outrage, in the right place, about the wrong fact (v.29). The Asian Jews were not lying. They were wrong.
  • The unbeliever — the offer at the point where the passage is bleakest: the shut doors of the temple are not the end of the story, because the man on the outside of them is about to preach from the steps he was carried up, and the Lord he preaches was carried out of the city first.

No bare imperative anywhere: the change rests on what the Lord has done and is doing, not on the hearer’s resolve.

Candidate Big Question

(candidate — final wording for the downstream one-sentence-sermon step, or your own pen)

“When you cannot find out what God is doing — and the people who love you cannot find out either — what is actually left to stand on?”

Shorter pulpit forms, one per sermon:

  • Sermon One: “What do you do when God tells you the truth and not what to do about it?”
  • Sermon Two: “What is left when you have done everything right and the doors shut anyway?”

Sermon One — Acts 21:1–17

Teaching Point

When God gives his people true information and no instruction, the end of the argument is not certainty about the course but the prayer Jesus prayed in the garden — and the Lord whose will that is has already walked this road himself.

  • Subject: What do you do with a word from God that tells you what will happen and not what to do?
  • Complement: You come to the end of your arguments and say what the friends said — τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω — and the will you are surrendering to is the will of a Lord who was bound before you were.

Aim

To bring hearers who are agonising over a decision — their own, or someone else’s — to stop demanding of God a certainty he has not promised; to separate carefully what the Spirit actually said from the conclusion they drew from it; and to pray the companions’ prayer as surrender rather than as resignation.

Structure

Verses Movement Function
1–6 A true word and a wrong conclusion Surface the need. The narrator vouches for the medium and not the message: they told Paul διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος not to go. Luke never says who was right. Wives and children on the beach; they knelt and prayed and he sailed anyway.
7–9 The promise landing in a subordinate clause Relief, and a hinge. Philip’s four daughters are Joel 2:17 fulfilled — θυγάτηρ occurs three times in Acts and the other is in the Pentecost quotation. God is quietly keeping his word in the margin of the page while the argument goes on. And the host is the man who baptised someone the Law shut out of the assembly.
10–14 The mime, the rebuke, and the prayer The heart of it. Agabus binds himself with Paul’s belt; the companions beg; Paul’s answer is costly, not heroic — συνθρύπτω, an NT hapax, “to crush small”: he is being worn down by the love of his friends and says so. Then the Gethsemane sentence, and it is theirs, not his.
15–17 The ascent, and the welcome Land it. Surrender is not fatalism. They stop arguing, pack, and go up with him — and they are received gladly. The sermon ends on a welcome, which is where Luke ends it.

Where Christ is

Not by making Paul a second Christ, but at v.14. The prayer that closes the argument is the prayer from the garden, in the mouths of people who could not see how it would turn out — and the One who prayed it first could see, and prayed it anyway. That is the ground of the surrender, and it is the only reason the surrender is not stoicism. Add 9:16 if there is room: what Paul says he is ready for at v.13 is exactly what the Lord said at his commissioning — ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματός μου παθεῖν.

Read aloud

Before the sermon or at v.14, read Luke 22:42. The congregation will not hear the echo otherwise, and the whole movement turns on it.

Sermon Two — Acts 21:18–36

Teaching Point

Luke clears Paul four times over and then shuts the temple doors behind him — because the vindication the gospel promises is not the kind a court can deliver, and the Lord’s will was being done in Jerusalem exactly while his servant was being dragged out.

  • Subject: What happens to a servant who does everything right and is destroyed anyway?
  • Complement: He is cleared by the narrator and not by the crowd, rescued into chains, and carried up the steps he will preach from — because his vindication is the Lord’s business and not the magistrate’s.

Aim

To move hearers from needing to be cleared — by the crowd, by the congregation, by the court — to entrusting their name to the Lord who was accused first; and, in the same breath, to make them far more careful about the inference they have already drawn about someone else.

Structure

Verses Movement Function
18–26 The report, and the remedy that fails Set the problem honestly. Tens of thousands of law-zealous Jewish believers (μυριάδες — say the number). Paul has been catechised about — the same verb and construction as Luke 1:4, and what they were taught is false. James’s plan is reasonable, public and expensive; the δέ of v.25 shows him carefully not touching Gentile freedom. Paul complies — and Luke narrates the compliance in the technical vocabulary of Numbers 6, so that a reader who knows the Law watches him obey it.
27–30 The truth is not what anyone is shouting about The turn. “When the seven days were about to be completed” — the rite is never finished; the clock runs out mid-sentence. Then the aside at v.29: they supposed it. Luke states flatly that the charge is a mistaken inference — the one accusation against Paul in Acts that the narrator explicitly calls false. Four uses of ἱερόν in five verses, and none at all in the preceding twenty-five: he goes in, is seen in it, is accused over it, is dragged out of it, and the doors shut. The last thing Acts says about the temple is that its doors were shut with Paul outside.
31–36 Carried up the steps Land it. The rescue is an arrest: two seizures in three verses, and the second one saves him. βαστάζω — the man commissioned to carry the name before Gentiles and kings (9:15) is carried by Gentile soldiers on his way to speak before a king. And at the last moment Luke changes the word: the violence is the crowd’s, but the ones who follow shouting Αἶρε αὐτόν are the people.

Where Christ is

In the inversion, and only there. τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ with ἀκολουθέω occurs twice in the New Testament — Luke 23:27, where the people follow Jesus mourning, and Acts 21:36, where they follow Paul baying. Same phrase, same verb, same tense, opposite errand. Luke’s point is not that Paul is a second Christ; the crowd’s role is inverted, the Gethsemane words were given to the companions, and Paul is rescued rather than crucified. The point is that the servant’s road runs where the Master’s ran — and that the One who was actually taken away in the end was taken away for the people now shouting.

Read aloud

Luke 23:18 and 23:26–27, before Acts 21:30–36. This is the one place in the passage where a modern congregation simply will not hear what Luke’s first readers heard unless it is read to them. The exegesis flags it explicitly.

Preaching on the ESV

Three places where the pulpit text and the study text diverge in a way that bears on these sermons. This is a note to the preacher, not a translation verdict.

Where What the ESV does What to do
21:13 (Sermon One) δεθῆναι becomes “to be imprisoned” The chain runs 20:22 (bound in the Spirit) → 21:11 (Agabus binds himself) → 21:13 → 21:33 (bound with two chains). The ESV varies the word at exactly the verse where Paul accepts the chain Agabus has just mimed. Read “bound” aloud at v.13 and say why in one sentence.
21:36 (Sermon Two) τὸ πλῆθος τοῦ λαοῦ becomes “the mob of the people” This destroys both the ὄχλος → λαός switch in consecutive clauses and the verbal link to Luke 23:27, which is the sermon’s Christological load-bearing point. Correct it from the pulpit: “the multitude of the people.” NASB95 has it right.
21:20 (Sermon Two) “thousands” — as does NASB95 μυριάς is ten thousand. Both versions understate the strongest single datum in Acts against assuming the church had left Israel. Give the number.

Two places where the ESV is the better text and can simply be used: 21:9 (“who prophesied” — a participle, an activity, not an office, which preserves the link to Joel’s προφητεύσουσιν at 2:17 that the sermon needs), and 21:26 (“when the days of purification would be fulfilled” — Numbers 6:13 settles this one in the ESV’s favour).

A standing caution, worth saying once. Across two consecutive runs — 20:28 (ποιμαίνειν) and 21:13 and 21:36 here — the ESV varies its rendering for English readability at precisely the points where Luke’s verbal repetition is carrying the structure. Expect it in Acts and check for it rather than rediscovering it each week.

Keep Honest

  1. Do not build a doctrine of guidance on 21:4. Neither “even Paul got it wrong” nor “their feelings overrode the Spirit” is available. The Greek attaches διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος to the telling; 20:23 shows the Spirit’s consistent content is that bonds await, not therefore do not go; and Luke declines to adjudicate. Preach what is there: the Spirit gave information, the people who loved Paul drew a conclusion, and the conclusion was theirs. Name the three readings if you must, argue for that one, and do not pretend it is settled.

  2. James and the elders are not the villains. They glorify God for the Gentile mission, state the charge against Paul is baseless, restate Gentile freedom unprompted, and the trouble comes from Asian Jews, not from them. Paul had taken a vow of his own accord at Cenchreae (18:18) with nobody asking him to. If the plan is to be criticised, criticise it as a plan that failed — and say why it failed: a fact nobody in that room could have known.

  3. Resist “Paul is like Jesus.” The strongest parallel is an inversion; the Gethsemane words are given to the companions; Paul is rescued. The exegesis supplies three controls and they should all stay in place. Luke is describing the road, not duplicating the Saviour.

  4. Do not be even-handed about 21:28. The accusation is false and Luke says so four sentences later. Say it plainly, and let the congregation feel what it is to be destroyed by an inference.

  5. Do not mine the travelogue. No devotional on Mnason’s spare room. If 21:9 is preached, preach it as 2:17 fulfilled — which is the reason Luke put it there.

  6. v.13 is not a hero shot. συνθρύπτω is “to pulverise”. Paul is not comforted by his friends’ grief; he says it is crushing him. Preachers routinely make this verse magnificent; Luke makes it costly. The magnificence is real, but it costs him something, and saying so is what stops the sermon becoming “be resolute like Paul.”

  7. The unfinished rite is the structural point and it is easy to read past. Luke tells us the offering was to be made for each man, then interrupts the seven days. He never says whether it happened. Do not tidy it, and do not build on it either.

Handing off

Both Big Questions above are candidates — the final wording belongs to the one-sentence-sermon step or to your own pen. The structures are sermon structure (headings and movement), which the dig report deliberately stops short of; they trace the passage’s own seams and can be taken into the manuscript stage as they stand.

Two things the exegesis left open that these sermons do not need but you may want. The κατηχέω/ἀσφαλής thread (Governing FCF) is the dig’s most distinctive claim and is held at moderate–high pending a Logos check — it is load-bearing for the governing idea but not for either sermon’s Teaching Point, so it can be preached as an observation rather than as a conclusion. And Agabus’s oracle says the Jews will bind Paul, where in the event the Jews beat him and the Romans bind him; a hearer may notice, so say the difficulty exists rather than letting them find it alone.
