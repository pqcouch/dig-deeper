# Luke's Purpose and Plan in the Three Accounts of Saul's Conversion

**Acts 9:1–31 · Acts 22:3–21 · Acts 26:9–23**

*A summary statement. The evidence, the counts, the warrant tags and the contested points are in `dig-deeper-saul-conversion-acts-9-22-26`; this document states the conclusions.*

**Date:** 19 November 2026

---

## The governing principle

One event, three texts, one author. The event happened once and does not vary, so **every difference between the three accounts is located in the text and nowhere else** — in what Luke selects, what he omits, who he lets speak, what words he chooses, and where he puts it. No difference between them can be explained at the level of what happened on the road, because what happened on the road is the one thing that is the same in all three.

That is why the first question to ask is not *what happened?* but *why has he written it this way, three times?* Each telling is built for a different hearer and does a different job, and the three jobs together make a single argument.

---

## 1 · Acts 9:1–31 — the narrator's deposition

### Purpose

To establish, on Luke's own authority and **before any charge has been laid**, that the mission to the nations was Christ's initiative and not Paul's. Everything that will be disputed in a courtroom from chapter 21 onward is settled here in advance, while nobody is yet arguing about it.

### Plan

**He tells it himself.** This is the only one of the three in the narrator's voice. Its authority does not depend on the character's credibility, which is exactly what will be attacked later.

**He gives the commission to Ananias, about Saul, in the third person.** *σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς* ("a chosen instrument") is spoken over a blind man in a house on Straight Street who is told none of it. **So the reader learns what Paul is for before Paul does**, and is never in doubt about it afterwards. The reader is given a vantage point no one in the scene has.

**He slows down for Ananias.** The road takes nine verses; Ananias takes ten, and more words. The weight of the chapter falls not on the light but on a frightened disciple who argues with the Lord, states his evidence, goes anyway, and calls the persecutor *ἀδελφέ* ("brother"). Luke lingers where he means the reader to look.

**He states the audience list the rest of the book will execute** — *ἐνώπιον ἐθνῶν τε καὶ βασιλέων υἱῶν τε Ἰσραήλ* ("before Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel") — and names suffering as the **content** of the call rather than its risk: *ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματός μου παθεῖν*.

**He places it before Cornelius, and then takes Saul off the stage.** 9:30 puts him on a boat to Tarsus, and he does not return until 11:25. In between, Peter goes to Caesarea, the Spirit falls on uncircumcised Gentiles, and Jerusalem concludes that God has granted repentance to the nations. **Luke refuses to let Paul start the Gentile mission.** When the charge of innovation is finally brought, it has already been answered structurally, thirteen chapters earlier.

**He closes the panel with the church at peace** (9:31). The man whose violence scattered the church in 8:1–3 is the man whose call closes that movement with the church built up and multiplying. The frame interprets the event: *this is what happened to the persecution*.

### What this telling will not bear

It is a **commissioning** narrative, not a conversion paradigm. 9:15–16 is about a mission and its cost. Luke's own conversion paradigm is 2:38.

## 2 · Acts 22:3–21 — the defence to Israel

### Purpose

To put on record that the mission to the nations is continuous with the God of the fathers — argued in Hebrew, in Jerusalem, to Israel, by the accused himself — **and to let that defence fail**, so that Israel's refusal stands on the record before any Roman magistrate hears the case.

### Plan

**The genre changes, and Paul names it.** *ἀπολογία* ("defence", 22:1) — the word's only other use in Acts is Festus explaining Roman trial procedure. This is a legal rebuttal, not a testimony.

**It is built to the shape of the accusation.** The charge of 21:28 was that Paul teaches *κατὰ τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ νόμου καὶ τοῦ τόπου τούτου* — against the people, the law, and this place. The speech answers all three, in that order: **the people** (he addresses them in Hebrew as *ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες*), **the law** (*κατὰ ἀκρίβειαν τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου*, and Ananias *κατὰ τὸν νόμον*), **the place** (*προσευχομένου μου ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ*).

**Ananias is re-introduced as a character witness.** In chapter 9 he was "a certain disciple". Here he is *ἀνὴρ εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον, μαρτυρούμενος ὑπὸ πάντων τῶν κατοικούντων Ἰουδαίων* — devout according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews living there. Nothing about the man has changed; the bench has.

**The commission is attributed to the God of Israel.** *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν προεχειρίσατό σε* — "the God of our fathers appointed you", using the verb Peter had used of God's appointment of the Messiah. To a crowd that believes he has abandoned the God of Israel, Paul says that the God of Israel appointed him.

**The temple vision is added, and appears nowhere else.** He is accused of defiling the temple. He answers that the commission to the Gentiles was given to him **inside it, at prayer** (22:17–21). It is the strongest move in the speech.

**It ends in mid-argument.** The crowd listens *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* — "up to this word" — and then demands his death. Luke does not rescue the speech. He lets twenty-one verses of careful, accurate, law-respecting defence produce a riot, and then has the Lord himself, nine verses later, call the failed defence *testimony given* (23:11).

### What this telling will not bear

It is not a model testimony and its structure is not a template. It is a rebuttal in the accusation's own vocabulary, and it fails.

## 3 · Acts 26:9–23 — the defence before a king

### Purpose

To relocate the whole event from a man's biography into **the hope of Israel on trial**, before the one audience in the book that includes a king — and to have the acquittal pronounced by the bench itself.

### Plan

**The audience supplies the term of 9:15 that had never been discharged.** Agrippa is the only king Paul addresses in Acts, and more than half of the book's occurrences of *βασιλεύς* stand in these two chapters.

**Everything mediated is removed.** No Ananias. No baptism, no washing away of sins — and **no blindness**. In the account given to a king there is no incapacity, no three days in the dark, no dependence on a stranger, and no Jewish intermediary of any kind. There is a light, a voice, a commission, and an obedience.

**In their place, the commission at its fullest and from its highest speaker.** Christ gives it directly, in the first person, at more length than the other two accounts give it combined — and he gives it in the language of the prophets' calls: *ἀνάστηθι καὶ στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου* (Ezekiel's call), *ἐξαιρούμενός σε … εἰς οὓς ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε* (Jeremiah's), *ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμοὺς αὐτῶν … ἀπὸ σκότους εἰς φῶς* (the Servant's task in Isaiah 42). **Paul's call is narrated as a prophet's call to a rebellious house** — and 26:19–21 reports exactly that outcome.

**The details added only here are each fitted to the court.** The heavenly voice spoke *τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ* — a fact withheld from the Hebrew speech, where the hearers were already hearing Hebrew, and supplied in the Greek speech before a Roman governor and a Herodian king, where no one present would have understood a word of it. **So that detail is not for the people in the room; it is for the reader.** The proverb about kicking against the goads is Greek, in a Greek speech, to a Greek-speaking court. The whole party falls, not Paul alone. The light is at midday and brighter than the sun.

**The content is claimed to be nothing new.** *οὐδὲν ἐκτὸς λέγων ὧν τε οἱ προφῆται ἐλάλησαν … καὶ Μωϋσῆς* — nothing beyond what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass: a suffering Christ, and *φῶς … τῷ λαῷ καὶ τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*, light to the people and to the nations. The charge is innovation; the answer is that there is no new content.

**And the case is made to rest on what can be checked.** Paul opens by appealing to living witnesses of his early life, *"if they are willing to testify"*, and closes with *οὐ γάρ ἐστιν ἐν γωνίᾳ πεπραγμένον τοῦτο* — "this was not done in a corner."

**The verdict is spoken by the bench.** *"This man could have been released."*

### What this telling will not bear

It is not an evangelistic template, and 26:28 is not a man who was almost persuaded.

## The three together

**The movement across them is exact.** The human mediator shrinks to nothing — Ananias at length, then briefly, then not at all — while the commission itself grows, nearly doubling by the third telling, and the one who speaks it rises: the Lord speaking *about* Saul, then Ananias speaking *to* Paul, then Christ speaking to him with no one in between. Luke is not abbreviating a story as it becomes familiar. **He is transferring weight from the mediator to the mandate.**

**What does not move is the one thing that matters most.** Three sentences stand in all three accounts in near-identical Greek: *Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις;* — *Τίς εἶ, κύριε;* — *Ἐγώ εἰμι Ἰησοῦς ὃν σὺ διώκεις.* Everything else is fitted to its hearers. **The identification of the risen Christ with his persecuted people is not.**

**And read in order, the three endings are a single argument:**

| | Telling | Ends with |
|---|---|---|
| **9** | the narrator, to the reader | the church at peace |
| **22** | the convert, to Israel, in Hebrew | *"Away with him!"* |
| **26** | the convert, to a king, in Greek | *"He could have been released."* |

**God did it; Israel refused it; Rome could find nothing against it.** That is the book Luke wrote, and these three tellings are where he says it most plainly.
