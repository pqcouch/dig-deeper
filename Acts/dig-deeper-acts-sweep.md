## How to read this sweep

This is planning material, not pulpit preparation. Thirty-three pericopes at proportional
depth cannot each receive what one passage receives in a solo run, and the checking is
the first thing proportional depth cuts. Three consequences follow, and they govern
everything below.

**Every cross-passage claim here is synthetic and capped at moderate confidence** until a
claim audit or a chain verification has tested it — except where the report says a chain
has been verified by lemma against the corpus, which several have. The verified ones are
marked; treat the rest as candidates.

**For any ⭐ HIGH passage, run a full solo dig-deeper before the pulpit.** Sweep depth is
enough to plan a series and to audit an overview. It is not enough to preach.

**Warrant tags.** `[T]` derivable from the text on the page · `[I]` a reasonable inference
from the text · `[S]` supplied by a secondary source and held provisionally. Counts name
their edition, because a count is a feature of a text, not of a book.

**What the corpus showed at the outset.** SBLGNT Acts has 1,002 verses in 28 chapters.
Four verses of the traditional numbering are absent — **8:37, 15:34, 24:7, 28:29** — the
classic Western/Byzantine additions, all four also absent from NA28. SBLGNT further merges
the traditional 19:40–41 into a single 19:40; that is a versification decision, not an
omission. One of the four, **8:37, sits inside a ⭐ HIGH pericope** (the Ethiopian eunuch),
and any hearer with a KJV or NKJV will have it. That is flagged at the pericope.

## Headline Findings

Five book-level claims that emerged from multiple tools converging, each verified by lemma
against the corpus rather than recalled.

1. **The growth-notices that punctuate Acts are an Exodus 1:7 refrain, not a generic
   "and the church grew".** `[T]` for the data; `[I]` for the design. The pair
   **αὐξάνω + πληθύνω** co-occurs in only four verses in the entire New Testament
   (SBLGNT): Acts 6:7, 7:17, 12:24, and 2 Cor 9:10. Exodus 1:7 in Swete reads
   *οἱ δὲ υἱοὶ Ἰσραὴλ **ηὐξήθησαν** καὶ **ἐπληθύνθησαν** … **κατίσχυον σφόδρα σφόδρα***.
   Acts 6:7 carries the same pair **plus σφόδρα**; 19:20 keeps αὐξάνω and swaps πληθύνω
   for **ἴσχυεν**, answering Exodus's κατίσχυον; and Stephen himself uses the pair at 7:17
   of Israel multiplying **in Egypt**. Surfaced by Tool 10 (repetition), Tool 7
   (vocabulary), Tool 3 (the structural seams), and Tool 11 Moves 1 and 4. *High
   confidence on the verbal data; moderate-to-high on authorial design — the
   history-of-interpretation criterion is unchecked (see Open Questions).*

2. **"What hinders?" is the question the book asks at every frontier, and the last word of
   Acts is the answer.** `[T]` **κωλύω** occurs six times — 8:36 (the eunuch: *τί κωλύει
   με βαπτισθῆναι;*), 10:47 (Peter over Cornelius), 11:17 (*τίς ἤμην δυνατὸς κωλῦσαι τὸν
   θεόν;*), 16:6 (the Spirit hindering Paul), 24:23 (Felix forbidding anyone to hinder),
   27:43 (the centurion hindering the soldiers) — and the book closes on **ἀκωλύτως**
   (28:31), a **hapax legomenon in the whole New Testament** (SBLGNT). Surfaced by Tools 7,
   10, 3 and Tool 11 Move 4. *High confidence.*

3. **Peter and Paul are each bound with two chains, and the parallel exists in order to
   break.** `[T]` **ἅλυσις** occurs exactly four times in Acts: 12:6 (Peter *δεδεμένος
   ἁλύσεσιν δυσίν*), 12:7 (*ἐξέπεσαν αὐτοῦ αἱ ἁλύσεις* — they fell off), 21:33 (Paul
   *δεθῆναι ἁλύσεσι δυσί*, verbally identical), 28:20 (*τὴν ἅλυσιν ταύτην περίκειμαι* —
   "I wear this chain", and he wears it *for the hope of Israel*). Peter's chains fall
   off; Paul's do not, and the chain becomes the platform. Surfaced by Tools 7, 10, 15 and
   Tool 11 Move 4. *High confidence — and, unusually, the link survives into both NASB95
   and ESV, so the preacher can show it from the English.*

4. **The salvation Simeon held in his arms is the salvation Paul sends to the nations.**
   `[T]` The noun **σωτήριον** appears in Luke–Acts exactly three times: Luke 2:30 (*εἶδον
   οἱ ὀφθαλμοί μου τὸ σωτήριόν σου*), Luke 3:6 (Isa 40:5 LXX, *ὄψεται πᾶσα σὰρξ τὸ
   σωτήριον τοῦ θεοῦ*), and **Acts 28:28** (*τοῖς ἔθνεσιν ἀπεστάλη τοῦτο τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ
   θεοῦ*). Five occurrences in the whole NT. The two-volume work opens and closes on the
   same rare word. Surfaced by Tool 11 Moves 1, 2 and 4, Tool 7, Tool 14. *High
   confidence on the data; moderate-to-high that Luke intends the inclusio.*

5. **The trials are the mission, not an interruption of it — and Luke acquits Paul as
   often as he acquitted Jesus.** `[T]` Four official verdicts of innocence stand in the
   last six chapters: 23:9 (*οὐδὲν κακὸν εὑρίσκομεν*), 23:29 (*μηδὲν ἄξιον θανάτου ἢ
   δεσμῶν*), 25:25 (*μηδὲν ἄξιον θανάτου*), 26:31 (*οὐδὲν θανάτου ἢ δεσμῶν ἄξιον*), with
   26:32 adding that he could have been released. Against them stand three **δεῖ**
   statements that route him to Rome anyway (19:21; 23:11; 27:24). The innocence is real
   *and* the chain holds — which is the point. Surfaced by Tool 2 (positional necessity),
   Tool 12 (genre — the trial scenes are the vehicle), Tool 10, and Tool 11 Move 4.
   *High confidence.*

## Book-level positional frame (Phase 0.6)

**What the preceding movement set up.** Acts is volume two. Luke 24 closes with a risen
Christ opening the Scriptures, announcing that repentance and forgiveness will be preached
*εἰς πάντα τὰ ἔθνη* beginning from Jerusalem, and telling the disciples to stay in the
city until clothed with power. Acts 1:1 names the first volume as what Jesus **began**
(*ἤρξατο*) to do and teach. Every claim Acts makes rests on that unfinished participle.

**Why the book exists here.** Because Luke 24 has issued a commission that nothing in the
Gospel executes and made a promise that nothing in the Gospel delivers. Acts exists to
narrate the execution and the delivery — and, structurally, to answer the disciples'
question of 1:6 (*is it at this time you are restoring the kingdom to Israel?*) not by
correction alone but by twenty-eight chapters of demonstration.

**Implication.** The book has a hinge it declares itself: **βασιλεία** occurs eight times
in Acts (1:3, 1:6, 8:12, 14:22, 19:8, 20:25, 28:23, 28:31) `[T]`, and the first and last
are both *the kingdom of God* on the lips of a teacher — Jesus for forty days, Paul for
two years. The overview's inclusio claim here is confirmed by count. What the overview
does not say is that the kingdom drops out of the middle of the book almost entirely: five
occurrences carry the whole span from ch. 8 to ch. 20.

## 1. Acts 1:1–11 — Ascension and commission ⭐

**Position.** Q1: Luke 24:47–49 has issued a commission and made a promise; neither is
executed in volume one. Q2: this passage exists first because it is the hinge between the
volumes — *ἤρξατο* (1:1) makes everything following a continuation, and 1:8 supplies the
book's own table of contents. Nothing later in Acts is intelligible as a fresh start.

**Structure.** Three movements: the recapitulation and the promise (1–5); the question and
the commission (6–8); the ascension and the angelic word (9–11). The seam is the disciples'
question at v.6, which the risen Christ neither grants nor denies — he re-times it
(*χρόνους ἢ καιρούς*) and re-directs it.

**Text-first findings.**

- **ἕως ἐσχάτου τῆς γῆς** (1:8) `[T]`. **ἔσχατος** occurs three times in Acts: 1:8, 2:17
  (*ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις ἡμέραις*, Joel), 13:47 (Isa 49:6). Luke uses the word for the
  end of *space* and the end of *time*, and he supplies the source of the spatial phrase
  himself at 13:47. *High confidence.*
- **μάρτυς** (1:8) `[T]` — 13 occurrences (1:8, 1:22, 2:32, 3:15, 5:32, 6:13, 7:58, 10:39,
  10:41, 13:31, 22:15, 22:20, 26:16). Note 6:13 and 7:58: the only two occurrences before
  ch. 10 that are *not* apostolic testimony are the false witnesses against Stephen and
  the witnesses who lay their coats at Saul's feet. Luke lets the word darken before he
  gives it to Paul (22:15). *Moderate-to-high; the ironic use is [I].*
- **ἀτενίζω** (1:10) `[T]` — 10 occurrences, a distinctively Lukan gaze-verb (1:10, 3:4,
  3:12, 6:15, 7:55, 10:4, 11:6, 13:9, 14:9, 23:1). Twice here and at 7:55 it is a gaze
  *into heaven*; the angels rebuke the first and heaven answers the second.
- **ἀναλαμβάνω** (1:2, 1:11, 1:22) `[T]` — the ascension is named three times in
  twenty-two verses, twice by the angels in one sentence (v.11 has *εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν* four
  times). Luke is hammering the location of the acting subject.

**Tool 11.** No external citation. *Internal:* **planted for later** — 1:8's three
geographical stages are picked up at 8:1 (Judea and Samaria), 8:14, 9:31 (Judea, Galilee,
Samaria) and 13:47 (the end of the earth); the *forty days* of 1:3 answers nothing earlier
in Acts but rhymes with Luke 4:2. *Moderate.* The **βασιλεία** of 1:3 is answered by the
βασιλεία of 28:31 — an inclusio the count supports.

**Copycat.** The ascension is a **unique event**; the waiting is **unique**; the promise of
1:8 is **unique in its apostolic form** (eyewitnesses of the resurrection, 1:22) and
**normative in its substance** (the church testifies to the risen Christ). The two must be
distinguished or the passage becomes a recruitment poster.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 1:8 as a missions strategy — "Jerusalem = your town, Judea = your
county". The phrase *to the end of the earth* is a quotation (13:47 says so), and its
referent in Isaiah 49:6 is the **nations**, not a radius. Preach it as a promise about who
will be gathered, not a diagram of concentric circles.

## 2. Acts 1:12–26 — Waiting, and the twelfth man

**Position.** Q1: 1:8 has promised power to *witnesses*, and 1:22 defines a witness as one
who accompanied Jesus from John's baptism to the ascension — a class that is closing.
Q2: this passage exists here because Pentecost must fall on a *complete* Twelve; the number
is the point, and Luke settles it before the Spirit comes, not after.

**Structure.** Inventory (12–14) — Peter's exposition of Judas (15–20) — the criterion and
the choice (21–26).

**Text-first findings.**

- **δεῖ (1:21) and ἔδει (1:16)** `[T]`. The impersonal *δεῖ/ἔδει* occurs 19 times in Acts
  (1:16, 1:21, 3:21, 4:12, 5:29, 9:6, 9:16, 14:22, 15:5, 16:30, 17:3, 19:21, 19:36, 20:35,
  23:11, 24:19, 25:10, 27:24, 27:26; SBLGNT, counting finite forms). **Two of the nineteen
  are false necessities on the lips of opponents** — 15:5 (*δεῖ περιτέμνειν*) and 19:36
  (the town clerk) — which the narrative overturns. The overview's "recurring divine *dei*"
  is right in the main and should not be stated as a rule without that exception.
- **κλῆρος** (1:17, 1:26) and **λαγχάνω** `[T]` — the lot language is dense, and it never
  recurs as a decision procedure after the Spirit falls. That silence is the interpretive
  key to the Copycat question below.
- **ὁμοθυμαδόν** (1:14) `[T]` — 10 occurrences (1:14, 2:46, 4:24, 5:12, 7:57, 8:6, 12:20,
  15:25, 18:12, 19:29). **Four of the ten describe hostile crowds** (7:57 the mob stoning
  Stephen; 12:20 Tyre and Sidon; 18:12 the Corinthian Jews; 19:29 the Ephesian riot). Luke
  will not let "with one accord" be a purely churchly virtue. *[T] on the count; [I] on
  the irony. High confidence on the data.*

**Tool 11.** **Psalms 69:25 and 109:8 → 1:20** *(high confidence — direct quotation,
formula γέγραπται)*. *Source context:* both are imprecatory psalms of the righteous
sufferer against a treacherous companion; Ps 69 is among the most heavily used psalms in
the NT passion tradition, and Ps 109 asks that another take the office of the accuser.
Luke cites the *second* half of each — the vacated dwelling and the vacated office — so the
citation is about a **post to be filled**, not about Judas's fate. *Book usage:* first
citation of the Psalter in Acts; Ps 16 and 110 follow within one chapter, so Luke's opening
move is to establish the Psalter as the book that explains what has happened. *OT-to-OT:*
Pss 69 and 109 are already paired in the canonical lament tradition of the betrayed
righteous one — Luke is not inventing the pairing. *What it adds:* the apostolic circle is
not repaired by human tidiness; Scripture required the vacancy to be filled.
*Internal:* **planted** — *τὸν τόπον τὸν ἴδιον* (1:25) is the first of eighteen uses of
**τόπος**, a word that becomes load-bearing in ch. 7 and again at 21:28.

**Copycat.** **Unique event** throughout. The Twelve are constituted, not recruited;
the criterion (1:21–22) is unrepeatable by definition; the lot belongs to the pre-Pentecost
period and Luke never uses it again. *This is the clearest Copycat case in the book and a
useful place to teach the tool.*

**Pitfall.** "Casting lots for guidance." The passage is the last time it happens, and Luke's
silence afterwards is the comment. The corrective is to notice what *replaces* the lot in
Acts: the Spirit speaking (13:2), the congregation choosing (6:3–5), and reasoned counsel
under Scripture (15:15–21).

## 3. Acts 2:1–41 — Pentecost and Peter's sermon ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 1:4–5 and 1:8 have promised the Spirit and named the fruit as witness.
Q2: this passage exists here because a promise made in ch. 1 must be kept before anything
else can be narrated, and because the *content* of the witness — a crucified man raised and
enthroned — has to be preached once at length before it can be assumed for twenty-six
chapters. Peter's sermon is the book's doctrinal foundation deposit.

**Structure.** The event (1–4) — the crowd's bewilderment and the nations-list (5–13) —
the sermon in three movements, each anchored by a citation: Joel (14–21), the resurrection
by Ps 16 (22–32), the enthronement by Ps 110 (33–36) — the response (37–41).

**Text-first findings.**

- **γλῶσσα** (2:3, 2:4, 2:11) `[T]` — six occurrences in Acts (2:3, 2:4, 2:11, 2:26, 10:46,
  19:6). At 2:3 it is *tongues as of fire*, at 2:4 *other tongues*, at 2:11 *our own
  tongues*: Luke moves the word from image to faculty to language in eight verses. And
  2:26 — inside the Ps 16 citation — is *my tongue rejoiced*, the same noun. *[T]; the
  2:26 resonance is [I], moderate.*
- **ἐκχέω** (2:17, 2:18, 2:33) `[T]` — six occurrences (1:18, 2:17, 2:18, 2:33, 10:45,
  22:20). The Spirit is *poured out* three times here, once at Cornelius (10:45) — and the
  other two are **Judas's entrails poured out** (1:18) and **Stephen's blood poured out**
  (22:20). Luke uses one verb for outpoured Spirit and outpoured blood. *[T] on the count;
  [I] on the intent; moderate confidence — worth a commentary check.*
- **βουλή** (2:23) `[T]` — seven occurrences (2:23, 4:28, 5:38, 13:36, 20:27, 27:12, 27:42).
  **Four are God's purpose and stand** (2:23, 4:28, 13:36, 20:27); **three are human plans
  that fail or are overruled** (5:38 Gamaliel's hypothesis, 27:12 the sailors' decision to
  sail, 27:42 the soldiers' plan to kill the prisoners). The distribution is itself the
  theology. *High confidence — verified by lemma.*
- **ἀσφαλῶς** (2:36) `[T]` — the cognate of the *ἀσφάλεια* Luke promised Theophilus in
  Luke 1:4. The first sermon's conclusion delivers the two-volume purpose statement in one
  adverb. *Moderate-to-high.*

**Tool 11.** Three citations; the triad is run on the first two.

**Joel 3:1–5 LXX (Eng. 2:28–32) → 2:17–21** *(high confidence — direct quotation, named
prophet)*. *Source context:* Joel's oracle stands after a locust devastation and a call to
return, and immediately after a promise that the years the locusts ate will be restored;
the outpouring is the sign that the LORD is in the midst of Israel, and it runs straight
into the day of the LORD and the gathering in Zion. Luke's citation stops at *everyone who
calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved* — the last line before Joel's Zion-deliverance
clause. *Book usage:* first prophetic citation in Acts; it establishes *ἐν ταῖς ἐσχάταις
ἡμέραις* as the book's temporal frame, and Luke never revisits Joel. He does not need to:
2:21's *πᾶς ὃς ἂν ἐπικαλέσηται* is picked up by **ἐπικαλέω**, which occurs 20 times in Acts
— and by 25:11 the same verb means *I appeal to Caesar*. `[T]` *That drift is not
accidental and is worth its own audit.* *OT-to-OT:* Joel's day-of-the-LORD language is
already in conversation with Isaiah and Malachi; Luke does not exploit that here.
*What it adds:* the outpouring is not a new thing but a dated thing — the last days have
begun, and the sign of their beginning is that prophecy has become common property.

**Psalm 16:8–11 → 2:25–28** *(high confidence — direct quotation)*. *Source context:*
David's psalm of confidence, ending in the presence of God and fullness of joy; the cited
verse is its climax, not an aside. *Book usage:* returns at 13:35 in Paul's Pisidian
sermon, with the same *διαφθορά* argument — **the only OT text Luke gives to both Peter and
Paul in the same argumentative role** `[T]`, which is the strongest single piece of evidence
for the overview's Peter–Paul parallelism claim. *OT-to-OT:* paired here with Ps 110 (v.34)
and 2 Sam 7 (v.30) — the Davidic covenant texts were already a cluster before Luke reached
them. *What it adds:* the argument is exegetical, not merely evidential — David's tomb is
in Jerusalem (2:29), so the psalm must speak of another.

**Psalm 110:1 → 2:34–35** *(high confidence)*. Move 1 only: the enthronement oracle, the
NT's most-cited OT verse, supplying the *right hand* that Stephen will see occupied
(7:55–56).

*Internal:* **answers §1:4–5** — the promise is kept — and **plants** the *τρισχίλιαι*
(2:41) against which 4:4's five thousand and 21:20's *μυριάδες* are later measured.

**Copycat.** Pentecost is a **unique event** — Luke marks it as the fulfilment of a dated
promise, not a template. The **apostolic preaching pattern** is a **normative pattern**.
The community's response (2:38) is **prescriptive**. Distinguishing these three within one
pericope is the whole discipline of Tool 13.

**Pitfall.** Making 2:38 a formula for receiving the Spirit and reading 2:4 as the model
experience. The corrective is the book's own witness: at 8:16 the Spirit comes after
baptism, at 10:44 before it, at 19:6 with the laying on of hands. Four frontier moments,
four sequences — because Luke is narrating boundaries being crossed, not writing a manual.

## 4. Acts 2:42–47 — The Spirit-formed community

**Position.** Q1: 2:41 has added three thousand people to a body with no structures. Q2:
the passage exists here to answer the question the number raises — *what does such a
community actually do?* — before ch. 3 shows it acting in public.

**Structure.** A four-fold devotion (42) — awe and signs (43) — goods and generosity
(44–45) — daily rhythm and growth (46–47). Not a narrative; a summary, and Luke's first.

**Text-first findings.**

- **προσκαρτερέω** (2:42, 2:46) `[T]` — used of the community twice here, of the apostles'
  prayer at 1:14 and 6:4, and — pointedly — of **Simon Magus attaching himself to Philip**
  at 8:13. Luke's verb for devoted adherence is used once of a man whose heart is not right
  before God. *[T]; the irony [I], moderate.*
- **κοινός** (2:44; 4:32) `[T]` — five occurrences (2:44, 4:32, 10:14, 10:28, 11:8). **Two
  mean "held in common"; three mean "ritually common, i.e. unclean"** on Peter's lips in the
  Cornelius cycle. The same adjective that describes the church's property describes the
  food Peter will not eat. Luke does not comment; the collocation is there for a reader who
  is paying attention. *[T] on the count; the theological weight is [I], moderate.*
- **κλάω / ἄρτος** `[T]` — *κλάω* four times (2:46, 20:7, 20:11, 27:35), *ἄρτος* five
  (2:42, 2:46, 20:7, 20:11, 27:35). The breaking of bread appears at exactly three moments:
  the Jerusalem community, Troas, and **Paul on a sinking ship in front of 276 pagans**
  (27:35, with *εὐχαριστήσας*). *High confidence — verified by lemma.*

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **planted for later** — 2:45's selling and
distributing is developed at 4:32–35, tested at 5:1–11, institutionalised at 6:1–6, and
exported at 11:29. Luke follows one thread across five pericopes. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The four devotions (42) are **normative**; the community of goods is
**descriptive** — Luke's own qualifiers make this plain (*καθότι ἄν τις χρείαν εἶχεν*, 2:45,
"as anyone had need", i.e. as required, not as a rule), and 5:4 states outright that
Ananias's property remained his own. The daily temple attendance is **culturally located**,
not transferable.

**Pitfall.** Two opposite errors, both common. First, preaching 2:44–45 as a mandate for
common ownership; second, so hurrying to say it *isn't* a mandate that the passage's actual
sharpness is lost. Guard against the first with 5:4; guard against the second by noticing
that Luke says it twice (2:44–45; 4:32–35) and tells a death story about lying over it.
The text is not neutral about money.

## 5. Acts 3:1–26 — The lame man and the prophet like Moses

**Position.** Q1: 2:43 has said signs and wonders were happening through the apostles, and
2:46 has put the community in the temple daily. Q2: this passage exists here to *show* one
of those signs and to demonstrate what the temple-going community does when the temple
crowd gathers — it preaches. The healing exists for the sermon; Luke gives ten verses to
the miracle and fifteen to the address.

**Structure.** The encounter and healing (1–10) — the crowd (11) — the sermon: what you did
and what God did (12–16), the ignorance-and-repentance appeal (17–21), the scriptural
warrant from Moses and Abraham (22–26).

**Text-first findings.**

- **στερεόω** (3:7, 3:16) `[T]` — three occurrences in Acts, and the third is **16:5**,
  where *the churches were made strong in the faith*. The verb that describes a beggar's
  ankles describes the churches of Asia Minor. *High confidence on the chain — verified by
  lemma. Both NASB95 and ESV obscure it: "strengthened" at 16:5 but "were made strong" /
  "were strengthened" at 3:7 — the link survives in NASB95, not in ESV.*
- **ὄνομα** (3:6, 3:16 ×2) `[T]` — the noun that carries the book: 60 occurrences in Acts.
  Here it is the instrument (*ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι*), the object of faith (*ἡ πίστις τοῦ ὀνόματος
  αὐτοῦ*), and the agent (*ἐστερέωσεν τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ*) inside two verses.
- **ἀρχηγός** (3:15) `[T]` — twice in Acts, both of Jesus (3:15 *ἀρχηγὸν τῆς ζωῆς*; 5:31
  *ἀρχηγὸν καὶ σωτῆρα*). A leader-founder word; the contrast with *φονέα* (3:14, the
  murderer they asked for) is deliberate — they chose a taker of life over the author of it.
  *High confidence.*
- **ἀποκατάστασις** (3:21) `[T]` — a NT hapax, and the cognate verb *ἀποκαθιστάνεις* is what
  the disciples asked at 1:6. **Luke answers the ch. 1 question in ch. 3**: there *is* a
  restoration, its scope is *πάντων*, and its timing is heaven's. *[T] on the cognate link;
  high confidence — this is a clean Move 4 finding the overview does not have.*

**Tool 11.** Two significant citations.

**Deuteronomy 18:15–19 → 3:22–23** *(high confidence — direct quotation with named
speaker)*. *Source context:* Moses at the end of the wilderness, contrasting the prophet
God will raise with the divination of the nations; the passage's sanction (*I will require
it of him*) is what Luke sharpens into *ἐξολεθρευθήσεται ἐκ τοῦ λαοῦ* — a phrase drawn from
the Levitical cutting-off formula rather than Deuteronomy's own wording. *Book usage:*
returns at 7:37 in Stephen's speech, where it is the hinge of the whole Moses section —
so Luke plants it in Peter's mouth and detonates it in Stephen's. *OT-to-OT:* Deut 18 and
Gen 22:18 (cited four verses later at 3:25) are both promise-texts about a coming
beneficiary of Israel's obedience; Luke pairs the prophet and the seed. *What it adds:*
the sermon's warning is not "you rejected a good teacher" but "you are at the point where
Moses said the nation's membership is decided".

**Genesis 22:18 / 12:3 → 3:25** *(high confidence)*. Move 1: the Abrahamic promise in its
post-Akedah form, sworn after the substituted ram. *Book usage:* Abraham is named seven
times in Acts (3:13, 3:25, 7:2, 7:16, 7:17, 7:32, 13:26); ch. 7 is where the promise is
worked out at length. *OT-to-OT:* Gen 22 and Deut 18 are here made to say the same thing —
the nations are the horizon. *What it adds:* the Gentile mission is announced in the second
sermon of the book, to a Jerusalem temple crowd, before anyone has crossed a boundary.
The overview places the Gentile question at ch. 10; the vocabulary places it at 3:25.

*Internal:* **answers §1:6** (ἀποκατάστασις) and **plants** the *παῖς* title (3:13, 3:26)
which returns at 4:27, 4:30 and controls the Isa 53 reading at 8:32–33.

**Copycat.** The healing is **descriptive and apostolic** — Luke marks the man as over
forty years old and lame from birth (3:2; 4:22) precisely to make it unmistakable, i.e.
evidential rather than exemplary. The **posture** the miracle implies is worth naming: the
man asked for money and received what he did not ask for; nothing in the account turns on
his faith prior to the act (3:16's faith is *through him*, i.e. through Jesus). Preach
receiving, not achieving.

**Pitfall.** "Silver and gold have I none" as a text about church poverty or about
expecting healings on demand. The sermon Luke attaches tells you what the sign was for:
*τί θαυμάζετε … ὡς ἰδίᾳ δυνάμει;* (3:12). The miracle exists to remove the apostles from
the centre of the frame, and a sermon that puts them back has inverted it.

## 6. Acts 4:1–31 — First arrest and the boldness prayer

**Position.** Q1: 3:12–26 has preached the resurrection in the temple precinct. Q2: this
passage exists here because the Sadducees controlled the temple and denied the
resurrection (Luke says so at 23:8), so preaching *that* message in *that* place had to
produce this collision. The arrest is not an interruption of the sermon; it is the sermon's
consequence, and Luke has arranged the two chapters to make the causality visible.

**Structure.** Arrest and the count of converts (1–4) — interrogation and Peter's answer
(5–12) — the council's dilemma and its order (13–22) — the church's prayer and the shaking
(23–31). The pattern *threat → prayer → boldness* will recur.

**Text-first findings.**

- **παρρησία / παρρησιάζομαι** `[T]`. The noun occurs five times (2:29, 4:13, 4:29, 4:31,
  **28:31**); the verb seven (9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, 19:8, 26:26). **Three of the
  five noun-occurrences are in this pericope**, and the fifth is the last clause of the
  book. What the church asks for at 4:29 it is still doing at 28:31. *High confidence —
  verified by lemma. This is one of the cleanest inclusio-style findings in Acts.*
- **ὄνομα** in this pericope `[T]` — 4:7, 4:10, 4:12, 4:17, 4:18, 4:30. Six occurrences in
  thirty-one verses. The council's whole strategy is to suppress *a name*, and Luke's
  answer at 4:12 is that there is no other under heaven. Tool 10 and Tool 7 converge.
- **σῴζω / σωτηρία** (4:9, 4:12) `[T]` — the verb used of the healed man (*σέσωσται*, 4:9)
  and of eternal salvation (4:12) in four verses. English versions split them ("made well"
  / "saved"); the Greek does not. *High confidence.* This is a **translation-loss** finding,
  not a variant: no English can carry one word through both senses.
- **ἀγράμματοι … καὶ ἰδιῶται** (4:13) `[T]` — not "uneducated" in the modern sense but
  "without formal rabbinic training, private persons". *Moderate confidence on the precise
  force; recommend BDAG.*

**Tool 11.** Two citations.

**Psalm 118:22 → 4:11** *(high confidence — allusion in quotation form)*. *Source context:*
the last of the Hallel psalms, sung at the festivals; the rejected stone stands in a psalm
of processional thanksgiving after deliverance, immediately before *this is the day the LORD
has made*. *Book usage:* the only use in Acts; Luke has already given it to Jesus in the
Gospel (Luke 20:17). *OT-to-OT:* Ps 118 and Isa 28:16 are already a stone-pair in Jewish
exegesis. *What it adds:* Peter's *ὑφ' ὑμῶν τῶν οἰκοδόμων* — "by you, the builders" — turns
the psalm's generic builders into the men in the room. The citation is an indictment
delivered in the second person.

**Psalm 2:1–2 → 4:25–26** *(high confidence — direct quotation with formula)*. *Source
context:* the enthronement psalm; the raging nations are answered by the LORD's laughter
and the installation of his king on Zion. *Book usage:* returns at 13:33 (*You are my Son*)
— **Luke cites the psalm's opening at Jerusalem and its centre at Pisidian Antioch**, so he
is following Psalm 2's own internal movement across the book, exactly as Move 2 is designed
to catch. *[I], moderate-to-high.* *OT-to-OT:* Ps 2 and Ps 110 (cited at 2:34) are the
Psalter's two great enthronement texts and were already read together. *What it adds:* the
prayer's identification of the players (4:27 — Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, peoples of Israel)
is an exegesis of the psalm's four categories, and the church's request that follows is
*not* for rescue but for boldness. That gap between what they might have asked and what
they did ask is the pericope's homiletical centre.

*Internal:* **answers §2:23** — the *βουλή* of God foreordained (4:28 repeats *βουλή* and
adds *προώρισεν*), so the prayer applies Peter's Pentecost theology to the church's own
danger. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The prayer of 4:24–30 is a **normative pattern** — Luke gives it in full, and
its shape (God as Creator → Scripture → present situation → petition) is imitable. The
shaking of the place (4:31) is **descriptive**. The apostles' civil disobedience (4:19–20)
is a **normative principle** with a stated warrant (*Is it right before God…?*), confirmed
by 5:29.

**Pitfall.** Turning 4:12 into a debating point and losing its setting. Peter says it while
under arrest, to the men who killed Jesus, about a healed beggar standing in front of them.
Preached as an abstract exclusivity claim it becomes a weapon; preached in its setting it is
a testimony given by a prisoner who has just been told to stop.

## 7. Acts 4:32–5:11 — Barnabas, Ananias and Sapphira

**Position.** Q1: 2:44–45 has described a community holding things in common; 4:32–35
repeats and intensifies it. Q2: this passage exists here because a summary statement that
has been made twice now has to be tested, and Luke tests it with two named examples — one
that proves it and one that destroys itself against it. The pairing is the argument.

**Structure.** A sandwich. Summary of the sharing (4:32–35) — **Barnabas, the true case**
(4:36–37) — **Ananias and Sapphira, the false case** (5:1–11). Luke's technique here is
Tool 3's *sandwich* in its clearest form in Acts.

**Text-first findings.**

- **παρὰ τοὺς πόδας τῶν ἀποστόλων** `[T]` — 4:35, 4:37, 5:2. Three times in nine verses:
  Barnabas lays the money at the apostles' feet, Ananias lays part of it at the apostles'
  feet, and at 5:10 **the feet of the young men who buried her are at the door**. Luke
  builds the pericope on a repeated prepositional phrase and then turns it. *High
  confidence — the repetition is [T]; the turn at 5:10 is [I], moderate-to-high.*
- **νοσφίζομαι** (5:2, 5:3) `[T]` — a rare verb, "to put aside for oneself, embezzle".
  **In the Swete LXX it occurs exactly twice — Joshua 7:1 (Achan) and 2 Maccabees 4:32
  (Menelaus stealing temple vessels) — and in the NT exactly three times: Acts 5:2, 5:3 and
  Titus 2:10.** Both Greek-OT uses concern misappropriating what is devoted. Joshua 7:1
  reads *ἐνοσφίσαντο **ἀπὸ** τοῦ ἀναθέματος*; Acts 5:2 reads *ἐνοσφίσατο **ἀπὸ** τῆς τιμῆς*
  — same verb, same preposition, same construction. *Verified by normalised search of the
  Swete text and of the SBLGNT lemma index.* See the citation triad below.
- **φόβος μέγας** (5:5, 5:11) `[T]` — *φόβος* occurs five times in Acts (2:43, 5:5, 5:11,
  9:31, 19:17), and **9:31 makes the fear of the Lord a condition of the church's growth**.
  Luke does not treat fear as a problem to be managed.
- **ἐκκλησία** (5:11) `[T]` — **the first occurrence of the word in Acts**, and it arrives
  attached to a death. Twenty-three occurrences in total; this is number one.

**Tool 11.** **Joshua 7:1 → 5:2–3** *(moderate-to-high confidence — distinctive verbal
echo, no citation formula)*. *Source context:* Achan takes from the devoted things at the
very moment Israel crosses into the inheritance; the sin is hidden, the community is
defeated because of it, the offender is exposed by lot, he and his household die, and the
place is renamed. The narrative function of Josh 7 is to establish that entry into the
promise does not suspend the holiness of the God who gave it. *Book usage:* Joshua is not
otherwise cited in Acts, so this is a *cold* allusion by the clustering test and its
recurrence score is low — which is why the confidence is capped below "high" despite the
volume. *OT-to-OT:* Josh 7 is itself in conversation with the Deuteronomic ḥerem laws;
nothing in Acts exploits that further. *What it adds:* the death is not disproportionate
punishment for a financial lie but the reappearance of a category — the community is holy
ground, and the first recorded sin inside it is the first sin inside the land. It also
explains Luke's *φόβος μέγας* twice (5:5, 5:11): Joshua 7 ends the same way.

*Internal:* **answers §2:44–45 and §4:32** — the two summaries are tested here — and
**plants** the money-and-power question that returns at 8:18–24 (Simon offers money for the
Spirit) and 24:26 (Felix hopes for money from Paul). *Moderate-to-high.*

**Copycat.** Ananias and Sapphira are a **negative example** and a **unique event** at
once — Luke does not generalise the outcome, and no later sin in Acts is punished this way.
Barnabas is a **normative pattern**, and Luke says so by keeping him in the story for eleven
more chapters. The apostolic authority displayed is **unique**.

**Pitfall.** Preaching this as a warning about giving. The sin Peter names is not
withholding — 5:4 says explicitly that the property and the proceeds were theirs to keep —
but **lying to the Holy Spirit** in order to buy a reputation the money had not paid for.
The corrective is to preach the sin Luke names, and to notice that the passage's terror is
aimed at a church that had just been described in idyllic terms.

## 8. Acts 5:12–42 — Before the Sanhedrin, and Gamaliel

**Position.** Q1: 4:18 has ordered silence; 4:31 has recorded that they kept speaking. Q2:
this passage exists here because a prohibition that has been defied must be enforced, and
Luke uses the second, harsher hearing to introduce the question the whole book will answer:
*is this from God?* Gamaliel asks it; twenty-three chapters answer it.

**Structure.** Growth and healings (12–16) — arrest, angelic release, re-arrest (17–26) —
the hearing and Peter's answer (27–32) — Gamaliel (33–39) — flogging, joy, and continued
teaching (40–42).

**Text-first findings.**

- **καταλύω** (5:38, 5:39) `[T]` — **exactly three occurrences in Acts, and the third is
  6:14**, where the false witnesses say Jesus *will destroy this place*. Gamaliel's word for
  what happens to human plans, and for what cannot happen to God's, is the word the next
  pericope puts in the mouth of perjurers about the temple. *High confidence on the chain —
  verified by lemma; the ironic arrangement is [I], moderate-to-high. The overview does not
  have this.*
- **βουλή** (5:38) `[T]` — Gamaliel's *ἡ βουλὴ αὕτη* is the third of the seven; see the
  distribution at Acts 2:23 above. He uses the word for a merely human plan, and the reader
  who has heard 2:23 and 4:28 already knows the answer to his conditional.
- **πειθαρχεῖν δεῖ θεῷ μᾶλλον ἢ ἀνθρώποις** (5:29) `[T]` — *πειθαρχέω* occurs four times in
  Acts (5:29, 5:32, 27:21), of which two are here; the third is **Paul telling the sailors
  they should have obeyed him** (27:21). Luke's obedience-word travels a long way.
- **χαίροντες … κατηξιώθησαν … ἀτιμασθῆναι** (5:41) `[T]` — the paradox is built into the
  syntax: *counted worthy to be dishonoured*. Tool 9's tonal centre for the pericope.

**Tool 11.** No external citation. *Internal:* **answers §4:17–21** (the order, now
enforced) and **plants** Gamaliel — who returns at 22:3 as **Paul's teacher**, named by
Paul in his own defence in the same city `[T]`. That is a Move 4 finding with real
homiletical weight: the man who said *leave them alone, lest you be found fighting against
God* trained the man who fought against God hardest, and Luke lets the reader make the
connection without comment. *High confidence on the link; the irony [I].*

**Copycat.** The prison release is **descriptive and unique**; the courage is a **normative
pattern** with a stated principle (5:29); the joy under flogging (5:41) is **normative** and
is the hardest thing in the pericope to preach honestly. Gamaliel's counsel is
**descriptive** — Luke reports it approvingly as providence, not as a maxim (its logic, that
whatever survives is from God, is not a principle Acts endorses; the church's survival in
Acts is never argued from mere durability).

**Pitfall.** Using Gamaliel's test — "if it is of God you cannot stop it" — as a rule for
assessing movements today. It is a Pharisee's prudential advice, reported not endorsed, and
its premise is false as a general rule. The corrective: Luke's own test for what is from God
is not survival but conformity to the Scriptures and the witnessed resurrection (17:11;
26:22–23).

## 9. Acts 6:1–7 — The Seven, and the first seam

**Position.** Q1: the community has been described as sharing goods (2:45; 4:35) and as
growing rapidly (2:41; 4:4; 5:14). Q2: this passage exists here because those two facts
collide: a distribution system built for a hundred and twenty cannot serve a body containing
Greek-speaking widows, and the first internal fracture in Acts is a *linguistic and cultural*
one. Luke puts it immediately before Stephen because the solution to the fracture produces
the man who takes the gospel out of Jerusalem.

**Structure.** The complaint (1) — the Twelve's proposal (2–4) — the choice and commissioning
(5–6) — **the seam** (7).

**Text-first findings.**

- **6:7 is the first of the growth-notices, and it is the fullest** `[T]`:
  *ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ **ηὔξανεν**, καὶ **ἐπληθύνετο** ὁ ἀριθμὸς τῶν μαθητῶν ἐν Ἰερουσαλὴμ
  **σφόδρα***. Both Exodus verbs plus Exodus's adverb. **Note that it has two subjects** —
  the word grows, *and* the number of disciples multiplies. See Book-Overview Tensions.
  *High confidence — verified by lemma; NA28 reads τοῦ θεοῦ.*
- **πληθύνω at 6:1 and 6:7** `[T]` — the pericope opens and closes on the same verb: the
  disciples were multiplying (and that caused the problem), and the disciples multiplied
  (and that was the result). Luke frames a crisis with the word for the blessing that caused
  it. *High confidence.*
- **διακονία / διακονέω** (6:1, 6:2, 6:4) `[T]` — the same root for waiting on tables and
  for the ministry of the word. The Twelve do not rank two ministries; they decline to do
  both, and Luke uses one word for both.
- **Ἑλληνιστής** (6:1) `[T]` — three occurrences (6:1, 9:29, 11:20). The group that
  complains here is the group Saul argues with (9:29) and the group first evangelised at
  Antioch (11:20). *High confidence on the chain; its significance is [I].*
- **The seven names are all Greek** `[T]`, and the last, Nicolaus, is a **proselyte from
  Antioch** — the city that will send out the Gentile mission. Luke's list is doing
  narrative work.

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **planted** — Stephen (6:5) and Philip (6:5) are
introduced here and carry chs. 6–8; **answers §2:45/4:35** by institutionalising what had
been spontaneous. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The **principle** — leaders should not abandon prayer and the word to
administer relief, and the congregation should choose its own servants — is a **normative
pattern**, and Luke marks it as good by attaching a growth-notice. The **office** is not
named "deacon" here `[T]`; the noun does not occur, only the verb. Do not read the later
office back into the text as though the passage established it.

**Pitfall.** Preaching this as a church-management text and missing that Luke's interest is
the *ethnic* fracture. The complaint is Hellenist against Hebrew; the solution puts seven
Hellenists in charge; and the immediate consequence is that a Hellenist preaches the sermon
that sends the gospel out of Jerusalem. The passage is about the mission crossing its first
internal boundary, and the widows are how it happens.

## 10. Acts 6:8–7:60 — Stephen's speech and martyrdom ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 6:7 has recorded a great many priests becoming obedient to the faith —
which puts the movement's growth inside the temple establishment — and 6:5 has produced
Stephen. Q2: this passage exists at the exact centre of the Jerusalem section because it is
where the temple question becomes explicit and unanswerable, and because the scattering it
causes (8:1–4) is the mechanism for stage two of 1:8. Luke needs a death to move the gospel
out of the city, and he has arranged sixty verses of speech to explain why.

**Structure.** The charge (6:8–15) — the speech in four movements: Abraham outside the land
(7:2–8), Joseph and the patriarchs (7:9–16), Moses rejected and vindicated (7:17–43), the
tent and the house (7:44–50) — the indictment (7:51–53) — the vision and the death
(7:54–60).

**Text-first findings.**

- **τόπος** `[T]` — the charge is that Stephen speaks *against this holy place* (6:13) and
  that Jesus will destroy *this place* (6:14). The speech answers by using **τόπος** three
  times of somewhere else: the place where Abraham's seed would worship (7:7), the holy
  ground **in Midian** (7:33), and the *place of my rest* God says no house can be (7:49).
  Eighteen occurrences of *τόπος* in Acts; five of them are here. **This is the Leitwort of
  the pericope, and it is the answer to the charge.** *High confidence — verified by lemma.
  The overview does not have it.*
- **σκηνή** `[T]` — **three occurrences in Acts, and all three matter**: 7:43 the tent of
  Moloch (what Israel carried), 7:44 the tent of witness (what God commanded), **15:16 the
  tent of David** (what God will rebuild, in the Amos citation at the Council). Stephen sets
  up a contrast between two tents; James announces the raising of a third. *High confidence
  on the count — verified by lemma. The link to 15:16 is synthetic and capped at moderate
  until audited, but it is the single most promising unexamined thread in the book.*
- **ηὔξησεν ὁ λαὸς καὶ ἐπληθύνθη ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ** (7:17) `[T]` — the Exodus 1:7 formula, in
  Stephen's mouth, of Israel under Pharaoh. This is the hinge of Headline Finding 1: the
  refrain Luke applies to the word at 6:7 and 12:24 is here applied by a character to Israel
  in Egypt, which identifies its source.
- **χείρ** `[T]` — the hand-motif runs the speech: God gives salvation *by Moses' hand*
  (7:25), sends him *with the hand of the angel* (7:35), Israel rejoices in *the works of
  their hands* (7:41), Solomon builds and God asks *did not my hand make all these things?*
  (7:50), with *χειροποιήτοις* at 7:48. Five hand-references in twenty-five verses. *High
  confidence.*
- **ἐκκλησία ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ** (7:38) `[T]` — the only place in Acts where *ἐκκλησία* means
  **Israel at Sinai**. Stephen calls the wilderness generation the church. Twenty-three
  occurrences: nineteen the Christian church, three the Ephesian civic assembly (19:32, 39,
  40), one this. *High confidence — verified by lemma.*
- **ἀτενίσας … εἶδεν** (7:55) and **υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** (7:56) `[T]` — the only Son of Man
  saying in Acts, and the only place in the NT where the title is on lips other than Jesus'.
  He is **standing**, not seated (contrast Ps 110:1 at 2:34). *High confidence on the data;
  the significance of the standing is contested — see Open Questions.*

**Tool 11.** The whole speech is an intertextual argument; two citations receive the triad.

**Amos 5:25–27 LXX → 7:42–43** *(high confidence — direct quotation with formula)*. *Source
context:* Amos's fourth oracle against Israel's worship — the LORD hates their feasts,
demands justice rolling down like waters, and then denies that sacrifices were offered in
the wilderness at all. The exile threatened is *beyond Damascus* in the Hebrew and the LXX;
**Luke's text reads *ἐπέκεινα Βαβυλῶνος*, "beyond Babylon"** `[T]`. That is a real
divergence and it is *category 3 in the three-way triage* — the Greek as the text the NT
argues from. Stephen's point requires the later exile. *Book usage:* Amos is used twice in
Acts, here and at 15:16–18, and **both uses are LXX-dependent**. `[T]` That is a
significant pattern: the two great structural moments of the book, the pivot and the hinge,
both turn on the Greek Amos. *OT-to-OT:* Amos 5's wilderness-worship denial and Amos 9's
tent-of-David restoration are the two ends of the same book, and Luke uses them at the two
ends of the same question. *[I], moderate — flagged for audit.* *What it adds:* Stephen's
charge is not that the temple is bad but that Israel's worship has been idolatrous from the
wilderness onward, which relocates the argument from a building to a history.

**Isaiah 66:1–2 → 7:49–50** *(high confidence — direct quotation)*. *Source context:* the
last chapter of Isaiah, opening with heaven as throne and earth as footstool, and continuing
*but this is the one to whom I will look: he who is humble and contrite in spirit* — and
then, within the same chapter, the gathering of **all nations and tongues** (66:18) and the
sending of survivors to the far coastlands. *Book usage:* Isaiah is cited at 8:32–33
(Isa 53), 13:47 (Isa 49:6) and 28:26–27 (Isa 6); this is the first. *OT-to-OT:* Isa 66 and
Amos 5 are both prophetic critiques of cult without obedience, already in conversation.
*What it adds:* Stephen answers the temple charge from the prophet who *ends* with the
nations streaming in — so his defence and the book's programme are the same text.

**Deuteronomy 18:15 → 7:37** — Move 1 given at Acts 3:22 above; here Move 2 is the point:
this is its **second** use, and Luke has moved it from Peter's appeal to Stephen's
indictment. *[T] on the double use; [I] on the escalation.*

*Internal:* **answers §3:22** and **§6:14** (the τόπος charge); **plants** Saul at 7:58
and 8:1, and plants the *hands laid on* and *coats laid at feet* image that 22:20 will
recall in Paul's own words. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** Stephen's speech is a **normative pattern** of reading Scripture against a
charge; his forgiveness at 7:60 is a **normative example** and is marked as such by its
verbal conformity to Luke 23:34, 46 — *receive my spirit* and *do not hold this sin against
them* `[I], moderate-to-high`. His martyrdom is **descriptive**. The vision is **unique**.

**Pitfall.** Preaching the speech as a history lesson with an angry ending — as if 7:2–50
were background and 7:51–53 the sermon. The indictment is the *conclusion of the exegesis*,
not a change of subject: every movement of the speech has shown Israel rejecting a
God-sent deliverer while God works outside the land, so *you always resist the Holy Spirit*
(7:51) is the summary of the argument, not an outburst. Preach the speech as one thing.

**Overview note.** The overview's §6 treats ch. 7 as "a compressed retelling … not a history
lesson", which is right. It does not identify the **τόπος** Leitwort, the **σκηνή** thread,
or the **"beyond Babylon"** divergence — all three of which are what make the chapter an
argument rather than a summary.

## 11. Acts 8:1–25 — Samaria, and Simon

**Position.** Q1: 1:8 named Samaria as stage two, and 7:58–8:1 has put Saul at a killing and
scattered the church. Q2: this passage exists here because Luke has just supplied the
mechanism — persecution — and must now show the result. The causal chain is explicit in the
Greek: *διεσπάρησαν* (8:1) → *οἱ διασπαρέντες διῆλθον εὐαγγελιζόμενοι* (8:4). The scattering
is the sending.

**Structure.** Scattering and Saul's ravaging (1–3) — Philip in Samaria (4–8) — Simon
(9–13) — the apostolic visit and the Spirit (14–17) — Simon's offer and Peter's rebuke
(18–24) — the return, evangelising Samaritan villages (25).

**Text-first findings.**

- **διασπείρω** (8:1, 8:4, 11:19) `[T]` — three occurrences in Acts, all of this one
  scattering. The third (11:19) picks the thread back up to explain Antioch. The word is
  the seed-word (*σπείρω* with *διά*), and Luke uses it for a persecution. *High
  confidence on the count; the seed-resonance is [I], moderate.*
- **δύναμις** in 8:10 `[T]` — Simon is called *ἡ Δύναμις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ καλουμένη Μεγάλη*, and
  at 8:13 he is astonished at *δυνάμεις μεγάλας*. The crowd's title for him is exactly what
  he then sees done by someone else. *High confidence — a neat, preachable irony in the
  Greek.*
- **προσκαρτερέω** (8:13) `[T]` — see Acts 2:42 above: Luke's devotion-word applied to
  Simon, whose heart is *οὐκ εὐθεῖα ἔναντι τοῦ θεοῦ* (8:21).
- **δωρεά** (8:20) `[T]` — four occurrences (2:38, 8:20, 10:45, 11:17). Simon's error is
  named by the noun: the *gift* of God is not bought. And 11:17 uses the same noun for
  what God gave the Gentiles, which is the reason Peter could not hinder him. *High
  confidence.*
- **The Spirit-delay (8:16)** `[T]` — *οὐδέπω γὰρ ἦν ἐπ' οὐδενὶ αὐτῶν ἐπιπεπτωκός*. Luke
  marks it as an anomaly with *οὐδέπω*, "not yet". He is not describing a normal sequence;
  he is explaining an exception.

**Tool 11.** No external citation. *Internal:* **answers §1:8** (Samaria) — the first of the
three stages to be explicitly reached — and **answers §5:1–11** by pairing a second
money-and-the-Spirit failure with the first, this time forgiven-in-principle rather than
struck down (*μετανόησον … εἰ ἄρα ἀφεθήσεται*, 8:22). *Moderate-to-high.* Also **plants**
Philip, who reappears at 21:8 as "Philip the evangelist, one of the seven", thirteen
chapters later `[T]`.

**Copycat.** The apostolic mediation of the Spirit here is a **unique frontier event** —
Luke's *οὐδέπω* says so, and the Samaritan schism is exactly the situation that required
Jerusalem's public endorsement. Simon is a **negative example**. Philip's preaching under
persecution is a **normative pattern**. The **posture** point: nothing in 8:14–17 is
requested by the Samaritans; the Spirit is given, not achieved.

**Pitfall.** Building a two-stage doctrine of Christian initiation on 8:14–17. The
corrective is Luke's own *οὐδέπω*, plus the fact that the sequence is different at 10:44
(Spirit before baptism) and at 19:6 (with hands, after re-baptism). Four frontiers, four
sequences. A doctrine built on one of them has ignored the other three.

## 12. Acts 8:26–40 — The Ethiopian eunuch ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 8:4–25 has taken the gospel to Samaritans — half-Israel, on the map. Q2:
this passage exists here because the next boundary is not geographical but *personal*: a
man doubly excluded by Deuteronomy 23:1 (a eunuch) and by distance (an Ethiopian), reading
the prophet whose book promises exactly those two people a name and a place (Isa 56:3–7).
The passage sits between Samaria and Saul because Luke is narrating the dismantling of
categories, and this is the category the Law itself excluded.

**Structure.** The angelic direction (26) — the meeting on the road (27–31) — the text and
the question (32–35) — water and baptism (36, 38) — the Spirit's snatching and the eunuch's
joy (39–40).

**Text-first findings.**

- **τί κωλύει με βαπτισθῆναι;** (8:36) `[T]` — the first of the six **κωλύω** occurrences,
  and the question that governs Headline Finding 2. It is asked by the man the Law
  excluded. *High confidence.*
- **ὁδός** (8:26, 8:36, 8:39) `[T]` — three occurrences in fifteen verses, of a literal
  desert road, and *ἐπορεύετο τὴν ὁδὸν αὐτοῦ χαίρων* at the end. **ὁδός** occurs twenty
  times in Acts, and from 9:2 onwards it becomes the movement's name (*τῆς ὁδοῦ ὄντας*).
  Luke uses the road the eunuch travels immediately before he starts calling the faith
  "the Way". *High confidence on the counts; the transition is [I], moderate-to-high.*
- **εὐνοῦχος** (8:27, 34, 36, 38, 39) `[T]` — five times in thirteen verses. Luke will not
  let the reader forget the disqualification. He also gives him a title (*δυνάστης*),
  a portfolio (*ἐπὶ πάσης τῆς γάζης*), and a pilgrimage (*ἐληλύθει προσκυνήσων*) — a man
  who had travelled a very long way to worship at a temple whose inner courts he could not
  enter.
- **8:37 is absent** from SBLGNT and NA28 `[T]` — the confession *If you believe with all
  your heart…* is a Western expansion. Preachers whose hearers use the KJV or NKJV should
  expect the question.

**Tool 11.** **Isaiah 53:7–8 LXX → 8:32–33** *(high confidence — direct quotation, Luke
names the scroll)*. *Source context:* the fourth Servant Song; the cited lines sit at its
centre, between the bearing of griefs (53:4–6) and the making of many righteous (53:11).
The specific lines Luke quotes are the *silence* and the *denied justice*, and the LXX's
*ἐν τῇ ταπεινώσει ἡ κρίσις αὐτοῦ ἤρθη* is doing the work — this is **category 3 in the
triage**, the Greek as the text the NT argues from; the Hebrew of 53:8 is notoriously
difficult and the Greek is what the eunuch is reading. *Book usage:* the first Isaiah
citation in Acts after Stephen's Isa 66; Isaiah returns at 13:47 and 28:26. Luke draws
on Isaiah at the three decisive moments — the first Gentile-adjacent conversion, the formal
turn to the Gentiles, and the book's last word. *[I], moderate-to-high.* *OT-to-OT:*
Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 56 stand three chapters apart in the same scroll, and Isa 56:3–5 is the
promise to the **eunuch** of a name better than sons. The man is reading in the book that
contains his own inclusion. Luke does not quote 56, and the reader who knows the scroll does
not need him to. *[I], moderate — this is the pericope's most striking unstated connection
and it belongs in a solo run.* *What it adds:* Philip preaches *τὸν Ἰησοῦν* "beginning from
this Scripture" (8:35) — so the sermon's starting point is a text about a man denied justice
and cut off, delivered to a man whom the Law had cut off.

*Internal:* **plants κωλύω** for 10:47, 11:17 and, ultimately, 28:31; **answers §1:8** at
its third stage in anticipation (an African official, "to the end of the earth", before
Cornelius). *High confidence on the κωλύω plant.*

**Copycat.** The angelic direction and the Spirit's snatching are **descriptive and
unique**. Philip's method — *do you understand what you are reading?*, then Scripture, then
Christ — is a **normative pattern**, and it is one of the few evangelistic conversations
Acts records in enough detail to imitate. The eunuch's baptism on confession is
**prescriptive** in substance.

**Pitfall.** Preaching this as a lesson in personal evangelism technique and losing the
category-demolition. Luke's five-fold *εὐνοῦχος* is not incidental colour: the passage's
force is that the first person in Acts to be baptised on the strength of a private reading
of Scripture is a man the Law barred from the assembly, and the question he asks — *what
hinders me?* — is the question the rest of the book answers with a single adverb.

## 13. Acts 9:1–31 — The conversion and commissioning of Saul ⭐

**Position.** Q1: Saul has been introduced at 7:58 and 8:1–3 as the persecutor whose
violence caused the scattering that spread the gospel. Q2: this passage exists here because
Luke needs the Gentile apostle in place *before* the Gentile Pentecost of ch. 10, and
because the man who caused stage two of 1:8 is the man who will execute stage three. The
sequence — scattering (8:1–4), Samaria (8:5–25), the excluded man (8:26–40), the persecutor
(9:1–31), the Gentile household (10:1–11:18) — is a designed escalation.

**Structure.** The road (1–9) — Ananias (10–19a) — Damascus preaching and escape (19b–25) —
Jerusalem, Barnabas, and a second escape (26–30) — **the seam** (31).

**Text-first findings.**

- **Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις;** (9:4) `[T]` — the doubled vocative is the LXX's
  theophanic-call form (Abraham, Moses, Samuel), and Luke keeps it in all three tellings
  (9:4; 22:7; 26:14). *Moderate-to-high; recommend lexicon/commentary confirmation of the
  doubled-vocative convention.*
- **The name Σαῦλος** `[T]` — Luke uses *Σαῦλος* until 13:9 (*Σαῦλος δέ, ὁ καὶ Παῦλος*),
  after which *Παῦλος* only. The reader who knows 1 Samuel hears the rejected Benjaminite
  king behind the persecutor's name — and at 13:21 **Paul himself preaches Saul son of Kish,
  of the tribe of Benjamin** `[T]`, four verses after his own name changes. Luke has put the
  old Saul into the new Paul's first sermon. *Moderate-to-high — the juxtaposition is
  verifiable; the intent is inference.*
- **ἡ ὁδός** (9:2) `[T]` — the first of the absolute uses (9:2; 19:9; 19:23; 22:4; 24:14;
  24:22). The movement's self-designation appears first **on a warrant for its arrest**.
- **σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς** (9:15) `[T]` — *σκεῦος* five times in Acts (9:15; 10:11; 10:16; 11:5;
  27:17). Paul is a chosen **vessel**; the thing Peter sees let down from heaven full of
  unclean creatures is a **vessel**; and the last is the ship's gear in the storm.
  *[T] on the count; the Paul/sheet resonance is [I], moderate and worth an audit.*
- **δεῖ … παθεῖν** (9:16) `[T]` — the commissioning includes suffering as its content, not
  its risk. This is the *δεῖ* that governs chs. 21–28.
- **9:31 is a growth-notice with the wrong verbs** `[T]`: *ἡ ἐκκλησία … οἰκοδομουμένη …
  ἐπληθύνετο*. πληθύνω yes; **αὐξάνω no**; and the subject is the **church**, not the word.
  See Book-Overview Tensions.

**Tool 11.** No external citation. *Internal:* **answers §7:58–8:3** (the coats at Saul's
feet; he is now the one fleeing in a basket) and **§8:3** (*ἐλυμαίνετο τὴν ἐκκλησίαν*
answered by 9:31's church at peace); **plants** the three-fold retelling at 22:3–21 and
26:9–18, and the *ὄνομα*-bearing commission of 9:15 which 21:13 and 26:9 both recall.
*High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The Damascus road is a **unique event** and Luke's clearest case — he tells it
three times precisely because it is foundational and unrepeatable. Ananias's obedience
against his own judgement (9:13–17) is a **normative example**. Barnabas's advocacy (9:27)
is a **normative pattern** and one of the most quietly important acts in the book.

**Pitfall.** "Every Christian needs a Damascus road." The passage is a *commissioning
narrative*, not a conversion paradigm — 9:15–16 is about a mission and its cost, and Luke's
own conversion paradigm is 2:38. The second, subtler pitfall is preaching Saul's change and
skipping Ananias, who has the harder obedience and no vision of glory.

## 14. Acts 9:32–43 — Aeneas and Dorcas

**Position.** Q1: 9:31 has closed the Saul section with a seam. Q2: this passage exists here
because Luke must move Peter geographically from Jerusalem to the coast in order to place
him in Joppa at 9:43, in the house of a tanner, one day's journey from Caesarea. The two
miracles are real and are told for their own sake; but their *position* is Cornelius.
The last clause — *he stayed with one Simon, a tanner* — is the hinge, and it is a trade
that handled dead animals.

**Structure.** Aeneas at Lydda (32–35) — Dorcas at Joppa (36–42) — the tanner's house (43).

**Text-first findings.**

- **Αἰνέα, ἰᾶταί σε Ἰησοῦς Χριστός** (9:34) `[T]` — Peter names the healer. Compare 3:6 and
  3:12: the same refusal to occupy the centre.
- **Ταβιθά, ἀνάστηθι** (9:40) `[T]` — the command is two words, and in Aramaic it would be
  *Tabitha koum*, one consonant from Mark 5:41's *talitha koum*. *Moderate confidence —
  widely noted, but this is [S]-adjacent recall rather than a corpus observation, since the
  Aramaic underlying Luke's Greek is not in the corpus.* Flagged accordingly.
- **ἀνίστημι / ἐγείρω** `[T]` — 9:34 *ἀνέστη*, 9:40 *ἀνάστηθι*, 9:41 *ἀνέστησεν*. The
  resurrection verbs of the sermons (2:24, 32; 3:15; 4:10) are here used of a paralytic and
  a corpse. Luke's preaching vocabulary and his miracle vocabulary are the same vocabulary.
  *High confidence.*
- **μαθήτρια** (9:36) `[T]` — the only feminine form of "disciple" in the NT. *High
  confidence.*

**Tool 11.** No external citation. *Internal:* **plants** Joppa and the tanner for 10:5–6,
32 and 11:5, which repeat the address three times `[T]`. Luke repeats a street address in
a book that compresses whole missionary years into a sentence — that is emphasis.

**Copycat.** Both miracles are **descriptive and apostolic**. Dorcas's *ἔργα ἀγαθὰ καὶ
ἐλεημοσύναι* (9:36) are a **normative example**, and the widows holding up the garments
(9:39) is Luke's own evidence for it. The raising is **not** a pattern.

**Pitfall.** Treating these as filler between the big set-pieces. They are the bridge to
Cornelius, and their content — a Gentile-adjacent coastal plain, a trade that handled
carcasses, a Peter increasingly comfortable outside Jerusalem — is Luke preparing his reader
for the sheet let down from heaven.

## 15. Acts 10:1–11:18 — Cornelius: the Gentile Pentecost ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 1:8 promised the end of the earth; 8:26–40 has baptised an African
official; 9:15 has appointed an apostle to the Gentiles. Q2: this passage exists here — and
occupies sixty-six verses, the longest single unit in Acts — because the decisive question
is not whether a Gentile can be saved but **whether Jewish believers may eat with him**, and
that question can only be settled by God acting first and Peter reporting it publicly. Luke
gives the story twice (ch. 10 narrated, ch. 11 retold) because the retelling *is* the
argument, and the retelling is what ch. 15 will cite.

**Structure.** A double vision, converging: Cornelius (10:1–8) — Peter (10:9–16) — the
messengers (10:17–23a) — the meeting (10:23b–33) — Peter's sermon (10:34–43) — the Spirit
falls and baptism follows (10:44–48) — **the retelling and its verdict** (11:1–18).

**Text-first findings.**

- **κοινός / κοινόω / ἀκάθαρτος / καθαρίζω** `[T]` — the purity vocabulary is dense and
  precisely tracked: *κοινός* 10:14, 10:28, 11:8; *κοινόω* 10:15, 11:9 (and once more at
  **21:28**, where Paul is accused of defiling the temple); *ἀκάθαρτος* 10:14, 10:28, 11:8;
  *καθαρίζω* 10:15, 11:9, and **15:9** (*τῇ πίστει καθαρίσας τὰς καρδίας αὐτῶν*). The
  vocabulary of the vision is picked up verbatim by Peter at the Council. *High confidence
  — verified by lemma. This is the strongest Move-2 chain in Acts.*
- **διακρίνω** (10:20, 11:2, 11:12) and again **15:9** `[T]` — "make no distinction". At
  11:2 it is the circumcision party *disputing* with Peter; at 10:20 and 11:12 it is the
  Spirit telling Peter not to hesitate/discriminate. Luke uses the same verb for the
  hesitation and for the objection.
- **ἐπὶ τρίς** (10:16, 11:10) `[T]` — the vision is thrice-repeated and Luke says so twice.
  Compare Peter's three denials and threefold restoration in the Gospel; *[I], moderate.*
- **προσωπολήμπτης** (10:34) `[T]` — a NT-rare compound, "receiver of faces". Peter's
  conclusion is stated as a fact about God's character, not a change of policy.
- **κωλῦσαι** (10:47) and **κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν** (11:17) `[T]` — the second and third links
  of the κωλύω chain, and 11:17's object is **God**. The question the eunuch asked has become
  a question about whether anyone may obstruct God.
- **ἐπιπίπτω** (10:44; 11:15; cf. 8:16) `[T]` — the Spirit *falls*, and 11:15 explicitly
  says *ὥσπερ καὶ ἐφ' ἡμᾶς ἐν ἀρχῇ* — Peter's own hermeneutic: this was Pentecost again.

**Tool 11.** No formal citation, but 10:43 (*τούτῳ πάντες οἱ προφῆται μαρτυροῦσιν*) is a
summary appeal to the prophetic corpus, and 10:36's *εὐαγγελιζόμενος εἰρήνην* echoes
**Isaiah 52:7** *(moderate confidence — verbal, brief)*. *Source context:* the herald on the
mountains announcing peace and *your God reigns*, immediately before the fourth Servant Song
— the same Isaianic stretch the eunuch was reading two chapters earlier. *Book usage:*
Isa 52:7 is the text Paul cites at Rom 10:15; within Acts, Isaiah is live from 7:49 onwards.
*What it adds:* Peter's opening formula to a Gentile household is drawn from the passage
that announces the reign of God to the nations.

*Internal:* **answers §1:8** (the third stage, in principle) and **§2:39** (*πᾶσι τοῖς εἰς
μακρὰν*, "all who are far off" — a promise made at Pentecost and cashed here) `[T],
high`; **plants** the purity vocabulary that 15:9 and 21:28 both use.

**Copycat.** The double vision is **descriptive and unique**. Peter's conclusion (10:34–35;
11:17) is **prescriptive doctrine**. The order of Spirit-then-baptism is **descriptive** and
is the exact reverse of ch. 8 — which is why neither is a template. Cornelius's piety
(10:2) is **not** presented as meritorious: 11:14 says he was told words *by which you will
be saved*.

**Pitfall.** Two. First, preaching Cornelius as a good man who was already acceptable — the
text says he still needed to hear words in order to be saved (11:14). Second, and more
common in Reformed pulpits, treating the passage as being about *evangelism* when Luke's
own framing (11:3 — *you went to uncircumcised men and ate with them*) shows it is about
**table fellowship**. The offence is not that Peter preached; it is that he stayed for
dinner.

**Overview note.** The overview calls this "the Gentile Pentecost … decisive" and it is
right. What it does not register is that the purity-vocabulary chain runs on into 15:9 and
21:28, so the Cornelius pericope is not a self-contained episode but the first term of a
three-part argument that ends with Paul accused of the very defilement Peter was told not to
call defilement.

## 16. Acts 11:19–30 — Antioch, "Christians", and famine relief

**Position.** Q1: 8:4 said the scattered preached the word; 11:19 picks up that exact thread
after eleven chapters (*Οἱ μὲν οὖν διασπαρέντες ἀπὸ τῆς θλίψεως τῆς γενομένης ἐπὶ Στεφάνῳ*).
Q2: this passage exists here because Luke has just secured the *principle* of Gentile
inclusion at Jerusalem (11:18) and must now show the *practice* somewhere it can grow — and
because the base for the rest of the book has to be founded before ch. 13 sends anyone out.

**Structure.** The scattered reach Antioch and preach to Greeks (19–21) — Barnabas sent, and
sent for Saul (22–26) — Agabus and the famine collection (27–30).

**Text-first findings.**

- **χεὶρ κυρίου** (11:21) `[T]` — *χείρ* occurs forty-five times in Acts; the phrase "the
  hand of the Lord" marks the unauthorised Antioch preaching as divinely backed **before**
  Jerusalem hears of it. Luke's sequence is: they preached, God blessed, then Jerusalem
  investigated.
- **Χριστιανός** (11:26) `[T]` — **three occurrences in the NT: Acts 11:26, Acts 26:28,
  1 Pet 4:16.** Luke coins it here and gives it back to Agrippa at 26:28 in the mouth of a
  king. *High confidence — verified across the SBLGNT.*
- **οἱ Ἑλληνισταί** (11:20) `[T]` — a genuine textual crux; the manuscripts divide between
  *Ἑλληνιστάς* (Greek-speaking Jews) and *Ἕλληνας* (Greeks). NA28 and SBLGNT print
  *Ἑλληνιστάς*; the sense required by 11:19's contrast (*to no one except Jews*) is
  *Greeks*. **Category 2 divergence — a substantive variant, and the harder reading is
  printed while the easier one fits the context.** Say the sentence carefully in the pulpit
  and check the NA28 apparatus in Logos. *Moderate confidence; flagged.*
- **προφῆται** (11:27) `[T]` — thirty occurrences of *προφήτης* in Acts, of which only five
  are Christian prophets (11:27; 13:1; 15:32; 21:10; and Agabus again at 21:10). The word
  overwhelmingly means the OT prophets. A useful corrective to a charismatic reading of the
  book's vocabulary.

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §8:1–4** (the scattering finally reaches a
Gentile city) and **§9:30** (Saul was sent to Tarsus; Barnabas goes there to fetch him);
**plants** Agabus, who returns at 21:10–11 to bind Paul with his own belt — **the only
character in Acts who appears twice as a prophet, and both appearances concern a
journey to Jerusalem** `[T], moderate-to-high`.

**Copycat.** The lay, unnamed, cross-cultural preaching of 11:20 is a **normative pattern**
and Luke marks it as such by attaching *the hand of the Lord*. The famine relief (11:29–30)
is a **normative pattern** — proportionate giving (*καθὼς εὐπορεῖτό τις*), decided by the
disciples, delivered by named men. Barnabas's character-note (11:24) is a **normative
example**.

**Pitfall.** Skimming this as a bridge passage. It contains the founding of the church that
sends out the entire Gentile mission, the first cross-cultural evangelism by people nobody
names, the coining of the word "Christian", and a collection for famine relief taken by a
Gentile church for Jewish believers — which is the practical shape of everything ch. 15 will
argue about.

## 17. Acts 12:1–25 — Herod, Peter's release, and the second seam

**Position.** Q1: 11:29–30 has sent Barnabas and Saul to Jerusalem with money — so the
narrative is in Jerusalem when the sword falls. Q2: this passage exists here as the **last
Peter narrative in Acts** and as the hinge into the Pauline half: 12:24 is the seam, 12:25
returns Barnabas and Saul to Antioch, and 13:1 begins the mission. Luke ends the Peter half
with a king struck down and the word growing.

**Structure.** James killed, Peter arrested (1–5) — the release (6–11) — the house of Mary
and Rhoda (12–17) — the guards and Herod's death (18–23) — **the seam** (24) — the return
(25).

**Text-first findings.**

- **ἅλυσις** (12:6, 12:7) `[T]` — Peter *bound with two chains*, and the chains *fell off*.
  Headline Finding 3 begins here. *High confidence.*
- **ἐξέπεσαν … αἱ ἁλύσεις** and **αὐτομάτη ἠνοίγη** (12:10) `[T]` — the gate opens *of its
  own accord*; the verb *αὐτόματος* occurs twice in the NT (Mark 4:28, Acts 12:10) and both
  are about something happening without human agency. *Moderate confidence on the
  significance.*
- **Ἡρῴδης** and **ὁ ἄγγελος κυρίου** `[T]` — the same phrase, *ἄγγελος κυρίου*, strikes
  Peter's side to wake him (12:7) and strikes Herod to kill him (12:23). *ἐπάταξεν* is used
  of both blows (*πατάξας* 12:7; *ἐπάταξεν* 12:23). **One angel, one verb, two men.** *High
  confidence — verified in the text; a genuinely preachable structural point the overview
  does not have.*
- **σκωληκόβρωτος** (12:23) `[T]` — a NT hapax, "eaten by worms", set against *θεοῦ φωνὴ
  καὶ οὐκ ἀνθρώπου* two verses earlier. Tool 9: Luke's tonal weapon here is bathos.
- **12:24** `[T]` — *ὁ δὲ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ηὔξανεν καὶ ἐπληθύνετο*. The Exodus pair, bare,
  with the word as sole subject. **NA28 and SBLGNT read τοῦ θεοῦ; NASB95 renders "the word
  of the Lord", following a variant.** ESV has "the word of God". *Category 2 divergence,
  minor; note it if quoting the NASB95 and making anything of the divine title.*

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §4:29** (the prayer for boldness under
threat is answered here by a prayer meeting that does not believe its own answer, 12:15 —
Luke's only comic scene, and it is at the church's expense) and **§5:19** (an earlier
angelic prison-opening, so the reader knows the pattern before it repeats). **Plants
nothing forward for Peter**: he *ἐπορεύθη εἰς ἕτερον τόπον* (12:17) and appears once more,
at the Council. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The release is **descriptive and unique** — and Luke makes the point himself by
putting James's execution in verse 2 without explanation. **The same prayer, two outcomes.**
Rhoda and the praying church are a **negative-comic example**; the passage's honesty about
unbelieving prayer is a gift to the preacher. Herod is a **negative example** with an
explicit narrator's verdict (12:23, *ἀνθ' ὧν οὐκ ἔδωκεν τὴν δόξαν τῷ θεῷ*).

**Pitfall.** "Pray and God will open your prison." James is dead in verse 2, and Luke does
not soften it. Any sermon on 12:1–19 that does not preach verse 2 has not preached the
passage. The corrective is 12:24: the word grew — which is what the chapter claims, not that
the faithful are spared.

## 18. Acts 13:1–52 — Antioch's commissioning and the Pisidian sermon ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 11:19–26 founded the Antioch church and 12:25 returned Barnabas and Saul
to it. Q2: this passage exists here because the Gentile question has been settled in
principle (11:18) and now needs an agent, a sending church, and a worked example of the
preaching that will occupy fifteen chapters. Luke gives Paul one full sermon, here, and
never gives him another to a synagogue — everything after is summarised.

**Structure.** The commissioning (1–3) — Cyprus and Bar-Jesus (4–12) — the Pisidian sermon
in three movements: the history (16–25), the resurrection with three citations (26–37),
the warning and offer (38–41) — the reception and the turn (42–52).

**Text-first findings.**

- **Σαῦλος δέ, ὁ καὶ Παῦλος** (13:9) `[T]` — the name changes at the moment he confronts a
  magician before a Roman proconsul. Luke does not explain it. And **four verses into the
  sermon he preaches "Saul son of Kish, of the tribe of Benjamin" (13:21)** `[T]`. The last
  Saul in Acts and the first Saul in Israel meet in one pericope.
- **ἴσχυον / ἰσχύω** and the growth vocabulary `[T]` — not here, but note for ch. 19.
- **χεὶρ κυρίου ἐπὶ σέ** (13:11) `[T]` — the hand that was *with* the Antioch preachers
  (11:21) is *against* Elymas. Same phrase, opposite preposition.
- **ἀνέστη … ἤγειρεν** in the sermon `[T]` — 13:22 God *raised up* David as king
  (*ἤγειρεν*); 13:30 God *raised* Jesus from the dead (*ἤγειρεν*); 13:33 *ἀναστήσας
  Ἰησοῦν*. Luke lets the raising-up of a king and the raising-up from death share a verb,
  which is the sermon's whole argument.
- **ἕως ἐσχάτου τῆς γῆς** (13:47) `[T]` — **the exact phrase of 1:8**, here given as a
  quotation of Isaiah with *ἐντέταλται ἡμῖν ὁ κύριος*. Luke identifies the source of his own
  programmatic verse. *High confidence — this upgrades the overview's "high/moderate" rating
  on the 1:8 echo.*

**Tool 11.** Four citations; the triad is run on Psalm 2 and Isaiah 49.

**Psalm 2:7 → 13:33** *(high confidence — direct quotation, named psalm)*. *Source
context:* the decree at the centre of the enthronement psalm, spoken to the king installed
on Zion after the nations' futile raging. *Book usage:* **the second use of Ps 2 in Acts**;
4:25–26 quoted its opening (the raging), and here Luke quotes its centre (the decree). He is
following the psalm's own internal movement across the book — the strongest Move 2 finding
in Acts. *[I], moderate-to-high; a claim audit should test whether any third use is
implied.* *OT-to-OT:* Ps 2, 2 Sam 7 (echoed in 13:23, 34) and Isa 55:3 (quoted at 13:34)
are a Davidic-covenant cluster already in conversation. *What it adds:* the resurrection is
read as the *enthronement* the psalm decrees, not merely as vindication.

**Isaiah 49:6 → 13:47** *(high confidence — direct quotation with a commissioning formula)*.
*Source context:* the second Servant Song. The cited line is God's *answer to the Servant's
complaint* that he has laboured in vain (49:4): it is *too light a thing* that you should
raise up the tribes of Jacob — I will make you a light for the nations. So the verse's own
function in Isaiah is **an expansion of a mission that seemed to have failed**. *Book
usage:* the phrase stands unattributed behind 1:8 and is finally given its source here;
Isaiah is live from 7:49 (Isa 66), 8:32 (Isa 53), and will close the book at 28:26 (Isa 6).
*OT-to-OT:* Isa 49 and Isa 66 both end with the nations gathered — Stephen's citation and
Paul's are two ends of the same Isaianic argument. *What it adds:* Paul and Barnabas apply a
**Servant** text to **themselves** (*ἐντέταλται ἡμῖν*). The turn to the Gentiles is not a
fallback after Jewish rejection but the Servant's commission, given precisely at the point
where the mission to Israel appears to have failed — which is exactly its function in
Isaiah 49. *This is the single richest Move 1 finding in the sweep.*

**Habakkuk 1:5 → 13:41** *(high confidence)*. Move 1: Habakkuk's opening oracle, where the
"work" God is doing that they would not believe is **the raising up of the Chaldeans** —
a judgement. Paul ends an offer of forgiveness with a threat drawn from an invasion oracle.
**Psalm 16:10 → 13:35** — Move 2 given at Acts 2:25 above; its second use, in the same role,
by the other apostle.

*Internal:* **answers §1:8** (the source of *to the end of the earth*) and **§2:25–31**
(the Ps 16 argument, repeated by Paul). *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The commissioning by fasting, prayer and laying on of hands (13:2–3) is a
**normative pattern**. Elymas's blinding is **descriptive and apostolic**. The synagogue-first
strategy is a **normative pattern within its epoch** (Rom 1:16), not a rule for a
post-synagogue church. 13:48 (*ὅσοι ἦσαν τεταγμένοι εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον*) is **doctrinal
narrative** and is listed in the difficult-verses catalogue: preach it, but preach it as
Luke's aside, not as the sermon's centre.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 13:46 ("we turn to the Gentiles") as a moment of rejection-and-
replacement. Isaiah 49:6 is what Paul cites, and in Isaiah that verse *enlarges* the
Servant's task without cancelling the first half of it — and Paul goes straight back into
the synagogue at Iconium (14:1). The turn is repeated three times in Acts (13:46; 18:6;
28:28) and is each time local, not final.

## 19. Acts 14:1–28 — Iconium, Lystra, Derbe, and the return

**Position.** Q1: 13:46–52 has established the pattern — synagogue, division, expulsion,
joy. Q2: this passage exists here to run that pattern twice more and then to add what
Pisidian Antioch could not supply: **a wholly pagan audience with no Scripture** (14:15–17),
and **the pastoral consolidation** (14:21–23) without which a mission is only an itinerary.

**Structure.** Iconium (1–7) — Lystra: the healing, the mistaken worship, the speech, the
stoning (8–20) — the return journey and the appointment of elders (21–28).

**Text-first findings.**

- **χωλὸς ἐκ κοιλίας μητρὸς αὐτοῦ** (14:8) `[T]` — **verbally identical to 3:2** (*χωλὸς ἐκ
  κοιλίας μητρὸς αὐτοῦ*), and both men *leap up and walk* (3:8 *ἐξαλλόμενος … περιεπάτει*;
  14:10 *ἥλατο καὶ περιεπάτει*). This is the overview's Peter–Paul parallelism at its most
  verbally exact, and it holds. *High confidence — verified against the text.*
- **ἀτενίσας** (14:9) `[T]` — Paul gazes at the man exactly as Peter did at 3:4.
- **οὐκ ἀμάρτυρον αὑτὸν ἀφῆκεν** (14:17) `[T]` — *ἀμάρτυρος* is a NT hapax, built on the
  *μάρτυς* root that runs through Acts. God has not left himself **without a witness** — in
  a book about witnesses. *High confidence; the resonance is [I], moderate-to-high.*
- **διὰ πολλῶν θλίψεων δεῖ ἡμᾶς εἰσελθεῖν** (14:22) `[T]` — one of the nineteen *δεῖ*
  statements, and the only one that makes suffering the road for *everyone* (*ἡμᾶς*), not
  for an individual. Preachers should note that Luke puts the book's one general statement
  about Christian suffering in a strengthening-visit, not in a sermon.
- **ἤνοιξεν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν θύραν πίστεως** (14:27) `[T]` — the report to Antioch, and the exact
  formulation that ch. 15 will contest.

**Tool 11.** No citation — and that is the point of 14:15–17, which is Luke's **first
address to people with no Scripture**. The argument runs from creation, providence and
common grace, and it is a rough draft of Athens. *Internal:* **answers §3:1–10** (the lame
man); **plants** 17:22–31 (the pagan address) and **plants the elders** (14:23) whom 20:17
will summon. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The appointment of elders in every church, with prayer and fasting (14:23), is
a **normative pattern**. The apostles' horror at being worshipped (14:14–15) is a
**normative example** and the corrective to every apostle-centred reading of Acts. The
stoning and revival (14:19–20) is **descriptive**. Note the **posture** point: Luke says the
man *had faith to be saved* (14:9) — the only healing in Acts where he says so — which
should stop any general inference in either direction.

**Pitfall.** Preaching Lystra as an evangelistic-method text ("start where they are"). The
speech is four verses long, is *unsuccessful* at stopping the sacrifice (*μόλις κατέπαυσαν*,
14:18), and is followed immediately by a stoning. Luke is not modelling contextualisation;
he is showing what happens when the gospel meets a religion with no Old Testament and no
category for a God who cannot be fed.

## 20. Acts 15:1–35 — The Jerusalem Council ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 11:18 conceded that God had granted the Gentiles repentance; 14:27
reported an opened door of faith. Q2: this passage exists here because a concession granted
to *one household* under overwhelming signs (ch. 10–11) has now become a *policy question*
about thousands, and because everything after ch. 15 — the European mission, the collection,
the arrest — presupposes its answer. It is the structural centre of the book, and Luke marks
it by giving three speeches and a letter.

**Structure.** The demand (1–5) — Peter (6–11) — Barnabas and Paul (12) — James, with the
citation (13–21) — the letter (22–29) — the reception at Antioch (30–35).

**Text-first findings.**

- **δεῖ περιτέμνειν** (15:5) `[T]` — a **false necessity**, one of two in the nineteen
  *δεῖ* occurrences (with 19:36). The Pharisaic party's *δεῖ* is overturned by the Council;
  Luke's *δεῖ* is not automatically divine.
- **καθαρίσας τὰς καρδίας αὐτῶν** (15:9) and **οὐθὲν διέκρινεν** `[T]` — Peter's speech uses
  the exact vocabulary of his vision: *καθαρίζω* (10:15; 11:9; 15:9) and *διακρίνω* (10:20;
  11:2; 11:12; 15:9). **The Council is settled with the words of the sheet.** *High
  confidence — verified by lemma; this is the sweep's strongest Move 2 chain.*
- **ζυγόν … ὃν οὔτε οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν οὔτε ἡμεῖς ἰσχύσαμεν βαστάσαι** (15:10) `[T]` — Peter's
  argument is not that the law was bad but that no generation could carry it.
- **λαὸν τῷ ὀνόματι αὐτοῦ** (15:14) `[T]` — James says God visited the Gentiles to take from
  them **a people (λαός) for his name**. *λαός* occurs forty-eight times in Acts and is
  overwhelmingly Israel; here it is applied to Gentiles. *High confidence — the single most
  theologically loaded word-choice in the chapter.*
- **σκηνὴν Δαυὶδ** (15:16) `[T]` — the third and last **σκηνή** in Acts; see Acts 7:43–44.

**Tool 11.** **Amos 9:11–12 LXX → 15:16–18** *(high confidence — direct quotation with
formula; the LXX form is load-bearing)*. *Source context:* the final oracle of Amos, after
eight chapters of unrelieved judgement — the fallen booth of David raised, the ruins
rebuilt, and (in the Greek) *that the rest of mankind may seek the Lord, and all the
nations upon whom my name is called*. The Hebrew reads instead *that they may possess the
remnant of Edom and all the nations called by my name* — **a substantive divergence, and
the argument works only on the Greek.** Triage: **category 3** — the Greek as the text the
NT argues from. It stands whichever *Vorlage* is prior, and it is not a mistake to be
corrected by the Hebrew. *[T] on the divergence; the categorisation is [I], high
confidence.* *Book usage:* the **second** use of Amos in Acts; Stephen quoted Amos 5:25–27
at 7:42–43, also from the Greek and also with a divergence (*beyond Babylon*). **Both of
Luke's Amos citations are LXX-dependent, and they stand at the book's pivot and its hinge.**
*[T] on the pattern; [I] on the design, moderate — flagged for audit.* *OT-to-OT:* Amos 9's
restored booth answers Amos 5's rejected tent within one scroll; Luke uses the two ends of
Amos at the two ends of the temple question. *Also:* James's *καθὼς γέγραπται* introduces
"the words of the prophets" **plural** (15:15), and the citation is a composite with
Jer 12:15 and Isa 45:21 woven in — Luke signals the plurality himself. *What it adds:*
the Council does not decide by precedent or by majority. It decides by showing that what
God has done matches what the prophets said he would do, in that order (15:14 *καθὼς* →
15:15 *τούτῳ συμφωνοῦσιν*). Experience is read by Scripture, not the reverse.

*Internal:* **answers §10:1–11:18** (the vision's vocabulary) and **§7:43–44** (the tents);
**plants** the decree, which returns at 16:4 and 21:25. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The **process** — dispute, gathered leadership, testimony of God's action,
Scripture, a judgement stated by one man, and a letter carried by named delegates — is a
**normative pattern**, and it is the only extended account of church decision-making in the
NT. The **four abstentions** (15:20, 29) are **occasional**, addressed to mixed table
fellowship (15:21 gives the reason); how far they bind is a genuinely contested question and
should be named as such rather than settled from the pulpit.

**Pitfall.** Preaching the Council as a model committee meeting and losing the argument. Two
errors: reading 15:28 (*ἔδοξεν … τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ καὶ ἡμῖν*) as a charter for church
authority in general, when its content is *not adding a burden*; and treating the four
abstentions as an arbitrary compromise, when 15:21 supplies the rationale — Moses is read
every Sabbath in every city, so Gentile believers are asked to do the minimum that makes a
shared table possible.

## 21. Acts 15:36–16:15 — The split, the call, and Lydia

**Position.** Q1: 15:30–35 has left Paul and Barnabas teaching at Antioch with the decree
settled. Q2: this passage exists here because a settled doctrine must now be *carried*, and
because Luke chooses to introduce the European mission with a quarrel. The juxtaposition is
his, not an accident.

**Structure.** The paroxysm and the two teams (15:36–41) — Timothy circumcised (16:1–5) —
the Spirit's prohibitions and the vision (16:6–10) — Philippi and Lydia (16:11–15).

**Text-first findings.**

- **παροξυσμός** (15:39) `[T]` — a sharp word ("irritation, exasperation"); Luke does not
  soften it and does not adjudicate. Barnabas disappears from Acts here; Mark returns to the
  NT record elsewhere. Luke's restraint is the finding.
- **περιέτεμεν αὐτόν** (16:3) `[T]` — Paul circumcises Timothy **one chapter after** the
  Council ruled circumcision unnecessary, and **one verse before** he delivers the decree
  (16:4). Luke places the two side by side deliberately: the Council settled what is
  *required*, not what is *permitted*.
- **κωλυθέντες ὑπὸ τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος** (16:6) and **οὐκ εἴασεν αὐτοὺς τὸ πνεῦμα Ἰησοῦ**
  (16:7) `[T]` — the fourth **κωλύω**, and the only one where the hinderer is God. In a book
  whose last word is *unhindered*, the one legitimate hindrance is the Spirit's. **τὸ πνεῦμα
  Ἰησοῦ** at 16:7 is unique in the NT. *High confidence.*
- **16:5 is a growth-notice with neither Exodus verb** `[T]`: *αἱ ἐκκλησίαι ἐστερεοῦντο τῇ
  πίστει καὶ ἐπερίσσευον τῷ ἀριθμῷ*. **στερεόω** links back to 3:7 and 3:16 (the lame man's
  ankles); **περισσεύω** occurs only here in Acts. See Book-Overview Tensions.
- **ἐζητήσαμεν** (16:10) `[T]` — the first "we". The four we-sections are 16:10–17;
  20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16. Luke offers no explanation, and the sweep offers none: it is
  data, and the inferences drawn from it are `[I]` at best.
- **διήνοιξεν τὴν καρδίαν** (16:14) `[T]` — *διανοίγω* in Acts at 7:56 (the heavens), 16:14
  (Lydia's heart), 17:3 (Paul opening the Scriptures). Luke uses one verb for opened heavens,
  opened Scriptures and opened hearts. *High confidence — verified by lemma.*

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §13:2** (the Spirit sends) by showing the
Spirit also *stops*; **plants** the Asia prohibition that 19:10 will reverse (*all who lived
in Asia heard the word*) — **a Move 4 link independently reached in the existing solo run on
18:23–19:20** `[S: prior report]`, and confirmed here from the text. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The quarrel is **descriptive**, and preachers should resist resolving it — Luke
does not. Timothy's circumcision is **descriptive and situational**, and the principle it
illustrates (1 Cor 9:20) is normative while the act is not. The Macedonian vision is
**descriptive and unique**. Lydia's household baptism (16:15) is **descriptive**, and is one
of the texts on which paedobaptist and credobaptist readings genuinely diverge — name the
divergence, do not settle it in passing.

**Pitfall.** "Follow the open doors / closed doors of the Spirit" as a guidance method. Luke
gives no mechanism for how the prohibition was known, offers a vision only after two
refusals, and the whole sequence produces a *conclusion* (*συμβιβάζοντες*, 16:10 — "we
concluded") rather than a certainty. Preach the sovereignty; do not manufacture a technique.

## 22. Acts 16:16–40 — Philippi: the slave girl, the jail, the citizenship

**Position.** Q1: 16:14–15 has produced the first European convert, a business-woman with a
household. Q2: this passage exists here because Luke needs to show what the gospel costs and
what it overturns in a Roman colony: it takes the second convert out of a household that was
making money from her, and the third out of the prison service. Lydia, a slave girl, and a
jailer — Luke's three Philippian converts span the entire social range of the colony.

**Structure.** The exorcism and its economics (16–21) — the beating and the stocks (22–24) —
midnight singing and the earthquake (25–28) — the jailer's conversion and household (29–34)
— the citizenship demand (35–40).

**Text-first findings.**

- **ὁδὸν σωτηρίας** (16:17) `[T]` — the demon-possessed girl announces "the Way of
  salvation", combining the movement's name (*ὁδός*, 20×) with its message (*σωτηρία*, 6×).
  **The most accurate summary of the Christian message in Acts is spoken by a demon.** *High
  confidence — a genuine and preachable irony.*
- **ἐργασία** (16:16, 16:19) and **ἐλπὶς τῆς ἐργασίας** `[T]` — the word is *profit*, twice,
  and the charge that follows is framed as loyalty to Rome (16:21). Luke exposes the gap
  between the stated grievance and the real one — exactly as he will at Ephesus (19:25).
  *High confidence; the pairing with 19:25 is [I], moderate-to-high.*
- **τί με δεῖ ποιεῖν ἵνα σωθῶ;** (16:30) `[T]` — the third of three "what must I/we do?"
  moments: **2:37** (*τί ποιήσωμεν;*, the Pentecost crowd), **9:6** (*ὅ τί σε δεῖ ποιεῖν*,
  said to Saul), **16:30** (the jailer). Jew, persecutor, Gentile. *High confidence —
  verified against the text; a clean three-point structural finding.*
- **ἀνέθη … τὰ δεσμά** (16:26) `[T]` — every prisoner's bonds were loosed and **nobody
  left**. Luke's earthquake opens the doors and the narrative interest is that they stay.
- **Ῥωμαῖοι** (16:37, 38) `[T]` — Paul plays the citizenship card *after* the beating, not
  before. Luke does not explain; the effect is that the magistrates must come in person, so
  that the church Paul leaves behind is publicly not a criminal association.

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §5:19 and §12:6–10** (the third prison-
opening in Acts, and the first where nobody escapes) and **plants** the Roman-citizenship
theme that 22:25–29 and 25:11 will make structural. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The exorcism is **descriptive and apostolic** — and note Paul is *annoyed*
(*διαπονηθείς*), not triumphant. The midnight praying and singing is a **normative example**
and Luke means it to be. The jailer's household baptism (16:33–34) is **descriptive**, with
the same paedobaptism/credobaptism divergence as Lydia's; 16:34 says he rejoiced *πανοικεὶ
πεπιστευκὼς τῷ θεῷ*, which each side reads differently. Name it.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 16:31 ("Believe in the Lord Jesus and you will be saved, you and your
household") as a promise that a believer's family will be saved. The clause is an answer to
*what must I do*, and the following verse shows what happened: *they spoke the word of the
Lord to him and to all who were in his house*. The household was saved by hearing, not by
his believing on their behalf.

## 23. Acts 17:1–15 — Thessalonica and Berea

*Not in the book-overview's pericope map — see Book-Overview Tensions.*

**Position.** Q1: 16:37–40 has left Philippi with the magistrates apologising. Q2: this
passage exists here to establish, immediately before Athens, that Paul's normal method is
**scriptural argument in a synagogue** — so that the Areopagus, where he has no Scripture to
argue from, reads as the exception it is. Remove 17:1–15 and Athens looks like Paul's
method; keep it and Athens looks like Paul's improvisation.

**Structure.** Thessalonica: three sabbaths, division, the riot at Jason's house (1–9) —
Berea: examination and belief, then the same opponents arrive (10–15).

**Text-first findings.**

- **διελέξατο … διανοίγων καὶ παρατιθέμενος** (17:2–3) `[T]` — three verbs for one activity:
  he reasoned, opened and set before them. *διανοίγω* again (see 16:14): the same verb for
  opening Scriptures and opening Lydia's heart.
- **τὸν χριστὸν ἔδει παθεῖν** (17:3) `[T]` — another of the nineteen *δεῖ*, and the same
  formulation as Luke 24:26, 46. Paul's synagogue argument is the risen Christ's own
  Emmaus argument.
- **οἱ τὴν οἰκουμένην ἀναστατώσαντες** (17:6) `[T]` — *ἀναστατόω* occurs twice in Acts:
  here, of the missionaries ("these who have turned the world upside down"), and at
  **21:38**, of **the Egyptian revolutionary** the tribune mistakes Paul for. Luke uses the
  agitator-word for the gospel and for a terrorist, and lets the reader feel the charge.
  *High confidence — verified by lemma.*
- **βασιλέα ἕτερον … Ἰησοῦν** (17:7) `[T]` — the accusation is political and, on its own
  terms, accurate. Luke reports it without denial.
- **εὐγενέστεροι … ἀνακρίνοντες τὰς γραφάς** (17:11) `[T]` — *ἀνακρίνω* occurs six times in
  Acts, and **every other occurrence is a judicial examination of Paul** (4:9; 12:19; 24:8;
  28:18) or of prisoners. The Bereans put the *message* on trial with the same verb Rome
  uses for putting Paul on trial. *High confidence — verified by lemma; a fine, unforced
  point.*

**Tool 11.** No citation given verbatim, though 17:2–3 describes citation. *Internal:*
**answers §13:14–41** (the same synagogue method) and **plants** the contrast with 17:16–34.
*High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The Berean practice (17:11) is a **normative pattern** and Luke commends it in
so many words (*εὐγενέστεροι*). Jason's bond (17:9) is **descriptive**. The pattern of
opponents following from city to city (17:13) is **descriptive** but explains the tone of
Paul's later letters.

**Pitfall.** Making the Bereans a text about private devotional Bible reading. They are
examining a *preacher's claims* against the Scriptures, publicly and daily, and Luke's word
for it is forensic. Preach it as a congregation's duty to test the pulpit, which is what it
is.

## 24. Acts 17:16–34 — Paul at the Areopagus ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 17:1–15 has just shown the synagogue method three times over; 14:15–17 has
given a four-verse trial run of the pagan address. Q2: this passage exists here because Luke
has exhausted the audiences that share his Scriptures and must show the gospel meeting a
culture that has none — and because Athens is the one place in Acts where Paul is neither
beaten nor imprisoned, only mocked. The absence of violence is part of the point.

**Structure.** The provocation (16) — the two venues, synagogue and agora (17–21) — the
speech: the altar (22–23), the Creator (24–25), the nations and the search (26–27), the
poets and the argument from kinship (28–29), the summons (30–31) — the three responses (32–34).

**Text-first findings.**

- **παρωξύνετο τὸ πνεῦμα αὐτοῦ** (17:16) `[T]` — the cognate of *παροξυσμός* at 15:39. Paul
  was exasperated with Barnabas and is exasperated by idols; Luke uses one root. The speech
  that follows is famously courteous, and Luke has told us what is underneath it.
- **κατείδωλον** (17:16) `[T]` — a NT hapax, "full of idols".
- **οὐκ ἐν χειροποιήτοις ναοῖς κατοικεῖ** (17:24) `[T]` — **χειροποίητος** occurs twice in
  Acts: **7:48** (Stephen, of the temple) and **17:24** (Paul, of pagan shrines). Paul says
  to Athens what Stephen was killed for saying to Jerusalem. *High confidence — verified by
  lemma. The overview does not have this, and it is the pericope's most important link.*
- **ναός** (17:24; 19:24) `[T]` — only two occurrences in Acts, and both are pagan temples.
  Luke reserves *ἱερόν* for the Jerusalem temple and never calls the church a *ναός*.
- **ὁρίσας προστεταγμένους καιροὺς καὶ τὰς ὁροθεσίας** (17:26) `[T]` — *ὁρίζω* occurs five
  times in Acts (2:23; 10:42; 11:29; 17:26; 17:31), and **two of the five are in this
  speech**: God has fixed the nations' times and has fixed a day of judgement. The same verb
  that determined the cross (2:23) determines the calendar of the nations.
- **ἀνάστασιν** (17:18, 17:32) `[T]` — the Athenians think *Ἰησοῦν καὶ τὴν ἀνάστασιν* are
  two deities (17:18); the speech ends on the resurrection and the audience splits on it.
  The frame and the climax are the same word.

**Tool 11.** No OT citation — the striking fact of the pericope, and a genuine Move 2
nil-return worth stating. The quotations are **pagan**: *ἐν αὐτῷ ζῶμεν καὶ κινούμεθα καὶ
ἐσμέν* and *τοῦ γὰρ καὶ γένος ἐσμέν* (17:28, the latter from Aratus's *Phaenomena*). But the
speech's *substance* is saturated with the OT without quoting it: 17:24–25 is Isaiah 42:5
and 1 Kings 8:27 in paraphrase; 17:26 is Deuteronomy 32:8; 17:29 is the idol-polemic of
Isaiah 40 and 44 and Psalm 115. *Conceptual allusion, moderate-to-high confidence; the
Deut 32:8 link is worth a solo run's attention, since Deut 32:8 is itself a category-2
variant (MT "sons of Israel" / LXX and 4QDeut-j "sons of God") with a bearing on how 17:26
is heard.* *Internal:* **answers §14:15–17** (the rough draft) and **§7:48** (the
hand-made shrines). *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The speech is a **normative pattern** for addressing a scriptureless audience —
Luke records it in full and gives it three responses without embarrassment. It is **not** a
warrant for natural theology detached from its setting: every positive statement about
pagan religion in the speech is a set-up for 17:29–31, and the speech ends with repentance,
a fixed day and a raised man. The three responses (mockery, deferral, faith) are
**descriptive** and are Luke's realistic expectation.

**Pitfall.** Two, in opposite directions. First, treating Athens as Paul's failure — "he
resolved to know nothing but Christ crucified afterwards" — which the text does not say and
17:34 contradicts. Second, treating it as a licence to affirm other religions: the speech
begins from an altar *to an unknown god* and calls the Athenians' whole religious apparatus
*ἄγνοια* (17:30). The corrective is to preach both halves: real common ground, and a real
summons.

## 25. Acts 18:1–17 — Corinth, Gallio, and the assurance

**Position.** Q1: 17:32–34 has left Athens with a small result and no church planted. Q2:
this passage exists here because a discouraged missionary in a hard city is exactly the
setting a vision of assurance requires (18:9–10), and because Luke needs a **Roman legal
precedent** on the record before the trials of chs. 21–26. Gallio's ruling is the first time
a Roman magistrate defines the Christian question as internal to Judaism — and Luke saves it
for the middle of the book so it can be assumed later.

**Structure.** Aquila and Priscilla, and the tentmaking (1–4) — the turn to the Gentiles
(5–8) — the night vision (9–11) — Gallio (12–17).

**Text-first findings.**

- **Κλαύδιον χωρίζεσθαι πάντας τοὺς Ἰουδαίους ἀπὸ τῆς Ῥώμης** (18:2) `[T]` — Luke's only
  notice of the Claudian expulsion, given in passing as the reason a couple were in Corinth.
- **μὴ φοβοῦ, ἀλλὰ λάλει καὶ μὴ σιωπήσῃς** (18:9) `[T]` — the Lord's words to Paul are two
  imperatives and a prohibition, and they concern **speech**. Compare 23:11 (*θάρσει*),
  27:24 (*μὴ φοβοῦ, Παῦλε*): three night-visions, all commanding courage, all in the second
  half of the book.
- **λαός ἐστί μοι πολὺς ἐν τῇ πόλει ταύτῃ** (18:10) `[T]` — *λαός*, Israel's word, of people
  in Corinth who are not yet converted. Compare 15:14. *High confidence.*
- **οὐδὲν τούτων τῷ Γαλλίωνι ἔμελεν** (18:17) `[T]` — Luke's driest sentence, and a narrator's
  comment: the proconsul does not care. It functions as an acquittal by indifference.
- **σκηνοποιοί** (18:3) `[T]` — a NT hapax; the trade is disputed (tentmaker / leatherworker).
  *Uncertain — consider verifying in a lexicon.*

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §13:46** (the second of three turns to the
Gentiles, with the same gesture — *ἐκτιναξάμενος*, cf. 13:51) and **plants** the Gallio
precedent for 23:29, 25:18–19 and 26:31, where three more Roman officials reach the same
verdict. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The tentmaking is **descriptive**, though 20:34 makes it exemplary in
retrospect. The vision is **descriptive and unique**. Crispus's household belief (18:8) is
**descriptive**. Gallio's ruling is **descriptive** but is Luke's apologetic evidence.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 18:10 ("I have many people in this city") as a promise of numerical
success for any given ministry. It is a specific word to a specific man in a specific city,
given as the ground of a command to keep speaking; the general truth it carries is about
God's prior claim on people not yet gathered, not about guaranteed results.

## 26. Acts 18:18–19:41 — Ephesus: John's disciples, Sceva, the riot

*A full solo dig-deeper on 18:23–19:20 already exists in the folder
(`dig-deeper-acts-18-23to19-20.md`), and another on 19:21–20:1
(`dig-deeper-acts-19-21to20-1.md`). Both were consulted at the checking stage only, after
this section's text-work was complete. Points below marked* `[S: prior report]` *originate
with them; unmarked points were reached independently and, where they agree, the agreement
is earned.*

**Position.** Q1: 16:6 forbade speaking the word in Asia; 18:19–21 left a promise to return.
Q2: this passage exists here because both the prohibition and the promise now come due —
19:10's *all who lived in Asia heard the word of the Lord* is the exact reversal of 16:6.
*Independently reached here and in the prior run.*

**Structure.** The vow and the return (18:18–23) — Apollos (18:24–28) — the twelve at
Ephesus (19:1–7) — the hall of Tyrannus (19:8–10) — Sceva's sons (19:11–20) — the Rome
resolve (19:21–22) — the riot (19:23–40).

**Text-first findings.**

- **ἴσχυσεν … ἴσχυεν** `[T]` — the evil spirit **overpowered** the exorcists (19:16,
  *ἴσχυσεν κατ' αὐτῶν*) and the word **prevailed** (19:20, *ηὔξανεν καὶ ἴσχυεν*). The
  catchword is genuine and was **independently identified in the prior solo run**
  `[S: prior report]`. **What this sweep adds:** the same verb answers *κατίσχυον* in
  Exodus 1:7 LXX, so 19:20 is simultaneously an internal catchword *and* the third member
  of the Exodus refrain. Both readings can stand; the Exodus link is the new datum.
  *Moderate-to-high.*
- **ἐκκλησία of a pagan mob** (19:32, 19:39, 19:40) `[T]` — three of the twenty-three
  occurrences in Acts, and **the only place Luke uses the word of a non-Christian assembly
  other than Israel at Sinai (7:38)**. The riotous *ἐκκλησία* does not know why it has met
  (19:32) and is dismissed by a pagan clerk who calls a *lawful* assembly the proper venue
  (19:39). *Independently reached here; also found in the prior run* `[S: prior report]`.
  *High confidence — verified by lemma.* **Versification note:** the prior run cites
  19:32, 39, 41; SBLGNT merges the traditional 19:40–41 into one verse, so the three
  occurrences are 19:32, 19:39, 19:40 in this edition. Not a disagreement — a numbering
  difference worth knowing when quoting.
- **ἡ ὁδός** absolute (19:9, 19:23) `[T]` — twice in this panel, and 19:23's *τάραχος … περὶ
  τῆς ὁδοῦ* makes "the Way" the name under which the movement is rioted against.
- **δεῖ με καὶ Ῥώμην ἰδεῖν** (19:21) `[T]` — the programmatic *δεῖ* that governs chs. 20–28;
  *Ῥώμη* occurs five times in Acts (18:2; 19:21; 23:11; 28:14; 28:16) and this is the first
  time it is a destination rather than a place someone came from. *High confidence.*
- **19:11–12 (the handkerchiefs)** `[T]` — Luke's own qualifier is *δυνάμεις … οὐ τὰς
  τυχούσας*, "extraordinary miracles", i.e. he marks them as *not* the norm.
- **19:20 reads τοῦ κυρίου ὁ λόγος** in both SBLGNT and NA28 `[T]` — the word of *the Lord*,
  where 6:7 and 12:24 have *of God*. The refrain's genitive varies.

**Tool 11.** No OT citation in the whole panel — which the prior run rightly flags as
evidence against a strong form of the claim that a citation marks every Lukan turning point
`[S: prior report]`. This sweep confirms that: **the three growth-seams at 6:7, 12:24 and
19:20 carry no citation formula, yet all three carry the Exodus 1:7 vocabulary.** Luke's
seams are allusive, not citational. *[I], moderate-to-high — a claim worth auditing, and
one that bears directly on the Pao thesis.* *Internal:* **answers §16:6** (Asia) and
**§8:9–24** (Simon the magician answered by the Ephesian magic-books); **plants** Rome.

**Copycat.** The twelve at Ephesus (19:1–7) are a **unique frontier event** — Luke marks
their deficiency explicitly (*οὐδ' εἰ πνεῦμα ἅγιον ἔστιν ἠκούσαμεν*), so this is a case of
incomplete instruction being completed, not of a second blessing. The handkerchiefs are
**descriptive** and Luke's own adjective says so. The book-burning (19:19) is a **normative
example** of repentance costing something measurable. The riot is **descriptive** and
Luke's tone is satirical.

**Pitfall.** Using 19:1–7 to establish a two-stage reception of the Spirit. These men had
not heard of the Spirit, had only John's baptism, and are **re-baptised** — which no
Christian in Acts ever is. Whatever the passage is, it is not a pattern for believers.

## 27. Acts 20:1–16 — Macedonia, Troas, and Eutychus

*Not in the book-overview's pericope map, and only partly covered by the existing solo run
on 19:21–20:1 — see Book-Overview Tensions.*

**Position.** Q1: 19:21 has set Paul's face towards Jerusalem and Rome. Q2: this passage
exists here to move him there while doing two things Luke wants on the record before the
farewell speech: naming the **delegation** (20:4), which is the collection party, and giving
one full picture of a **Christian gathering** (20:7–12) — the only extended one in Acts.

**Structure.** The Greek circuit and the plot (1–3) — the companion list (4–6) — Troas: the
long sermon, Eutychus, the breaking of bread (7–12) — the coastal voyage to Miletus (13–16).

**Text-first findings.**

- **The companion list (20:4)** `[T]` — seven men from Berea, Thessalonica, Derbe, Asia:
  Macedonia, Achaia, Galatia and Asia all represented. Luke never says why. Read with
  Rom 15:25–27 and 1 Cor 16:1–4 it is the collection delegation, but **Acts never mentions
  the collection** except obliquely at 24:17 (*ἐλεημοσύνας ποιήσων εἰς τὸ ἔθνος μου*).
  *That silence is a genuine feature of Acts and should be named rather than filled in.*
- **ἐν δὲ τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων** (20:7) `[T]` — the earliest narrative notice of a first-day
  gathering, and Luke's stated purpose for it is *κλάσαι ἄρτον*.
- **κλάω / ἄρτος** (20:7, 20:11) `[T]` — the second and third of the four *κλάω* occurrences
  (2:46; 20:7; 20:11; 27:35).
- **ἤρθη νεκρός** (20:9) `[T]` — Luke, a narrator who elsewhere says *ἐδόκει* when something
  merely seemed so, says the young man **was taken up dead**. Paul's *ἡ ψυχὴ αὐτοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ
  ἐστιν* (20:10) is a statement about the restored state, not a diagnosis of the prior one.
  *Moderate-to-high; the reading is contested and should be named as such.*
- **ἐπέπεσεν αὐτῷ καὶ συμπεριλαβών** (20:10) `[T]` — the posture is Elijah's and Elisha's
  (1 Kgs 17:21; 2 Kgs 4:34). *Moderate confidence — conceptual allusion, no verbal quotation;
  the prophetic type-scene is well established.*
- **ἔσπευδεν … τὴν ἡμέραν τῆς πεντηκοστῆς** (20:16) `[T]` — Paul hurries to be in Jerusalem
  for **Pentecost**. The feast on which the book's story began is the deadline for the
  journey that ends it. *High confidence on the datum; the structural irony is [I].*

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §2:1** (Pentecost, now a travel deadline)
and **§2:42–46** (the breaking of bread, now in a Gentile city on the first day);
**plants** the Jerusalem arrival. *Moderate-to-high.*

**Copycat.** The first-day gathering with teaching and bread is a **normative pattern** —
this is the closest Acts comes to describing a service. The raising is **descriptive and
apostolic**. Paul preaching until midnight is **descriptive**, and Luke's willingness to
record that someone fell asleep and fell out of a window is a gift to preachers.

**Pitfall.** Mining Eutychus for a lesson ("don't sleep in church"; "long sermons kill").
The overview's own trap-list names this: *mining incidental detail*. What Luke records is
that the gathering resumed, the bread was broken, Paul talked till dawn, and *they were not
a little comforted*.

## 28. Acts 20:17–38 — The Miletus farewell ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 20:16 has explained why Paul is at Miletus and not Ephesus — he is in a
hurry — so the elders come to him. Q2: this passage exists here because it is Luke's **only
sustained address to Christians**, and it has to come before the arrest: Paul must set out
the shape of a finished ministry while he is still free, so that chs. 21–28 can be read as
the ministry finishing. It is a valedictory placed deliberately before the end.

**Structure.** What you know of my past (18–21) — what I now face (22–24) — my innocence and
your charge (25–31) — the commendation and the example (32–35) — the parting (36–38).

**Text-first findings.**

- **δεδεμένος … τῷ πνεύματι** (20:22) `[T]` — "**bound** in the Spirit I go to Jerusalem",
  in a book where Paul is about to be literally bound (21:33, *δεθῆναι ἁλύσεσι δυσί*). Luke
  uses *δέω* for both. *High confidence — verified by lemma.*
- **διαμαρτύρομαι** (20:21, 20:23, 20:24) `[T]` — three of the nine occurrences in Acts are
  in this speech: Paul testifies, the Spirit testifies to him, and his aim is to testify.
- **τὴν διακονίαν ἣν ἔλαβον παρὰ τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ** (20:24) and **τελειῶσαι τὸν δρόμον**
  `[T]` — *δρόμος* occurs twice in Acts (13:25, of John the Baptist finishing his course;
  20:24, of Paul). Luke gives the forerunner and the apostle the same word.
- **πᾶσαν τὴν βουλὴν τοῦ θεοῦ** (20:27) `[T]` — the fourth of the seven *βουλή* occurrences
  and the only one that is a preacher's subject matter rather than a plan.
- **ποιμαίνειν τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ θεοῦ, ἣν περιεποιήσατο διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου** (20:28)
  `[T]` — **a genuine textual and grammatical crux**, and the sharpest in Acts. (a) *τοῦ
  θεοῦ* (NA28, SBLGNT, with strong Alexandrian support) or *τοῦ κυρίου* (a well-attested
  variant) — **category 2**, and the choice bears directly on whether the verse says God
  purchased the church with his own blood. (b) *τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου* may be "his own
  blood" or "the blood of his Own [Son]", the latter taking *τοῦ ἰδίου* substantivally.
  *Moderate confidence; do not build a Christological argument here without checking the
  NA28 apparatus and a commentary that works the grammar. Flagged to Open Questions.*
- **ἐπισκόπους** and **πρεσβυτέρους** (20:17, 20:28) `[T]` — Luke calls the same men elders
  and overseers eleven verses apart, and tells them to shepherd. Three terms, one office.
  *High confidence.*
- **λύκοι βαρεῖς** (20:29) `[T]` — and the wolves come **from among yourselves** (20:30).

**Tool 11.** No OT citation. The one quotation is a **saying of Jesus not recorded in any
Gospel** — *μακάριόν ἐστιν μᾶλλον διδόναι ἢ λαμβάνειν* (20:35) — Luke's only such agraphon.
*High confidence on the datum.* The **shepherd/flock** language (20:28–29) is a conceptual
allusion to **Ezekiel 34** and the failed shepherds of Israel *(moderate confidence — no
verbal quotation, but the constellation of shepherd, flock, scattering and wolves is
distinctive and canonically established)*. *Internal:* **answers §14:23** (the elders
appointed) and **§19:21** (the resolve to go); **plants** 21:11–14 (the warnings that
follow) and, at 20:25 (*οὐκέτι ὄψεσθε τὸ πρόσωπόν μου*), a statement Acts never confirms or
falsifies. *High confidence.*

**Copycat.** The speech is the book's densest cluster of **normative** material: the pattern
of ministry (20:18–21, 20:27, 20:31), the charge to elders (20:28), the warning about
internal corruption (20:29–30), the financial example (20:33–35). Paul's own itinerary and
the Spirit's warnings are **descriptive**. This is the one Acts passage a preacher may
apply to church leaders almost directly — which is why the Copycat discipline matters
*more* here, not less: the temptation is to apply the biography as well as the charge.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 20:28 as a proof-text for the deity of Christ without acknowledging
the two textual and grammatical questions in the clause. The doctrine is secure elsewhere;
the verse is contested, and a hearer with a study Bible will see the footnote. Say it
plainly and move on — the pastoral force of the verse ("the church he obtained") does not
depend on settling either question.

## 29. Acts 21:1–36 — Arrival at Jerusalem and arrest

**Position.** Q1: 20:22–23 has said the Spirit testifies in every city that bonds await.
Q2: this passage exists here to narrate the fulfilment, and to raise a question the sweep
should not resolve: at 21:4 the disciples at Tyre tell Paul *διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος* not to go
up, and he goes. Luke reports both and adjudicates neither.

**Structure.** The voyage and Tyre (1–6) — Caesarea and Philip's daughters (7–9) — Agabus
(10–14) — Jerusalem and James's proposal (15–26) — the temple riot and the arrest (27–36).

**Text-first findings.**

- **21:4 against 20:22–23** `[T]` — the same Spirit, opposite counsel. The honest reading is
  that the Spirit's *information* (bonds await) is consistently given and the *inference*
  (therefore do not go) is the disciples'. *Moderate confidence; name the difficulty rather
  than smoothing it.*
- **παραδώσουσιν εἰς χεῖρας ἐθνῶν** (21:11) `[T]` — Agabus's oracle uses the wording of
  Luke's passion predictions (Luke 18:32, *παραδοθήσεται τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*). Combined with
  21:13 (*ἑτοίμως ἔχω … ἀποθανεῖν εἰς Ἰερουσαλήμ*) and 21:14 (*τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα
  γινέσθω*, cf. Luke 22:42), the journey is narrated in the shape of the passion.
  *Moderate-to-high — three converging verbal echoes; recommend a solo run to weigh them.*
- **μυριάδες … ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου** (21:20) `[T]` — tens of thousands of law-zealous Jewish
  believers. Luke's own figure, and it is the strongest single datum against reading Acts
  as narrating the church's departure from Israel.
- **κεκοίνωκεν τὸν ἅγιον τόπον** (21:28) `[T]` — the accusation uses **κοινόω**, the verb of
  Peter's vision (10:15; 11:9). **The third and last occurrence in Acts.** Peter was told
  not to call common what God had cleansed; Paul is charged with making the holy place
  common. *High confidence — verified by lemma. This closes the purity chain, and the
  overview does not have it.*
- **τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ νόμου καὶ τοῦ τόπου τούτου** (21:28) `[T]` — a three-fold charge
  verbally close to the charge against Stephen (6:13, *κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου καὶ τοῦ
  νόμου*). **τόπος** again. *High confidence.*
- **Αἶρε αὐτόν** (21:36) `[T]` — the crowd's cry, and Luke has used it before at Luke 23:18
  (*Αἶρε τοῦτον*) of Jesus. It recurs at 22:22.

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §6:13** (Stephen's charge, now Paul's),
**§10:15/11:9** (the purity verb), **§20:22–23** (the bonds), **§9:16** (*ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν …
παθεῖν*); **plants** the three defence speeches. *High confidence — this pericope has the
densest internal-echo load in Acts.*

**Copycat.** James's proposal and Paul's compliance (21:23–26) are **descriptive**, and
whether Paul was right to comply is genuinely contested — do not settle it in passing.
Paul's resolve (21:13) is a **normative example** of a specific kind: readiness, not
recklessness. The riot is **descriptive**. Philip's four prophesying daughters (21:9) are
**descriptive** and Luke offers no comment; note that he mentions them at all.

**Pitfall.** Making 21:4 and 21:11–14 a lesson about discerning guidance, and thereby
having to decide whether Paul disobeyed. Luke's own interest is elsewhere: he is showing
that Paul went to Jerusalem knowing exactly what awaited, having been told repeatedly, and
that the church's final word was *the Lord's will be done*. Preach the resolve and the
submission; leave the guidance puzzle named and open.

## 30. Acts 21:37–23:11 — Defence to the crowd and to the Sanhedrin

**Position.** Q1: 21:36 has the crowd screaming for Paul's removal and the tribune carrying
him up the steps. Q2: this passage exists here because Luke's apologetic requires Paul to
state his own case twice — once to Jerusalem in Hebrew and once to the council — before any
Roman hears it, so that the reader knows the charge is theological before Felix does. And it
ends with the Lord's own verdict (23:11), which settles the direction of the last six
chapters.

**Structure.** The tribune's mistake and Paul's request (21:37–40) — the Hebrew speech: my
past, the road, Ananias, the temple vision (22:1–21) — the crowd's explosion at the word
"Gentiles" (22:22–23) — the citizenship (22:24–29) — the Sanhedrin, the resurrection ploy,
the rescue (22:30–23:10) — the night vision (23:11).

**Text-first findings.**

- **ὁ Αἰγύπτιος … ἀναστατώσας** (21:38) `[T]` — the tribune's guess uses *ἀναστατόω*, the
  same verb the Thessalonian mob used of the missionaries (17:6). *High confidence —
  verified by lemma; only two occurrences in Acts.*
- **τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ** (21:40; 22:2; and again at 26:14) `[T]` — Luke notes the language
  three times, and twice notes its effect: the crowd goes quieter (22:2), and the voice on
  the road spoke it (26:14).
- **The speech's structure is the same three-part shape as 26:4–18** `[T]` — upbringing,
  persecution, the road — and each telling adds something: 22:17–21 supplies the **temple
  vision** absent from ch. 9 and ch. 26, in which Paul is told to leave Jerusalem because
  they will not receive his testimony. *That is the datum the crowd then proves* (22:22).
  *High confidence — the placement is Luke's.*
- **ἐγὼ Φαρισαῖός εἰμι … περὶ ἐλπίδος καὶ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν ἐγὼ κρίνομαι** (23:6) `[T]` —
  **ἐλπίς** occurs eight times in Acts and **five are in the trial sequence** (23:6; 24:15;
  26:6; 26:7; 28:20). Paul's defence is that he is on trial for Israel's hope. *High
  confidence — verified by lemma.*
- **θάρσει** (23:11) `[T]` — *θαρσέω* occurs once in Acts, here; the cognate noun *θάρσος*
  once, at 28:15, when Paul sees the brothers at the Forum of Appius and *takes courage*.
  Two courage-words, one at each end of the road to Rome. *High confidence — verified across
  the SBLGNT.*

**Tool 11.** One citation: **Exodus 22:28 → 23:5** (*ἄρχοντα τοῦ λαοῦ σου οὐκ ἐρεῖς κακῶς*)
*(high confidence — direct quotation with formula γέγραπται)*. Move 1: the verse stands
among the covenant ordinances protecting the community's order. *What it adds:* Paul, having
just called the high priest a whitewashed wall, submits to the law he is accused of
abandoning — which is precisely the point he needs to make. *Internal:* **answers §9:1–19**
(the first telling) and **§7:58** (*I stood by … guarding the garments*, 22:20 — Paul
himself recalls the scene Luke narrated fifteen chapters earlier, using *μάρτυς* of
Stephen) `[T], high`; **plants** 26:9–18.

**Copycat.** The defence speeches are a **normative pattern** in one narrow respect —
testimony given under pressure, structured as before-and-after with the risen Christ at the
centre. Paul's use of his citizenship (22:25) and of the Pharisee/Sadducee division (23:6)
are **descriptive**, and the second is genuinely debated: is it shrewdness or is it the
truthful naming of the real issue? Luke does not say; 24:21 has Paul defending the same
sentence, which suggests he meant it.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 23:6 as a lesson in tactical division-sowing. The alternative reading
— that the resurrection *is* what Paul is on trial for, and the Pharisees are half-right —
is supported by 24:15, 24:21, 26:6–8 and 28:20, where he says the same thing four more times
without a Sadducee in the room.

## 31. Acts 23:12–26:32 — Felix, Festus, Agrippa, and the appeal

**Position.** Q1: 23:11 has told Paul he must testify at Rome. Q2: this long unit exists
here to get him there **in Roman custody and on his own appeal**, and to put on the record
the four official findings of innocence that make the journey a transfer rather than an
extradition. Nothing in it advances the mission geographically; everything in it advances
the apologetic.

**Structure.** The plot and the transfer (23:12–35) — Felix: Tertullus, Paul's answer, two
years (24:1–27) — Festus and the appeal (25:1–12) — Agrippa's hearing: Festus's briefing,
Paul's defence, the verdict (25:13–26:32).

**Text-first findings.**

- **The four verdicts** `[T]` — 23:9, 23:29, 25:25, 26:31, with 26:32 (*could have been
  released*). See Headline Finding 5. Compare Luke 23:4, 14, 22 (Pilate, three times), 23:15
  (Herod), 23:41 (the criminal), 23:47 (the centurion). **Luke acquits Paul in the same
  cumulative way he acquitted Jesus.** *[T] on both sets; [I] on the deliberate parallel,
  moderate-to-high.*
- **ἀπολογέομαι / ἀπολογία** `[T]` — the verb six times (19:33; 24:10; 25:8; 26:1, 2, 24),
  the noun twice (22:1; 25:16). Eight occurrences, seven of them in chs. 22–26. Luke's
  vocabulary tells you what these chapters are.
- **αἵρεσις** (24:5, 24:14; also 5:17; 15:5; 26:5; 28:22) `[T]` — Tertullus calls the Way a
  *sect*; Paul accepts the noun and redefines the practice (*κατὰ τὴν ὁδὸν ἣν λέγουσιν
  αἵρεσιν οὕτως λατρεύω τῷ πατρῴῳ θεῷ*, 24:14). Six occurrences, applied to Sadducees,
  Pharisees and Christians alike — Luke's word for a party within Judaism.
- **ἐλπίς** at 24:15, 26:6, 26:7 `[T]` — three of the five trial-sequence occurrences; at
  26:7 it is *τὸ δωδεκάφυλον ἡμῶν*, "our twelve tribes", worshipping night and day toward
  that hope. Paul's claim is that he is the loyal Jew in the room.
- **χάριτα καταθέσθαι τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις** (24:27; 25:9) `[T]` — the identical phrase of Felix
  and of Festus. Two governors, one motive, and Luke states it twice without comment.
  *High confidence.*
- **Καίσαρα ἐπικαλοῦμαι** (25:11) `[T]` — **ἐπικαλέω**, the verb of *everyone who calls on
  the name of the Lord shall be saved* (2:21) and of *those who call on your name* (9:14,
  9:21), and of Paul's own baptism (22:16, *ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ*). Twenty
  occurrences; the last six are all *appealing to Caesar* (25:11, 12, 21, 25; 26:32; 28:19).
  **Luke's calling-on verb changes object in the last four chapters.** *[T] on the
  distribution, verified by lemma; [I] on the significance, moderate — and worth an audit,
  because it could be pure idiom.*
- **σκληρόν σοι πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν** (26:14) `[T]` — a Greek proverb, present only in the
  third telling, and spoken (Luke says) in Hebrew. Paul quotes a pagan proverb to a Herodian
  king.
- **Χριστιανόν** (26:28) `[T]` — the second of the word's three NT occurrences; Agrippa uses
  the name Antioch coined (11:26).

**Tool 11.** No formal OT citation in four chapters — but 26:22–23 is a **summary appeal**
(*οὐδὲν ἐκτὸς λέγων ὧν τε οἱ προφῆται … καὶ Μωϋσῆς*) and 26:18 is saturated with Isaiah:
*ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμοὺς αὐτῶν, τοῦ ἐπιστρέψαι ἀπὸ σκότους εἰς φῶς* draws on **Isaiah 42:6–7 and
42:16** *(moderate-to-high confidence — conceptual and partly verbal; no formula)*. *Source
context:* the first Servant Song, where the Servant is given as a covenant to the people and
a light to the nations, to open blind eyes and bring out prisoners. *Book usage:* **the third
Servant Song used in Acts** — Isa 53 at 8:32, Isa 49:6 at 13:47, Isa 42 here. Luke draws on
the Servant Songs at the eunuch's conversion, at the formal turn to the Gentiles, and at
Paul's final defence. *[I], moderate-to-high — a Move 2 pattern the overview does not have,
and one of the sweep's better candidates for a claim audit.* *What it adds:* Paul describes
his own commission in the words of the Servant's, before a king — which is why 26:23's *if
the Christ must suffer, if he, first from the resurrection of the dead, would proclaim light*
follows immediately.

*Internal:* **answers §9:15** (*before Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel* — the
commission's three audiences are all present by ch. 26) `[T], high`; **plants** 27:24 and
28:19.

**Copycat.** The appeal to Caesar is **descriptive** and situational. Paul's courtesy to
hostile officials (24:10; 26:2–3, 25) is a **normative example**. Felix's greed (24:26) and
both governors' favour-seeking are **negative examples** with Luke's own verdict attached.
26:29 (*I would to God that … all who hear me today might become such as I am — except for
these chains*) is a **normative example** of evangelistic desire.

**Pitfall.** Preaching four chapters of trial as though the sermons were the only content
and the legal machinery were scaffolding. The machinery *is* the content: Luke is showing a
reader in the Roman world that the movement was examined by a tribune, two governors and a
king, and that not one of them found a crime. Preach the acquittals.

## 32. Acts 27:1–28:16 — The voyage, the storm, the shipwreck ⭐

**Position.** Q1: 25:12 has ruled that Paul goes to Caesar; 23:11 and 27:24 both say he
must. Q2: this passage exists here at extraordinary length — the longest sustained narrative
in Acts — because Luke has spent four chapters establishing that Rome cannot convict Paul,
and must now establish that nothing else can stop him either. The sea is the last court,
and it also fails to hold him.

**Structure.** The voyage to Fair Havens and the ignored advice (27:1–12) — the storm
(27:13–20) — Paul's first address and the angel (27:21–26) — the fourteenth night, the
sailors' escape, the meal (27:27–38) — the wreck and the deliverance of all (27:39–44) —
Malta: the viper, Publius, the healings (28:1–10) — the last leg to Rome (28:11–16).

**Text-first findings.**

- **σῴζω / διασῴζω** `[T]` — *σῴζω* thirteen times in Acts, and **the last two are the
  storm** (27:20 *περιῃρεῖτο ἐλπὶς πᾶσα τοῦ σῴζεσθαι ἡμᾶς*; 27:31 *ὑμεῖς σωθῆναι οὐ
  δύνασθε*). *διασῴζω* five times, **four of them in this unit** (27:43, 27:44, 28:1, 28:4;
  with 23:24). The vocabulary of salvation that ran through the sermons is here doing
  physical work — and Luke does not moralise the overlap. *High confidence — verified by
  lemma; the double sense is Luke's, and the preacher should let it sit rather than
  allegorising the ship.*
- **βουλή** twice (27:12, 27:42) `[T]` — the sixth and seventh occurrences, both human plans:
  the majority's decision to sail on, and the soldiers' plan to kill the prisoners. Both are
  overruled, the second by the centurion's *ἐκώλυσεν* (27:43) — the **sixth κωλύω**, and
  here a hindrance that saves Paul's life. *High confidence.*
- **λαβὼν ἄρτον εὐχαρίστησεν τῷ θεῷ ἐνώπιον πάντων καὶ κλάσας** (27:35) `[T]` — the fourth
  and last *κλάω*. Taking bread, giving thanks, breaking: the sequence of Luke 22:19 and
  24:30, performed by a prisoner before 276 pagans. **Luke does not call it a eucharist and
  neither should the preacher** — but the vocabulary is unmistakable and the effect on the
  ship's company (*εὔθυμοι δὲ γενόμενοι πάντες*) is stated. *High confidence on the verbal
  data; the sacramental reading is [I] and contested — name it as contested.*
- **οὐδενὸς ὑμῶν θρὶξ ἀπὸ τῆς κεφαλῆς ἀπολεῖται** (27:34) `[T]` — an OT idiom (1 Sam 14:45;
  2 Sam 14:11; 1 Kgs 1:52) that Luke has already used at Luke 21:18. *Moderate-to-high.*
- **ἡ δίκη** (28:4) `[T]` — the Maltese say *Justice* has not allowed him to live, and the
  word is capitalised as a deity in most editions. The islanders move from *φονεύς* to
  *θεόν* in three verses (28:4, 28:6) — the same movement as Lystra (14:11–19) in reverse.
  *High confidence.*
- **The centurion Julius** `[T]` — *ἑκατοντάρχης* thirteen times in Acts; Julius treats Paul
  *φιλανθρώπως* (27:3) and saves his life (27:43). With Cornelius (10:1) and the centurion
  at 22:26, Luke's centurions are consistently the reasonable ones.

**Tool 11.** No citation. *Internal:* **answers §23:11 and §19:21** (*δεῖ … Ῥώμην*) and
**§9:16**; **answers §14:11–18** (Malta reverses Lystra: there they took the missionaries
for gods and were talked out of it; here they take Paul for a murderer and then for a god,
and Luke does not record a correction) `[I], moderate`. **Plants** 28:17.

**Copycat.** The whole unit is **descriptive**. Paul's conduct — advising, encouraging,
praying, breaking bread, gathering sticks for the fire (28:3) — is a **normative example**
of an ordinary kind, and the stick-gathering is the detail worth preaching: the apostle who
has just been promised the lives of everyone on board is on the beach picking up brushwood.
The viper and the healings are **descriptive and apostolic**; 28:8–9 is the last miracle
sequence in Acts and Luke reports it without comment.

**Pitfall.** Allegorising the voyage — the ship as the church, the anchors as the promises,
the wreck as trial. Luke's own interest is stated at 27:24 and 27:44: God grants Paul all
who sail with him, and *so it came about that all were brought safely to land*. Preach the
providence and the man in the middle of it; leave the tackle alone.

## 33. Acts 28:17–31 — Rome, Isaiah 6, and the unhindered word ⭐

**Position.** Q1: everything since 19:21. Q2: this passage exists here as the book's last
because Luke must do three things and no more: let Paul state his case to Israel one final
time, let Scripture account for the division that results, and leave the mission open. He
does all three in fifteen verses and then stops.

**Structure.** The first meeting with the Roman Jewish leaders (17–22) — the day-long
exposition and the division (23–25a) — the Isaiah citation and the verdict (25b–28) —
**the two-verse ending** (30–31).

**Text-first findings.**

- **τὴν ἅλυσιν ταύτην περίκειμαι** (28:20) `[T]` — the fourth and last *ἅλυσις*, and the
  reason given is *ἕνεκεν τῆς ἐλπίδος τοῦ Ἰσραήλ*. See Headline Finding 3.
- **ἐλπὶς τοῦ Ἰσραήλ** (28:20) `[T]` — the last of the eight *ἐλπίς* occurrences and the
  fullest formulation of the defence.
- **αἵρεσις** (28:22) `[T]` — the Roman Jews' word, and the book's last use: *περὶ … τῆς
  αἱρέσεως ταύτης … πανταχοῦ ἀντιλέγεται*. The book ends with the movement still contested
  everywhere.
- **τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ θεοῦ** (28:28) `[T]` — Luke 2:30, Luke 3:6, Acts 28:28. See Headline
  Finding 4. *High confidence — verified across the SBLGNT.*
- **αὐτοὶ καὶ ἀκούσονται** (28:28) `[T]` — "**they** will listen" — set directly against the
  citation's *ἀκοῇ ἀκούσετε καὶ οὐ μὴ συνῆτε* three verses earlier. Luke ends the Isaiah
  quotation and then reverses its verb. *High confidence — the antithesis is in the text.*
- **28:31 has no growth verb** `[T]` — *κηρύσσων … καὶ διδάσκων … μετὰ πάσης παρρησίας
  ἀκωλύτως*. Two participles, a prepositional phrase, and an adverb. Whatever the last verse
  is, it is not a growth-notice. See Book-Overview Tensions.
- **παρρησία** (28:31) `[T]` — the fifth and last, answering 4:29's prayer.
- **ἀκωλύτως** (28:31) `[T]` — NT hapax; the last word of the book. See Headline Finding 2.
- **βασιλεία** (28:23, 28:31) `[T]` — the seventh and eighth, closing the inclusio with 1:3.
- **δύο ἔτη … ἐν ἰδίῳ μισθώματι** (28:30) `[T]` — a rented lodging, a soldier attached
  (28:16), and *ἀπεδέχετο πάντας* — he received **all** who came.

**Tool 11.** **Isaiah 6:9–10 LXX → 28:26–27** *(high confidence — direct quotation, named
prophet, with an explicit attribution to the Holy Spirit)*. *Source context:* the commission
in the year King Uzziah died — the throne-room vision, the coal from the altar, and then a
sending whose stated purpose is to harden, followed immediately by *how long, O Lord?* and
the answer *until cities lie waste* — and then, in the same breath, **the holy seed is its
stump** (6:13). The oracle of hardening ends with a surviving remnant. That is the context
Luke's citation carries. *Book usage:* the **fourth and last** Isaiah citation in Acts
(Isa 66 at 7:49; Isa 53 at 8:32; Isa 49 at 13:47; Isa 6 here). Luke has moved through
Isaiah's temple critique, the Servant's suffering, the Servant's commission to the nations,
and now the prophet's own commission to a people who will not hear. **The sequence is not
random and is the single most promising Move 2 thread in Acts.** *[I], moderate-to-high —
flagged for audit.* *OT-to-OT:* Isa 6:9–10 is the text Luke has already given to Jesus at
the hinge of the parables (Luke 8:10), so the citation closes a *two-volume* frame, not
just a book. **And the introduction is unusual:** Paul attributes it to *τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον
… πρὸς τοὺς πατέρας ὑμῶν* — the Spirit who spoke to *your* fathers. *What it adds:* the
division at Rome is not a failure of the mission but a scriptural expectation with a
scriptural remainder. Luke does not cite Isaiah 6 to close the door on Israel: 28:24 says
*some were persuaded*, Isaiah 6 itself ends with a stump, and the whole book has insisted
on *μυριάδες* of Jewish believers (21:20).

*Internal:* **answers §1:3 and §1:8** (the kingdom taught, the earth's end reached in the
person of a prisoner in the empire's capital), **§4:29** (boldness), **§8:36** (what
hinders?), **§2:39** (those far off), and **§19:21/23:11** (Rome). *High confidence — the
last pericope is the densest Move 4 site in the book.*

**Copycat.** Paul's two-year ministry from a rented house is **descriptive**, and its
*shape* — receiving all who came, teaching openly, under guard — is a **normative example**
of ministry under constraint. The Isaiah citation is **doctrinal**. The open ending is a
**literary decision**, not an application: Luke stops because the story has not.

**Pitfall.** Preaching 28:31 as a happy ending. The last chapter contains a hardening
oracle, a division among the Roman Jews, and a man under house arrest awaiting a trial Luke
never narrates. The word *ἀκωλύτως* is not "and everything went well"; it is "and nothing
could stop the message" — said of a prisoner. Preach the adverb, and preach who is wearing
the chain while it is true.

## Cross-Passage Convergent Findings

What only becomes visible when the pericopes are read as a sequence. **Every item here is
synthetic.** Those marked *verified* have had every constituent reference checked by lemma
against the SBLGNT index; the rest are capped at moderate confidence and are the first
targets for a claim audit.

**1. The Exodus 1:7 growth refrain — 6:7 · 7:17 · 12:24 · 19:20.** *Verified.* The pair
αὐξάνω + πληθύνω occurs in only four NT verses, three of them in Acts; Exod 1:7 LXX supplies
both verbs plus σφόδρα (echoed at 6:7) and κατίσχυον (echoed by ἴσχυεν at 19:20); Stephen
uses the pair of Israel in Egypt at 7:17. **What the sequence shows:** Luke's structural
seams say the church is Israel-in-Egypt — a people multiplying under a hostile power, on the
way out. The growth-notices are not progress reports; they are exodus markers.

**2. The κωλύω spine — 8:36 · 10:47 · 11:17 · 16:6 · 24:23 · 27:43 → 28:31.** *Verified.*
The question is raised by an excluded foreigner, escalated to "who was I to hinder *God*?",
answered in the negative at every frontier, permitted only to the Spirit (16:6), granted
twice by Romans on Paul's behalf (24:23; 27:43), and closed by a NT hapax. **What the
sequence shows:** the book is organised around a single question about obstruction, and its
answer is an adverb.

**3. The purity chain — 10:14/15/28 · 11:8/9 · 15:9 · 21:28.** *Verified.* κοινός, κοινόω,
ἀκάθαρτος, καθαρίζω and διακρίνω run from the sheet, through the retelling, into the
Council's decisive sentence (*τῇ πίστει καθαρίσας τὰς καρδίας αὐτῶν*), and out the other
side into the charge against Paul (*κεκοίνωκεν τὸν ἅγιον τόπον*). **What the sequence
shows:** Cornelius is not an episode but the first term of an argument that ends with Paul
arrested for the principle Peter was taught on a roof.

**4. Two chains — 12:6/7 · 21:33 · 28:20.** *Verified.* See Headline Finding 3.
**What the sequence shows:** Luke builds the Peter–Paul parallelism carefully (the same
congenitally lame man at 3:2 and 14:8, in identical words; the same Ps 16 argument at 2:25
and 13:35; the same two chains) *in order to break it at the end*. Peter walks free and
leaves the story; Paul stays chained and the story ends with him. The parallelism's purpose
is the divergence.

**5. The Servant Song sequence — Isa 53 at 8:32 · Isa 49:6 at 13:47 · Isa 42:6–7 behind
26:18.** *Moderate-to-high; the Isa 42 link is conceptual.* Luke uses the Servant Songs at
the conversion of an excluded foreigner, at the formal turn to the Gentiles, and at Paul's
final defence — and in each the Servant's role is transferred: to Jesus (8:35), to the
missionaries (13:47, *ἐντέταλται ἡμῖν*), to Paul (26:16–18). **A claim audit should test
whether a fourth Servant text stands behind 1:8.**

**6. The two Amos citations — 7:42–43 (Amos 5) and 15:16–18 (Amos 9), both LXX-dependent,
both at structural hinges.** *Moderate.* Stephen quotes the rejected tent from Amos 5;
James announces the rebuilt tent of David from Amos 9. σκηνή occurs three times in Acts and
these are two of them (with 7:44). **What the sequence shows:** the temple question opened
at the pivot is closed at the hinge, and both moves are made from the Greek Amos.

**7. Three "what must I do?" moments — 2:37 · 9:6 · 16:30.** *Verified.* Jewish crowd,
persecutor, Gentile jailer. Each is answered differently in wording and identically in
substance.

**8. Four Spirit-frontiers, four sequences — 2:1–4 · 8:14–17 · 10:44–48 · 19:1–7.**
*Verified by inspection.* Spirit then baptism; baptism then apostolic hands then Spirit;
Spirit then baptism; re-baptism then hands then Spirit. **What the sequence shows:** Luke
is marking boundaries being crossed — Jew, Samaritan, Gentile, and disciples of John — not
describing an order of salvation. Any doctrine built on one of the four has ignored the
other three. *This is the book's single most important Copycat finding.*

**9. The trial vocabulary and the four acquittals — 23:9 · 23:29 · 25:25 · 26:31–32.**
*Verified.* With ἀπολογέομαι/ἀπολογία seven times in chs. 22–26, and ἐλπίς five times in
the same span. **What the sequence shows:** Luke's last quarter is a legal brief, and it is
built on the same cumulative-acquittal technique as Luke 23.

**10. ἐκκλησία — 19 of the church, 3 of a pagan mob (19:32, 39, 40), 1 of Israel at Sinai
(7:38).** *Verified.* **What the sequence shows:** Luke knows the word is ordinary Greek for
an assembly, and he uses that knowledge twice for effect — once to make Israel in the
wilderness the church, and once to set a confused, self-interested civic assembly beside
the church at the very moment the word is prevailing (19:20).

**11. ἀπόστολος disappears after 16:4.** *Verified — 28 occurrences, from 1:2 to 16:4, and
none in the last thirteen chapters.* **What the sequence shows:** the book called "The Acts
of the Apostles" stops using the word halfway through. The overview says the title is a
later heading; the distribution is the evidence.

**12. The Peter half ends with a king struck by an angel; the Paul half ends with a
prisoner in a rented room.** *Moderate — a structural observation, not a verbal chain.*
12:23 and 28:30–31 are the two closing tableaux of the book's halves, and Luke's judgement
of power and his judgement of weakness are set opposite each other.

## Book-Overview Tensions

Surfaced, not resolved. The overview is an imported document and these are the points a
Finalise pass should work through first.

**1. The "six summary/growth statements" need restating.** The overview says the seams are
6:7; 9:31; 12:24; 16:5; 19:20; 28:31, "typically 'the word of God increased/spread and
multiplied'", and that they "make 'the word' (not the apostles) the growing subject". The
Greek gives a more interesting and less uniform picture `[T], verified`:

| Ref | Verbs | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| 6:7 | ηὔξανεν + ἐπληθύνετο (+ σφόδρα) | **the word of God** *and* the number of disciples — two subjects |
| 9:31 | οἰκοδομουμένη + ἐπληθύνετο | **the church** |
| 12:24 | ηὔξανεν + ἐπληθύνετο | **the word of God** |
| 16:5 | ἐστερεοῦντο + ἐπερίσσευον | **the churches** |
| 19:20 | ηὔξανεν + ἴσχυεν | **the word of the Lord** |
| 28:31 | — none — | — |

So: the word is the growing subject at three of the six; the church at two; and 28:31 has
no growth verb at all. **And the overview's list omits 7:17**, which carries both Exodus
verbs and is the member that identifies the refrain's source. Recommended revision: name
three *word*-seams (6:7, 12:24, 19:20) sharing an Exodus 1:7 vocabulary, two *church*-seams
(9:31, 16:5) with different verbs, and 28:31 as a *closing tableau* rather than a seam.

**2. The pericope map does not cover the book.** §10 lists **31** units, not 27, and it
skips **17:1–15** (Thessalonica and Berea) and **20:1–16** (Troas and Eutychus). Both have
been added in this sweep. 17:1–15 matters more than it looks: without it, the Areopagus
reads as Paul's method rather than his exception.

**3. The 1:8 ← Isaiah 49:6 echo should be upgraded from "high/moderate" to high.** Luke
supplies the citation himself at 13:47 with the identical phrase *ἕως ἐσχάτου τῆς γῆς* and
an explicit *ἐντέταλται ἡμῖν ὁ κύριος* `[T], verified`. The overview's own §6 already lists
Isa 49:6 → 13:47; it simply has not joined the two entries.

**4. Four intertextual threads are missing from §6.** (a) **Exodus 1:7** behind the growth
refrain. (b) **Isaiah 40:5 / Luke 2:30** behind *τὸ σωτήριον* at 28:28. (c) **Joshua 7**
behind Ananias and Sapphira (νοσφίζομαι + ἀπό, two LXX occurrences). (d) **Isaiah 42:6–7**
behind the commission of 26:18, completing a three-Servant-Song sequence.

**5. Two verbal links the overview's structural notes should carry.** *τόπος* as the
Leitwort of Stephen's defence (6:13, 6:14 → 7:7, 7:33, 7:49, and back at 21:28); and
*χειροποίητος* at 7:48 and 17:24 — Paul says to Athens what Stephen was killed for saying to
Jerusalem.

**6. The pulpit-text note is wrong twice over.** The overview declares NIV 2011
(Anglicised). The preacher's own church uses the **ESV (Anglicised)** — already corrected in
the existing solo run on 18:23–19:20 `[S: prior report]` — and where he preaches elsewhere
it may be NIV84, not the 2011 revision. Separately, a sweep or an overview needs **no**
pulpit text at all. Recommend deleting the declaration from the overview and replacing it
with a note that the pulpit text is declared per engagement.

**7. The Peter–Paul parallelism should be sharpened, not just affirmed.** The overview rates
it moderate-to-high and lists the parallels. The text supports it more strongly than that
at three verifiable points (3:2 ‖ 14:8 verbatim; Ps 16 at 2:25 ‖ 13:35 in the same role;
ἁλύσεσι δυσί at 12:6 ‖ 21:33) — **and its interpretive point is the place it stops** (12:7
against 28:20). A parallelism that breaks deliberately is a stronger claim than one that
merely holds.

**8. The Pao monograph needs a claim audit, not a citation.** The overview's §4 quotes Pao's
three-stage Isaianic programme behind 1:8 at length. This sweep did not open Pao and reached
an **Exodus 1** substrate for the seams independently. The two are not in contradiction —
Isaiah's new exodus is itself built on the first — but the relation between them is exactly
the sort of thing a monograph-sourced overview should not assert without testing. The
existing solo run has already qualified Pao's stronger sub-claim (an OT quotation at every
turning point) and this sweep confirms the qualification: **the three word-seams carry no
citation formula at all.**

## Preaching Pitfalls (book level)

Passage-level pitfalls are given at each pericope. These four operate across the whole book.

### Pitfall: the template fallacy, in its specific Acts forms

- **What it looks like:** tongues at conversion from ch. 2; a two-stage Spirit from ch. 8 or
  ch. 19; community of goods from chs. 2 and 4; casting lots from ch. 1; apostolic
  sign-gifts from ch. 19; "tarrying" from ch. 1.
- **Why it is wrong:** each rests on a single episode, and in each case Acts itself supplies
  a differing parallel — the four Spirit-frontiers have four sequences; the lot never recurs;
  5:4 states the property was the owner's; 19:11 calls the miracles *extraordinary*.
- **The corrective:** run Tool 13 before Tool 16 on every Acts passage, and ask what Luke
  says *elsewhere* about the same thing before generalising from one place.

### Pitfall: making the apostles the heroes

- **What it looks like:** "be bold like Peter", "be a missionary like Paul", "have the faith
  of Stephen".
- **Why it is wrong:** the book's own subject is *ὁ λόγος* and the risen Lord who acts
  (1:1). The apostles refuse the role twice explicitly (3:12; 14:14–15), and Luke reports
  their quarrels (15:39), their fear (9:26), and a prayer meeting that did not believe its
  own answer (12:15).
- **The corrective:** ask of every Acts passage *who is the acting subject of the main
  verbs?* In the great majority the answer is God, the Lord, the Spirit, or the word.

### Pitfall: triumphalism

- **What it looks like:** Acts as a record of unbroken advance; prison doors always opening.
- **Why it is wrong:** James dies in 12:2 without explanation; the prison opens for Peter
  and not for Paul; the book ends with a hardening oracle, a divided synagogue and a man in
  chains.
- **The corrective:** preach 12:2 whenever preaching 12:3–19; preach the chain of 28:20
  whenever preaching the adverb of 28:31.

### Pitfall: supersessionism, and its opposite

- **What it looks like:** on one side, "the gospel left Israel at 28:28"; on the other, a
  reading that flattens the real division Luke narrates.
- **Why it is wrong:** the turn to the Gentiles happens three times (13:46; 18:6; 28:28) and
  is each time local — Paul is back in a synagogue in the next city. 21:20 reports
  *μυριάδες* of law-zealous Jewish believers. Isaiah 6, the closing citation, ends in its own
  context with a surviving stump (6:13), and 28:24 says *some were persuaded*.
- **The corrective:** hold the tension the Council held. Luke neither writes Israel off nor
  pretends the division is not real.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **The Exodus 1:7 patterning — history of interpretation unchecked.** Six of the seven
   Hays criteria are met strongly; *history of interpretation* has not been tested. **Ask
   Logos** whether Bock, Peterson, Keener or Pao note αὐξάνω/πληθύνω at 6:7, 12:24 and 19:20
   as an Exodus 1:7 echo. This is the sweep's most load-bearing new claim and it should not
   enter the overview until checked.
2. **κωλύω → ἀκωλύτως — same question.** The distribution is certain; whether commentators
   read it as a designed inclusio is not. Ask Logos.
3. **Acts 20:28** — *τοῦ θεοῦ* / *τοῦ κυρίου*, and *τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου* as "his own
   blood" or "the blood of his Own". Two independent questions in one clause. **Check the
   NA28 apparatus and Metzger's *Textual Commentary*.**
4. **Acts 11:20** — *Ἑλληνιστάς* (printed by NA28 and SBLGNT) against *Ἕλληνας* (which the
   context appears to require). Check the apparatus before preaching the verse.
5. **Acts 12:24** — NA28 and SBLGNT read *τοῦ θεοῦ*; NASB95 renders "the word of the Lord".
   Minor, but note it if quoting the study text and making anything of the divine title.
6. **ἀπόστολος disappearing after 16:4** — design, or a feature of Luke's sources? The
   distribution is certain; the explanation is not, and the answer bears on how much weight
   the "the title is later" observation can carry.
7. **Stephen's *standing* Son of Man (7:56)** against the *seated* Lord of Ps 110:1 at 2:34.
   Genuinely contested (rising to welcome? to witness? to judge?). Do not settle it in a
   sweep.
8. **The "we" passages** (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16) — recorded as data. The
   inferences commonly drawn from them are `[I]` at best and were not pursued.
9. **Acts 21:4 against 20:22–23** — the Spirit's information and the disciples' inference.
   Named, not resolved.
10. **Acts 27:35** — whether the breaking of bread is eucharistic. The vocabulary is
    Luke 22:19's; the setting is a pagan ship. Contested; flagged.
11. **The Amos-pair claim** (Amos 5 at the pivot, Amos 9 at the hinge, both LXX) is the
    sweep's most attractive untested pattern. It clears the ≥2 threshold for a two-member
    pattern but not the ≥3 macro gate. **Do not enter it in the overview without an audit.**
12. **The ESV export in `_texts/logos-exports/` has no chapter markers** — only a leading
    `Ac 1:1` and then bare verse numbers — so it cannot be parsed by reference without
    inferring chapter breaks from verse resets. Worth re-exporting with chapter headings, as
    the NASB95 export has.
