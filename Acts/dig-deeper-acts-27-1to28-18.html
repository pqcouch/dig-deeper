Primary texts: NA28 text and apparatus (Logos export), read first; SBLGNT as the observation layer for every count, chain and ordered-phrase search; Swete’s Greek Old Testament for the Old Testament contacts tested. Study text: NASB95 Pulpit text: ESV (Anglicised), declared for this engagement. The export compared is the US-spelling ESV, so the comparison is of wording, not spelling. Scope note: The extent asked for runs through the whole voyage and arrival (27:1–28:16, which the overview treats as one ⭐ unit) and then stops two verses into Paul’s first address to the Roman Jewish leaders. That address is a single sentence running to 28:20. I worked the extent as asked. Three consequences follow. (a) 28:17–18 are read as the opening of a speech, not as a complete unit. Their forward plants are listed in Open Questions for the 28:17–31 run. (b) The overview’s seam at 28:16/17 is crossed. The text supports that seam: the first person plural stops after εἰσήλθομεν (28:16), and 28:17 opens with a new time-marker and a new audience. The crossing is therefore a choice of extent, not a finding against the map. (c) Nothing is claimed about the closing of Acts beyond what 28:17–18 themselves set up. The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book level. The overview’s statement is that Luke writes to give a mixed church certainty about what Jesus “began to do and teach” and continues to do through his witnesses, under a purpose no opposition can obstruct [S: overview]. I hold it in view and rest nothing on it.

Passage level. The passage is the long middle of a journey that God has said must happen (23:11) and the court has ordered (25:12) [T]. Every force that might stop the journey acts in it and none succeeds: professional judgement (27:11), weather (27:14–20), the sailors’ flight (27:30), a military plan to kill the prisoners (27:42), a viper (28:3). [T] on the sequence; [I], high, on the purpose. Luke tells it at length and in the first person, so the reader is given a record to trust, not a summary to accept.

The passage also carries a defence. By the time Paul speaks in Rome (28:17–18), Luke has let him describe his own case in the terms of Jesus’ case (Headline 1). The voyage arrives at a Jesus-shaped verdict. [I], moderate-to-high.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement. 23:11–26:32 has set up three things that this passage spends. (a) A destination fixed by the Lord: οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην μαρτυρῆσαι (“so you must testify also in Rome”, 23:11). (b) A legal route: Καίσαρα ἐπικέκλησαι, ἐπὶ Καίσαρα πορεύσῃ (“you have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go”, 25:12). (c) A run of findings that nothing in Paul deserves death or chains, ending in ἀπολελύσθαι ἐδύνατο ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὗτος εἰ μὴ ἐπεκέκλητο Καίσαρα (“this man could have been released if he had not appealed to Caesar”, 26:32) [T]. The passage opens on the execution of (b): Ὡς δὲ ἐκρίθη τοῦ ἀποπλεῖν ἡμᾶς (“when it was decided that we should sail”, 27:1). The first person plural returns at that point [T].

Necessity answer. Once the courts have failed to hold Paul, the remaining obstacles are not arguments but sea, soldiers and snake. They can be answered only by a word God has spoken. That is why the angel’s word is placed in the middle of the storm and not at departure, and why it restates the destination in the court’s own terms: Καίσαρι (27:24), the man to whom he appealed (25:11–12, 21). [I], moderate-to-high.

There is also a backward reason. 26:32 leaves the reader asking whether the appeal was a mistake. Luke’s answer is to have God name Caesar after the appeal (27:24), as he had named Rome before it (23:11). [T] on the order; [I] on the purpose. The passage then lets Paul close the account (28:17–18) by describing the Roman hearings as Jesus’ hearing was described (Headline 1). The voyage is therefore both the outworking of 23:11 and the bridge to a verdict.

Implication. Preach 27–28 as an adventure and it is a good story. Preach it after 23:11, 25:12 and 26:32 and it becomes the demonstration that the Lord’s “must” and Caesar’s court run on the same rails, and that neither the court’s failure to release nor the sea can stop the witness.

Revisit after Tool 11. Move 4 confirmed and sharpened this. The night-word at 27:23–24 is built on 18:9–10 as well as 23:11, so the voyage answers the Corinth promise too (no one will harm you, 18:10 → 28:5). The positional answer stands and gains a second anchor.

Immediately before (26:30–32). The private verdict of Festus, Agrippa and Bernice; Agrippa’s “could have been released”.

Immediately after (28:19–31, outside the extent). The rest of the sentence (the appeal explained, the chain, “the hope of Israel”), the day-long exposition, Isaiah 6, and ἀκωλύτως (“unhindered”).

The passage’s own boundary. The first person plural runs from 27:1 to 28:16. εἰσήλθομεν (28:16) is its last verb, and 28:17 begins Ἐγένετο δὲ μετὰ ἡμέρας τρεῖς with Paul as subject [T]. What the “we” is — eyewitness report, convention or both — is not stated by the text; every explanation of it is [I] at best.

Structural devices.

  • The centre is a word, not an event. The voyage’s turn is 27:21–26, a speech placed between the loss of hope (27:20) and the fourteenth night. The two “fearing” clauses (φοβούμενοι, 27:17 and 27:29) bracket the angel’s μὴ φοβοῦ (27:24) [T]. Before the speech the crew act alone and lose; after it they act on Paul’s word and live. [I], high.
  • Paired warnings, opposite responses. 27:10 and 27:31 are both Paul’s advice to those in command. The first is set aside by ἐπείθετο (27:11); the second is acted on with τότε (“then”, 27:32) [T].
  • An inclusio on permission. ἐπιτρέπω (“to permit”) stands at 27:3 (Julius ἐπέτρεψεν Paul to go to his friends) and at 28:16 (ἐπετράπη τῷ Παύλῳ μένειν καθ᾽ ἑαυτόν, “Paul was permitted to stay by himself”). These are its only two uses in the voyage and among five in Acts (21:39, 40; 26:1; 27:3; 28:16, SBLGNT) [T]. The voyage opens and closes with a prisoner given latitude.
  • Two hospitalities. φιλανθρώπως (27:3) and φιλανθρωπία (28:2) each stand once in Acts, at the two edges of the sea (SBLGNT) [T]. The first is shown by a Roman, the second by “barbarians”.
  • The count in the middle. The head count (αἱ πᾶσαι ψυχαί, 27:37) falls between the meal and the wreck, so the people are numbered before they are brought through [T].
  • The arrival told twice. καὶ οὕτως εἰς τὴν Ῥώμην ἤλθαμεν (28:14) states the arrival; 28:15–16 then narrate the approach and εἰσήλθομεν [T].

4. Linking Words

The speech (27:21–26) runs on γάρ … γάρ … διό … γάρ … δέ (laid out above). Two points. Paul gives the conclusion (take courage) before the ground (an angel stood by me). And the δέ of 27:26 does not soften the promise. It adds the form of the rescue: a wreck on an island. [T]

27:31 — the only conditional in the passage. ἐὰν μή … οὐ δύνασθε. It follows the unconditional promise without comment [T]. Luke sees no contradiction in a promise fixed in its outcome and carried out by obedience in its means.

27:34 — two γάρ clauses in sequence. τοῦτο γὰρ πρὸς τῆς ὑμετέρας σωτηρίας ὑπάρχει, οὐδενὸς γὰρ ὑμῶν θρὶξ ἀπὸ τῆς κεφαλῆς ἀπολεῖται. Eat, for this serves your preservation, for not a hair will perish. The second grounds the first: eating makes sense because the promise holds. The ESV keeps both “for”s but changes what the first asserts (Tool 8). [T]

28:4–6 — two confident inferences. πάντως (“undoubtedly”, 28:4) introduces the first verdict; μεταβαλόμενοι (“changing their minds”, 28:6) the second. Both are reported by the narrator and neither is corrected [T].

28:17–18. One sentence: οὐδὲν ἐναντίον ποιήσας … δέσμιος … παρεδόθην εἰς τὰς χεῖρας τῶν Ῥωμαίων, οἵτινες ἀνακρίναντές με ἐβούλοντο ἀπολῦσαι διὰ τὸ … The relative οἵτινες makes the Romans the subject of the examining and of the wish to release. διὰ τό gives the wish its reason, which is the Luke 23:22 formula. [T]

6. Narrator’s Comment

Five intrusions and two silences.

  • 27:9 — objective danger. ὄντος ἤδη ἐπισφαλοῦς τοῦ πλοὸς διὰ τὸ καὶ τὴν νηστείαν ἤδη παρεληλυθέναι. The narrator states the risk before Paul speaks, so the warning is not a private hunch. [T]
  • 27:13 — irony. δόξαντες τῆς προθέσεως κεκρατηκέναι (“supposing they had gained their purpose”). Their confidence is marked as supposition [T]. πρόθεσις is a purpose word; in 11:23 it is τῇ προθέσει τῆς καρδίας, the believers’ resolve (SBLGNT). The crew have a purpose; the passage is about another one. [I], low-to-moderate.
  • 27:43 — motive. βουλόμενος διασῶσαι τὸν Παῦλον. Luke tells us why the prisoners lived, and the reason is Paul [T].
  • 28:2 — evaluation. οὐ τὴν τυχοῦσαν φιλανθρωπίαν (“no ordinary kindness”) [T].
  • 28:15 — Paul’s inner state. εὐχαριστήσας τῷ θεῷ ἔλαβεν θάρσος. θάρσος (“courage”) is a New Testament hapax, and its verb is the Lord’s Θάρσει at 23:11, the only form of that verb in Acts (SBLGNT) [T]. The courage the Lord commanded in Jerusalem Paul “takes” at the sight of the Roman believers. [I], moderate.

Silences.

  • No correction at Malta. When the Maltese say “he is a god” (28:6), the narrator does not correct it. Elsewhere Luke records an immediate disclaimer (10:26; 14:14–15) or an immediate judgement (12:22–23). That is an established pattern, so the absence is noticeable rather than guessed [T] on the pattern. Whether the silence is meant is [I], moderate at most.
  • No speech on Malta. At Jerusalem (3:1–26) and at Lystra (14:8–18) a healing leads to a speech; at Lydda it leads to many turning to the Lord (9:35). On Malta healings lead to honours and provisions (28:10) and nothing is said of preaching or of faith [T] on the data. Absence argument, capped at moderate: Luke may simply be compressing.

7. Vocabulary

Five words that bear on the passage’s meaning. Other recurrences are under Tool 10.

σῴζω / διασῴζω / σωτηρία (“to save”, “to bring safely through”, “salvation, preservation”). Distributions in Headline 4. In Acts σῴζω is the gospel verb: “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved” (2:21), “no other name … by which we must be saved” (4:12), “what must I do to be saved?” (16:30). In 14:9 it is used of a lame man’s healing. Its last two Acts uses are the storm’s (27:20, 31). Luke does not mark a change of sense; the same word continues. High on the data; moderate that Luke means the reader to hear both registers at once.

βουλή (“plan, counsel”). Seven Acts verses (SBLGNT). Five are about God’s plan or tested against it: τῇ ὡρισμένῃ βουλῇ … τοῦ θεοῦ (2:23), ἡ βουλή σου προώρισεν (4:28), Gamaliel’s ἐὰν ᾖ ἐξ ἀνθρώπων ἡ βουλὴ αὕτη … καταλυθήσεται (“if this plan is of men, it will fail”, 5:38), David τῇ τοῦ θεοῦ βουλῇ (13:36), πᾶσαν τὴν βουλὴν τοῦ θεοῦ (20:27). The last two are the voyage’s two human plans, and both fail: the majority’s plan to sail (27:12) and the soldiers’ plan to kill the prisoners (27:42, with τοῦ βουλήματος at 27:43). [T] on the distribution. That Luke sets human βουλαί against the divine one is [I], moderate-to-high: the word is chosen where he could have used another, and Gamaliel has already stated the principle. A Vocabulary find that no English version can carry.

χαρίζομαι (“to grant as a favour”). Four Acts verses: 3:14 (ἄνδρα φονέα χαρισθῆναι ὑμῖν, the crowd asked for a murderer to be “granted” them), 25:11 and 25:16 (no one can “grant” Paul to his accusers), 27:24 (κεχάρισταί σοι ὁ θεὸς πάντας) (SBLGNT). παρίστημι (“to stand by”, “to stand before”). παρέστη μοι (27:23, the angel stood by Paul) and Καίσαρί σε δεῖ παραστῆναι (27:24, Paul must stand before Caesar). Thirteen Acts verses (SBLGNT). The same verb names the angel’s coming and Paul’s appointment; no English version keeps it. [T] on the data; moderate that the play is intended.

προσλαμβάνομαι (“to take, to take to oneself”). 9. Tone and Feel

  • 27:1–12 is a log: ports, winds, days, two uses of μόλις (“with difficulty”). The only human friction is 27:10–11.
  • 27:13–20 passes agency from people to weather. ἐφερόμεθα (“we were being carried”, 27:15), ἐφέροντο (27:17), διαφερομένων (27:27). The crew act only by losing things, until “all hope” is being taken away. Sun and stars unseen for many days is the darkness of the sea and of the mind.
  • 27:21–26 is one standing voice, steady, with the same command twice.
  • 27:27–32 is night-time suspense in small particulars: soundings at twenty and fifteen fathoms, four anchors, “prayed for day” (ηὔχοντο ἡμέραν γενέσθαι).
  • 27:33–38 slows to the pace of a meal: urge, take, give thanks, break, begin to eat, all eat. The count follows, then the wheat goes over the side.
  • 27:39–44 is abrupt and violent, then flat: “and so it happened that all were brought safely to land”.
  • 28:1–6 is warm (fire, rain, cold) and gently comic (a confident verdict, a long wait, a reversed verdict).
  • 28:7–10 is matter-of-fact about healings.
  • 28:11–16 quickens to an itinerary, and then one clause of relief (28:15).
  • 28:17–18 has the formal register of a defence.

The soundtrack runs from ship’s log, to dread, to one quiet voice, to a meal at dawn, to firelight and laughter, to a road to Rome. For the preacher: the dread of 27:20 should be allowed its weight before 27:21 is heard. The promise lands because hope had gone.

11. Quotation/Allusion

No direct Old Testament quotation stands in 27:1–28:18. The neighbouring quotations (Exod 22:28 at 23:5; Isa 6:9–10 at 28:26–27) lie outside it. Candidates were derived first from the passage’s own vocabulary. The overview maps none in this extent.

Live sources. In Acts the confirmed live sources are the Psalms, Isaiah, the Torah’s patriarchal and Mosaic narratives, Joel and Amos [S: overview]. None is verbally present in the voyage. Any Old Testament candidate is therefore a cold allusion and needs distinctive wording to carry weight.

Systematic sweep. Swete was scanned against the storm and wreck (27:13–44) in six runs with different window sizes. The scan was run earlier in this session and not repeated for this rerun, because it is mechanical. Only one partner ranked high across the runs: Jonah 1, with a median rank of 3; it ranked first to third in four runs and lower in the two whole-chapter runs. No other partner was stable. Jonah 1:4–6, 1:16 → 27:17–19, 27:29, 27:38, 27:43–44 (moderate-to-low)

Source context. A prophet flees west by ship from his commission. The Lord ἐξήγειρεν πνεῦμα (“raised a wind”) and the ship ἐκινδύνευεν συντριβῆναι (“was in danger of breaking up”, 1:4). The sailors ἐφοβήθησαν (“were afraid”), cry each to his god, and ἐκβολὴν ἐποιήσαντο τῶν σκευῶν … εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν, τοῦ κουφισθῆναι (“made a casting-out of the gear into the sea, to lighten it”, 1:5). The captain pleads ὅπως διασώσῃ ὁ θεὸς ἡμᾶς καὶ μὴ ἀπολώμεθα (“that God may bring us safely through and we not perish”, 1:6). The prophet is thrown overboard and the sea is still. The sailors fear the Lord καὶ εὔξαντο εὐχάς (“and made vows”, 1:16).

The data.

  • ἐκβολὴν ποιέω stands once in Swete (Jonah 1:5) and once in the New Testament (Acts 27:18, ἐκβολὴν ἐποιοῦντο).
  • κουφίζω stands in ten Swete verses, one of them Jonah 1:5, and once in the New Testament (Acts 27:38).
  • The σκευ- root (Jonah σκευῶν; Acts τὸ σκεῦος 27:17, τὴν σκευήν 27:19), εἰς τὴν θάλασσαν (Jonah 1:5; Acts 27:38), διασῴζω (1:6; 27:43–44), ἀπόλλυμι (1:6; 27:34), φοβέομαι (1:5, 10, 16; 27:17, 29) and εὔχομαι (1:16; 27:29) are all shared.

[T] (Swete, SBLGNT). This is a larger contact than single-word coincidence, but most of the shared words belong to any storm at sea.

The rival. As far as I know, ἐκβολή is the ordinary Greek word for jettisoning in a storm; Aristotle uses it of exactly this in his Ethics [S]. So the rarest shared phrase may reflect the activity, not a text. Uncertain — consider verifying in a lexicon with citations.

Hays criteria.

  • Availability: high. Luke knows Jonah: Luke 11:29–32, Jesus’ “sign of Jonah”, preached to Gentiles.
  • Volume: modest. One rare phrase and several common words, spread over twenty-seven verses.
  • Recurrence: nil. Jonah is never cited in Acts.
  • Thematic coherence: real but inverted. In Jonah a disobedient prophet endangers the ship and the sailors are saved by throwing him out. In Acts an obedient prophet’s presence saves the ship’s company, and the plan to kill the prisoners is stopped. The route matches as well: both voyages run west from the land of Israel.

Verdict: moderate-to-low. A preacher may mention the contrast as a reader’s association, but should not present it as Luke’s allusion. Move 1 only, as the rule requires for an allusion at this level. Book usage: no earlier use in Acts. OT-to-OT: no pairing in this passage.

1 Samuel 14:45 → 27:34 (low-to-moderate)

Source context. Jonathan has unknowingly broken Saul’s oath and is to die. The people refuse: Εἰ σήμερον θανατωθήσεται ὁ ποιήσας τὴν σωτηρίαν τὴν μεγάλην ταύτην … εἰ πεσεῖται τριχὸς τῆς κεφαλῆς αὐτοῦ ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν (“shall he die who has wrought this great salvation? … not a hair of his head shall fall to the ground”). The data. In Swete the hair-of-the-head-falling idiom stands with κεφαλή only in 1 Sam 14:45, and that verse also has σωτηρία. Acts 27:34 joins σωτηρία to θρὶξ ἀπὸ τῆς κεφαλῆς. Rival. The immediate source of 27:34 is Jesus’ saying at Luke 21:18 (θρὶξ … οὐ μὴ ἀπόληται). Luke 21:18 and Acts 27:34 are the only two New Testament verses with hair + head + ἀπόλλυμι (SBLGNT, ordered phrase). The σωτηρία could be coincidence.

12. Genre

Narrative in the first person plural, in the shape of a sea voyage, ending in the opening of a speech of defence. The observable features are a log-like itinerary, a speech-and-crisis sequence, a miracle report and a formal address. Whether Luke uses a Greco-Roman sea-voyage convention, and what that implies about invention, is debated [S]. The text’s own claim is to be the record of one of “us”. Reading rule: Luke narrates what happened under God’s purpose. He does not issue commands through the narrative, and he leaves a good deal uninterpreted. Moderate confidence.

Manner of the healing. Paul prays first, then lays on hands (28:8). The healing is not presented as Paul’s power. The others are simply cured. The congregation’s posture is that of the Maltese: receiving, not achieving. [I], moderate.

Commonly misread as prescriptive. The viper (28:3–6) as a promise of immunity, and the four anchors and the boat as an allegory of the Christian life. See Pitfalls.

14. Bible Timeline

New Testament, this side of the cross and resurrection, in the age of the Spirit’s witness. The Acts narrative here is commonly dated to the late 50s or early 60s [S]. The passage is the arrival leg of the book’s geographical programme (1:8; 19:21; 23:11). It is also the first leg in which the travellers are met at their destination by believers already there (28:14–15). The church has reached Rome before the apostle has. No timeline-sensitive promise (land, prosperity) is being misapplied here; the main danger is reading the unique promise of 27:24 as a general one.

15. Who Am I?

  • Paul: recipient of a unique revelation and the one for whose sake the others are spared. The reader is not Paul.
  • The ship’s company: recipients. They hear a promise they did not earn (κεχάρισται, granted), and they still have to stay in the ship, eat and swim. A reader can recognise themselves here.
  • The Maltese: foils, and a representative of a common instinct, which is to read misfortune as proof of hidden guilt and escape as proof of divinity.
  • The brothers from Rome: representatives of a church that goes out to meet a prisoner.
  • Christ: not named. His words shape the passage (Luke 21:18; 22:19; 23:20–22). See Christological Reading.

16. So What?

Author’s intended response. That readers trust God to carry his announced purpose through everything that opposes it — weather, bad judgement, violent plans, misreadings, chains. That they hear this trust as joined to ordinary obedience, not as a substitute for it. And that they recognise in a chained witness, whose case is told in the words of Jesus’ case, the way the word reaches Rome.

Worldview. History runs on God’s βουλή, not on the majority’s (27:12) or the soldiers’ (27:42). He fixes ends, and he works through persons: one prisoner’s presence keeps 275 others.

Behaviour.

  • Stop: treating a promise of God as a reason to do nothing (27:31); reading God’s verdict off events (28:4–6); despising those outside the church, who in this passage show “no ordinary kindness” (28:2).
  • Start: giving thanks to God in front of everyone (27:35); speaking courage on the ground of what God has said; going out to meet believers in chains (28:15).

Motivation. The διό of 27:25: take courage, because God has spoken and he will do as he said. The γάρ of 27:34: eat, because not a hair will perish.

Four audiences.

  • First hearers: believers asking why Paul reached Rome in chains and by wreck.
  • Today’s believers: people in storms who need 27:24 and 27:31 held together.
  • Unbelievers: the sailors and the Maltese, who receive kindness, food, rescue and healing through a believer’s presence.
  • Leaders: a prisoner who warns, comforts, feeds and prays, with no position on board.

Prayer. A prayer suited to the passage thanks God for a word that holds when sun and stars are hidden. It asks for grace to stay in the ship, to eat when there is bread, to look after those beside us, and to give thanks before everyone.

Original Language Observations

  • ἐπισφαλής (“unsafe”, 27:9). New Testament hapax (SBLGNT). The narrator’s word, not Paul’s. Moderate on the nautical nuance; lexicon check flagged.
  • λοιπὸν περιῃρεῖτο (27:20). λοιπόν (“from then on”) with an imperfect passive: hope was being taken away, a process. High.
  • ἔδει μέν … πειθαρχήσαντάς μοι (27:21). μέν without a matching δέ. The reproach is conceded and Paul moves on (καὶ τὰ νῦν). πειθαρχέω is the verb of 5:29 and 5:32 (obeying God rather than men); only these three uses in Acts (SBLGNT). [T]. Paul claims a word to be obeyed. Low-to-moderate on a link to 5:29.
  • κερδῆσαί τε τὴν ὕβριν ταύτην καὶ τὴν ζημίαν (27:21). “To gain” damage means to be spared it (avoiding a loss counts as a gain). Elsewhere in the New Testament ζημία and κερδαίνω stand together only in Phil 3:7–8, in a different sense. I treat 27:21 as idiom. [S]; uncertain, flagged.
  • τοῦ θεοῦ, οὗ εἰμι [ἐγὼ] ᾧ καὶ λατρεύω (27:23). Two relative clauses: possession (“whose I am”) and service (“whom I serve”). NA28 brackets ἐγώ; omitted by B, C, L, Ψ and the Byzantine text. λατρεύω* echoes 24:14. Before pagans, Paul names his God by the claim that God has on him. [T]
  • κεχάρισταί (27:24). Perfect: the grant is made and stands. High on the form.
  • πιστεύω … τῷ θεῷ ὅτι (27:25). Dative of the one trusted, plus the content of the trust. The same construction as Luke 1:45. [T]
  • μηθὲν προσλαβόμενοι (27:33). “Taking nothing [in addition]”. Not necessarily total abstinence for fourteen days, but no proper meal. [I], moderate.
  • λαβὼν ἄρτον εὐχαρίστησεν … καὶ κλάσας ἤρξατο ἐσθίειν (27:35). Participle, finite verb, participle, finite verb. Luke 22:19 has λαβὼν ἄρτον εὐχαριστήσας ἔκλασεν καὶ ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς. Here the giving-thanks is the finite verb (it is the main action), and the end-point is Paul’s eating, not distribution. [T], high. This bears directly on the sacramental question.
  • ἡ δίκη (28:4). “Justice” or “penalty”; with the article, plausibly the personified Dike of Greek religion [S]. Moderate. New Testament elsewhere: 2 Thess 1:9; Jude 7 (SBLGNT).
  • πίμπρασθαι (28:6). “To swell up”, or “to burn with fever”. New Testament hapax. Moderate on the sense.
  • πυρετοῖς καὶ δυσεντερίῳ (28:8). A plural of fever (“bouts of fever”) and a New Testament hapax for dysentery. The physician question is [S] and not pursued.

Textual Variants

NA28 text and apparatus are the citation of record, and witnesses are named as the apparatus prints them. Codex Bezae is not extant for these chapters, as far as I know [S]. The “Western” readings here are carried by minuscules 614 and 2147, the Harklean Syriac’s marked readings and some Old Latin.

Where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied. Six places repay the question.

  1. 27:14 — εὐρακύλων / εὐροκλύδων. NA28 prints εὐρακύλων with 𝔓⁷⁴, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus, the first hand of Vaticanus, 33 (apparently), the Latin and (partly) the Coptic. εὐροκλύδων has the second corrector of Vaticanus, L, Ψ, the Byzantine majority and the Syriac. εὐρακύλων looks like a Greek–Latin hybrid (Euros with Aquilo) [S]. εὐροκλύδων is a regular Greek formation (“wave-stirring east wind”). The tidying turned a sailors’ name into a Greek word. High confidence for NA28; no preaching impact beyond the name.
  2. 27:34 — ἀπολεῖται / πεσεῖται. NA28’s ἀπολεῖται has 𝔓⁷⁴, א, A, B, C, 33, 81 and others, the Vulgate, the Peshitta and the Bohairic. πεσεῖται has L, Ψ, 323, 614, 945, 1241, 1505, 1739, the Byzantine majority, Gigas, the Harklean and the Sahidic. The tidying moved the verb to the Old Testament idiom (1 Sam 14:45) and away from Jesus’ saying (Luke 21:18). High for NA28. Preaching impact: the promise is Jesus’ own wording, not a proverb.
  3. 27:34 — ὑμετέρας / ἡμετέρας. A, L, 326, 614, many others, w and the Harklean read ἡμετέρας (“our” preservation), putting Paul among the endangered. Moderate for NA28’s ὑμετέρας on attestation. The pronoun swap is easy in Greek pronunciation of the period [S].
  4. 27:35 — the addition ἐπιδιδοὺς καὶ ἡμῖν. 614, 2147, the Harklean marked reading and the Sahidic add “giving to us also” after “began to eat”. The tidying supplied a distribution, and so moved the meal towards the Supper (Luke 22:19, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς) and towards a meal of the believers (“us”). High that NA28’s shorter text is original. Preaching impact: real. The earliest text has Paul eat before all; the tradition made it a shared, believers’ meal. Read without the addition, the scene is thanksgiving before unbelievers, not a eucharist among believers.
  5. 28:13 — περιελόντες / περιελθόντες. NA28 prints περιελόντες (“having cast off”) with the first hand of Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Ψ and (Gigas). περιελθόντες (“having sailed around”) has 𝔓⁷⁴, the corrector of Sinaiticus, A, L, the Byzantine text, the Latin and the Syriac. The NASB95 and ESV translate the second. The difference is one letter. NA28’s choice yields περιαιρέω a third time in the voyage (Tool 10). Moderate: the external evidence is divided and the internal arguments run both ways.
  6. 28:16 — the handing-over expansion. L, 323, 614, 945, 1241, the Byzantine majority, Gigas, p, the Harklean marked reading and the Sahidic read ὁ ἑκατόνταρχος παρέδωκεν τοὺς δεσμίους τῷ στρατοπεδάρχῳ, τῷ δὲ Παύλῳ ἐπετράπη (“the centurion handed over the prisoners to the camp-commander, but Paul was permitted …”). 614, 2147, the Old Latin and the Harklean add ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς (“outside the barracks”). NA28’s shorter text has 𝔓⁷⁴, א, A, B, Ψ, 048, 066, 81, 1175, 1505, 1739, 2464, the Vulgate and the Bohairic. The tidying supplied the formal procedure and a location, and so blurred the inclusio of ἐπιτρέπω (27:3 / 28:16) by making Paul one prisoner among others. High for NA28.

Other readings considered.

  • 27:37: Vaticanus and the Sahidic read ὡς for διακόσιαι (“about seventy-six”). Alexandrinus and a Sahidic manuscript read πέντε for ἕξ (275). NA28’s 276 stands on the rest. A Greek numeral written as letters is easily misread, which explains the Vaticanus reading [S]. No impact.
  • 27:16: Καῦδα / Κλαυδα / Κλαύδην / Γαύδην. The NASB95’s “Clauda” follows the second. No impact.
  • 27:19: ἔρριψαν (“they threw”) against ἐρρίψαμεν (“we threw”, L, Ψ, 614, 1241, 1505, the Byzantine text, Syriac). The Byzantine reading puts the narrator’s group among the jettisoners; the KJV follows it. No impact.
  • 28:1: Μελιτήνη (B, Latin, Harklean, Bohairic) and Μιλήτη* (𝔓⁷⁴). They do not settle the island’s identity.
  • 28:18: 614 and the Harklean marked reading add πολλά after ἀνακρίναντές με (“having examined me at length”). No impact.
  • 28:19 (outside the extent): a long Western addition, borrowing 22:22’s “away with our enemy”, and a statement that Paul appealed “to redeem my soul from death”. Noted only because it shows the Western text, too, hearing 28:17–19 as a passion account.

Historical and Cultural Background

All [S], standard reference knowledge not checked against a source in this run; confidence stated for each point.

  • The grain ships. Alexandrian ships carried Egypt’s grain to Italy, and the wheat thrown out at 27:38 fits. Josephus reports a ship carrying about six hundred, which makes 276 credible. High / moderate.
  • The Fast and the sailing season. “The Fast” is the Day of Atonement, in autumn. Open-sea sailing grew dangerous from mid-September and was largely suspended from about mid-November to March. Moderate-to-high on the general point.
  • Fair Havens, Phoenix, the Syrtis, “Adria”. Southern Crete’s harbours are identified but debated in detail. The Syrtis is the shoal coast off North Africa. “Adria” in antiquity covered the central Mediterranean, not only the Adriatic proper. Moderate-to-high.
  • Seamanship. Undergirding cables, a lowered “gear” or sea-anchor, soundings and anchoring from the stern all fit the practice of the period. Moderate.
  • Malta. The traditional identification. Alternatives in the Adriatic and the Ionian Sea are minority views. πρῶτος matches a title known from Maltese inscriptions. Moderate.
  • Roman custody. Soldiers answered with their lives for escaped prisoners, which makes 27:42 intelligible. House arrest with a soldier attached (28:16) was a light form of custody. Moderate-to-high.
  • The Dioscuri. Castor and Pollux, patrons of sailors. High.
  • “Barbarians.” People who did not speak Greek. The Maltese spoke a Punic dialect. Moderate-to-high.
  • Puteoli and the Appian Way. Puteoli was the main port of arrival for Rome. Appius’s Forum (about 43 Roman miles out) and Three Taverns (about 33) are stations on the Appian Way. Moderate.

Original Audience Reception

First hearers: Theophilus and a mixed church that knew Roman custody and sea travel, and that may have heard of Paul’s voyage as an embarrassment. [S]; moderate.

What they brought:

  • The common view that disaster at sea or a snakebite proves guilt. The Maltese voice it (28:4).
  • Familiarity with grain ships and with the twin gods on their prows.
  • Jonah and Psalm 107 in Greek.
  • Luke’s Gospel: the storm on the lake (Luke 8:22–25), the Supper (22:19) and Pilate (23:20–22).

What would have struck them:

  • Comfort: a God who speaks at night to a prisoner, and who keeps 276 alive for one man’s sake.
  • Surprise: that the prisoner gives orders the soldiers obey (27:32), and that he gives thanks to the God of Israel in front of pagan sailors (27:35).
  • Disturbance: the Twin Brothers on the prow, passed without comment; the “god” verdict uncorrected; and a story that brings Paul to Rome still in chains.
  • Recognition: Paul’s account of his trial in the words of Jesus’ trial (28:18).

What we bring that they did not: a tendency to allegorise the ship, to make 28:3–6 a promise of immunity, and to read the voyage as an adventure detached from 23:11 and 25:12.

Candidate Fallen Condition Focus. When things go badly we read God’s verdict off events — guilt in misfortune, divinity in escape — and when we hear his promise we either stop acting or stop trusting. The Maltese embody the first half, the sailors’ flight and the deferring centurion the second.

Biblical-Theological Themes

  • Salvation. In Luke–Acts the word of rescue from the sea and the word of the gospel are the same word. The passage stands between σωτηρία in 4:12 and τὸ σωτήριον τοῦ θεοῦ in 28:28. [T] on the words; moderate on the theological weight.
  • God’s plan against human plans. βουλή (Tool 7), with Gamaliel’s principle (5:38–39) worked out at sea. Moderate-to-high.
  • The servant follows the master. Paul’s trial is narrated in Jesus’ words (Luke 23 → Acts 28:18); his meal in the Supper’s words; Jesus’ promise (Luke 21:18) is spoken by Paul. Moderate-to-high. This is the passage’s richest biblical-theological strand.
  • The sea. The Old Testament’s sea texts form a background. The passage quotes none, and the only stable Greek contact is Jonah (moderate-to-low). Low as a theme claim.
  • Hospitality to strangers. Julius, the Maltese, Publius and the brothers. Gentile kindness to God’s servant is a thread Luke values (Luke 7:1–10; 10:30–37). Moderate.

Christological Reading

A New Testament passage in which Christ is not named. The Christological content is carried by echo, and it is strong:

  • Jesus’ trial governs the account of Paul’s trial (Luke 23:20–22 → 13:28 → 28:18). Moderate-to-high.
  • Jesus’ promise is spoken in the storm (Luke 21:18 → 27:34). High.
  • The table’s shape (Luke 22:19 → 27:35). High on the words, contested on the sacrament.
  • The risen Lord’s commission stands behind the angel’s δεῖ (23:11 → 27:24). High.

A tempting reading to refuse. Paul as Christ calming the storm. Luke 8:22–25 has Jesus rebuke the wind; Paul rebukes nothing, and the storm runs its course. The relation is better read as a contrast. On the lake the Lord is in the boat and the disciples cry ἀπολλύμεθα (“we are perishing”, Luke 8:24). On the Adriatic the Lord is not narrated as present, and his word holds the ship anyway. [I], moderate-to-low; not to be preached as typology.

Moralism check. “Be like Paul in a crisis” is not the point. The point is the Lord whose word reaches Rome through a chained witness and whose own passion shapes that witness.

Difficult / Contested Verses

  • 27:35 — eucharist or ordinary meal? The data: the Supper’s three verbs, in Luke–Acts only here and Luke 22:19. Against: Paul eats and does not distribute; most of those present are pagans; the stated purpose is survival (27:34). The Western-type addition shows that early readers made it a believers’ meal. Contested; no verdict. Name both readings from the pulpit.
  • 27:22–24 and 27:31 — promise and condition. Not a contradiction to be solved but a relation to be preached. Luke records both without comment.
  • 27:42–43 — the plan to kill the prisoners. Reported without moral comment. The narrator’s only comment is the centurion’s motive.
  • 28:3–6 — the viper. No cause is given. Do not supply one, and do not make it a promise (Mark 16:18 is a textually disputed ending and is not Luke).
  • 28:6 — “he is a god”. Uncorrected, against Luke’s pattern (Tool 6). The preacher should name the silence, not explain it.
  • 28:8–9 — healing without recorded faith or preaching. Descriptive. It neither commands nor rules out healing today.
  • 28:18 — “they wished to release me”. Paul’s summary passes over Felix’s hope of a bribe (24:26) and Festus’s wish to please the Jews (25:9). The summary is selective and Luke lets it stand. See Book-Overview Tensions.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: Allegorising the voyage

  • What it looks like: “The ship is the church; the four anchors are faith, hope, love and prayer; the boat is worldly security.”
  • Why it’s wrong: Luke tells us what interests him: a promise, a people granted, a prisoner obeyed, a meal, a verdict. The ship, its tackle and its cargo are lost by design (27:22, 26). None of the allegory’s equations is in the text.
  • The corrective: Preach the promise and the people who are carried by it.

Pitfall: Setting promise against means

  • What it looks like: “God has promised, so relax”, or “it all depends on staying in the boat”.
  • Why it’s wrong: 27:22–24 is unconditional and 27:31 is conditional, and Luke sets them side by side without comment.
  • The corrective: The promise fixes the outcome and the form. The means are how the promise is carried out. Hold both, and say why the soldiers’ obedience (27:32) is the right response to the promise, not a lapse from trusting it.

Pitfall: Making 28:3–6 a promise of immunity

  • What it looks like: “God protects his servants from harm.”
  • Why it’s wrong: Paul is still in chains at 28:20; James was killed at 12:2. What 28:5 fulfils is a specific word to Paul (18:10). The Maltese misread events in both directions and the narrator endorses neither.
  • The corrective: Preach the danger of reading God’s verdict off events, and the faithfulness of God to a specific word he gave.

Pitfall: Preaching 27:35 as a eucharist — or ignoring its shape

  • What it looks like: “Paul celebrated the Lord’s Supper on the ship”, or else passing over the bread as a breakfast.
  • Why it’s wrong: The words are the Supper’s, but the act is Paul’s thanksgiving and his own eating before pagans. The distribution was added later by the tradition.
  • The corrective: Name the echo. Preach the public thanksgiving and the restored courage that followed it (27:36), and present the sacramental reading as a contested position.

Pitfall: Letting the ESV decide 27:34 and 28:18

  • What it looks like: “Eat for strength”; “the Romans found no reason for the death penalty”.
  • Why it’s wrong: The Greek says the food serves their σωτηρία, and it puts Pilate’s verdict on Jesus into Paul’s mouth.
  • The corrective: At 27:34 say “for your salvation” and explain it. At 28:18 read Luke 23:22 beside it.
