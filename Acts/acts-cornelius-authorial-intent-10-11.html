Luke’s Authorial Intention in the Telling and Re-telling of Cornelius

Acts 10:1–48 · Acts 11:1–18

Primary texts: SBLGNT with its lemma index, read from _texts/; the NA28 and NASB95 Acts exports consulted where a reading is load-bearing Study text: NASB95 Pulpit text: None — no sermon in view Date: 20 November 2026 Book-overview context: book-overview-acts v2.5 Governing prior work: dig-deeper-acts-10-1to11-18 (20 November 2026), the solo dig on this unit

Scope note — what this study is, and what it is not

This is not a second exegesis of Acts 10–11. A full solo dig on the unit was completed this morning, and it is a thorough one: forty-one verified chains, five headline findings, five proposed intertextual rows, and a declaration that no commentary or research tool was consulted at any point. Re-working the sixteen tools over the same sixty-six verses would produce a reformatting, not a study.

What this adds is a single question laid across the unit: why does Luke tell this twice? The solo dig answers it in passing, under Structure and under the Positional Necessity Check. Here it is the whole subject, and asking it as the whole subject turns up things a passage-run does not reach for — chiefly a set of measurements of what the second telling keeps and loses, and a link between Peter’s re-telling and Luke’s own preface that the solo dig does not make.

Method, stated so the overlap is honest. The Greek of 10:1–11:18 and 11:4’s vocabulary were read from the corpus, the comparison between the tellings was worked and measured, and the lemma chains were verified before the solo dig was opened. Where this study reached a finding the dig already holds, the dig has priority and is credited: that is true of the διακρίνω chain, of the list of what the re-telling adds and drops, and of the positional answer. Where this study goes beyond it, that is marked. Nothing below is offered as a correction of the dig except one refinement of its wording, at §4, which is argued rather than asserted.

The frame: one event, two tellings, four reports

Patrick’s point about authorial intent, carried over from the Saul study, applies here with one difference that makes it sharper.

With Saul, three accounts of one event meant that every difference between them was located in the text and nowhere else, since the event does not vary. That holds here too. But Acts 10–11 adds something the Saul material does not have: the repetition is inside a single pericope, and the re-telling is not separated from the telling by thirteen chapters. The reader has just read the thing being re-told. There is no possibility of the second account functioning as a reminder. Luke spends eighteen verses re-narrating what he has narrated in the preceding forty-eight, to a reader who cannot have forgotten it — which is an extravagant use of narrative space unless the re-telling is doing work the telling could not do.

And the repetition runs deeper than two tellings. The angel’s instruction to Cornelius is reported four times, by four different speakers, in sixty-six verses [T]:

Reporter Where Words (SBLGNT)
The narrator 10:3–6 71
Cornelius’s messengers, in summary 10:22 34
Cornelius himself 10:30–32 61
Peter, reporting Cornelius 11:13–14 37

The three full reports fall 71 → 61 → 37, and the last is at third hand — Peter telling Jerusalem what Cornelius told him about what an angel told Cornelius. Peter’s vision is told twice (10:9–16 and 11:5–10). So the unit is not a narrative with a re-telling attached; it is built out of repetition throughout, and the question of why is the question of what the unit is for.

Part One — Why Luke recorded it as he did in Acts 10, and why there

The positional answer, which the solo dig states and this study accepts

[S: 10:1–11:18 run] The dig’s Phase 0.6 settles this and settles it well: by chapter 10 everything required for the Gentile mission is in place except the one thing the book has not yet tested — whether a Jewish believer may share a table with Gentiles and survive the report. A Gentile has already been baptised (8:38); the apostle to the Gentiles has already been commissioned (9:15). The outstanding question is the one actually put at 11:3 — εἰσῆλθες πρὸς ἄνδρας ἀκροβυστίαν ἔχοντας καὶ συνέφαγες αὐτοῖς, “you went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them” — and it is a question about the table, not about salvation.

The dig also supplies the detail that makes the placement unmistakable: 9:43 leaves Peter lodging with Simon a tanner, and βυρσεύς stands in three New Testament verses, all in this stretch (9:43; 10:6; 10:32) [T]. Before the vision about clean and unclean creatures, Luke has lodged the apostle in a house that handled their carcasses.

One thing to add from the Saul study. That study found that Luke calls Saul before Cornelius and then removes him to Tarsus (9:30), so that Peter and not Paul opens the Gentile door — the apologetic answer to the charge of innovation, built structurally. Acts 10 is the other half of that arrangement. The two chapters are a single piece of construction: the apostle to the Gentiles is commissioned and shelved, and the apostle to the circumcision is sent to the Gentile. Neither episode makes its full sense without the other, and Luke has put them side by side.

How Luke tells it: three decisions that carry the purpose

(a) He narrates it himself, and he narrates it at length. Chapter 10 is 836 words of Greek [T]. Nothing is reported at second hand that can be shown directly: the reader sees the angel, sees the sheet, hears the voice, hears the sermon, and sees the Spirit fall. Everything Peter will later assert in Jerusalem, the reader has already watched. That is the precondition of the re-telling’s working at all, and it is why the order cannot be reversed.

(b) He puts the Gentile first, and gives him the better part. The narrative opens in Caesarea, not Joppa, and the dig’s four-day clock makes the priority inescapable — God addressed Cornelius a full day before he addressed Peter. More pointedly, Luke’s own dossier on Cornelius (10:1–2) is the narrator’s verdict rather than any character’s: εὐσεβής, φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν, ἐλεημοσύνας πολλάς, δεόμενος διὰ παντός. And the dig shows how loaded that vocabulary is — δεκτός with μνημόσυνον stands in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Bible, Sirach 32:9, and δεκτός elsewhere in Swete is the altar’s word for an offering declared fit [S: 10:1–11:18 run]. The asymmetry of response is Luke’s too, and it is [T]: the Gentile obeys immediately; the apostle refuses three times.

(c) He makes God the subject of every decisive verb. The dig sets this out under Author’s Purpose and it is worth repeating because it is the chapter’s spine: an angel comes in (10:3), a voice comes (10:13), the Spirit says (10:19), ἐγὼ ἀπέσταλκα (10:20), God showed me (10:28), the Spirit fell (10:44), God gave (11:17), God granted (11:18). Peter’s verbs are reactive — perplexed, going down, catching up. His one unprompted act is to refuse.

So: the purpose of the first telling

To put on the reader’s own record, at full length and on the narrator’s authority, that God admitted the Gentiles before the church had any view on the matter. The chapter is written so that when the objection is raised at 11:2, the reader is already in a position to judge it — and to judge it as the Jerusalem church cannot, because the reader has seen what Jerusalem has only been told.

That is the reader’s privileged perspective in Sailhamer’s sense, and here it is not incidental but the whole mechanism. Luke gives the reader the evidence first and the hearing second.

Part Two — Why Peter re-tells it in Jerusalem as he does

The re-telling is a selection, not a summary — and the measurements show it

This is where the study goes beyond the dig. The dig says, rightly, that the re-telling adds three things and drops two. What it does not do is measure them, and the measurement changes the description [T], SBLGNT, bracketed sigla stripped:

Block in chapter 10 Words In Peter’s re-telling Words Survives
The vision (10:9–16) 113 11:5–10 100 88%
The messengers and the going (10:17–23a) 141 11:11–12 42 30%
Cornelius’s angel (10:3–6 / 10:30–32) 71 11:13–14 37 52%
The sermon (10:34–43) 185 “as I began to speak” (11:15) 5 3%
The Spirit falling (10:44–48) 85 11:15–17 62 73%
Whole of chapter 10 836 11:4–17 248 30%

The re-telling keeps three words in ten overall — but it keeps nearly nine in ten of the vision, and three in a hundred of the sermon. That is not compression. A summary shortens everything roughly in proportion; this shortens almost nothing and deletes almost everything. It is a selection, and the thing it selects by is the charge.

The charge at 11:3 is about going in and eating. The vision is the evidence that bears on it, so the vision is reproduced nearly whole — including, at 11:8, Peter’s own refusal, which does him no credit and which he could have left out. The sermon bears on nothing that was alleged, so it goes. Peter answers the accusation that was actually brought, and nothing else.

What is dropped, and the drop is larger than the dig records

The dig names the sermon and the baptism. Three further things go, and they are the three richest things in chapter 10 [T], verified by lemma against the SBLGNT:

  • εὐσεβής (10:2, 10:7), ἐλεημοσύνη (10:2, 10:4, 10:31), μνημόσυνον (10:4), δίκαιος (10:22), δεκτός (10:35) — every one of them stands in chapter 10 and not one appears anywhere in 11:1–18. The whole piety dossier, on which the dig builds two of its five headline findings, is absent from Peter’s defence.
  • προσωπολήμπτης (10:34), a New Testament hapax, and with it the thesis of 10:34–35 — that God shows no partiality and that in every nation the God-fearer is acceptable. The chapter’s theological centre is not repeated.
  • The whole of the christological proclamation of 10:36–43, including the resurrection, the appointed Judge, and forgiveness through the name.

Put together, this is the finding. In Jerusalem Peter does not argue that Cornelius deserved it. He does not argue that God is impartial. He does not preach. He narrates. Every ground on which a man might be thought to have made his own case — the Gentile’s merit, the apostle’s doctrine, the apostle’s preaching — is removed, and what is left is a sequence of things that happened to him.

Checking stage — established, and two further omissions to add. [revised: logos-research] Tannehill reaches the same reading: in response to the objection at 11:3 Peter does not begin by arguing through logical inference but retells the narrative of his own experience, and it is that narrative which proves convincing — “actions that appeared inappropriate out of context appeared appropriate when put back into the context of a narrative, for the narrative enabled the hearers to understand that the determinative force behind the change was God” [S: Tannehill, *Narrative Unity* 2:143–144, 276]. That is this section’s conclusion in a commentator’s words, and it was published in 1994.

Parsons supplies two omissions this study missed, both of which strengthen the case [S: Parsons, *Acts*, Paideia, 157; *Luke: Storyteller, Interpreter, Evangelist*, 181]:

  • Simon the tanner disappears. βυρσεύς stands at 9:43, 10:6 and 10:32 and nowhere in the re-telling — plausibly to avoid telling a circumcised audience that Peter had been lodging in a tanner’s house. The detail Luke planted to prepare the vision is the detail Peter suppresses when defending it.
  • Peter’s hunger disappears. 10:10’s ἐγένετο πρόσπεινος καὶ ἤθελεν γεύσασθαι has no counterpart at 11:5 — on Parsons’s reading, so that the first explicit mention of eating in the re-telling is God’s command rather than Peter’s appetite.

Both cut the same way as the piety and the sermon: everything removed is something that might make the act look like Peter’s own, and everything retained is something done to him.

The three additions are warrants, not doctrine

What Peter adds is not exposition either. Each addition is a warrant — an authority external to himself [T]:

  • 11:14 — the angel’s promise that Peter’s words will save Cornelius’s household, ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ σὺ καὶ πᾶς ὁ οἶκός σου. This clause stands nowhere in chapter 10. The angel’s speech at 10:5–6 has no soteriological content at all; the messengers at 10:22 report only “to hear words from you”. The saving purpose is disclosed for the first time in the re-telling.
  • 11:16 — ἐμνήσθην τοῦ ῥήματος τοῦ κυρίου, the recollection of Jesus’ own word about baptism in the Spirit, which is Acts 1:5. Peter does not reason to his conclusion; he remembers a dominical saying and finds the event has fulfilled it.
  • 11:17 — ἐγὼ τίς ἤμην δυνατὸς κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν; The inference is drawn as a rhetorical question whose answer is nobody, and it escalates 10:47’s κωλύω from the water to God himself [T]: κωλύω stands six times in Acts (8:36; 10:47; 11:17; 16:6; 24:23; 27:43), and two of the six are these.

So the shape is: I was given a vision, I was sent, I was told these men were sent, God had promised to save them, the Lord had said this would happen, and the Spirit fell before I had finished my first sentence. Peter’s entire defence is a report of things done to him and around him.

And the sermon is interrupted in both tellings

Worth noticing together, because neither telling lets Peter finish. In chapter 10 the Spirit falls ἔτι λαλοῦντος τοῦ Πέτρου τὰ ῥήματα ταῦτα — “while Peter was still speaking” (10:44). In chapter 11 the same moment is ἐν δὲ τῷ ἄρξασθαί με λαλεῖν — “as I began to speak” (11:15). The narrator says Peter had not finished; Peter says he had barely started. The second is the stronger claim and Peter makes it about himself. The preaching cannot be credited with the result, and Peter is the one who insists on it.

The re-telling is narrated in Luke’s own preface vocabulary

This is the study’s main addition to the morning’s run, and it answers Patrick’s framing directly. [T] on the distributions, verified by lemma against the SBLGNT.

Luke’s preface claims to have followed everything carefully and to write καθεξῆς — “in orderly sequence” (Luke 1:3) — so that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he has been taught. That word stands in five New Testament verses, all in Luke–Acts: Luke 1:3, Luke 8:1, Acts 3:24, Acts 11:4, Acts 18:23. And Acts 11:4 is the only place in either volume where it describes a character giving an account of events.

The verb it governs is ἐκτίθημι, which stands in four New Testament verses, all in Acts: 7:21 (Moses exposed as an infant, a different sense), 11:4, 18:26 (Priscilla and Aquila expounding the way ἀκριβέστερον to Apollos), and 28:23 (Paul expounding the kingdom from morning to evening). As a verb of knowledge-transfer it is three occurrences, all in Acts, and all three are orderly, accurate or sustained exposition.

So Acts 11:4 carries both of Luke’s terms for reliable narration at once: ἀρξάμενος δὲ Πέτρος ἐξετίθετο αὐτοῖς καθεξῆς — “Peter began and set it out to them in order.”

The implication for authorial intent is direct. Luke’s preface says that he has written an orderly account resting on the testimony of eyewitnesses. At 11:4 he shows an eyewitness doing exactly that, in his own preface’s words, and he shows the method working: the hearing ends with the objection withdrawn (11:18). Peter’s defence is not merely reported by a historian who claims an orderly method; it is narrated as an instance of that method. The re-telling is Luke’s procedure performed inside his own book.

Checking stage — established, and more fully than stated here. [revised: logos-research] The link is not drawn in this morning’s run, but it is well known. Johnson notes that Peter tells the events καθεξῆς, the same adverb Luke uses in the Gospel prologue of his whole two-volume work, and that Luke understands the recitation of events in sequence to have a peculiarly convincing quality [S: Johnson, *The Acts of the Apostles*, Sacra Pagina 5:200]. More strikingly, Johnson elsewhere names the same three places this project assembled across two studies — Acts 9:27, 11:4 and 15:12–14 [S: Johnson, "Luke-Acts, Book Of", *Anchor Yale Bible Dictionary* 4:405]. A specialised essay has developed the point by comparing Peter’s account with the narrator’s longer version in Acts 10, which is the exercise this study undertook [S: in Wedderburn and Christophersen, *Paul, Luke and the Graeco-Roman World*, JSNTSup 217:162–163].

And one correction to how the link should be stated. Parsons observes that καθεξῆς “has little to do with chronological or linear order”; Peter, and behind him the narrator, is arranging the events so that the audience will find them rhetorically persuasive [S: Parsons, *Acts*, Paideia, 157]. That matters for the use made of it below: the orderliness Luke claims and Peter performs is persuasive arrangement, not chronological sequence — which fits the selection-by-charge finding of the previous section exactly, and would not fit a claim that Peter simply narrated in the right order.

What the hearing contributes: the verb that settles it

The dig’s fourth headline finding is the right close, and this study confirms it independently. διακρίνω stands four times in Acts and all four are in this one argument [T]: the Spirit tells Peter to go μηδὲν διακρινόμενος (10:20); the circumcision party διεκρίνοντο with him (11:2); Peter reports that he went μηδὲν διακρίναντα (11:12); and at the Council the subject becomes God — οὐθὲν διέκρινεν μεταξὺ ἡμῶν τε καὶ αὐτῶν (15:9).

The word travels from command, to objection, to compliance, to divine precedent — and the re-telling is the hinge, because 11:12 is where Peter puts his obedience on the record in the Spirit’s own word. Without the re-telling, 15:9 has nothing to quote. That is the plainest statement of what the second account is for: the argument has to be on the record before it can be cited, and chapter 15 cites it twice (15:7–9, and James’s Συμεὼν ἐξηγήσατο at 15:14).

So: what the re-telling contributes to Luke’s intention

It converts a private act into a public precedent, and it does so by evidence rather than argument. Luke could have had Peter win the debate. Instead he has him decline to debate: no appeal to the Gentile’s piety, no doctrine of impartiality, no sermon — a narrative, set out in order, with three warrants that are not Peter’s own. And the verdict matches the method: ἀκούσαντες δὲ ταῦτα ἡσύχασαν καὶ ἐδόξασαν τὸν θεόν (11:18). They do not concede to Peter; they fall silent and glorify God, which is the only response a narrative of this kind leaves available.

§4 — A refinement of the dig’s own formulation, offered as an argument

The solo dig closes its comparison with this sentence: “Luke’s re-teller gives the Jerusalem church the theology and withholds the narrative colour.”

The second half will not hold, and the first is imprecise. What is withheld is 185 words of christological proclamation — the resurrection, the appointed Judge of living and dead, forgiveness through the name — together with a New Testament hapax carrying the chapter’s thesis, and the Greek Bible’s altar-vocabulary applied to a Gentile. That is not narrative colour; it is the most theological material in the pericope. And what is added — a promise, a remembered dominical word, and an inference from them — is not best described as theology either; all three are warrants, items of evidence with an authority outside the speaker.

The sharper statement, which the measurements support: Peter gives Jerusalem the evidence and withholds the exposition. The dig’s observation is right and its wording under-describes what it found.

The checking stage supports the refinement. Tannehill’s formulation — Peter “does not begin by arguing through logical inference but retells the narrative of his own experience” [S: Tannehill, *Narrative Unity* 2:143] — is the evidence/exposition distinction rather than the theology/colour one, and it was reached independently of both this study and the dig.

Set against the Saul study

The two studies now stand together, and the comparison is instructive because the two re-tellings do opposite things with the same instinct.

What they share. Saul’s conversion and Cornelius are the only events in Acts that Luke narrates more than once [T]. Both are moments on which the legitimacy of the Gentile mission rests. Both commissions come in a trance — ἔκστασις stands at 10:10, 11:5 and 22:17, and both men afterwards have to defend what they received to Jerusalem [S: 10:1–11:18 run]. And in both, Luke marks the act of re-telling with preface vocabulary: καθεξῆς and ἐκτίθημι at 11:4, διηγέομαι — the cognate of Luke 1:1’s διήγησις — at 9:27, where Barnabas recounts Saul’s conversion to the apostles.

Where they diverge. In the Saul accounts the human mediator shrinks to nothing while the mandate grows and its speaker rises: Ananias at length, then briefly, then absent, while the commission nearly doubles and passes from the Lord-speaking-about-Saul to Christ-speaking-to-Paul. In the Cornelius re-telling it is the human contribution that is stripped out and nothing grows in its place. The piety, the preaching and the doctrine go, and what is left standing alone is what God did.

The instinct is the same and the application is opposite, because the two defences answer different charges. Paul is accused of apostasy and must show continuity, so his tellings accumulate authority. Peter is accused of an act, and must show he did not initiate it, so his telling sheds everything that could look like initiative. In each case the shape of the re-telling is dictated by the accusation, which is the strongest single piece of evidence that the shapes are designed.

Critical assessment — where this reading could be wrong

1. The measurements are of blocks I defined. The 836/248 comparison is secure, but the block boundaries in the table are my segmentation of chapter 10, and a different segmentation would move the percentages. The finding that survives any reasonable segmentation is the contrast between the vision and the sermon, which is extreme enough to be robust: nearly nine in ten against three in a hundred. The intermediate rows should be read as indicative.

2. “Selection not summary” may over-read an ordinary rhetorical habit. Ancient speakers routinely shortened to the point at issue, and a defence that answered the charge is what any competent speaker would give. The claim that this is designed by Luke rather than natural to Peter is [I], and the honest support for it is that Luke composed both, so the contrast between them is his. But a reader who thinks Luke is simply reporting a sensible speech has not made an error the text can refute.

3. The dropped-piety finding is the strongest thing here and should still be stated carefully. That none of the six terms recurs in 11:1–18 is [T] and mechanical. That their absence is argumentative — that Peter is deliberately declining to rest the case on Cornelius’s merit — is inference. A plainer explanation is available: the piety vocabulary belongs to the narrator’s introduction of a character, and introductions are not repeated. I think the argumentative reading is right, because 10:34–35’s thesis is also dropped and that is not a character introduction; but the plainer reading should be on the page.

4. The prediction made here before the library pass was run was correct, and it is the most useful thing in this section. The draft said: “On the record of the Saul study, where two rounds returned ‘already established’ five times out of six, the prior should be that somebody has seen this.” The pass then returned “already established” on all three questions — the preface link (Johnson, 1992), the selection-by-charge reading (Tannehill, 1994), and the Ezekiel 4:14 echo inherited from the dig (Keener). [revised: logos-research]

So the prior is now well attested across three studies and five rounds, and it should be stated as a working rule for this project: on a book as heavily worked as Acts, an observation that feels new is more likely to be an observation one has not yet looked up. The right response is not to stop making the observations — this study’s measurements and its assembly of the 9:27 / 11:4 cluster were worth making, and the cluster turned out to be Johnson’s too — but to check before claiming, and to write the claim at the level the check leaves it.

5. This study consumes a run it did not audit. The solo dig’s forty-one chains were not re-verified here; its findings are taken as sound. Of its claims, the ones this study leans on most — the διακρίνω chain, the βυρσεύς distribution, the positional answer — were independently re-derived, so those are earned. The piety-vocabulary findings (Sirach 32:9, δεκτός as altar-language) are taken on trust and carry whatever confidence the dig assigned them.

And one question that belonged to both studies, now settled. [revised: 20 Nov 2026 — definition adopted] Is 15:7–11 a third telling? No. The project now works with a stated criterion: a retelling is the repetition of a large proportion of the original narration. Peter at 15:7–11 narrates nothing — no Caesarea, no Joppa, no vision, no messengers, no house, no sermon. In 98 words, of which only 63 touch the event at all, he appeals to an outcome the council already knows: God chose, God gave the Spirit, God made no distinction (οὐθὲν διέκρινεν, 15:9 — the fourth and last of the διακρίνω chain). That is a citation of the event in the service of a ruling, not a repetition of it.

Two things must now be kept apart. Johnson’s cluster — 9:27, 11:4, 15:12–14 — is evidence about Luke’s vocabulary for giving an orderly account (διηγέομαι, ἐκτίθημι, ἐξηγέομαι), and as such it stands. It is not evidence that all three are retellings, because reporting that an event happened and narrating it again are different acts, and only the second is what these two studies are measuring. The three sort cleanly: Barnabas at 9:27 reports (32 words, three verbs), Peter at 15:7–11 cites (98 words, no narration), Peter at 11:4–17 retells (248 words, the sequence given again in order). So the counts are: Saul’s conversion, one telling and two retellings in selected detail; Cornelius, one telling and one retelling — that one by a key witness. The Saul study’s “told three times” formulation needs no restatement, and the flag there is withdrawn.

Johnson had the preface link and the three-place cluster in 1992; Tannehill had the selection-by-charge reading in 1994; Keener has the Ezekiel echo that the solo dig flagged this morning as its most likely candidate for being either long-established or newly seen — it was long-established.

What this study is therefore worth. Three things, none of which is a discovery. It measures what the commentators describe, which turns “the retelling is shaped by the charge” into figures that can be checked or refuted — 88% of the vision kept against 3% of the sermon, and six piety terms at nil. It joins the Cornelius re-telling to the Saul re-tellings as one authorial device, which neither this morning’s dig nor the Saul study did, and which Johnson’s cluster independently supports. And it closes the solo dig’s first open question as a by-product. A preacher should take the exegesis from the dig, the shape of the argument from here, and the credit from Johnson and Tannehill.
