### 10:1–8 — Caesarea: the angel and Cornelius

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools.

1. **Peter at Caesarea says what Jesus said at Nazareth, and the two verdicts share their vocabulary.** `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, on the design. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 11 Move 4 and the cross-volume check, Tool 14, Christological Reading. δεκτός ("acceptable") stands in **five verses of the New Testament** (SBLGNT) and **three are in Luke–Acts: Luke 4:19, Luke 4:24 and Acts 10:35.** In the Gospel the word is twice negative in effect — the *acceptable* year of the Lord proclaimed, and then no prophet *acceptable* in his own country — and the two examples Jesus gives are the widow of Sidon and Naaman the Syrian. καθαρίζω ("to cleanse") first appears in Luke's Gospel at **4:27**, of **Naaman**: *οὐδεὶς αὐτῶν ἐκαθαρίσθη εἰ μὴ Ναιμὰν ὁ Σύρος* ("none of them was cleansed except Naaman the Syrian"). Its first appearance in Acts is **10:15**, *ἃ ὁ θεὸς ἐκαθάρισεν* — spoken to Peter on his way to a Gentile soldier, whom Peter then calls **δεκτός**. Nazareth tried to kill Jesus for saying it; Jerusalem falls silent and glorifies God.

2. **The pericope's verdict on Cornelius is spoken in the technical vocabulary of Second-Temple Jewish piety-and-acceptance, and one verse of the Greek Old Testament holds three of its terms.** `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, on the register. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 11 (register), Original Audience Reception, Biblical-Theological Themes. **δεκτός with μνημόσυνον ("memorial") stands in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Bible — Sirach 32:9 in Swete's numbering** — and that verse reads *θυσία ἀνδρὸς δικαίου δεκτή, καὶ τὸ μνημόσυνον αὐτῆς οὐκ ἐπιλησθήσεται* ("the sacrifice of a **righteous man** is **acceptable**, and its **memorial** shall not be forgotten"). Acts 10 gives all three: the prayers and alms ascend εἰς μνημόσυνον (10:4), Cornelius is ἀνὴρ δίκαιος (10:22), the God-fearer is δεκτός (10:35). Elsewhere in Swete δεκτός is **sacrificial**-acceptance vocabulary (Leviticus 1:3, 1:4, 17:4, 22:19–21, 23:11), and in Isaiah it is twice used of **foreigners'** offerings becoming acceptable at the altar (56:7; 60:7). So Luke is not saying Cornelius was a nice man; he is pronouncing over a Gentile the formula by which an offering was declared fit for the altar.

3. **The thing that falls on the Gentiles is, in Leviticus 11's own verb, the thing that defiles.** `[T]` on the verbs and their distributions; `[I]`, **moderate**, on the inversion. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 1, Original Languages. The vision's container is a **σκεῦος** ("vessel") — and the one chapter of the Torah that joins σκεῦος to ἀκάθαρτος ("unclean"), ἑρπετά ("creeping things") and πετεινά ("birds") is **Leviticus 11**, whose ruling is that whatever such a creature *falls upon* becomes unclean. The verb there is **ἐπιπίπτω** ("to fall upon"), and in the whole of Leviticus it stands in **three verses, all in chapter 11** (11:32, 11:37, 11:38), always of defilement by contact. In Acts the subject of ἐπιπίπτω is **τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον** (10:44; 11:15), and the two lemmas stand together in only three New Testament verses — Acts 10:44, Acts 11:15 and Revelation 11:13. What Leviticus 11 said falls and defiles, Acts says falls and sanctifies.

4. **διακρίνω is used four times in Acts and only in this one argument, and across those four the subject migrates from Peter to God.** `[T]`, **high**. Surfaced by: Tool 4, Tool 7, Tool 8, Tool 11 Move 4. The lemma stands at **10:20, 11:2, 11:12 and 15:9** — and nowhere else in the book (SBLGNT). Peter is told to go *μηδὲν διακρινόμενος* (10:20); the circumcision party *διεκρίνοντο* with him (11:2); Peter reports that he went *μηδὲν διακρίναντα* (11:12); and at the Council the subject becomes God: *οὐδὲν διέκρινεν μεταξὺ ἡμῶν τε καὶ αὐτῶν* (15:9). In Swete the active verb is overwhelmingly **judicial** — Moses adjudicating (Exodus 18:16), Solomon asking for a heart *διακρίνειν τὸν λαόν σου ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ* (3 Reigns 3:9), God summoning heaven and earth *διακρῖναι τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ* (Psalm 49:4) — so the word is not "hesitate" but "adjudicate", and the question the pericope settles is who has the right to try the case. **Neither NASB95 nor the ESV lets an English reader see one word in the four places.**

5. **The charge brought against Peter is, word for word, the charge brought against Jesus — and the verb of the charge is also the verb of Peter's apostolic credential.** `[T]`, **high** on the distribution; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, on the design. Surfaced by: Tool 7, Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 4 and the cross-volume check, Tool 13, Original Audience Reception. συνεσθίω ("to eat with") stands in **five verses of the New Testament**: Luke 15:2, Acts 10:41, Acts 11:3, 1 Corinthians 5:11 and Galatians 2:12. **Three of the five are the same accusation.** Luke 15:2: *προσδέχεται τοὺς ἁμαρτωλοὺς καὶ συνεσθίει αὐτοῖς*. Acts 11:3: *εἰσῆλθες πρὸς ἄνδρας ἀκροβυστίαν ἔχοντας καὶ συνέφαγες αὐτοῖς*. Galatians 2:12: Peter, at Antioch, *μετὰ τῶν ἐθνῶν συνήσθιεν* — until he withdrew. And the only other occurrence in Acts is **10:41**, where eating with the risen Jesus is what qualifies a man to be a witness. The apostolic credential and the apostolic scandal are the same act.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Three things have been set up and left unresolved.

First, **1:8** promised the end of the earth, and **8:26–40** has baptised an African court official on a desert road — a man the law had cut off (Deuteronomy 23:1) reading Isaiah about a man denied justice. That episode ended with a question nobody answered: *τί κωλύει με βαπτισθῆναι;* ("what prevents me from being baptised?", 8:36). Second, **9:15** has appointed Saul a σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς ("a chosen vessel") to carry the name *before the Gentiles* — and the reader knows what Paul is for before Paul does. Third, and most immediately, **9:32–43** has brought Peter down the coastal plain to Lydda and Joppa, healed a paralytic, raised a dead woman who was *full of good works and alms* (9:36), and parked him — in the pericope's last verse — in the house of **Simon a tanner** (9:43).

That last detail is the hinge. 9:43 is a narrator's plant, not a travel note: βυρσεύς ("tanner") stands in **three verses of the New Testament** and all three are in this stretch (9:43; 10:6; 10:32), the trade being one that handled the carcasses of dead animals. Before the vision about clean and unclean creatures, Luke has already lodged the apostle in a house that smelled of them.

**Necessity answer.** The passage exists *here* for a reason that can be stated precisely: **everything required for the Gentile mission is now in place except the one thing the book has not yet settled — whether Jewish believers may share a table with Gentiles.** A Gentile has already been baptised (8:38) and an apostle to the Gentiles has already been commissioned (9:15); what has not happened is a Jewish believer eating in a Gentile house and surviving the report. So the question at 11:3 is not *may Gentiles be saved* but *εἰσῆλθες … καὶ συνέφαγες αὐτοῖς* — you went in, and you ate with them. And it exists here rather than later because the Council of chapter 15 will cite it: **the retelling is the argument**, and the argument has to be on the record before it can be quoted.

**Implication.** Two consequences follow for preaching. (a) The unit's climax is not 10:44 but **11:18** — the sentence in which the Jerusalem church stops objecting. Luke spends eighteen verses re-telling a story he has just told, which is an extravagant use of narrative space unless the *public settling* is the point. (b) The vision is not about food. Peter says so himself at 10:28: *κἀμοὶ ὁ θεὸς ἔδειξεν μηδένα κοινὸν ἢ ἀκάθαρτον λέγειν **ἄνθρωπον*** — God showed me to call no **man** common or unclean. The sheet held animals; Peter draws a conclusion about people, and the text supplies the licence for the move rather than leaving the preacher to make it.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens the answer. The unit's Old Testament register is **Levitical and sapiential**, not Isaianic — Leviticus 11 behind the vessel, Leviticus and Isaiah behind δεκτός, Sirach and Tobit behind the memorial of prayers and alms. That is unusual for Acts, where the overview's live sources are Isaiah, the Psalter and the Exodus seam-vocabulary. It fits the positional answer exactly: the question on the table is a **purity** question, so the vocabulary on the table is purity vocabulary.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** To give a mixed church certainty that the risen Jesus is still acting, by his Spirit, through witnesses, under a purpose no opposition can obstruct — and that the Gentile mission *is* that purpose rather than a deviation from it (overview, v2.5). `[S: overview]`

**Passage-level.** To put beyond argument that **God, not the church, admitted the Gentiles** — and to do it by narrating the admission twice, so that the second telling is a public defence that the Jerusalem church accepts. The passage's work is evidential. Luke is not persuading the reader that Gentiles may be saved; he is showing the reader the meeting at which the Jewish church agreed, and showing that the agreement was forced by God's prior action rather than reached by deliberation.

Three features of Luke's own composition carry this. (a) **The grammar of agency.** The decisive verbs have God, the Spirit, the angel or the voice as subject: an angel comes in (10:3), a voice comes (10:13), the Spirit says (10:19), *ἐγὼ ἀπέσταλκα* ("I have sent them", 10:20), God showed me (10:28), the Spirit fell (10:44), God gave (11:17), God has granted (11:18). Peter's own verbs are reactive — he is perplexed (10:17), he goes down (10:21), he is at a loss what the vision means. `[T]` (b) **Peter never claims to have understood in advance.** His interpretative sentence at 10:34 begins *ἐπ᾽ ἀληθείας καταλαμβάνομαι* — "I am now grasping", present tense, a man catching up. `[T]` (c) **The one thing Peter does on his own initiative is refuse** (10:14), and he is corrected twice more for it.

**Book-overview note:** this is the overview's own Moralism Check demonstrated from the text rather than asserted — "the apostles refuse the role twice, explicitly" is cited there from 3:12 and 14:14–15; **10:26 is a third instance and the overview does not list it.** *ἀνάστηθι· καὶ ἐγὼ αὐτὸς ἄνθρωπός εἰμι* ("Stand up; I too am just a man") belongs in that note. `[T]`

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above.*

#### Immediate context

**Before (9:32–43).** Peter at Lydda and Joppa: Aeneas healed, Tabitha raised, and the unit's closing verse lodging Peter with Simon the tanner. The movement has taken Peter *out of Jerusalem and down to the coast* — which is where the Gentile world begins. Caesarea, where the pericope opens, is the Roman provincial capital and the sea-port, and it is where **Philip stopped** at 8:40. The gospel has been sitting in Caesarea, in the person of an evangelist, for two chapters without entering a Gentile house.

**After (11:19–30).** The scattered reach Antioch, speak to non-Jews, and the disciples are first called Χριστιανοί (11:26). The placement is purposeful: the Jerusalem verdict of 11:18 is immediately followed by the first properly mixed congregation. And Luke binds the two with a rare verb — **χρηματίζω** ("to be divinely instructed; to be called") stands in **two verses of Acts**, 10:22 (Cornelius *ἐχρηματίσθη*, divinely instructed) and 11:26 (the disciples *χρηματίσαι* … Χριστιανούς). `[T]`, moderate on the design: the same verb carries the Gentile's summons and the mixed church's new name.

**Section.** The overview's fourth movement, **9:32–12:24**, "The Gentile threshold", closed by the growth-notice at 12:24. This unit is that movement's centre of gravity.

#### A correction to the sweep

The sweep's unit on this passage says it "occupies sixty-six verses, the longest single unit in Acts". The verse count is right and the superlative is wrong. Counted against the overview's own preaching-unit map in the SBLGNT, **10:1–11:18 is the third longest unit**: 23:12–26:32 has 109 verses and 6:8–7:60 has 68, before 10:1–11:18's 66. `[T]` The point the sweep was making survives — sixty-six verses is an extravagant allocation, and about a third of them are a re-telling — but the claim should be stated as *the longest single-scene narrative outside the custody material*, or simply dropped.

### 3. Structure

Seven scenes, alternating location, with the last standing outside the alternation:

| Verses | Scene | Where | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10:1–8 | The angel and Cornelius | Caesarea | The Gentile is addressed first |
| 10:9–16 | The vessel and the voice | Joppa | The apostle is prepared, and refuses |
| 10:17–23a | The gate | Joppa | The two revelations meet; Peter becomes a host |
| 10:23b–33 | The meeting | Caesarea | Cornelius re-tells scene 1; Peter states the problem |
| 10:34–43 | The sermon | Caesarea | The gospel, with a new opening formula |
| 10:44–48 | The Spirit falls | Caesarea | God acts; baptism follows |
| **11:1–18** | **The retelling and the verdict** | **Jerusalem** | **The public settling** |

Three structural devices are doing work.

**(a) A converging double vision.** Scenes 1 and 2 are simultaneous and neither party knows it — the messengers are on the road while Peter is on the roof (10:9). This is Luke's device, and **he has just used it**: 9:10–12 pairs a vision to Ananias with a vision to Saul, and in that scene too the Lord tells one man what the other has seen. `[T]` Two double-visions in consecutive pericopes is a near-range structural echo of the kind the overview's health note says only solo depth finds.

**(b) A four-day clock, stated twice.** *τῇ ἐπαύριον* ("on the next day") at 10:9, 10:23b and 10:24, with Cornelius's *ἀπὸ τετάρτης ἡμέρας* ("four days ago") at 10:30 confirming the count from inside the story. `[T]` The clock matters because it makes the scene-order inescapable: God addressed the Gentile a full day before he addressed the apostle.

**(c) The hours.** **The ninth hour at 10:3 and 10:30; the sixth hour at 10:9.** In Luke–Acts the two hours are named together in only one other place — **Luke 23:44**, the darkness from the sixth hour until the ninth. `[T]` (ἔνατος stands in Acts at 3:1, 10:3 and 10:30; ἕκτος only at 10:9; SBLGNT.) *Moderate confidence* on whether Luke intends the crucifixion hours to be heard here: the hours are also the ordinary Jewish hours of prayer, and 3:1 names the ninth as such. But the sequence is **reversed** — ninth, then sixth — and a reader who has heard Luke 23:44 has met this pair before.

**The retelling is not a repetition.** Set 11:5–17 beside 10:9–48 and three things are *added* in the second telling and absent from the first: the promise that Peter's words will **save** Cornelius's household (11:14), the recollection of **the Lord's own word** (11:16), and the question *who was I to withstand God?* (11:17). Two things are *dropped*: the sermon, and the baptism. Luke's re-teller gives the Jerusalem church the theology and withholds the narrative colour. `[T]`, high.

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative, so the connectives are mostly sequential. Five carry weight.

- **10:20** *μηδὲν διακρινόμενος ***ὅτι*** ἐγὼ ἀπέσταλκα αὐτούς* — ←*for*←. The ground of Peter's going is not the men's credentials but the Spirit's claim to have sent them. Note the emphatic ἐγώ. `[T]`
- **10:28–29** *κἀμοὶ ὁ θεὸς ἔδειξεν … ***διὸ καὶ*** ἀναντιρρήτως ἦλθον* — →*therefore*→, and it is the pericope's only explicit inference drawn by a human being. Peter's reasoning is stated once, and it runs from God's showing to his own coming. `[T]`
- **10:34–35** *οὐκ ἔστιν προσωπολήμπτης ὁ θεός, ***ἀλλ᾽*** ἐν παντὶ ἔθνει …* — the ἀλλά supplies the positive half of a denial, and the positive half is the load-bearing clause. A sermon that preaches only "God shows no partiality" has preached the negative and left the sentence unfinished. `[T]`
- **10:46–47** *ἤκουον ***γὰρ*** αὐτῶν λαλούντων γλώσσαις* — ←*for*←, grounding the astonishment of 10:45 in audible evidence. Luke is careful: the circumcised believers did not infer the Spirit's coming, they **heard** it.
- **11:17** *εἰ ***οὖν*** τὴν ἴσην δωρεὰν ἔδωκεν … ἐγὼ τίς ἤμην δυνατὸς κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν;* — a first-class conditional with οὖν drawing the whole defence to its point. The protasis is treated as fact; the apodosis is a rhetorical question whose answer is *nobody*. **This is the sentence the Council will later be arguing about.** `[T]`

**Pulpit note.** The ESV renders 10:20's ὅτι as "for I have sent them" and keeps the inference visible; NASB95's "for I have sent them Myself" adds the emphasis of ἐγώ, which is in the Greek. Both are good here.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as Hebrew parallelism** — this is Greek narrative prose with an embedded speech, and the speech is not in parallel cola.

There is, however, a structural parallelism worth naming because it is the pericope's spine: **the two revelations are told in matching terms and in deliberately unmatched order.**

| | Cornelius | Peter |
|---|---|---|
| Time | ninth hour (10:3) | sixth hour (10:9) |
| State | gazing, afraid (10:4) | hungry, in a trance (10:10) |
| Agent | an angel of God | a voice |
| Address | by name — *Κορνήλιε* | by name — *Πέτρε* |
| Content | send for a man | rise, kill and eat |
| Response | immediate obedience (10:7–8) | *μηδαμῶς, κύριε* (10:14) |

The asymmetry in the last row is the point, and it is `[T]`: **the Gentile obeys at once and the apostle refuses three times.**

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Luke's hand shows in five places, and they are interpretive rather than decorative.

- **10:1–2** — the dossier on Cornelius is narrator's verdict, not reported speech: εὐσεβής ("devout"), φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν ("fearing God"), ἐλεημοσύνας πολλάς ("many alms"), δεόμενος διὰ παντός ("praying continually"). Luke tells the reader what to think of the man before the man speaks. `[T]`
- **10:9** — *ὁδοιπορούντων ἐκείνων καὶ τῇ πόλει ἐγγιζόντων* ("while they were on their way and approaching the city"). Inside information: the reader is told what Peter does not know. This is the device that makes scene 2 a convergence rather than a coincidence. `[T]`
- **10:16** — *τοῦτο δὲ ἐγένετο ἐπὶ τρίς* ("this happened three times"). Luke counts for the reader. He counts again at 11:10, in Peter's mouth. **ἐπὶ τρίς stands in two verses of the New Testament**, these two (SBLGNT). `[T]`
- **10:45–46** — the circumcised believers' astonishment is reported *with its evidence*, which is the narrator certifying the event rather than the characters' impression of it.
- **11:18** — *ἡσύχασαν καὶ ἐδόξασαν τὸν θεόν* ("they quieted down and glorified God"). The narrator's verdict on the hearing, and the last word is God's, not Peter's. `[T]`

`[S: overview]` **ἡσυχάζω** stands in **two verses of Acts**, 11:18 and 21:14, and both elements of 11:18 recur six verses apart at 21:14 and 21:20 — a row the overview already carries.

### 7. Vocabulary

Six words carry this passage, and five of them are rare.

**κοινός ("common") and κοινόω ("to make common"), 10:14, 10:15, 10:28; 11:8, 11:9** `[T]`, high

The adjective stands in **twelve verses of the New Testament** and the verb in **eleven** (SBLGNT). Three facts about their distribution matter.

(a) **In Acts the adjective stands in five verses, and the first two are not about purity at all**: 2:44 and 4:32, where the believers *εἶχον ἅπαντα κοινά* / *ἦν αὐτοῖς ἅπαντα κοινά* — "they had all things in common". Then 10:14's *οὐδέποτε ἔφαγον **πᾶν κοινόν*** and 10:28 and 11:8. So the one word Luke has used for the Jerusalem church's shared property is the word Peter uses for what he will not eat. *Moderate confidence* that Luke intends the play: the two senses are entirely distinct in Hellenistic usage, and the risk of over-reading is real. What is `[T]` is that πᾶς + κοινός stands in Acts at 2:44, 4:32 and 10:14, and no other place.

(b) **Neither word occurs anywhere in Luke's Gospel.** `[T]`, verified by lemma against the SBLGNT, with positive controls run (καθαρίζω returns seven Lukan verses; κοινός returns Mark 7:2 and 7:5, so the search reaches both books). The ritual sense of κοινός belongs to **Matthew 15 and Mark 7** — the hand-washing controversy, four occurrences of the verb in each — and Luke has none of it. The declaration that cleanses foods comes, in Luke's two volumes, not as teaching in the Gospel but as a **vision** to Peter in Acts. This is a substantial compositional fact and it has consequences for preaching (see Pitfalls).

(c) **κοινός in this ritual sense is not Septuagint vocabulary.** In Swete the adjective never means "ritually defiled"; the Greek Old Testament's word for profane is **βέβηλος**, which stands in 106 verses. `[T]`, with the positive control run (the κοιν- pattern returns Leviticus 6:2's κοινωνία and twenty-eight further Swete verses, so the search reaches Swete). Peter's κοινόν is therefore late Second-Temple Jewish Greek, not biblical Greek — which is itself part of the point: the category he is defending is one the Torah does not name in that word.

(d) **κοινόω runs forward.** Its three Acts occurrences are 10:15, 11:9 and **21:28**, where the Jerusalem mob charges that Paul *κεκοίνωκεν τὸν ἅγιον τόπον* ("has defiled the holy place") by bringing a Gentile into the temple. `[S: overview]` — the overview's echo table already carries this row at high confidence, and it should be credited there rather than claimed here. What this run adds is the direction: **the verb God forbids Peter to use of people is the verb the mob uses against Paul, and the thing Paul is accused of is the thing Peter was commanded.**

**σκεῦος ("vessel, object"), 10:11, 10:16; 11:5** `[T]`, high on the count

Five verses in Acts: **9:15, 10:11, 10:16, 11:5, 27:17** (SBLGNT). The sweep flagged the 9:15/10:11 resonance as worth an audit; here is the audit.

*For the link.* The two uses stand in consecutive pericopes, and 9:15's is loaded: *σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς ἐστίν μοι οὗτος τοῦ βαστάσαι τὸ ὄνομά μου **ἐνώπιον ἐθνῶν*** — a chosen vessel to carry the name *before the Gentiles*. The next time Luke writes σκεῦος, a vessel comes down from heaven holding what Gentiles eat, and the apostle who sees it goes to a Gentile. Nothing else in Acts is called a σκεῦος except a ship's gear in a storm.

*Against the link.* σκεῦος is an ordinary word for any container or implement; the two referents share nothing but the noun; and 9:15's genitive (ἐκλογῆς, "of choosing") is the weight-bearing word there, not σκεῦος itself. A vessel *of choice* and a vessel *of creatures* are not obviously one image.

*Verdict.* **Moderate, and no higher.** The juxtaposition is `[T]` and genuinely striking in context; the design is `[I]` and uncorroborated. The honest form of the claim is the one the count supports: *within three chapters Luke names two vessels, one a man commissioned to the Gentiles and one a sheet full of what Gentiles eat* — and leaves the reader to notice. The sweep's rating stands; this run has not raised it.

**δεκτός ("acceptable"), 10:35** `[T]`, high

Five verses in the New Testament: **Luke 4:19, Luke 4:24, Acts 10:35, 2 Corinthians 6:2, Philippians 4:18** (SBLGNT). Two things follow.

(a) **In Swete the word is overwhelmingly cultic** — the technical term by which an offering is declared fit: *δεκτὸν αὐτῷ ἐναντίον Κυρίου* of the burnt offering (Leviticus 1:3), again at 1:4, 17:4, 22:19, 22:20, 22:29, 23:11; the firstfruits sheaf *δεκτὸν ὑμῖν* (23:11); and negatively at Jeremiah 6:20 and Malachi 2:13, where offerings are *not* acceptable. `[T]` **Twice in Isaiah it is used of foreigners**: *τὰ ὁλοκαυτώματα αὐτῶν καὶ αἱ θυσίαι αὐτῶν ἔσονται **δεκταί** ἐπὶ τὸ θυσιαστήριόν μου* (56:7, the house of prayer for all nations) and *ἀνενεχθήσεται **δεκτά** ἐπὶ τὸ θυσιαστήριόν μου* of Kedar's flocks (60:7). So when Peter says a God-fearer in any nation is δεκτός, he is using the altar's word. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, that Peter's usage carries the cultic sense.

(b) **Luke's two Gospel uses are the Nazareth sermon** — the *acceptable* year of the Lord (4:19, citing Isaiah 61:2) and *no prophet is acceptable in his own country* (4:24), whose two illustrations are the widow of Sidon and Naaman the Syrian. See Tool 11 Move 4 and Headline Finding 1.

**μνημόσυνον ("memorial"), 10:4** `[T]`, high

Three verses in the New Testament: Matthew 26:13, Mark 14:9, **Acts 10:4** (SBLGNT). In the Gospels it means "in memory of her"; **only here does it carry the Septuagint's cultic sense.** In Swete that sense is precise: the **memorial portion** of the grain offering which the priest burns on the altar (Leviticus 2:2, 2:9, 2:16, 5:12), and the engraved stones on the high priest's shoulders and breastpiece, *μνημόσυνον ἔναντι τοῦ θεοῦ* when he enters the holy place (Exodus 28:12, 28:23), and the atonement silver (Exodus 30:16). `[T]`

Two refinements, both from the corpus rather than from recall. First, **the Greek Old Testament never joins ἀναβαίνω ("to go up") to μνημόσυνον**: the collocation *ἀνέβησαν εἰς μνημόσυνον* is Luke's own, and it joins the ascent of sacrificial smoke to the priestly memorial. `[T]`, with the positive control run (μνημόσυνον with "Lord" or "God" returns twenty-five Swete verses, so the search reaches the word). Second, **the one Greek Old Testament text where a memorial *of prayer* is brought before God is Tobit 12:12** — *ἐγὼ προσήγαγον τὸ μνημόσυνον τῆς προσευχῆς ὑμῶν ἐνώπιον τοῦ ἁγίου* — in both of Swete's Tobit texts. `[T]` Tobit is the story of a pious Jew in exile whose piety is prayer, almsgiving and burying the dead; three verses earlier, **Tobit 12:9** says *ἐλεημοσύνη … ἀποκαθαριεῖ πᾶσαν ἁμαρτίαν* ("almsgiving … cleanses away every sin"). See Tool 11, register.

**ἀλλόφυλος ("of another race"), 10:28** `[T]`, high

**The only occurrence in the New Testament** (SBLGNT). In Swete it stands in **245 verses, 27 of them in Judges** — because it is the Septuagint's standing word for **the Philistines**. `[T]` (The whole-Swete figure is a verse count on a prefix search; it is a floor, not a ceiling, and the Judges figure is what makes the point.) The word reaches well beyond Judges — Psalms, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Amos, Obadiah, Zephaniah, Zechariah, Joel, Judith — but its dominant referent is Israel's archetypal uncircumcised enemy.

So the word Peter chooses, standing inside a Gentile house, is not "Gentile" (ἔθνος, which he uses at 10:35 and which Luke uses at 11:1 and 11:18) but **the Septuagint's word for Philistine** — and the man in front of him is an officer of an occupying army. *Moderate–high* on the register; the lexeme and its distribution are `[T]`, the rhetorical force is `[I]`.

**ἀθέμιτος ("unlawful"), 10:28** `[T]`

Two verses in the New Testament: Acts 10:28 and **1 Peter 4:3** (SBLGNT), where it describes the Gentile life the readers have left. Worth noting for its own sake: the word by which Peter describes the barrier is the word Peter's letter uses of what lies on the far side of it. Note also that Peter does not say the Torah forbids this; he says *ὑμεῖς ἐπίστασθε ὡς ἀθέμιτόν ἐστιν* — "you know how out-of-bounds it is." He is describing a custom, accurately, without citing a text. `[T]`

**προσωπολήμπτης ("one who receives faces"), 10:34** `[T]`, high

The agent-noun stands **once in the Greek Bible** — here (verified across Swete and the SBLGNT, with the positive control run: the πρόσωπ- pattern returns 1,192 Swete verses, so the nil is an absence and not a broken search). The wider family is **New Testament only**: προσωπολημψία at Romans 2:11, Ephesians 6:9, Colossians 3:25, James 2:1; προσωπολημπτέω at James 2:9; ἀπροσωπολήμπτως at **1 Peter 1:17**. Eight verses, and Acts 10:34 is the only one in a narrative. `[T]`

What the family compresses is a **Septuagint judicial idiom**: *οὐ λήμψῃ πρόσωπον πτωχοῦ οὐδὲ θαυμάσεις πρόσωπον δυνάστου, ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ κρινεῖς τὸν πλησίον σου* (Leviticus 19:15); *οὐκ ἐπιγνώσονται πρόσωπον, οὐδὲ λήμψονται δῶρον* (Deuteronomy 16:19); Sirach 32:16. `[T]` The idiom's home is **the lawcourt**, which is why it sits so well with διακρίνω: Peter's first sentence about God is a sentence about **judicial impartiality**, and the question the chapters settle is a question of jurisdiction. *Whether the noun is Luke's coinage or current Hellenistic-Jewish Greek the corpus cannot say* — a Logos question.

### 8. Translations

The study text and the pulpit text agree through most of the unit. Four divergences bear on findings, and one of them is the most consequential translation issue in the passage.

**(a) 10:14, 10:15, 10:28; 11:8, 11:9 — κοινός: "unholy" (NASB95) against "common" (ESV).** `[T]`

NASB95 renders κοινόν as "unholy" throughout; the ESV renders it "common" and reserves "unclean" for ἀκάθαρτον. **The ESV is closer.** κοινός does not mean "unholy"; it means *common*, in the extended sense of *not set apart* — and the opposite Luke supplies is not ἅγιος ("holy") but καθαρός ("clean"). NASB95's "unholy" also collapses the distinction between the pair's two members, so that *κοινὸν καὶ ἀκάθαρτον* reads as near-synonymous where the Greek has two categories. **What the congregation will hear:** with the ESV, "common" — which will puzzle them, and the puzzlement is useful, because it is the word's strangeness that makes 10:28's leap to *μηδένα … ἄνθρωπον* audible. With NASB95, "unholy", which sounds like a moral verdict on the creatures and leads the hearer away from the purity system and toward sin.

**(b) 10:20, 11:2, 11:12 and 15:9 — διακρίνω: a four-verse chain that both versions break.** `[T]`

| Verse | Greek | NASB95 | ESV |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10:20 | μηδὲν διακρινόμενος | "without misgivings" | "without hesitation" |
| 11:2 | διεκρίνοντο πρὸς αὐτόν | "took issue with him" | "criticized him" |
| 11:12 | μηδὲν διακρίναντα | "without misgivings" | "making no distinction" |
| 15:9 | οὐδὲν διέκρινεν | "made no distinction" | "made no distinction" |

**Four occurrences, one lemma, four different English words in NASB95 and four in the ESV.** Neither version lets an English reader see that the word God uses of himself at 15:9 is the word he used to Peter at 10:20, or that the circumcision party's objection at 11:2 is made in the same verb. The ESV gets 11:12 and 15:9 right and loses 10:20; NASB95 gets 15:9 right and loses the other three. **This is the overview's honesty note 6 exactly** — *check the repetition in both versions wherever a chain is load-bearing, and expect either of them to break it* — and it is now a seventh instance. **What the congregation will hear:** four unrelated ideas (hesitation, criticism, distinction, distinction) where Luke wrote one. The preacher who wants the chain must give it to them in so many words.

**(c) 10:39 — ἐπὶ ξύλου: "on a cross" (NASB95) against "on a tree" (ESV).** `[T]`

**The ESV is right and the difference is load-bearing.** ξύλον is "wood, tree", and *κρεμάσαντες ἐπὶ ξύλου* is the formula of **Deuteronomy 21:22–23** — *κεκαταραμένος ὑπὸ θεοῦ πᾶς κρεμάμενος ἐπὶ ξύλου* ("cursed by God is everyone hanged on a tree"). In the New Testament the participle-plus-phrase *κρεμάσαντες ἐπὶ ξύλου* stands in **two verses, Acts 5:30 and 10:39**, both Peter, with Galatians 3:13 quoting the Deuteronomy verse itself. `[T]` NASB95's "on a cross" translates the referent and deletes the citation: an English hearer cannot hear the curse-formula in it. **What the congregation will hear:** with the ESV, "on a tree" — the Deuteronomic word, available to the preacher. With NASB95, a Roman execution and no Torah.

**(d) 10:35 — δεκτός: "welcome to Him" (NASB95) against "acceptable to him" (ESV).** `[T]`

**The ESV is closer**, and here the pulpit text is the better text. δεκτός is the altar's word (Tool 7), and "acceptable" keeps the register; "welcome" is warmer and less precise, and it loses the sacrificial overtone on which Headline Finding 2 rests. **What the congregation will hear:** with the ESV, "acceptable", which the preacher can open up into Leviticus and Isaiah 56. With NASB95, hospitality language, which quietly turns a verdict about an offering into a statement about God's friendliness.

**One place where both versions lose the same thing.** At **10:26** both render *ἤγειρεν αὐτόν* as "raised him up" / "lifted him up", which is correct — but ἐγείρω is also the verb of **10:40**, *τοῦτον ὁ θεὸς ἤγειρεν*, "God raised him". The identical form ἤγειρεν stands twice in the chapter (ἐγείρω is twelve verses in Acts; SBLGNT). *Moderate*, since ἐγείρω is common and English has no way to carry both senses; but it is worth the preacher's knowing that Peter's lifting of Cornelius off the floor and God's raising of Jesus are, in Luke's Greek, the same word in the same form, fourteen verses apart.

**Ancient versions check.** Where the moderns agree on something weighty, the question is whether the ancient versions diverge. **Considered, and it resolves into the apparatus rather than into a translation-tradition split** — this is a New Testament passage, so the Greek *is* the base text and the Latin and Syriac witnesses are part of the apparatus evidence reported under Textual Variants below. The one item worth naming here: at **10:30** the Latin and Syriac traditions, with a wide Greek constituency, read *νηστεύων* ("fasting") where NA28 does not, so a reader of the Vulgate meets a fasting Cornelius and a reader of the ESV does not. Reported under Textual Variants. No `**Translation-tradition split:**` is claimed.

**Pulpit divergence note.** **The ESV is the declared pulpit text, and in this passage it is the better witness at three of the four divergences that matter** — "common" at 10:14, "on a tree" at 10:39, "acceptable" at 10:35 — while losing the διακρίνω chain at 10:20, where NASB95 loses it too. The preacher reading the ESV therefore has the harder words available to him and should use them rather than smoothing them.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Two registers, and the join between them is the passage's emotional event.**

The first is **ceremonious**. Scenes 1, 4 and 5 are full of formal courtesies: a named officer, a cohort named, a household summoned, relatives and *ἀναγκαίους φίλους* ("close friends") assembled, a man prostrate, a speech begun with *ἀνοίξας … τὸ στόμα* ("opening his mouth"), and Cornelius's *σύ τε καλῶς ἐποίησας παραγενόμενος* ("and you have been kind enough to come"). Luke is staging a formal reception. `[T]`

The second is **comic, and the comedy is at Peter's expense**. He is hungry; he sees food; he refuses it; he is corrected; the same thing happens twice more; the sheet goes back up; he is left standing on a roof *διηπόρει* ("thoroughly at a loss", 10:17) with a riddle and no answer, and while he is still puzzling, the answer knocks at the gate. A man who has just raised the dead (9:40) cannot work out a picture of lunch. *Moderate–high*: Luke does not tell the reader to smile, but the pacing — three refusals, an unexplained withdrawal, and an interruption — is the pacing of a joke, and the device (a prophet told to eat something he will not eat, arguing with God) is one the Old Testament has used before (Ezekiel 4:14, where the prophet's protest is almost word for word Peter's: *μηδαμῶς, κύριε … οὐδέποτε … πᾶν ἀκάθαρτον*). `[T]` on the Ezekiel wording; see Tool 11.

**The join.** At 10:44 the register changes without warning: *ἔτι λαλοῦντος τοῦ Πέτρου* — "while Peter was still speaking". The sermon is **interrupted**. Luke does not let him finish. The formal reception and the comedy of the roof both break off into something neither party controls, and the next sound in the room is Gentiles speaking in tongues and magnifying God. `[T]`

**Soundtrack.** A courtly processional for the first half, with a solo woodwind playing something slightly absurd over the rooftop scene — and then, at 10:44, the orchestra coming in early, over the top of the soloist, before he has finished his phrase. The preacher should let 10:44 cut in.

### 10. Repetition

Every count below is by lemma against the SBLGNT, and every claimed reference has been confirmed to contain the word.

**(a) The purity pair, five times.** κοινός ("common") at 10:14, 10:28, 11:8 and ἀκάθαρτος ("unclean") at 10:14, 10:28, 11:8 — **the two lemmas stand together in three verses of the whole New Testament, and all three are here** (10:14, 10:28, 11:8). `[T]`, high. And in all three, κοινός comes first. The pair's three occurrences mark the three stages: Peter's refusal, Peter's conclusion about *people*, Peter's report.

**(b) "Rise", seven times in two and a half chapters.** The direct second-person command *ἀνάστηθι* (with its by-form *ἀνάστα*) stands in **nine verses of the New Testament, eight of them in Acts** — 8:26, 9:6, 9:34, 9:40, 10:26, 12:7, 14:10, 26:16, the ninth being Ephesians 5:14. `[T]`, verified on the word-form against the SBLGNT index. **Five of the eight fall between 8:26 and 10:26**: to Philip, to Saul, to a paralytic, to a corpse, and to Cornelius. Add the participial *ἀναστάς* addressed to Peter at 10:13 and 10:20 (and recalled at 11:7), and the sequence runs: Peter tells a paralysed man to rise (9:34), tells a dead woman to rise (9:40), is himself twice told to rise (10:13, 10:20), and then tells a prostrate Gentile to rise (10:26). *Moderate–high* on the design; `[T]` on the distribution.

**(c) ξενίζω ("to be a guest; to entertain"), four times here of seven in Acts.** 10:6, 10:18, 10:23, 10:32, with 17:20, 21:16 and 28:7 elsewhere. `[T]` The force is in the voice-change: at 10:6, 10:18 and 10:32 Peter is the one *being lodged* in another man's house, and at **10:23 he becomes the host** — *εἰσκαλεσάμενος οὖν αὐτοὺς ἐξένισεν* ("so he invited them in and lodged them"). Luke marks the turn with the same verb. The man who will be accused of entering a Gentile house has first taken Gentile messengers into his own lodging. *Moderate–high.*

**(d) ἐλεημοσύνη ("alms"), three times of Cornelius.** 10:2, 10:4, 10:31. The lemma stands in **thirteen verses of the New Testament, eight of them in Acts** — 3:2, 3:3, 3:10, 9:36, 10:2, 10:4, 10:31, 24:17 (SBLGNT). `[T]` Two neighbours matter: **9:36**, where Tabitha is *πλήρης ἔργων ἀγαθῶν καὶ ἐλεημοσυνῶν* in the immediately preceding pericope, and **Luke 11:41** and **12:33**, the Gospel's only two. **Cornelius's alms are the only alms in Acts that God is said to have received.** `[T]`

**(e) φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν ("fearing God"), three times.** 10:2 (Luke's own verdict), 10:22 (the messengers' report), 10:35 (Peter's principle). The phrase travels from narrator to characters to apostolic doctrine, which is Luke certifying it at three levels. `[T]` And **ἀνὴρ δίκαιος καὶ φοβούμενος (10:22) and φοβούμενος … καὶ ἐργαζόμενος δικαιοσύνην (10:35) are the only two New Testament verses joining righteousness-vocabulary to the God-fearing participle** `[T]` — so Luke states the principle in 10:35 in the words the messengers had used of the man in 10:22.

**(f) Three times, and the number is counted twice.** *ἐπὶ τρίς* at 10:16 and 11:10, the only two New Testament occurrences. The adverb **τρίς** stands in ten New Testament verses, and in Luke–Acts in exactly four: **Luke 22:34, Luke 22:61, Acts 10:16, Acts 11:10** `[T]` — twice of Peter's denial and twice of Peter's vision, and nowhere else in the two volumes. The sweep rated the denial connection `[I]`, moderate, and suggested the threefold *restoration* as the middle term; **that half must be withdrawn, because the threefold restoration is John 21 and is not in Luke–Acts.** What stands instead is stronger and is worked under Tool 11 Move 4: the *remembering* formula.

**(g) The hinge-root used at both ends.** *ἔκστασις* ("trance") falls on Peter at 10:10 and is recalled at 11:5; *ἐξέστησαν* ("they were amazed") is what happens to the circumcised believers at 10:45. Same root, opposite ends of the chapter, different parties: the apostle is put out of himself to be taught, and the onlookers are put out of themselves by what they then see. `[T]` on the forms; *moderate* on the design. ἔκστασις is four verses in Acts (3:10, 10:10, 11:5, 22:17) and ἐξίστημι eight (2:7, 2:12, 8:9, 8:11, 8:13, 9:21, 10:45, 12:16).

**(h) ἀτενίζω ("to gaze intently"), twice.** Cornelius at the angel (10:4) and Peter into the sheet (11:6). Fourteen New Testament verses, ten of them in Acts (SBLGNT). `[T]` Both revelations are received by a man **staring**, which is Luke's habitual verb for a person taking in something from heaven (1:10 and 7:55 both gaze *εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν*).


### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**There is no formula-citation in these sixty-six verses.** Peter's nearest approach is 10:43, *τούτῳ πάντες οἱ προφῆται μαρτυροῦσιν* ("to him all the prophets bear witness") — a summary appeal to the prophetic corpus without a text. The overview's honesty note 3 therefore governs the whole tool: *absence of a citation formula is not absence of a source*, and the searches below were run against the Greek text rather than against formulae.

**Book-level source inventory for Move 2.** The overview's live sources, in rank order, are Luke's own Gospel, Isaiah (twelve uses across nine texts), the Psalter, Exodus, Deuteronomy, Genesis, Amos, Ezekiel, Joel, the Elijah–Elisha cycle and Numbers. **The intertextual map has no row for any verse in 10:1–11:18.** Everything below is therefore new to the map, except where credited to the sweep or to another run.

**A correction to this run's own work, recorded rather than quietly fixed.** An earlier pass of this report claimed that κοινός never carries the ritual sense in the Greek Old Testament and that the Septuagint's word for profane is only βέβηλος. **That claim was false**, and the error was mine: the search reached Swete correctly and returned sixty-six verses of the κοιν- family, but the filter I applied to its output looked for a book pattern that does not match Swete's label for Maccabees, so the two decisive verses were silently dropped. The corrected finding is at **C** below, and it is a better finding than the one it replaces. **The method lesson is general and worth carrying: a positive control that tests the search does not test the filter applied to the search's output. Control the pipeline that actually produced the claim.**

**A. Leviticus 11 → 10:11–16; 11:5–10** *(moderate–high — register, not citation)* `[T]` on the lexical overlap; `[I]` on the register claim

*Source context.* Leviticus 11 is the clean-and-unclean food law, and its closing verse states its own purpose: *διαστεῖλαι ἀνὰ μέσον τῶν ἀκαθάρτων καὶ ἀνὰ μέσον τῶν καθαρῶν* ("to distinguish between the unclean and the clean", 11:47). Three features of the chapter matter here. (i) It is **the one chapter in the Torah that joins σκεῦος ("vessel") to ἀκάθαρτος, ἑρπετά and πετεινά** `[T]`, and its ruling at 11:32–33 is about what happens to a vessel: one defiled by such creatures is washed and is clean at evening, while an earthen one *συντριβήσεται* — "shall be broken". (ii) **ἐπιπίπτω ("to fall upon") stands in Leviticus in three verses, all in chapter 11** (11:32, 11:37, 11:38), always of a dead unclean creature falling on something and defiling it `[T]`. (iii) The chapter's own class-terms are **κτήνη** ("livestock"), πετεινά, ἑρπετὰ τῶν πετεινῶν and ἑρπετὰ τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς — **not τετράποδα**, which Leviticus uses only three times and twice of the *unclean* quadrupeds specifically (7:11, 27:27, with 20:15) `[T]`.

*Book usage.* Leviticus is not in the overview's map at all, and this is its first appearance in a solo Acts run. But the vocabulary runs on within Acts: ἀκάθαρτος stands in five verses (5:16, 8:7, 10:14, 10:28, 11:8) and καθαρίζω in three (10:15, 11:9, **15:9**). `[T]` `[S: overview]` for the chain to 15:9 and 21:28, which the echo table already carries.

*OT-to-OT.* Leviticus 11:47's purpose-verb is **διαστέλλω**, and Leviticus 20:25's is **ἀφορίζω** — neither is διακρίνω. So the Acts chain (Tool 7, Tool 8) is **not** a verbal pick-up of Leviticus's own distinguishing-word; it is the same conceptual act in a different and judicial verb. That distinction should be kept: a preacher may say Acts settles what Leviticus 11 was for, but not that Acts quotes Leviticus 11's word for it.

*What it adds.* Three things, and the third is the one worth preaching. First, the **vessel** is not incidental scene-dressing: Peter sees the one object Leviticus 11 legislates about, full of the creatures Leviticus 11 legislates against. Second, in Leviticus the one who *cleanses* is the priest — *καθαριεῖ αὐτὸν ὁ ἱερεύς*, over and over through chapters 13 and 14 `[T]` — whereas Acts 10:15 says *ἃ ὁ **θεὸς** ἐκαθάρισεν*. **God has taken the priest's grammatical place.** Third, Leviticus 11's verb for defiling contact is the verb Acts uses for the Spirit's descent; see Headline Finding 3.

**B. Ezekiel 4:13–14 → 10:14; 11:8** *(moderate–high — inverted echo)* `[T]` on the wording; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the design

This is, on the verbal evidence, the strongest single Old Testament link in the pericope, and it is new to the project.

*The wording.* Ezekiel 4:14 (Swete): *καὶ εἶπα **Μηδαμῶς, Κύριε** θεὲ τοῦ Ἰσραήλ· εἰ ἡ ψυχή μου οὐ μεμίανται ἐν **ἀκαθαρσίᾳ**, καὶ θηριάλωτον καὶ θνησιμαῖον οὐ βέβρωκα ἀπὸ γενέσεώς μου ἕως τοῦ νῦν, **οὐδὲ εἰσελήλυθεν εἰς τὸ στόμα μου** πᾶν κρέας ἕωλον.* Acts 11:8: ***Μηδαμῶς, κύριε**, ὅτι **κοινὸν ἢ ἀκάθαρτον** οὐδέποτε **εἰσῆλθεν εἰς τὸ στόμα μου**.*

Four elements are shared, in the same order: the interjection **μηδαμῶς** with the vocative **κύριε**; a lifelong negation of eating; the root **ἀκαθαρ-**; and the phrase **εἰς τὸ στόμα μου**. The rarity is checkable and tight. **μηδαμῶς stands in nineteen verses of Swete and two of the New Testament, and the two are Acts 10:14 and 11:8** `[T]`. **The phrase εἰς τὸ στόμα μου with a word of the ἀκαθαρ- family in the same verse stands in exactly two verses of the whole Greek Bible — Ezekiel 4:14 and Acts 11:8** `[T]`.

*Source context.* Ezekiel 4 is the sign-act of the siege. God commands the prophet to bake his bread over human dung, and gives the reason in the verse immediately before the protest: *Οὕτως φάγονται οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ Ἰσραὴλ **ἀκάθαρτα ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν*** — "Thus shall the sons of Israel eat unclean things **among the Gentiles**" (4:13) `[T]`. The prophet protests, and **God concedes**: *Ἰδοὺ δέδωκά σοι βόλβιτα βοῶν ἀντὶ τῶν βολβίτων τῶν ἀνθρωπίνων* (4:15). The protest is granted; the sign is softened. Within Ezekiel the scene's function is to make the coming exile *tangible* on the prophet's own body, and the thing that makes it unbearable is precisely eating unclean among the nations.

*Book usage.* Ezekiel is a live source in the overview, raised from one row to seven at v2.2 — chapter 34 and 22:27 behind the Miletus speech, and 13:10–15 named behind 23:3. `[S: overview]` **Ezekiel 4 is new.** And the three uses are not random: Ezekiel supplies Acts with its shepherd language, its whitewashed-wall image and now its unclean-food protest — in each case a passage where the prophet's own body or speech carries the sign.

*OT-to-OT.* Ezekiel 4:13's *ἀκάθαρτα ἐν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* is itself the covenant-curse tradition of Deuteronomy 28 and Hosea 9:3–4 ("they shall eat unclean bread in Assyria"), and Leviticus 11 is the law the curse threatens to break. So the Ezekiel protest already stands downstream of **A**: it is the prophet refusing to enact the undoing of Leviticus 11.

*What it adds, and why "inverted echo" is the right category.* The overview's four-way table (honesty note 4) sets the test for an inverted echo: *does the later text realise what the source negated, or negate what the source realised?* Here the shape matches and the **outcome is reversed**. Ezekiel protests and **God yields**; Peter protests in Ezekiel's words and **God does not yield** — the command is repeated *ἐπὶ τρίς* and the refusal is answered by *ἃ ὁ θεὸς ἐκαθάρισεν*. And the reversal runs the whole way down: in Ezekiel, eating unclean among the nations is the **curse of exile**; in Acts, it is the **means of the mission**. The thing Ezekiel could not bear to signify is the thing Peter is sent to do.

*Confidence, stated honestly.* `[T]` on the two distribution facts. `[I]`, **moderate–high**, that Luke intends the echo. Against it: μηδαμῶς with a vocative is a natural Septuagintal idiom of refusal (Jonah 1:14, 1 Samuel 24:7, Genesis 18:25 all have it), and *εἰς τὸ στόμα μου* with a negated eating-verb also stands at Daniel 10:3 in both Greek texts, where it is a fast and carries no purity question. For it: **no other Greek Bible text joins all four elements**, the shared subject-matter is exact rather than approximate, and an echo of a *prophet's refused protest about unclean food* is precisely what a scene about a prophet's refused protest about unclean food would reach for. **This is the project's first candidate for a new intertextual-map row in these chapters, and it should be put to Logos before it is preached as established.**

**C. 1 Maccabees 1:47, 1:62–63 → 10:14; 11:8** *(high on the lexical fact; moderate–high on the register)* `[T]`

*Source context.* **κοινός in the ritual sense stands in the Greek Old Testament in two verses, both in 1 Maccabees**: Antiochus's decree *θύειν ὕεια καὶ **κτήνη κοινά*** ("to sacrifice swine and profane animals", 1:47), and the resistance — *καὶ ὀχυρώθησαν ἐν αὑτοῖς **τοῦ μὴ φαγεῖν κοινά**· καὶ ἐπεδέξαντο ἀποθανεῖν, ἵνα μὴ μιανθῶσιν τοῖς βρώμασιν … καὶ ἀπέθανον* ("and they stood firm **not to eat profane things**, and chose to die rather than be defiled by foods … and they died", 1:62–63) `[T]`. The Torah's own word for profane is **βέβηλος**, which stands in 106 verses of Swete `[T]`; κοινός in this sense is a **Maccabean** development.

*Book usage.* Not in the overview's map. Within Acts, see Tool 7 for the adjective's five verses.

*What it adds.* It changes the emotional weight of Peter's sentence entirely. *οὐδέποτε ἔφαγον πᾶν κοινόν* is not scruple or squeamishness; **it is the martyrs' confession, in the martyrs' word.** The men of 1 Maccabees 1:62 held out *τοῦ μὴ φαγεῖν κοινά* and were killed for it, within living memory of Luke's readers. Peter is standing where they stood, saying what they said — and being told to stop. Any sermon that treats Peter's refusal as mere narrowness has missed what the word cost the people who coined it. The same register stands behind 4 Maccabees 7:6, where Eleazar the priest *οὐκ ἐμίανας τοὺς ἱεροὺς ὀδόντας* — "did not defile your sacred teeth".

**D. Genesis 1:24–26 and 9:1–3 → 10:12–13; 11:6** *(moderate)* `[T]` on the vocabulary; `[I]` on the allusion

*The vocabulary.* **τετράποδα with ἑρπετά and πετεινά in one verse stands in three verses of the whole Greek Bible — Acts 10:12, Acts 11:6 and Romans 1:23 — and in no verse of Swete** `[T]`, with the positive control run (the τετραποδ- pattern returns fifteen Swete verses). But the classes themselves are Genesis's: **Genesis 1:24** reads *τετράποδα καὶ ἑρπετὰ καὶ θηρία τῆς γῆς* — three of the four classes Peter lists at 11:6 — and Genesis 1:26, 1:28 and 1:30 supply *τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ* `[T]`. **Acts 11:6's four classes are Genesis 1:24's three plus Genesis 1's birds.** And τετράποδα, which is not Leviticus 11's class-word, *is* Genesis 1:24's.

*Source context.* Two Genesis texts are candidates and they are not rivals but stages. **Genesis 1:24–30** is the creation of the land animals and the birds, with the mandate to rule them. **Genesis 9:1–4** is the Noachic grant after the flood: *Αὐξάνεσθε καὶ πληθύνεσθε* (9:1), the creatures given *ὑπὸ χεῖρας ὑμῖν* (9:2), *καὶ πᾶν ἑρπετὸν ὅ ἐστιν ζῶν ὑμῖν ἔσται **εἰς βρῶσιν**· ὡς λάχανα χόρτου ἔδωκα ὑμῖν τὰ πάντα* (9:3) — every living creature given as food — with one reservation: *πλὴν κρέας ἐν αἵματι ψυχῆς οὐ φάγεσθε* (9:4). Note also that the clean/unclean distinction already exists in Genesis, at the ark: *τῶν κτηνῶν τῶν καθαρῶν … τῶν κτηνῶν τῶν μὴ καθαρῶν* (7:2–3) `[T]`.

*Book usage.* **Genesis 9:1 is already in the overview's map**, as the creation blessing standing behind Exodus 1:7 and so behind the four growth-notices. `[S: overview]` So this is a **second, independent use of Genesis 9 in Acts**, and the two are not in tension: the growth-notices take the multiply-and-fill half, and the vision takes the eat-everything half, of the same divine speech.

*OT-to-OT.* Genesis 9:1 reworks Genesis 1:28 verbatim (*αὐξάνεσθε καὶ πληθύνεσθε καὶ πληρώσατε τὴν γῆν*), so the Noachic grant is already a re-issue of the creation mandate within Genesis. A reader who hears Genesis 9 here hears Genesis 1 through it.

*What it adds.* The sheet does not hold Leviticus's menu; **it holds Genesis's creation, in Genesis's words**, with the Noachic grant of all of it as food standing over it. That is why the voice says *πάντα* and why Peter's objection is answered by an appeal to what God has done rather than to what the law permits: the ground of the command is creation and the Noachic grant, which are older and wider than Sinai. *Moderate, and deliberately not higher:* the verbal volume is three class-nouns and the word *πᾶν*, the command's own verbs (θῦσον καὶ φάγε) do not match Genesis 9:3's (*ἔσται εἰς βρῶσιν*), and no source in the library has been consulted.

**A note on where this does *not* go.** It is tempting to run Genesis 9:4's blood reservation straight into the Jerusalem decree's *αἷμα* and *πνικτόν* (15:20, 15:29; 21:25). **That ground is already taken and settled the other way:** the project's own claim audit on Acts 15 tested the proposal that the four abstentions answer the sojourner laws of **Leviticus 17–18** and returned *Confirmed with nuance*, with Genesis 9:4 serving as one of its controls. `[S: Acts 15 claim audit]` The audit's decisive evidence was that "the sojourner in your midst" stands in exactly the five Leviticus verses carrying those four laws. So the Noachic connection belongs here, at 10:12–13, as a statement about the **grant**; the decree's source is Leviticus 17, and this run does not reopen it.

**E. Deuteronomy 12:15, 12:21 → 10:13; 11:7** *(moderate — register)* `[T]` on the collocation

*The collocation.* **θύω in the second person with ἐσθίω in the same verse stands in three verses of Swete, all in Deuteronomy — 12:15, 12:21 and 27:7** `[T]`. The first two are the permission for **profane slaughter away from the sanctuary**: *ἐν πάσῃ ἐπιθυμίᾳ σου **θύσεις καὶ φάγῃ** κρέα … ἐν πάσῃ πόλει· **ὁ ἀκάθαρτος ἐν σοὶ καὶ ὁ καθαρὸς** ἐπὶ τὸ αὐτὸ φάγεται αὐτό* (12:15), restated at 12:21–22. The third (27:7) is the altar in the land.

*Source context.* Deuteronomy 12 is the centralisation law, and 12:15 is its concession: now that the altar is far away, you may slaughter and eat at home, and — the clause that matters — **the unclean and the clean among you shall eat it together.**

*What it adds.* The voice's two imperatives are, in Septuagintal Greek, the formula of a permission rather than a cultic command: *θῦσον καὶ φάγε* is Deuteronomy's "slaughter and eat". And the one place in the Torah where a text says in so many words that the unclean and the clean eat **at the same table** is this one. *Moderate:* the Acts forms are imperatives where Deuteronomy's are futures, and the collocation is natural Greek for any permission to eat meat; what makes it worth reporting is that the collocation is **confined to Deuteronomy** in the Greek Old Testament, and that its content is table-fellowship across the purity line — which is the pericope's subject.

**A candidate that does not reach the threshold, recorded so it is not re-proposed as a finding.** The aorist imperative **θῦσον** stands in three verses of the whole Greek Bible — **Psalm 49:14** (Swete), Acts 10:13 and 11:7 `[T]` — and Psalm 49 is the psalm that denies God eats flesh or drinks blood and says *ἐὰν πεινάσω, οὐ μή σοι εἴπω* ("if I were hungry, I would not tell you", 49:12), which would sit arrestingly against Peter's hunger at 10:10. **The objection is decisive and comes first: the rarity is an artefact of how seldom anyone is commanded to sacrifice in the second person singular, not of any distinctiveness in the word.** Recorded under Open Questions, not as a finding.

**F. Psalm 14:2 in Swete (English Psalm 15:2) → 10:35** *(high on the phrase; moderate–high on the allusion)* `[T]`

*The phrase.* **ἐργαζόμενος δικαιοσύνην stands in two verses of the whole Greek Bible: Psalm 14:2 in Swete's numbering and Acts 10:35** `[T]`, verified by ordered phrase search. The wider pairing of the two lemmas in one verse gives seven verses across Swete and the New Testament — Psalm 14:2, Zephaniah 2:3, 1 Enoch 10:18, Acts 10:35, Romans 4:5, Hebrews 11:33, James 1:20 — **but in that exact participial form, two.**

*Source context.* Psalm 14 in Swete (Psalm 15 in English) is the **entrance psalm**. It opens *Κύριε, τίς παροικήσει ἐν τῷ σκηνώματί σου; καὶ τίς κατασκηνώσει ἐν τῷ ὄρει τῷ ἁγίῳ σου;* — "Lord, who shall sojourn in your tent, and who shall dwell on your holy hill?" The answer, in v.2, is the verse Peter uses: *πορευόμενος ἄμωμος **καὶ ἐργαζόμενος δικαιοσύνην**, λαλῶν ἀλήθειαν ἐν καρδίᾳ αὐτοῦ*. The psalm's function in its own setting is to state the **conditions of admission to the sanctuary** — a liturgy of the gate.

*Book usage.* The Psalter is one of the overview's live sources, with six uses across five psalms (Psalms 16, 110, 2, 118, 69/109), and Psalm 16 is the one text Luke gives to both Peter and Paul in the same role. `[S: overview]` **Psalm 14/15 is new.** And it belongs to a different kind of psalm from all the others: every psalm in the map so far is messianic or imprecatory, used to argue about Jesus or about an office. This one is used to answer a question about **who may come in**.

*OT-to-OT.* Psalm 14/15's question is Isaiah 56's question, and Isaiah 56 answers it of **foreigners** — *τὰ ὁλοκαυτώματα αὐτῶν … ἔσονται δεκταί ἐπὶ τὸ θυσιαστήριόν μου· ὁ γάρ οἶκός μου οἶκος προσευχῆς κληθήσεται πᾶσιν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* (56:7). So the psalm's entrance-condition and the prophet's admission of foreigners are already in conversation in the Greek Old Testament, and **δεκτός is the word that joins them** (see **G**). Acts 10:35 holds both halves in one sentence: *ἐν παντὶ ἔθνει* answers Isaiah 56, and *ἐργαζόμενος δικαιοσύνην* answers Psalm 14:2.

*What it adds.* This is the most precise thing in the sermon. Peter is asked, in effect, who may be admitted; he answers in the words of **the psalm that exists to answer that question about the temple**, and he removes the psalm's one unstated assumption — that the person asking is an Israelite — by prefixing *ἐν παντὶ ἔθνει*. **Peter answers the gate-liturgy of the temple with "in every nation."** *Moderate–high rather than high on the allusion*, for one reason only: no secondary source has been consulted, and the phrase could in principle be stock piety. The exclusivity of the form is `[T]` and is what carries it.

**G. Sirach 32:9 in Swete, with Tobit 12:9 and 12:12 → 10:2, 10:4, 10:22, 10:31, 10:35** *(moderate–high — register)* `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]` on the register

*The distributions.* **δεκτός with μνημόσυνον in one verse stands in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Bible — Sirach 32:9 in Swete's numbering** `[T]`: *θυσία ἀνδρὸς **δικαίου δεκτή**, καὶ τὸ **μνημόσυνον** αὐτῆς οὐκ ἐπιλησθήσεται* ("the sacrifice of a **righteous man** is **acceptable**, and its **memorial** shall not be forgotten"). The verse before it reads *προσφορὰ δικαίου λιπαίνει θυσιαστήριον, καὶ ἡ εὐωδία αὐτῆς ἔναντι Ὑψίστου* (32:8). **And ἐλεημοσύνη with a word of the καθαρ- family in one verse stands in two verses of the whole Greek Bible — Tobit 12:9 and Luke 11:41** `[T]`; Tobit 12:9 reads *ἐλεημοσύνη … **ἀποκαθαριεῖ** πᾶσαν ἁμαρτίαν*, and three verses later **Tobit 12:12** is the Greek Old Testament's only *memorial of prayer brought before God*.

*Numbering caution, stated because the corpus insists on it.* **Swete numbers this Sirach passage 32:8–9.** The Greek Sirach carries a well-known displacement in this region, so printed editions following Rahlfs number it differently; cite it by Swete and confirm the Rahlfs reference before putting it in print. The corpus holds no Rahlfs Sirach, so this run cannot settle the second number. `[T]` on Swete; the Rahlfs reference is **unchecked**.

*Source context.* Sirach 32:1–13 (Swete) is instruction on **offering**: do not appear before the Lord empty, the righteous man's offering makes the altar rich, give to the Most High as he has given to you. Its question is *whose offering God accepts*, and its answer is framed in three terms — righteous man, acceptable, memorial. Tobit 1 and 12 frame the same question for a Jew in exile with **no altar**: the brethren ate Gentile bread and Tobit kept himself from it (1:10–11); his piety is prayer, almsgiving and burying the dead; and the angel tells him that alms cleanse sin and that the memorial of his prayer was carried before the Holy One.

*Book usage.* Neither Sirach nor Tobit is in the overview's map, and neither has appeared in any Acts run. `[T]` This is a genuinely new register for the book.

*OT-to-OT.* Sirach and Tobit are doing the same work from opposite ends: Sirach from inside the cult, Tobit from outside it. Both convert the question "is this offering acceptable?" into the question "is this man's piety acceptable?" — and Tobit does it by substituting **prayer and alms** for sacrifice, which is exactly the substitution Acts 10:4 makes.

*What it adds, and the honest limit.* Luke does not quote these books and almost certainly is not alluding to them. What he does is **narrate and adjudicate Cornelius's piety in the register in which that question was asked and answered in Second-Temple Greek-speaking Judaism** — the overview's "register" category, whose test is not "would a reader catch a quotation?" but "is the writer narrating the thing in the source's own technical terms?" Six items clear that bar: ἐλεημοσύνη, προσευχή, μνημόσυνον, ἀνὴρ δίκαιος, δεκτός, and the ascent-to-God-ward of the whole. And the limit should be said plainly: **Sirach 32:9's subject is a sacrifice and Acts 10:4's is prayers and alms.** That substitution is not a weakness in the parallel; it is the parallel's content.

**H. Isaiah 52:7, with Nahum 1:15 → 10:36** *(moderate–high)* `[T]` on the distribution; `[S: sweep]` for the Isaiah identification

*The distribution.* **A word of the εὐαγγελιζ- family with a word of the εἰρην- family in one verse stands in four verses of the whole Greek Bible: Nahum 1:15 in Swete's numbering, Isaiah 52:7, Acts 10:36 and Ephesians 6:15** `[T]`. The sweep named Isaiah 52:7 at moderate confidence; this run confirms the identification and adds a correction to it.

**The correction.** Isaiah 52:7 reads *ὡς πόδες **εὐαγγελιζομένου ἀκοὴν εἰρήνης***, with εἰρήνη in the **genitive** governed by ἀκοή. Acts 10:36 has *εὐαγγελιζόμενος **εἰρήνην***, a participle with a **direct accusative** — and the Greek Old Testament text with that construction is **Nahum 1:15**: *οἱ πόδες εὐαγγελιζομένου καὶ ἀπαγγέλλοντος **εἰρήνην*** `[T]`. So the syntax is Nahum's and the theology is Isaiah's. The two should both be named, and which one is "the" source should not be decided by default.

*Source context.* Isaiah 52:7's herald announces peace and good news on the mountains and ends *Βασιλεύσει σου ὁ θεός* — "your God shall reign" — which Acts 10:36 answers with *οὗτός ἐστιν πάντων κύριος*. Nahum 1:15's herald announces peace to Judah with the promise that the oppressor shall not pass through again. Isaiah's is **the better contextual fit**: it stands at the head of the stretch that runs into the fourth Servant Song, and it is the Isaianic announcement of God's reign to the ends of the earth.

*Book usage.* Isaiah is the overview's most-used prophet, twelve uses across nine texts, and the standing instruction is to give any candidate Isaianic echo an elevated prior. `[S: overview]` Two neighbours sharpen this one. **Isaiah 53:7–8 is what the eunuch was reading two chapters earlier** (8:32–33) — the same Isaianic stretch, forty-six verses later `[S: sweep]`. And **Isaiah 57:19's *τοῖς εἰς μακράν* is what Peter put in place of Joel's Zion-limit at 2:39** `[S: 2:1–41 run]`. So Peter's opening formula to a Gentile household comes from the same Isaianic peace-to-the-far-off complex he had already drawn on at Pentecost — which is Move 2 doing exactly what the overview says it does on formula-free Acts material.

*OT-to-OT.* Nahum 1:15 and Isaiah 52:7 are themselves in conversation — the same image of feet on the mountains bringing news of peace — and Isaiah is almost certainly the later of the two. A reader who hears either hears both.

*What it adds.* Peter's first words to a Gentile household are the herald's words, and the herald's announcement in its own context is **that God reigns**. 10:36's parenthesis *οὗτός ἐστιν πάντων κύριος* is therefore not an aside; it is the herald's own punchline, with *πάντων* ("of all") standing where Isaiah had *σου* ("your").

**L. Leviticus 19:15 and Deuteronomy 16:19 → 10:34** *(high — register)* `[T]`

*Move 1 only.* **προσωπολήμπτης** compresses the Septuagint's judicial idiom *λαμβάνειν πρόσωπον*: *οὐ λήμψῃ πρόσωπον πτωχοῦ οὐδὲ θαυμάσεις πρόσωπον δυνάστου, **ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ κρινεῖς** τὸν πλησίον σου* (Leviticus 19:15); *οὐκ ἐπιγνώσονται πρόσωπον, οὐδὲ λήμψονται δῶρον* (Deuteronomy 16:19); and Sirach 32:16 `[T]`. *What it adds:* **the idiom's home is the lawcourt**, and that is why 10:34 and the διακρίνω chain belong together. - ***Internal:* planted for §24:25 — the inverted Cornelius.** **ἔμφοβος γενόμενος stands in two verses of Acts: 10:4, Cornelius before the angel, and 24:25, Felix before Paul** `[S: macro-synthesis / 23:12–26:32 run]`. **What this run adds:** in the New Testament the word stands in five verses, and **the other three are Luke 24:5, Luke 24:37 and Revelation 11:13** `[T]` — so in Luke–Acts ἔμφοβος marks the women at the empty tomb, the disciples before the risen Jesus, Cornelius before the angel, and Felix before a prisoner's preaching about judgment. - ***Internal:* planted for §14:15 and §12:22–23 — the refusal of worship.** 10:25–26 is the first of three scenes in which a man is offered divine honours. Peter refuses (10:26); Paul refuses in almost the same words (*ἡμεῖς ὁμοιοπαθεῖς ἐσμεν ὑμῖν ἄνθρωποι*, 14:15); **Herod accepts, and is struck down** (12:22–23). `[T]` on the three scenes; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the pattern — and it is a **three-member** pattern, which is the threshold the overview's macro gate sets. **προσκυνέω stands in four verses of Acts — 7:43, 8:27, 10:25, 24:11 — and 10:25 is the only one where the object is a human being** `[T]`.

### 12. Genre

Historical narrative with an embedded speech, and **two vision-reports, each given twice** — Cornelius's by the narrator at 10:3–6 and by Cornelius himself at 10:30–32; Peter's by the narrator at 10:9–16 and by Peter at 11:5–10. `[corrected: 20 Nov 2026 — the earlier wording read “a vision-report told three times” and then listed four instances, conflating the two visions.]` The reading rules that matter here are two.

**(a) Vision-report is not allegory.** The sheet is not a code to be decrypted item by item. Peter himself supplies the single interpretation at 10:28 and it is about **people**, not about a taxonomy of creatures. A sermon that assigns meanings to the quadrupeds has changed genre.

**(b) A thrice-told tale is told for the telling.** Luke's willingness to spend eighteen verses re-narrating what the reader has just read is a generic signal: this is **courtroom narrative**, where the same events are recited because the recital is the proceeding. The 21:37–23:11 run found that a *retrospective* unit echoes long rather than near (overview v2.4); this unit is **both** — narrative in chapter 10 and retrospective in chapter 11 — which is why its Move 4 return is unusually rich in both directions.

### 13. Copycat

Character by character, since this is where the overview's Trap 1 bites hardest.

| Who | What | Label |
|---|---|---|
| **Cornelius** — praying, giving alms, fearing God | Commended by the narrator (10:1–2) and by God (10:4, 10:31) | **Normative pattern**, with a hard limit: 11:14 says he was told words *ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ* ("by which you will be saved"), so the piety is real and is not salvation |
| **Cornelius** — prostrating before Peter | Rebuked on the spot (10:26) | **Negative example**, and gently handled |
| **Cornelius** — obeying a vision at once (10:7–8) | Narrated without comment but set against Peter's refusal | **Normative pattern**, by contrast |
| **Peter** — refusing the voice | Corrected twice more (10:15, 10:16) | **Negative example** — and the only thing in the pericope Peter does on his own initiative |
| **Peter** — going *μηδὲν διακρινόμενος* | Commanded (10:20) and reported as obeyed (11:12) | **Direct command**, to Peter; the principle generalises at 10:34–35 |
| **Peter** — lodging Gentile messengers (10:23) | Narrated with the pericope's own verb | **Normative pattern** |
| **Peter's sermon** | Interrupted (10:44) | **Descriptive** as to its outcome; its content is apostolic doctrine |
| **The Spirit falling before baptism** | Narrated once, and it is the **reverse of chapter 8** | **Unique event.** See below |
| **The circumcision party** | Objects (11:2), is answered, falls silent and glorifies God (11:18) | **Normative pattern in its ending**, not in its objection: a church that changes its mind when shown God's act |

**The order of Spirit and baptism is the pericope's main copycat trap, and the text disarms it.** Here the Spirit falls on unbaptised hearers mid-sermon and baptism follows (10:44–48). In Acts 8:14–17 the Samaritans are baptised first and receive the Spirit later, through apostolic hands. In Acts 19:5–6 both happen together. **Three frontier episodes, three different orders** `[T]` — which is itself Luke's argument that none of them is a template. The thing that is constant is not a sequence but a verdict: God gave *τὴν ἴσην δωρεάν* ("the same gift", 11:17), and nobody may obstruct it.

**Cornelius's piety is not meritorious, and 11:14 is the control.** But the opposite error must be named too, because the text resists it: Luke does **not** say Cornelius's prayers and alms were worthless. He says they *ascended as a memorial before God* (10:4) and were *remembered* (10:31). The honest form is that God answered a man's prayers by sending him the gospel.

### 14. Bible Timeline

This side of the cross, the resurrection, the ascension and Pentecost, and at the point in the book's own arc where the third stage of 1:8 becomes irreversible. The timeline tool's real work here is twofold.

**(a) Within Luke's two volumes.** The pericope stands at the exact distance from Luke 4:16–30 that makes Headline Finding 1 legible: Jesus announced in his home synagogue that the acceptable year had come and that God's prophets had gone to a Sidonian widow and a Syrian commander, and was nearly thrown off a cliff for it. **Between Nazareth and Caesarea stand the cross, the resurrection and Pentecost** — and what was intolerable as a claim is now a fact to be reported. The preacher who sets 10:34–35 beside Luke 4:24–27 is not making a connection; he is reading Luke's own two-volume design.

**(b) In redemptive-historical terms.** The question Peter is answering is a **Mosaic-covenant** question — what makes a man clean, what makes a table lawful — and the ground he answers it from is **older and wider than Sinai**: creation and the Noachic grant (Tool 11 D), the character of God as impartial judge (10:34), and the act of God in Christ (10:38–43). That is the right shape for the timeline here: the food laws are not abolished by being declared bad, they are **fulfilled by being outrun** — and what outruns them is a verdict God has already passed.

**Timeline misreading to avoid.** The standard reference-book line is that Acts 10 shows the dietary laws fulfilled in Christ, with Mark 7:19 as its companion. **For a preacher in Luke–Acts that pairing has to be handled carefully**, because Luke's Gospel contains neither κοινός nor κοινόω and has no form of the Mark 7 controversy (Tool 7b). Within Luke's own two volumes, the declaration comes *here*, as a vision to Peter, and its stated application is to **people** (10:28). Reaching to Mark 7 is legitimate canonical reading; presenting it as Luke's own argument is not.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Cornelius.** Not a Christ-figure and not a model of natural goodness. He is the **God-fearer** — a real and well-attested first-century category (Historical Background below) — and the reader's place with him is that of someone whose religion is sincere, costly and insufficient. The identification most hearers in a Christian congregation will actually want is with Cornelius *after* 10:44, and that is legitimate.

**Peter.** The reader's natural place, and the uncomfortable one. He is right about the law, obedient in the main, and wrong about the scope of God's intention — and he is corrected three times before he moves. **This is the identification the pericope is built for**, and it is the one that makes 11:17's question land: *who was I to withstand God?*

**The circumcision party (11:2).** The second place the reader may stand, and Luke treats them with notable respect: they bring a real objection, they receive a real answer, and they change their minds. A congregation hearing this should be able to recognise itself in people who had to be persuaded.

**Christ.** Not a character in the scene, and the preacher should not make Peter or Cornelius into one. Christ is the **content** of the sermon (10:36–43) and the **ground** of the verdict: the reason a Gentile is acceptable is not that God has relaxed a standard but that *this one is Lord of all* (10:36) and that forgiveness comes *διὰ τοῦ ὀνόματος αὐτοῦ* (10:43).

**Not Christ:** the apostle. Peter's *καὶ ἐγὼ αὐτὸς ἄνθρωπός εἰμι* (10:26) is the pericope's own guard against the moralistic reading, and it is spoken by the man the sermon might otherwise make its hero.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the author's intended response.** Luke wants a mixed church to be **certain** that the Gentiles' place at the table was God's doing and was publicly settled, and therefore to stop treating it as an open question. The response he is after is the response he narrates at 11:18: **objection ceasing, and God glorified for it.**

**Stage 2 — worldview, behaviour, motive.**

*Worldview.* Two convictions, and the first governs the second. **God is not προσωπολήμπτης** (10:34) — he is impartial as a judge is impartial, which is a statement about his character and not about his leniency. And **God acts before the church understands** (the agency grammar of Tool 1): the church's task at a frontier is to notice what God has already done and stop obstructing it.

*Behaviour — stop.* Stop calling κοινόν what God has cleansed — and note that Peter applies this to **persons**, not to foods (10:28). The concrete form is table-fellowship: the charge at 11:3 is not that Peter preached but that he **ate**. A congregation that will evangelise a group it will not eat with has not yet understood this passage.

*Behaviour — start.* Two things the text actually models. **Lodge the people you have been sent to** (10:23 — Peter becomes host before he becomes guest). And **give an account in order** (11:4, *ἐξετίθετο αὐτοῖς καθεξῆς*) when the church questions you, rather than resenting the question.

*Motive.* Not inclusiveness as a value but **the same gift** (11:17): *τὴν ἴσην δωρεὰν ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ὁ θεὸς ὡς καὶ ἡμῖν*. The ground of welcome is that God has already given to them what he gave to us, and that what he gave was given to us too and not earned by us.

**Four audiences.**

- **For me.** Where am I saying *μηδαμῶς, κύριε* to something God has already decided? The question is sharper than it looks, because Peter's refusal was **scripturally defensible** and spoken in the martyrs' own word.
- **For a Christian friend.** For anyone whose religion is real and who does not yet know that it is answered rather than accepted: Cornelius's prayers were heard, and what he was sent was a preacher.
- **For the church community.** The charge at 11:3 is about a shared table, and a church's table is where its doctrine of acceptance is visible. The ending at 11:18 is a church model: a body that can be argued out of a settled position by evidence of what God has done.
- **For an unbeliever.** 10:34–35 and 10:43 together: God shows no partiality, and forgiveness is *πάντα τὸν πιστεύοντα εἰς αὐτόν* — everyone believing in him. No nation is at a disadvantage and no person is at an advantage.

**Prayer in response.** That God would show us where we are obstructing him and give us the grace Peter was given — to be corrected three times and still go.

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **10:15 / 11:9 — *σὺ μὴ κοίνου*: present imperative with μή, plus an emphatic σύ.** `[T]` The construction with the present stem is characteristically **"stop doing"** rather than "never do", and the pronoun is unnecessary unless contrastive. So the force is: *what God cleansed — **you**, stop calling common*. The correction is aimed at a practice Peter is already engaged in, not at a hypothetical. Both English versions render it as a prohibition of the act rather than of its continuance; the ESV's "do not call" and NASB95's "no longer consider" differ here, and **NASB95's "no longer" is the better rendering of the aspect** — a point where the study text beats the pulpit text. *Moderate–high.*

- **10:15 — *ἃ ὁ θεὸς ἐκαθάρισεν*: aorist active, God the subject, neuter plural relative.** `[T]` Three things follow. The **tense** places the cleansing in the past: it is a report of something already done, not a dispensation being granted on the spot. The **voice and subject** put God where Leviticus puts the priest (Tool 11 A). And the **neuter plural** refers to the creatures, which is what makes Peter's move to *μηδένα … ἄνθρωπον* at 10:28 an inference rather than a translation — an inference the Spirit's command at 10:20 and the Spirit's descent at 10:44 both ratify.

- **10:34 — *καταλαμβάνομαι*: present middle.** `[T]` "I am coming to grasp." Not *κατείληφα* (perfect, "I have grasped") and not an aorist. Peter is reporting a process completing itself as he speaks. The NASB95's "I most certainly understand now" catches the "now"; the ESV's "Truly I understand" loses it. *Moderate.*

- **10:36 — the syntax is genuinely broken, and the apparatus knows it.** `[T]` *τὸν λόγον [ὃν] ἀπέστειλεν* leaves an accusative with no governing verb, which is why NA28 brackets the relative pronoun, why some witnesses add *γάρ*, and why the English versions all supply something ("The word which He sent…—", "As for the word that he sent"). **This is the one place in the passage where the preacher should not lean on a precise construction**; the sense is secure and the grammar is not.

- **11:15 — *ἐν δὲ τῷ ἄρξασθαί με λαλεῖν*: articular infinitive with accusative subject.** `[T]` "As I was beginning to speak." Peter's own account **dates the Spirit's descent to the beginning of his speech**, where the narrative at 10:44 has it mid-way (*ἔτι λαλοῦντος*). Not a contradiction — a man reporting that he had barely started — but worth noticing, because it is Peter minimising his own contribution in front of a hearing. *Moderate–high* on the observation; `[I]` on the motive.

- **11:17 — a first-class conditional with a rhetorical apodosis.** `[T]` *εἰ οὖν … ἔδωκεν … ἐγὼ τίς ἤμην δυνατὸς κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν;* The protasis is granted as fact and the conclusion is left to the hearers to draw. Peter ends his defence not with a claim but with a question — which is why 11:18 can be a verdict rather than a concession.

#### Sound-features

Transliteration is given only where the sound carries something.

- **10:15 / 11:9 — *ἐκαθάρισεν … κοίνου*.** No sound-play; the two words share nothing. What they share is a **grammatical** opposition — God's aorist against Peter's imperative — and that is where the force sits, not in the ear.
- **10:10 / 10:45 — *ἔκστασις … ἐξέστησαν*** (*ékstasis … exéstēsan*). The same root at both ends of the chapter, and here the ear does carry it: the trance that came on Peter and the astonishment that came on the onlookers are the same word. *Moderate.*

#### Recommended verification

- Whether **προσωπολήμπτης** is attested in Hellenistic-Jewish Greek outside the New Testament, or is a Christian coinage from the Septuagint idiom. The corpus cannot answer this; a lexicon can.
- Whether the **Ezekiel 4:14 echo** (Tool 11 B) has any history of interpretation. The run's verdict rests on distribution alone.
- The **Rahlfs reference for Sirach 32:8–9 in Swete's numbering** (Tool 11 G).

### Textual Variants

The NA28 apparatus for these chapters is unusually busy, and — as the overview's standing instruction now requires — it has been read **as a reader's commentary on internal echo** as well as for text-critical data. Five items matter, and four of them are instances of that instruction.

#### 10:6 and 10:32 — the tradition supplies the promise Luke withheld

- **The issue.** At **10:6** a small group of witnesses adds *οὗτος λαλήσει σοι τί σε δεῖ ποιεῖν* ("this man will tell you what you must do"), and **NA28's own apparatus flags the addition "cf. 11,14"**. At **10:32** a much broader constituency adds *ὃς παραγενόμενος λαλήσει σοι* ("who, when he comes, will speak to you").
- **Manuscript evidence.** 10:6: a marginal reading in one minuscule, a few witnesses, and the Clementine Vulgate — slight. 10:32: 𝔓¹²⁷ (as far as it can be read), C D E L Ψ 33, the bulk of the minuscules, the majority text, the Old Latin and Syriac; against 𝔓⁴⁵ 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B 81 453 2818, the Vulgate and the Bohairic.
- **Most likely original.** The shorter text, with NA28. *High confidence* — the longer readings are explicable as harmonisation and the shorter is not explicable as omission.
- **Why it is a finding and not a footnote.** **Luke withholds the content of Peter's errand from both narrative tellings and supplies it only in Peter's Jerusalem report** — *ὃς λαλήσει ῥήματα πρὸς σὲ ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ σὺ καὶ πᾶς ὁ οἶκός σου* (11:14). At two separate points scribes filled that gap, and at one of them the edition's own apparatus names 11:14 as the source. That is external evidence that the asymmetry between the tellings is **marked** — exactly what the overview's honesty note 5 predicts, and an **eighth instance** of it. And the detail that was imported is the one that matters most theologically: that Cornelius needed *words by which to be saved*.
- **Preaching implication.** Use 11:14, and say that it comes in the retelling. A preacher who reads 11:14 back into chapter 10 is doing in the pulpit what the scribes did in the margin — and losing Luke's point, which is that the saving purpose of the errand is stated when the church is being persuaded, not when the story is being told.

#### 10:19 — "three men", "two men", or no number

- **The issue.** NA28 prints *ἰδοὺ ἄνδρες **τρεῖς** ζητοῦντές σε*. **B reads δύο** ("two"); D L Ψ, much of the minuscule tradition, the majority text, part of the Old Latin and the Harklean Syriac **omit the numeral altogether**.
- **Manuscript evidence.** txt: 𝔓⁷⁴ א A C E 33 81 323 453 945 1175 1739, the Latin, the Peshitta, the Harklean margin and the Coptic. Against: B alone for δύο; a wide but secondary constituency for the omission.
- **Most likely original.** *τρεῖς*, with NA28. *High confidence* on the external evidence.
- **What the tradition tidied.** 10:7 says Cornelius summoned *δύο τῶν οἰκετῶν καὶ στρατιώτην* — **two servants and a soldier**, which is three men. **B's δύο harmonises back to the two servants and loses the soldier**; the majority omission removes the arithmetic problem altogether. And 11:11, where Peter says *τρεῖς ἄνδρες*, is **unvaried**. So the apparatus shows two independent attempts to tidy a number that is in fact exactly right — and the soldier is the detail that goes missing in both. **Ninth instance of the standing instruction**, and a small but real vindication of Luke's accuracy: he counted his own party correctly across sixty verses, and the scribes did not.
- **Preaching implication.** Minor, but worth a sentence: the man who brought the summons was a soldier of the same cohort, which is why 10:7's εὐσεβῆ ("devout") is there.

#### 10:30 — a fasting Cornelius

- **The issue.** NA28 prints *ἤμην τὴν ἐνάτην προσευχόμενος* ("I was praying at the ninth hour"). A broad tradition reads *νηστεύων καὶ … προσευχόμενος* ("**fasting** and praying"), and E goes further: "fasting and praying from the sixth hour until the ninth."
- **Manuscript evidence.** For the fasting: 𝔓⁵⁰, A in its corrector, D in both hands, Ψ 33 614 1175 1241 1505, the majority text, the Old Latin, the Syriac, the Sahidic and Middle Egyptian. Against: 𝔓⁷⁴ א A* B C 81 323 945 1739, the Vulgate, the Bohairic.
- **Most likely original.** The shorter reading, with NA28 — the external support is early and good and the addition is the kind of pious expansion that spreads easily. *Moderate–high.*
- **What it tidied, and the preaching implication.** **A wide and early tradition made Cornelius an ascetic**, and a reader of the Vulgate meets a praying Cornelius while a reader of the Latin and Syriac meets a fasting one. The direction of the tidying is informative: scribes **raised** Cornelius's piety. Which means that the text as NA28 prints it keeps his piety at exactly the level Luke gave it — prayer and almsgiving, the two staples of diaspora devotion (Tool 11 G) — and no higher. **A sermon should not add the fasting**, and should notice that the manuscript tradition wanted to.

#### 10:12 — three classes of creature, or four

- **The issue.** NA28 prints *πάντα τὰ τετράποδα καὶ ἑρπετὰ τῆς γῆς καὶ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ* — **three** classes. A large group (E L Ψ 614 1241 1505, the majority text, the Harklean) inserts *καὶ τὰ θηρία* ("and the wild beasts"), giving **four**; C 33 104 323 2818 reorder with the beasts included; 945 and 1739 drop the quadrupeds.
- **Manuscript evidence.** txt: 𝔓⁴⁵ (as readable), 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C in its corrector, 81, 1175, the Latin and the Coptic. The four-class readings are secondary.
- **Most likely original.** Three classes at 10:12, with NA28. *High confidence.*
- **What it tidied.** **11:6 has four classes** — *τὰ τετράποδα τῆς γῆς καὶ τὰ θηρία καὶ τὰ ἑρπετὰ καὶ τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ* — and the tradition harmonised 10:12 **up** to match it. **Tenth instance of the standing instruction.** And it matters for Tool 11 D: the harmonisation obscures the fact that Luke's two lists differ, and that the fuller one in Peter's mouth at 11:6 is the one that matches **Genesis 1:24's** three plus Genesis 1's birds. The scribes made the two tellings agree; Luke made the second fuller than the first, as a man recalling a vision more carefully under questioning would.

#### The Western text at 10:25 and 11:2 — expansion, not variant

- **The issue.** D (with the Old Latin, the Harklean margin and Middle Egyptian) rewrites **10:25** as a small scene of its own: a servant runs ahead to announce Peter's arrival and Cornelius leaps out to meet him. At **11:2** D and 𝔓¹²⁷ (as readable) insert a whole itinerary — Peter wished after a long time to go to Jerusalem, called the brethren together, strengthened them, taught through the regions, and arrived and reported the grace of God — before the objection.
- **Assessment.** These are the characteristic Western expansions and nothing turns on them text-critically. *High confidence* that NA28's text is original. But the **11:2** expansion is worth a note for a different reason: **it converts a confrontation into a preaching tour**, softening the force of *διεκρίνοντο πρὸς αὐτόν*. The Western scribe was uncomfortable with Peter being put on the spot by the Jerusalem church — which is further evidence, from the opposite direction, that being put on the spot is what the text says.

**Nothing else in these chapters affects preaching.** 10:11's "bound at four corners" (𝔓⁴⁵ Ψ 33), 10:16's *πάλιν*, 10:48's *τότε προσεταξεν*, 11:17's omission of *ὁ θεός* in D, 11:18's *ἐδόξαζον* — all minor.

**One thing the apparatus does *not* supply, and it should be said.** 10:14's *πᾶν κοινὸν **καὶ** ἀκάθαρτον* against 11:8's *κοινὸν **ἢ** ἀκάθαρτον* — "and" in the first telling, "or" in the second — is **unvaried in the tradition**. The variation is Luke's, not a scribe's, and it is the sort of small difference between tellings that the 10:12 harmonisation above shows scribes elsewhere could not leave alone.

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time.** Within a few years of the events of chapters 8–9, under Roman provincial administration, before the Herodian interlude of chapter 12.
- **Location.** **Caesarea Maritima**, Herod's harbour city and the seat of the Roman governor of Judaea — a Gentile city on the Jewish coast, with a substantial Jewish minority and a long history of friction between the two. And **Joppa**, an old Jewish port about thirty miles south, reached in a day's walk or less. The clock of 10:9–24 is consistent with that distance. The gospel's path in this pericope runs **from the Gentile capital to the Jewish port and back**, which is itself the shape of the argument.
- **Political context.** An occupying army, administered from the city where the pericope opens and closes. Cornelius is not a neutral foreigner; he is an officer of the force that holds the country.

#### What the original audience knew

- **A centurion of the Italian cohort.** A *cohors Italica* is an attested auxiliary formation of Roman citizens, and a centurion commanded perhaps eighty men and was the backbone of the army's command structure — a career soldier of some standing and means, but not an aristocrat. *Moderate–high confidence* on the unit's identification; the detail that matters exegetically is the simpler one, that he is a serving officer of the occupation.
- **"God-fearer".** *φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν* is, in Acts, close to a technical term for a Gentile attached to the synagogue — monotheist, attending, giving alms, keeping what he could — **without circumcision**, and therefore without membership. Luke uses the phrase and its cousin σεβόμενος repeatedly in the synagogue scenes. The category is the hinge of the whole pericope: Cornelius is **as close to Israel as an uncircumcised man could get**, which is why he is the right test case and why 11:3's charge is *ἄνδρας ἀκροβυστίαν ἔχοντας* — "men having uncircumcision". *High confidence* on the category's existence; *moderate* on how formalised it was.
- **Why a Jew would not enter a Gentile house.** Peter's *ἀθέμιτον* (10:28) describes a practice, and he does not cite a text, because there is no text. The avoidance is a **custom** built out from the food laws, the laws of corpse- and idol-defilement, and the practical certainty that a Gentile household's food, vessels and shrines could not be vouched for. Peter's own word for the problem is *κολλᾶσθαι ἢ προσέρχεσθαι* ("to associate with or approach"). *High confidence* that the custom existed and was variable in practice; **avoid the overstatement that the Torah forbade it.**
- **Alms and prayer as diaspora piety.** For a Jew or a God-fearer outside Jerusalem, prayer at the appointed hours and almsgiving *were* the available worship, and the Second-Temple literature treats them as the functional equivalent of sacrifice (Tool 11 G). An audience that knew Tobit and Sirach would hear 10:2 and 10:4 as a complete description of a devout man, not as a list of virtues.
- **A tanner's house by the sea.** Tanning used urine and lime and handled the hides of dead animals; it was sited downwind and, by the later rabbinic sources, regarded as a trade of low status. Whether first-century Jewish opinion rated it as the Mishnah does is **uncertain — the rabbinic material is later than Luke and should be cited with care.** What is secure and sufficient is that Peter was lodging with a man whose work was carcasses, immediately before a vision about them. `[T]`
- **Prostration.** Falling at a man's feet (10:25) was an available act of homage in the eastern provinces and not necessarily worship of a god; Peter's refusal treats it as more than courtesy. The honour-shame reading is straightforward: a Roman officer prostrating himself before a Galilean fisherman is a social inversion his assembled relatives and friends would have registered.
- **Four days, and the hours.** The third hour, sixth and ninth were the customary hours of prayer; Acts 3:1 names the ninth as such. An audience would hear 10:3 and 10:9 as two men at prayer at the proper times — and 10:30's "four days ago" as the ordinary way of reckoning.

#### What this changes about how we read

**First, it stops the passage being about food.** The background makes clear that the live question in a mixed city was not *what may I eat* in the abstract but *whose house may I eat in* — and Luke's own framing at 11:3 says the same. The dietary vision is the **vehicle**; the table is the **issue**.

**Second, it stops Cornelius being a generic nice man.** He is a God-fearer: a man whose religion had taken him as far as an uncircumcised man's religion could go, and who was therefore standing precisely at the line this pericope moves. The passage is not about whether God accepts decent people. It is about whether the line itself still holds.

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section.** New Testament narrative, and specifically **volume two of a two-volume work**. The canonical reader arrives having read Luke's Gospel, and the whole Old Testament behind it.
- **What the canonical reader knows.** Nazareth (Luke 4:16–30) — that Jesus announced the acceptable year and was nearly killed for naming a Sidonian widow and a Syrian commander. The centurion of Luke 7:1–10, **of whom the Jewish elders said "he loves our nation and built us our synagogue"**, and whose house Jesus did not in fact enter. The centurion at the cross who said "certainly this man was righteous" (Luke 23:47). Peter's three denials and the look that followed. And, from Acts, the eunuch's unanswered question at 8:36.
- **Canonical register.** The reader's ears are full of **Luke's own Gospel** (the overview's controlling intertext) and of the Greek Old Testament's purity and acceptance vocabulary. A reader who knows Leviticus 11, Ezekiel 4, Sirach and 1 Maccabees hears this pericope quite differently from one who knows only the English word "clean".

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were.** A mixed Jewish-and-Gentile Christian body, with Theophilus named (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1) and *ἀσφάλεια* — certainty — stated as the aim (Luke 1:4).
- **Their situation.** The movement was by now overwhelmingly Gentile, opposed in the synagogue, and repeatedly on trial. The pressing question was whether this was the fulfilment of Israel's promises or a defection from them. `[S: overview]`
- **What they brought.** Jewish members brought a purity system that was not a technicality but **the shape of covenant faithfulness**, and within living memory the thing people had died for (Tool 11 C). Gentile members brought the experience of having been, until very recently, exactly what Peter calls ἀλλόφυλος.

#### Where the passage fits their world

**For the Jewish hearer**, Peter's refusal is the sympathetic line in the pericope, and the thing that needs explaining is not his scruple but God's insistence. **For the Gentile hearer**, the decisive sentence is not 10:35 but **11:18** — because what a Gentile believer needed was not a principle but a **verdict from Jerusalem**. The passage is constructed to give both of them what they needed, which is why it is told twice.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking.** That an apostle argued with God about food three times and lost. That God addressed a Roman officer **first**. And — for a Jewish hearer — that the martyrs' confession (*οὐδέποτε ἔφαγον πᾶν κοινόν*) was the wrong answer.
- **Surprising.** That the Spirit came **before** baptism and before the sermon finished, which is the reverse of chapter 8. And that Cornelius, having been told to send for Peter, was told nothing about why (Textual Variants, 10:6).
- **Comforting.** For the God-fearer: *αἱ προσευχαί σου … ἀνέβησαν εἰς μνημόσυνον* — the prayers were heard before the preacher arrived. For the Gentile believer: *τὴν ἴσην δωρεάν*, the same gift, not a lesser one.
- **Disturbing.** For the Jewish believer: that God had done this without consulting the church, and that the church's only available response was to stop objecting. For everyone: that God is *οὐ προσωπολήμπτης*, which cuts both ways — an impartial judge shows no favour to the insider either.
- **Funny.** The rooftop (Tool 9). A first-century hearer would have enjoyed Peter arguing with heaven about lunch, and the enjoyment is part of how the passage disarms the hearer's own resistance.

#### What we bring that they didn't

- **A modern instinct that inclusion is self-evidently good**, which makes Peter's refusal look like bigotry rather than fidelity, and so removes the passage's whole difficulty — and with it the point.
- **"Clean" as hygiene.** The English word pulls toward dirt; the category is about *fitness to approach God*.
- **Individualism.** 10:2's *σὺν παντὶ τῷ οἴκῳ αὐτοῦ* and 11:14's *σὺ καὶ πᾶς ὁ οἶκός σου* describe a household, and 10:24's assembled relatives and close friends are the unit that hears the sermon.
- **A settled answer.** We read knowing how the question came out. They did not, and the eighteen verses of chapter 11 exist because they did not.

#### Implication for the sermon

Recovering the reception changes the sermon's **sympathies**. Peter must be preached as a good man with a good reason, not as a bigot — otherwise the congregation identifies with God against Peter and learns nothing. And the climax must be allowed to be **11:18**, the sound of a church changing its mind, rather than 10:44, the spectacular bit.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and the relation between them is **climactic convergence** — they meet in a single reality the passage names.

#### 1. Clean and unclean, and who divides

- **Where this passage sits.** At the point where the dividing itself is taken out of human hands. Genesis already has clean and unclean beasts at the ark (7:2–3) before any law exists; Leviticus 10:10 and 11:47 make dividing between them **the priest's commission**; Ezekiel 4:13 makes eating unclean among the nations the **curse of exile**, and Ezekiel 22:26 charges the priests with having failed to divide at all; 1 Maccabees makes refusing the κοινά a **martyr's act**. Then Acts 10:15 puts God in the subject position of the cleansing verb and forbids the apostle the verb of defiling, and Acts 15:9 says God *made no distinction*.
- **What comes before.** The whole system, and — crucially — the fact that its purpose-statement is *διαστεῖλαι*, "to distinguish" (Leviticus 11:47). The system was never about dirt; it was a pedagogy of discrimination.
- **What comes after.** 15:9's verdict; 21:28's charge against Paul; and, beyond Acts, Romans 14:14's *οὐδὲν κοινὸν δι᾽ ἑαυτοῦ* and Revelation 21:27's *οὐ μὴ εἰσέλθῃ … πᾶν κοινόν* — where the category returns, at the end, as a **moral** rather than a ritual one. The theme is not abolished; it is relocated.
- **What it contributes.** It identifies what is actually happening in the vision: not a rule being repealed but **a jurisdiction being asserted.** God does not say the distinction never mattered; he says he has already made it, and the apostle is not to make it again.
- **Confidence:** high.

#### 2. The nations brought in, and the sanctuary vocabulary that brings them

- **Where this passage sits.** At the moment the admission formula is spoken over an uncircumcised man. The vocabulary is the sanctuary's: **δεκτός** (the altar's verdict on an offering — Leviticus 1:3; and, of foreigners, Isaiah 56:7 and 60:7), **μνημόσυνον** (the memorial portion, Leviticus 2:2, and the high priest's shoulder-stones, Exodus 28:12), and the entrance-psalm's own clause **ἐργαζόμενος δικαιοσύνην** (Psalm 14:2 in Swete).
- **What comes before.** Genesis 12:3's blessing to the nations — which Peter had already preached to a temple crowd at 3:25 `[S: overview]`; Isaiah 56's house of prayer for all nations, which Stephen's Isaiah 66 citation had already pointed toward `[S: overview]`; and the long prophetic hope of foreign offerings accepted at the altar.
- **What comes after.** The Council's ruling (15:16–18, on Amos 9 in the Greek); Paul's arrest for the principle (21:28); and Ephesians 2:11–22, where the temple metaphor does explicitly what Acts 10 does narratively.
- **What it contributes.** It tells the preacher **what kind of sentence 10:35 is.** It is not a statement about human worth or about God's general benevolence; it is a **verdict of admission**, spoken in the words by which an offering was pronounced fit to be brought in. The Gentile is not tolerated at the edge of the court; he is declared acceptable at the altar.
- **Confidence:** high on the vocabulary; moderate–high on the theme's identification.

#### The confluence

**Climactic convergence**, and the point of convergence is a single word. The purity theme ends with God as the subject of *ἐκαθάρισεν*; the nations theme ends with the Gentile as the subject of *δεκτός ἐστιν*. **Both are passives of divine action** — something God has done to the creatures, and a standing God has given to the man — and both are reported rather than achieved. That is why 11:17's question is unanswerable: if the cleansing and the acceptance are God's acts, there is no human office left from which to object.

### Christological Reading

#### Type of connection

**Not typology.** This is a New Testament narrative and Christ is named; the question is what the passage does with him. Two of the five relations are live.

**Trajectory.** The pericope stands on the Servant trajectory the overview tracks `[S: overview]`, but at an unusual point in it. Acts 10:38's *ἔχρισεν αὐτὸν ὁ θεὸς πνεύματι ἁγίῳ καὶ δυνάμει* is **Isaiah 61:1**, the verse Jesus read at Nazareth; 10:35's δεκτός is **Isaiah 61:2**, the next verse. So the Christology here is not drawn from the fourth Servant Song (as at 8:32–35) but from the **Nazareth text** — the Servant as the **anointed herald** rather than as the sufferer. Which fits the pericope's work: what is being established is the scope of the herald's commission.

**Contrast, within the passage.** Peter is offered worship and refuses, *καὶ ἐγὼ αὐτὸς ἄνθρωπός εἰμι* (10:26). Three verses of Acts record such an offer, and **only Herod accepts** (12:22–23). The contrast is not between Peter and Christ but between creatures who refuse divine honours and the one to whom they belong — *οὗτός ἐστιν πάντων κύριος* (10:36).

#### How the passage preaches Christ

The sermon at 10:36–43 is the pericope's Christology and it is dense: **anointed** with the Spirit and power (10:38), doing good and **healing** those under the devil's power, **killed** by hanging on a tree (10:39, the Deuteronomic curse), **raised** on the third day by God (10:40), **appointed** judge of living and dead (10:42), and the source of **forgiveness through his name** to everyone believing (10:43).

The two things the preacher should take from it are structural, not doctrinal. First, **it is the standard apostolic outline given to Gentiles with one addition and one subtraction**: added is *οὗτός ἐστιν πάντων κύριος* (10:36) and *ἐν παντὶ ἔθνει* (10:35); subtracted is any argument from Israel's Scriptures by citation — 10:43 appeals to the prophets collectively and quotes none. Peter preaches the same Christ and drops the proof-texts. `[T]`

Second, **the sermon's one universal claim is a claim about judgment**: *κριτὴς ζώντων καὶ νεκρῶν* (10:42). The ground on which a Gentile is admitted is the same ground on which he is accountable — which is the only form in which 10:34's "no partiality" is good news rather than sentiment.

#### Trajectory

- **What the passage anticipates.** A church in which the table is shared; and, beyond it, the judgment at which the impartiality of 10:34 is finally exercised.
- **What Christ fulfils.** The acceptance the sanctuary vocabulary promised and could not deliver to an uncircumcised man — delivered, here, not by relaxing the standard but by *τοῦ ὀνόματος αὐτοῦ* (10:43).
- **Already / not yet.** The verdict is in (11:18) and the practice is not settled: chapter 15 will have to rule on it and Galatians 2 records Peter himself retreating from it.

#### Moralism check

- **The "be like X" temptation.** Two forms, and both are available. *Be like Cornelius* — devout, generous, prayerful — which makes the passage a commendation of religious sincerity and contradicts 11:14. *Be like Peter* — bold, boundary-crossing — which makes the passage a lesson in courage and ignores that Peter had to be told three times and then interrogated.
- **The gospel grounding.** 11:17: *τὴν ἴσην δωρεὰν ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ὁ θεὸς **ὡς καὶ ἡμῖν***. The welcome is grounded in a gift that was equally unearned on both sides of the table. That clause is the sermon's safeguard against both moralisms at once.
- **Christ as hero, not example.** The actor in this pericope is God, and the subject of the sermon's verbs is Christ. The apostle's contribution is an inference he reaches late and a question he cannot answer.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 10:2, 10:4, 10:31 — was Cornelius saved before Peter came?

- **Category:** doctrinal; and the most pastorally live question in the passage.
- **The difficulty.** Luke says God heard his prayers and remembered his alms (10:4, 10:31) and that he was δεκτός (10:35) — and also that Peter would speak words *ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ* ("by which you will be saved", 11:14) and that forgiveness comes to the one believing (10:43).
- **Rhetorical function in context.** 10:4 and 10:31 are **the ground of the summons**, not a verdict on his standing: their work in the narrative is to explain why an angel came. 11:14 is **the content of the errand**, spoken in a defence. The two are doing different jobs in different scenes, which is why they do not read as a contradiction in Luke's own argument.
- **Interpretive options.** (a) Cornelius was a believer in the God of Israel awaiting the Messiah, and the gospel completed rather than began his faith. (b) He was religious and unsaved, and his prayers were answered by the sending of a preacher. (c) The question is anachronistic: Luke is tracking a frontier, not individual soteriology.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** **11:14 is the controlling text, because it is Luke's own summary of what the visit was for**, and it uses the future: *σωθήσῃ*. That tells against (a) as a flat reading. But (b) must not be pressed into saying the piety counted for nothing, since Luke says twice that God received it. The position the text supports is narrow and worth stating in those terms: **God answered this man's prayers by sending him the gospel, and the gospel is what saved him.** *Moderate–high confidence.*
- **Suggested handling.** Address it briefly and plainly; it is the question an honest hearer will have, and leaving it unaddressed invites either universalism or a cruelty about sincere religion that the text does not license.

#### 10:44–48 — Spirit before baptism, and what follows from it

- **Category:** doctrinal (charismatic gifts; baptism), and contested among orthodox Christians.
- **The difficulty.** The Spirit falls on unbaptised hearers mid-sermon, with tongues, and baptism follows. In 8:14–17 the order is reversed; in 19:5–6 they coincide. Each has been used as a template.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** The function of 10:44–48 in Luke's own argument is **evidential**: it exists to be *reported* at 11:15–17 and *cited* at 15:8. The tongues are there because 10:46 says the circumcised believers **heard** them — the episode is constructed to produce a witness.
- **The main interpretive options.** Named without resolution: that the Spirit's coming here is the normal Christian experience in its normal order; that it is a frontier sign marking a new stage and so not normative in its order; that it is normative as to gift and non-normative as to sequence.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** **The three frontier episodes have three different orders**, and that variation is `[T]`. Whatever a reader's wider position, this text will not bear a sequence-template, and Luke's own use of it (11:15–17; 15:8) is as evidence that God gave the same gift — not as a pattern for how he gives it. *High confidence* on the negative point; the positive question is genuinely contested and is the preacher's to settle elsewhere.
- **Suggested handling.** Acknowledge the variation in one sentence; preach the verdict (*the same gift*), not the sequence. A congregation that knows the preacher has noticed the problem will trust him on the rest.

#### 10:14 — a prohibition in Scripture overruled by a vision

- **Category:** doctrinal and apologetic.
- **The difficulty.** Peter refuses on grounds the Torah supplies, and the voice overrules him. A thoughtful hearer will ask what that implies about the authority of a command, and a sharp-eyed one will ask what stops anyone claiming a vision against a text.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The text supplies three constraints, and they should be named. **(i) The ground of the overruling is not the vision but an act of God in the past** — *ἃ ὁ θεὸς ἐκαθάρισεν*, aorist. **(ii) It was confirmed three times, and then independently by the Spirit's instruction (10:19–20) and by the Spirit's descent (10:44)** — Luke does not let a single private revelation carry it. **(iii) It was submitted to the church and examined (11:1–18), and later ruled on by a council that cited Scripture (15:15–18).** So the passage is, among other things, an account of how such a claim is properly tested — which is the opposite of what it is usually feared to license. *Moderate–high.*
- **Suggested handling.** Worth a short, confident paragraph. This is the apologetic question the passage actually raises, and the text answers it better than most sermons do.

#### 10:28 — "unlawful"

- **Category:** apologetic, and a small factual trap.
- **The difficulty.** Peter says it is ἀθέμιτον for a Jew to associate with a foreigner, and a hearer with a concordance will not find the law.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** There is no such Torah command, and Peter does not claim one — he says *ὑμεῖς ἐπίστασθε*, "you know", and describes a custom (Historical Background). *High confidence.*
- **Suggested handling.** One clause of accuracy. Saying "the Torah forbade it" is the commonest factual error preached from this verse, and it weakens the sermon, because the interesting thing is that the barrier was **custom built on law** — which is exactly the sort of barrier a church is most likely to have of its own.

#### 10:39 — the curse formula

- **Category:** doctrinal, and under-preached rather than contested.
- **The difficulty.** Hardly a difficulty, but a hidden depth that the NASB95's "on a cross" removes (Tool 8c): Peter tells a Gentile household that Jesus was executed in the manner Deuteronomy 21:23 calls *cursed by God*.
- **Suggested handling.** If the ESV is in the pulpit, the word "tree" is there to be used. It is the sharpest thing in the sermon and it sits two verses before the resurrection.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: preaching it as the abolition of the food laws

- **What it looks like:** "Here God declares all foods clean — this is where Christians get bacon." Often with Mark 7:19 produced as the companion text.
- **Why it's wrong:** Three reasons, in ascending order. The vision's own stated application is to **people**, and Peter supplies it: *μηδένα κοινὸν ἢ ἀκάθαρτον λέγειν **ἄνθρωπον*** (10:28). The charge the church actually brings is about **eating with men**, not about eating pork (11:3). And within Luke's own two volumes the Mark 7 material does not exist — **κοινός and κοινόω occur nowhere in Luke's Gospel** (Tool 7b), so reaching for Mark 7 imports another evangelist's argument and presents it as Luke's.
- **The corrective:** Preach the vision as the vehicle and the household as the issue. The dietary consequence is real and is drawn elsewhere in the New Testament; it is not what this passage is doing, and making it the point costs the sermon everything the passage is for.

### Pitfall 2: making Peter a bigot

- **What it looks like:** Peter as the narrow, prejudiced apostle whom God has to drag into the modern world — with a knowing tone, and the congregation invited to feel superior to him.
- **Why it's wrong:** Peter's refusal is **scripturally grounded and costly**. His words are the words of the men who died rather than eat κοινά (Tool 11 C) and the words of a prophet whose identical protest God had once granted (Tool 11 B). He is not being squeamish; he is being faithful to what he has been given. A sermon that makes him contemptible has removed the only reason the passage is difficult — and has invited the congregation to identify with God against the apostle, which is the one position from which nobody learns anything.
- **The corrective:** Let Peter be right about the law and wrong about the scope. Then the question lands: *where am I conscientiously obstructing something God has already done?*

### Pitfall 3: making Cornelius the point — in either direction

- **What it looks like:** Either "Cornelius shows that sincere people of every religion are accepted" or, in reaction, "Cornelius's piety counted for nothing; he was as lost as anyone."
- **Why it's wrong:** Both halves of the text have to be held. God **received** the prayers and alms (10:4, 10:31) — the vocabulary is sacrificial and Luke chose it (Tool 11 G). And Peter was sent with words *ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ* (11:14). The first error contradicts 11:14; the second contradicts 10:4, and lands badly on any hearer with a devout non-Christian parent.
- **The corrective:** The narrow, accurate sentence: **God answered this man's prayers by sending him the gospel.** It keeps both texts and it preaches.

### Pitfall 4: making the sequence of Spirit and baptism the lesson

- **What it looks like:** In one tradition, "the Spirit comes before baptism — so look for a second experience"; in another, an anxious explanation of why nothing here is repeatable.
- **Why it's wrong:** Acts has **three frontier episodes with three different orders** — Samaritans baptised then Spirit through apostolic hands (8:14–17), Cornelius's household Spirit then baptism (10:44–48), Ephesus both together (19:5–6). `[T]` Luke's own use of this episode is at 11:15–17 and 15:8, and in both he draws from it a conclusion about **the same gift**, never about an order. The overview's Trap 1 names this as the template fallacy's main Acts form.
- **The corrective:** Preach *τὴν ἴσην δωρεάν* (11:17). Name the variation in a sentence so the congregation knows you have seen it, and move on.

### Pitfall 5: stopping at 10:48

- **What it looks like:** A sermon that climaxes with the Spirit falling and the household baptised, and treats chapter 11 as an appendix — or, in a two-part series, hands 11:1–18 to someone else.
- **Why it's wrong:** Luke spends **eighteen verses re-telling what he has just told**, and the retelling is where 11:14, 11:16 and 11:17 — the saving purpose, the Lord's remembered word, and the unanswerable question — are supplied. More decisively, **chapter 15 cites the retelling, not the event** (Phase 0.6). The hearer who needs this passage most is the one who needs to know that the church was persuaded, and that is 11:18.
- **The corrective:** If it must be two sermons, split at 10:33 or 10:43, not at the chapter break. If it is one, drive at 11:18 and let 10:44 be the penultimate beat.

### Pitfall 6: a small factual error that is easy to avoid

- **What it looks like:** "The Old Testament forbade a Jew to enter a Gentile's house."
- **Why it's wrong:** It does not, and Peter does not say it does — *ὑμεῖς ἐπίστασθε ὡς ἀθέμιτόν ἐστιν* describes what everyone knows, not what is written (Tool 7; Historical Background).
- **The corrective:** Say "custom built out from the law", which is both accurate and more useful — because a barrier of that kind is the sort a church is most likely to have built for itself.

## Open Questions / Uncertainties

1. **The Ezekiel 4:13–14 echo (Tool 11 B) has no known history of interpretation.** The verdict rests on two distribution facts and on the inverted shape. **This is the run's first Logos question**, and the right form of it is a citation search rather than a Study Assistant question: *does any commentator on Acts 10:14 cite Ezekiel 4:14?* A nil would be informative either way, given how exact the wording is.

2. **Psalm 14:2 in Swete behind 10:35 (Tool 11 F)** — the phrase is exclusive to two verses in the whole Greek Bible, and no secondary source has been consulted. Second Logos question, and it should ask who, if anyone, has read 10:35 against the entrance psalm.

3. **Psalm 106:20 in Swete behind 10:36–38 (Tool 11 I)** — recorded as a candidate at moderate confidence. The elements are spread across three verses of Acts and 10:36's syntax is contested. Worth one query; not worth a map row yet.

4. **Is προσωπολήμπτης attested outside the New Testament?** A lexicon question (BDAG is not in the library; LSJ is). If it is a coinage, Acts 10:34 is its first attestation and the point is worth making; if it is current Hellenistic-Jewish Greek, the point is smaller and should be stated smaller.

5. **The Rahlfs reference for Sirach 32:8–9 as Swete numbers it** (Tool 11 G). The corpus holds no Rahlfs Sirach; the Greek Sirach's displacement in this region means the printed reference differs, and it should be confirmed before the finding appears in print.

6. **Closed by this run, and recorded as closed.** The sweep offered "Peter's three denials and threefold restoration" as the middle term for *ἐπὶ τρίς*. **The restoration half is withdrawn** — it is John 21 and is not in Luke–Acts — and what stands in its place is the *remembering* formula at Luke 22:61 / Acts 11:16, verified by distribution (Tool 10f; Tool 11 Move 4). The gate working, and the record kept rather than deleted.

7. **Closed by this run, against this run.** An earlier pass claimed κοινός never carries the ritual sense in the Greek Old Testament. **It does, at 1 Maccabees 1:47 and 1:62**, and the error was a book-prefix mismatch in my own output filter — the trap the corpus notes already record for cross-edition prefixes, in a new form. Corrected at Tool 11 C, which is a better finding than the one it replaced. **Method lesson for the skill: a positive control that tests the search does not test the filter applied to the search's output.** Worth drafting into the Phase 10.5 gate.

8. **A candidate deliberately left below the threshold.** The collocation *numeral + ἀρχαῖς* ("in N divisions") stands in the Greek Old Testament at **Judges 9:34** (*τέτρασιν ἀρχαῖς*, Abimelech's ambush) and **1 Samuel 13:17** (*τρισὶν ἀρχαῖς*, a raid by **ἀλλόφυλοι**) `[T]`, and *τέσσαρσιν ἀρχαῖς* itself stands only at Acts 10:11 and 11:5 in the whole Greek Bible `[T]`. A Greek-reading hearer who knew the Septuagint would have met that construction only in **troop deployments**, and the vision is on its way to a cohort. **The objection comes first and is strong:** ἀρχή for the corner or end of a cloth is ordinary Greek and is certainly Luke's sense, and both English versions rightly render "corners". Recorded as a curiosity, not a finding, and not to be preached.

9. **Still open from elsewhere, and this unit touches it.** The overview's standing Logos queue includes Metzger on 11:20, eight verses past this unit's close. Nothing here bears on it.

## Book-Overview Tensions

Five items for the next Finalise pass. None of them unsettles the overview's structure; four of them fill a hole in it.

1. **The intertextual map has no row for 10:1–11:18, and this run proposes five.** In priority order: **Leviticus 11** behind the vision (register, moderate–high); **Ezekiel 4:13–14** behind the refusal (**inverted echo**, moderate–high, pending Logos); **Psalm 14:2 in Swete** behind 10:35 (allusion, moderate–high, pending Logos); **Sirach 32:9 with Tobit 12:9 and 12:12** behind the piety-and-acceptance vocabulary (register, moderate–high); and **Genesis 1:24–26 with 9:1–3** behind the creature list and the command (allusion, moderate). Two further rows are already secure and belong in the map as well: **Isaiah 52:7 with Nahum 1:15** at 10:36 (the sweep's row, with the syntactic correction) and **Deuteronomy 21:22–23** at 10:39.

2. **The live-sources ranking needs three additions, and one of them is a new category.** **Leviticus** is not in the map at all and is the host text of the book's pivotal vision. **Ezekiel** rises from two uses to three. And **Sirach and Tobit** are a kind of source the map has no room for — Second-Temple Jewish writings used as *register* rather than as Scripture cited. The map's four-way honesty note (citation / allusion / register / inverted echo) already has the right category; what it lacks is the acknowledgement that Acts' register-sources include books outside the Hebrew canon.

3. **The sweep's "longest single unit in Acts" is wrong and should be corrected wherever it is repeated.** 10:1–11:18 is 66 verses and the **third** longest unit on the overview's own map, after 23:12–26:32 (109) and 6:8–7:60 (68). `[T]`

4. **Honesty note 6 gains a seventh instance, and this one is new in kind: a chain that *both* versions break, in different places.** At 10:20, 11:2, 11:12 and 15:9 the single lemma διακρίνω receives four different English words in NASB95 and four in the ESV, and neither version preserves the chain. Previous instances recorded one version breaking what the other kept; **this is the first where the chain is invisible in both.**

5. **Honesty note 5 — "where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied" — gains three instances, bringing it to ten.** The importation of 11:14's promise into **10:6 and 10:32**, which NA28's own apparatus flags as harmonisation; **B's δύο at 10:19** against the arithmetic of 10:7; and the insertion of *τὰ θηρία* at **10:12** to match 11:6. All three are the tradition levelling Luke's deliberately unequal tellings, which is external evidence that the inequality is marked.

**One confirmation worth recording.** The overview's Moralism Check lists two places where the apostles explicitly refuse the hero's role (3:12; 14:14–15). **10:26 is a third, and it completes a pattern the overview does not yet name** — because Acts' third man offered divine honours is **Herod**, and he accepts them and dies (12:22–23). Three members, within one movement of the arc map, which clears the macro gate's threshold.
