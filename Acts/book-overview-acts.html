Two claims were checked in Logos before being allowed into this document [revised: logos-research]:

  • The Exodus 1:7 echo is not a new proposal — it is established. Tannehill notes that Exod 1:7 has both auxanō and plēthynō of Israel’s growth “as the exodus approached”, and that applying them to the word and the church at 6:7 may mark a parallel between the church’s situation and Israel’s; he also makes the point that the word, not merely the people, grows, and links the metaphor to the sower of Luke 8:4–15. The Reformation Study Bible reads Exod 1:7, 12, 20 and calls it a new exodus for the new Israel. Schreiner adds the Gen 1:28 layer — the creation mandate fulfilled. No source was found rejecting it. Confidence: high.
  • The κωλύω thread is only half-corroborated. Wall notes that ἀκωλύτως stands in the emphatic final position to characterise the unhindered spread of the gospel, not merely Paul’s conditions of custody — so the terminal weight is recognised. No commentator was found who reads the six earlier κωλύω occurrences as a deliberate thread culminating in that adverb. The distribution is certain [T]; the design remains this project’s own reading. Confidence: moderate, and marked as such throughout.

What Changed at v2.3

The Arc Map is rebuilt: seven movements become nine. This is the only substantive change, plus its knock-on effects. Nothing in the Intertextual Map, the honesty notes, the Preaching Units or the Christological Trajectory is altered.

The question The answer
Does the Arc Map need changing? Yes. The seventh movement held 6,023 words — 32.7% of the book — against a mean of 2,065 for the other six. That is a failure of resolution, not a feature of Acts
Why did it happen? The map’s binding device stops working at 19:20. Every summary-notice in Acts — αὐξάνω, πληθύνω, προστίθημι, στερεόω, περισσεύω — is exhausted by then [T]. With no marker to work from, the map covered the last third with one panel

The clinching datum The handover between devices happens in consecutive verses: the last growth-notice is 19:20, the first Rome-ward δεῖ is 19:21 [T]. v2.2’s seventh movement began in the right place and simply ran on for three stages of a device it had not identified

What v2.3 does not claim. That the nine-movement shape is the only defensible one; that the δεῖ statements are uniform (the first is Paul’s, the other two are divine speech); or that the register evidence at ch. 21 is worthless — it is good evidence about where the book changes character, which is not the same question as where Luke marks a panel.

Presenting Situation

Author. Formally anonymous [T]. Three things stand. (a) It is volume two of a single work: both volumes address Theophilus, and 1:1 refers back to “the first book” about all that Jesus began (ἤρξατο) to do and teach [T]. (b) The narrator is at some points a travelling companion — the four “we” sections (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16) slip into first-person without explanation [T]. (c) Second-century tradition names that companion Luke [S]. Lukan authorship is [S]/[I], not [T] — the book does not name him — and it should be stated that way, however settled it feels.

Date. Genuinely contested; [S], moderate/uncertain, and no exegetical weight should rest on it. The ending’s silence about Paul’s outcome is a real datum on any dating, and is better read as design (see the Arc Map) than as a dating clue.

Audience. Named: “most excellent Theophilus” (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1) [T]. The stated aim is ἀσφάλεια — certainty — concerning what he has been taught (Luke 1:4) [T], and the first sermon’s conclusion delivers that word back as an adverb: ἀσφαλῶς οὖν γινωσκέτω πᾶς οἶκος Ἰσραήλ (2:36) [T]. Beyond Theophilus, the implied readership is a mixed Jewish-and-Gentile Christian body [I].

Occasion / pressure. The book answers an anxiety: is this Jewish-messianic movement, now overwhelmingly Gentile, opposed in the synagogue and repeatedly on trial before Rome, the fulfilment of Israel’s promises — and is it safe to belong to it? [I], moderate confidence on the precise reconstruction. The assurance motive itself is high confidence because Luke states it.

Two features of the text support the reconstruction independently of any historical hypothesis, and are worth naming because they are [T]:

  • Four official verdicts of innocence in the last six chapters — 23:9; 23:29; 25:25; 26:31 — with 26:32 adding that Paul could have been released. This is the same cumulative-acquittal technique Luke used of Jesus (Luke 23:4, 14, 22, 15, 41, 47). A book with nothing to prove about its movement’s legality does not do this twice.
  • 21:20’s μυριάδες of law-zealous Jewish believers. Whatever else Acts is doing, it is not narrating a church that has left Israel.

Purpose, in one sentence. To give a mixed church certainty that the risen Jesus is still acting — by his Spirit, through witnesses, under a purpose no opposition can obstruct — and that the Gentile mission is that purpose, not a deviation from it.

The binding device changes at 19:20/19:21 — and that is the map’s main finding

(Restated at v2.3. Movements 7–9 replace v2.2’s single 19:21–28:31 panel; the reasoning is set out here rather than in the What Changed table because it is exegetical, not editorial.)

Panels 1–6 are closed by summary-notices. The vocabulary is [T], lemma-verified: αὐξάνω at 6:7, 7:17, 12:24, 19:20 · πληθύνω at 6:1, 6:7, 7:17, 9:31, 12:24 · προστίθημι at 2:41, 2:47, 5:14, 11:24, 12:3 · στερεόω and περισσεύω at 16:5. Every summary-notice device in Acts is exhausted by 19:20. Luke never writes another one.

That fact was doing damage to v2.2’s map. The device covers 1:1–19:20 and produces six panels of strikingly even size; with nothing to mark the rest, the map let a single panel run to 6,023 words — 32.7% of the book in one movement, against a mean of 2,065 for the other six. The seventh panel was not a description of the last third; it was the map’s silence about it.

But Luke does not stop marking. He changes device. The three Rome-ward δεῖ statements are as short, as formulaic and as evenly spaced as the growth-notices, and each launches what follows:

Ref Words Speaker
19:21 Μετὰ τὸ γενέσθαι με ἐκεῖ δεῖ με καὶ Ῥώμην ἰδεῖν Paul, reporting his own resolve as necessity
23:11 ὡς γὰρ διεμαρτύρω τὰ περὶ ἐμοῦ εἰς Ἰερουσαλὴμ οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην μαρτυρῆσαι the Lord, standing by him the night after the Sanhedrin
27:24 Καίσαρί σε δεῖ παραστῆναι an angel, in the storm

The handover happens in consecutive verses. The last growth-notice is 19:20; the first Rome-ward δεῖ is 19:21 [T]. v2.2’s seventh movement therefore began in exactly the right place — it simply did not notice that a new device had started, and ran on for three stages of it.

Two honest qualifications. (a) The first δεῖ is Paul’s (εἰπὼν ὅτι … δεῖ με); the second and third are divine speech. The pattern is not uniform, though the shape — Paul says I must, and God twice confirms it — is arguably the point. [I], moderate. (b) 23:11 sits between the Sanhedrin hearing and the plot of 23:12, which is a real narrative seam and not merely a convenient one.

The two smallest panels are now the opening (1:1–2:47) and the closing (27:1–28:31), which is defensible as design rather than as a defect of resolution.

A correction to the device’s own description, while auditing it. v2.2 said “panels closed by growth-notices”, but the 1:1–2:47 / 3:1 boundary rests on 2:47’s προστίθημι summary, not on the αὐξάνω/πληθύνω family. The device is better described as summary-notices of varied vocabulary — which is what it always was, and which is why 16:5’s στερεόω + περισσεύω counts too.

The whole map sits inside a βασιλεία inclusio. βασιλεία occurs eight times (1:3, 1:6, 8:12, 14:22, 19:8, 20:25, 28:23, 28:31) [T]; the first and last are the kingdom of God taught at length — by Jesus for forty days, by Paul for two years. Between 8:12 and 20:25 it is sparse: five occurrences carry twelve chapters.

The ending (28:30–31). Deliberately open: no verdict, no death, no closure. Two participles, a prepositional phrase and an adverb. Read the abruptness as design — the mission is unfinished, and the reader is inside it.

Microscript

The risen Jesus, having begun to act in volume one, keeps acting from heaven by his Spirit through chosen witnesses: his word grows as Israel grew in Egypt, crossing every boundary — Samaritan, eunuch, persecutor, Gentile household — until the question each frontier raises, what hinders?, is answered by a settlement at Jerusalem and by a prisoner teaching the kingdom in Rome unhindered, while the man wears the chain and the word does not.

The book’s dominant note is the unobstructed word: the growing subject of the seams, the thing the councils cannot suppress, and the last thing the book says about itself.

Christological Trajectory

Acts preaches a living, reigning, acting Christ. The grammar carries it: 1:1’s ἤρξατο makes the Gospel the beginning and Acts the continuation, and through the book the acting subjects of the main verbs are God, the Lord, the Spirit, or the word — rarely the apostles [T].

Ground. Jesus is κύριος and χριστός by God’s act (2:36) — enthroned at the right hand (2:33–35, Ps 110), the Davidic heir whose throne is heaven (2:30; 13:23), the prophet like Moses (3:22; 7:37), the suffering Servant (8:32–35, and the Servant Songs transferred at 13:47 and 26:18), the Author of life (ἀρχηγός, 3:15; 5:31), the appointed Judge of living and dead (10:42; 17:31), and the one name under heaven for salvation (4:12) — ὄνομα 60× [T].

Union. He is so identified with his people that persecuting them is persecuting him: Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις; (9:4; repeated 22:7; 26:14) [T]. This is the book’s sharpest Christological datum and it is spoken, not narrated.

The pattern of the transferred commission. Luke uses the Servant Songs three times and each time the Servant’s role moves outward — to Jesus (Isa 53 at 8:35), to the missionaries (Isa 49:6 at 13:47, ἐντέταλται ἡμῖν), to Paul (Isa 42:6–7 behind 26:18). [S: sweep], moderate–high. Preaching Acts Christologically means preaching the church’s mission as the Servant’s own, not as the church’s initiative.

Moralism check. The temptation Acts invites is unusually strong because its characters are attractive: be bold like Peter, faithful like Stephen, tireless like Paul. Three text-level guards:

  • The apostles refuse the role twice, explicitly (3:12 why do you stare at us, as though by our own power?; 14:14–15 we also are men, of like nature with you) [T].
  • Luke reports their quarrel (15:39 παροξυσμός), their fear of Paul (9:26), and a prayer meeting that did not believe its own answer (12:15) [T].
  • The growing subject of the seams is the word, not the workers [T] — a point Tannehill makes independently [S: Tannehill].

Preaching Traps

1. The template fallacy — the biggest, and it has four specific forms

Where it bites: 1:12–26 (lots), 2:1–41 (tongues at conversion), 2:42–47 and 4:32–5:11 (community of goods), 8:14–17 and 19:1–7 (a two-stage Spirit), 19:11–12 (handkerchiefs). Why it is wrong: each rests on one episode, and Acts itself supplies a differing parallel. The four Spirit-frontiers have four different sequences — Spirit then baptism (2); baptism, then apostolic hands, then Spirit (8); Spirit then baptism (10); re-baptism, then hands, then Spirit (19) [T]. The lot never recurs after the Spirit comes. 5:4 says the property was the owner’s. Luke calls the Ephesian miracles οὐ τὰς τυχούσας, extraordinary. Corrective: run Copycat before application on every Acts passage, and ask what Luke says elsewhere about the same thing before generalising.

2. Making the apostles the heroes

Where it bites: 4:1–31, 5:12–42, 9:1–31, 16:16–40 — anywhere courage is on display. Why it is wrong: see the moralism check above; the book’s subject is the word and the Lord who acts. Corrective: ask of each passage, who is the subject of the main verbs?

3. Triumphalism

Where it bites: 12:1–25 above all, and 16:25–34. Why it is wrong: James is executed in 12:2 without explanation, in the same chapter as Peter’s release; the prison opens for Peter and not for Paul; the book ends with a hardening oracle and a man in chains. Corrective: preach 12:2 whenever preaching 12:3–19, and the chain of 28:20 whenever preaching the adverb of 28:31.

4. Detaching the speeches from their settings

Where it bites: 17:16–34 most of all — the Areopagus mined for natural theology, or written off as Paul’s failure. Why it is wrong: the speech starts from an altar to an unknown god, calls the city’s religion ἄγνοια (17:30), and ends with repentance, a fixed day and a raised man; 17:34 records converts. Corrective: preach both halves — real common ground, and a real summons.

5. Supersessionism, and its opposite

Where it bites: 13:46; 18:6; 28:28. Why it is wrong: the turn to the Gentiles happens three times and is each time local — Paul is in a synagogue again in the next city [T]. 21:20 reports μυριάδες of law-zealous Jewish believers. Isaiah 6’s own context ends in a surviving stump, and 28:24 says some were persuaded. Corrective: hold the tension the Council held.

6. Mining incidental detail

Where it bites: Eutychus (20:9), the viper (28:3), the ship’s tackle (27:17–19). Why it is wrong: Luke’s own interest is stated — they were not a little comforted (20:12); all were brought safely to land (27:44). Corrective: preach the providence and leave the rigging alone.
