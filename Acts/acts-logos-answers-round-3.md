## 1. Paul's verdict at 28:18 and the trial of Jesus (A13)

**Asked:** Which commentators discuss a link between Paul's statement in Acts 28:18 (the Romans "wished to release me because there was no ground for death in me") and Pilate's verdicts on Jesus in Luke 23:13–22, or Paul's own words about Jesus in Acts 13:28? What wording do they point to, and does anyone treat it only as part of a general Jesus–Paul trial parallel rather than a specific echo? With page references.

**Cited:**
- A. T. Robertson, *Word Pictures in the New Testament* (1933), on 13:28: Paul "uses this same idiom about his own innocence" at 28:18. Robertson also cites Luke 23:22 and the Matthean trial for Jesus' innocence. No page given.
- F. Thiele and C. Brown, "Guilt, Cause …", *NIDNTT* 2:139. *αἰτία* at 13:28 and 28:18 is the legal ground for a capital charge, unjustified in both places.
- W. D. Mounce, *Complete Expository Dictionary* 562. *αἰτία* as the legal ground for condemnation in the Pilate scenes (John 18:38; 19:4, 6) and Acts 13:28.

**Assessment:**
- **History is present but thin**, and it comes from a word-study commentary and two theological dictionaries. No critical commentary on Acts surfaced.
- What there is supports the audit's reframing. Robertson ties 28:18 to **13:28** as "the same idiom". The dictionaries put the Pilate verdicts, 13:28 and 28:18 in one field of unjust capital charges, not as a source and its echo.
- No one names Luke 23:20–22 as the specific source of 28:18.

**Effect:** A13 stays **moderate–high** as a series parallel, with history `[S: Robertson; NIDNTT]`. Luke 23:20–22 as the specific source stays **moderate**, with no history found.

**Follow-up worth one question:** ask by name what Keener, Bock (BECNT), Peterson (PNTC) or Witherington say on 28:18, if any of them is owned.

## 2. The night assurances (A14)

**Asked:** Which commentators discuss how the angel's message in Acts 27:23–24 relates to Paul's night visions in 18:9–10 and 23:11? What wording do they compare, and does anyone argue that the shared phrases are only stock assurance formulas? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **David E. Aune, *Prophecy in Early Christianity and the Ancient Mediterranean World* (Eerdmans, 1991), 266–267.** Acts 18:9–10, 23:11 and 27:23–24 are three dream or vision **oracles of assurance**, conforming to the Old Testament and early Jewish form: an admonition followed by a reason. At 23:11 *θάρσει* is "virtually synonymous with the more frequent *μὴ φοβοῦ*".
- J. Pelikan, *Acts* (Brazos Theological Commentary), 186. He quotes 18:9–10 and 27:24 together.
- W. J. Conybeare and J. S. Howson, *The Life and Epistles of St. Paul* (1893), 326. They set "Fear not: for I am with thee" beside "Fear not: thou must stand before Cæsar".

**Assessment:**
- **Aune is the decisive source**, a specialist monograph. He reads the three as one series in a set form. That is exactly the audit's reframing: the shared phrases belong to a stock assurance form (compare the nine Swete verses joining *μὴ φοβοῦ* with *μετὰ σοῦ*). The links are a recapitulation within a series, not a borrowing from Corinth.
- Nothing cited argues that 27:23–26 is built on 18:9–10 specifically.

**Effect:**
- The reframed claim — 27:23–24 as the last of Paul's assurance oracles, recapitulating 18:9 and 23:11 — rises from **moderate to moderate–high** `[S: Aune 266–267]`.
- "Built on 18:9–10" stays **low–moderate**.
- Aune also supplies a name for the form, which the 27:1–28:18 dig's v1.1 patch should use: **oracle of assurance**.

## 3. Acts 7:46–50 against Ps 132, 1 Chr 28:2 and 1 Kgs 8:27 (A2, A22, A23)

**Asked:** How do commentators explain the relation between Acts 7:46–50 and Psalm 132:5, 1 Chronicles 28:2 and 1 Kings 8:27? What are the main views, who holds each, and what evidence do they give? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **Steve Walton, "A Tale of Two Perspectives?", in *Heaven on Earth*, ed. T. D. Alexander and S. Gathercole (Paternoster, 2004), 141–142.**
  - Walton holds the "transcendence" view: Stephen rejects confining God to the temple, not the temple itself.
  - Citing **Sylva**, he notes four elements in the same sequence in 1 Kgs 8:14–30 and Acts 7:46–50: David's desire, Solomon's building, the question of God's earthly dwelling, and the assertion of transcendence. He also notes that Solomon's own prayer (1 Kgs 8:27) says what Acts 7:48–50 says.
- **G. K. Beale, *The Temple and the Church's Mission* (NSBT 17, 2004), 217.** On the "critical" view, Stephen's Isaiah citation implies that Solomon's temple did not fulfil the promise of a house for God.
- J. T. Squires, "Acts", *Eerdmans Commentary on the Bible* (2003), 1230. Stephen presents Solomon's building as without divine sanction.
- S. Pimentel, *Witnesses of the Messiah* (2002), 76. *χειροποίητος* is used in the Septuagint of idols.

**Assessment:**
- **History is strong for 1 Kgs 8 (A23).** Sylva's sequence argument, reported by Walton, is a structural parallel and is better evidence than the single verse the audit cited.
- No source mentions **Ps 132** or **1 Chr 28:2**, so A22 has no history.
- The audit's reframing — Isaiah's question set against the Davidic–Solomonic temple tradition, not against Ps 132 alone — is supported by both views as reported. The views differ only on whether Solomon is criticised.
- **Caution:** the answer's body gives different page numbers from its footnotes (Walton 72–73 against 141–142; Beale 157 against 217; Pimentel 48 against 76). The footnote figures are used here; check them in the books.
- The Assistant's label "the more persuasive view" is its own gloss.

**Effect:**
- **A23 rises from moderate to moderate–high** `[S: Walton, citing Sylva]`, and should be restated as the **1 Kgs 8:14–30 sequence**, not 8:27 alone.
- A2's reframing stands at **moderate**.
- **A22 stays moderate**, with no history.
- The 6:8–7:60 dig should name the two interpretive views (transcendence, held by Walton and Sylva; critique, held by Beale and Squires) under Difficult Verses.

## 4. Acts 7:6 and Exodus 2:22 / 18:3 (A20)

**Asked:** Which commentators discuss the wording *πάροικον ἐν γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ* in Acts 7:6 against Genesis 15:13's "in a land not their own"? Do any connect it with Exod 2:22 / 18:3, and does anyone reject the link? With page references.

**Cited:**
- Zodhiates, *Complete Word Study Dictionary: NT*. It notes *ἀλλότριος* at Acts 7:6 and in the Greek of Exod 2:22 and 18:3, but draws no inference.
- **B. S. Childs, *The Book of Exodus* (OTL), 35.**
  - He notes the term's use at Acts 7:6.
  - He traces *גֵּר* ("sojourner") from the landless person to Israel in Egypt.
  - He cites Philo's use of Exod 2:22.
  - He reads Acts 7:29 as keeping the term's concrete sense of exile.

**Assessment:**
- **No commentator was found making the Acts 7:6 → Exod 2:22 link.** Childs comes nearest: he puts Acts 7:6 and 7:29 in the same discussion as Exod 2:22, without saying that 7:6 borrows from it.
- The answer's reference to "pages 840–841 in the TWNT/TDNT" is garbled and is ignored.

**Effect:** A20 stays **moderate–high** on the corpus evidence: *πάροικος* + *γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ* stands only at Exod 2:22 and 18:3 in Swete and only at Acts 7:6 in the New Testament. The history of interpretation is **weak**, `[S: Childs 35, adjacent]`. This is a finding the text supports and the library has not noticed. It is worth keeping, and it should be marked as unreviewed by scholarship.

## 5. "Wonders and signs" (A25)

**Asked:** Acts uses "wonders and signs" at 2:19, 2:22, 2:43, 6:8 and 7:36, but "signs and wonders" at 4:30, 5:12, 14:3 and 15:12. Which commentators discuss the order, what explanations do they give, and does anyone think it insignificant? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **R. N. Longenecker, "Acts", *Expositor's Bible Commentary* 9:317.** "Wonders and signs" appears where Luke follows his sources; "signs and wonders" where he composes freely.
- **K. H. Rengstorf, *TDNT* (abridged), 1020 and 1172.** The reversed order appears where typology is prominent, though the distinction is "not entirely clear". He also suggests a difference of emphasis between the two orders.
- **H. E. Remus, "Miracle (NT)", *Anchor Yale Bible Dictionary* 4:857.** Distinctions between the terms are untenable, since they are used interchangeably.

**Assessment:**
- **History is present and divided.** Three explanations are on record:
  1. *Source and redaction* (Longenecker). This conflicts with the corpus: the "wonders and signs" stretch, 2:19–7:36, is where Luke has just reworked Joel, not merely followed it.
  2. *Typology* (Rengstorf). This fits Moses at 7:36 and the Joel frame.
  3. *No significance* (Remus). Remus speaks of the terms' meaning, not of their order, so he does not meet the order pattern head on.
- None of the three notes the audit's datum that Luke **adds** *σημεῖα* to Joel at 2:19 and so sets the order himself.

**Effect:** A25 stays **moderate–high** for the pattern `[T]` and **moderate** for its meaning `[S: Rengstorf; contra Longenecker, Remus]`. The 6:8–7:60 dig's v1.1 Headline 4 should state the pattern and name the competing explanations.

## 6. ἐκβολή and κουφίζω in Acts 27, and Jonah 1:5 (A18)

**Asked:** What do the lexicons (BDAG, LSJ, Moulton and Milligan) record for *ἐκβολή* / *ἐκβολὴν ποιεῖσθαι* and for *κουφίζω* of a ship, with extra-biblical examples? Do any commentators connect the wording with Jonah 1:5? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **Thayer, *Greek-English Lexicon*, 193.** *ἐκβολή* is "the throwing overboard of goods … whereby sailors lighten a ship in a storm", with classical examples from Aeschylus, Aristotle and Lucian. It notes *ποιεῖσθαι ἐκβολήν* at Acts 27:18.
- Swanson, *Dictionary of Biblical Languages*: "jettisoning (a ship's cargo)".
- AMG *Comprehensive Dictionary*, 622: *κουφίζω* "to make lighter".
- **R. P. Thompson, *Acts* (New Beacon Bible Commentary), 409.** Luke's voyage parallels Jonah 1: storm, fearful sailors crying to their gods, lightening the ship. Thompson names *κουφίζω* (Jonah 1:5; Acts 27:38) and *ἐκβολή* (Jonah 1:5; Acts 27:18).
- J. C. Laney, *Lexham Geographic Commentary on Acts–Revelation*, 422. Jonah's sailors did the same.

**Assessment:**
- BDAG, LSJ and Moulton–Milligan did not surface. Either they are not owned or the Assistant did not reach them; check directly.
- **Thayer confirms the idiom rival for *ἐκβολή*.** The word is classical Greek for jettisoning cargo in a storm, so its presence in Acts 27:18 needs no Jonah behind it. *κουφίζω* of a ship has no extra-biblical evidence on record here.
- **History for the Jonah link is present but from mid-tier works** (a Wesleyan commentary and a geographic commentary). Thompson names exactly the two words the audit found rare in Swete.

**Effect:** A18 stays **moderate, uncertain**. The idiom rival is now evidenced for *ἐκβολή* `[S: Thayer 193]`. History of interpretation for Jonah 1 is present `[S: Thompson 409; Laney 422]`. To settle it, open BDAG at *ἐκβολή* and *κουφίζω* directly; this is the Exegetical Guide's job and costs no credits.

## 7. The two Amos citations (A0, A27)

**Asked:** Which commentators discuss a connection between Amos 5:25–27 at Acts 7:42–43 and Amos 9:11–12 at Acts 15:16–18, for example through *σκηνή* or the tent and house sequence of 7:43–47? What do they argue, and does anyone reject the link? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **B. J. Tabb, *After Emmaus* (Crossway, 2021), 160.**
  - Two readings of "the tent of David" at 15:16: the restored eschatological temple, where *σκηνή* is the tabernacle word of 7:43–44 (attributed to Richard Bauckham), or David's dynasty (Edward Glenny's counter, appealing to the "house" promise).
- Strauss and Longman (eds), *Baker Expository Dictionary of Biblical Words*, 800. *σκηνή* at 15:16 refers to David's line.

**Assessment:**
- **No source treats the two citations as a designed pair.** The nearest is Bauckham's temple reading of 15:16 (reported second-hand by Tabb). That reading uses 7:43–44's tent language to interpret 15:16, which is the audit's tent-sequence reading (A27) seen from the other end.
- Glenny's dynastic reading is a live alternative. On it, the "tent" of 15:16 is David's house in the sense of 2 Sam 7, and the link to 7:43–47 weakens.

**Effect:** A0 / A27 stays **moderate**, with history **indirect** `[S: Bauckham via Tabb 160; contra Glenny]`. The overview's restated row should say that 15:16's "tent" is read both as temple (Bauckham) and as dynasty (Glenny), and that only the first supports the link.

## 8. The variant at Acts 7:46

**Asked:** What do the Lexham Textual Notes and the critical commentaries say about *οἴκῳ Ἰακώβ* against *θεῷ Ἰακώβ* at Acts 7:46? Which reading do they prefer, and on what evidence? With page references.

**Cited:**
- Brannan and Loken, *Lexham Textual Notes on the Bible*. The evidence is closely divided; either reading may refer to the temple.
- **P. W. Comfort, *New Testament Text and Translation Commentary* (Tyndale, 2008), 360.**
  - *οἴκῳ* has the stronger documentary support (especially B with D) and is the harder reading, departing from Ps 131:5 (Greek).
  - *θεῷ* has wider spread, fits v. 47, and agrees with the Psalm.
  - English versions divide.
- **Westcott and Hort, *Appendix*, 92.** *θεῷ* is an ancient correction of a primitive error in *οἴκῳ*.
- T. K. Seim, *The Double Message* (2004), 140–141. An interpretive note on the tent and house contrast.

**Assessment against the edition:**
- The NA28 apparatus gives *θεῷ* with the corrector of Sinaiticus, A, C, E, Ψ, 33, 81, the Byzantine text, the Latin, the Syriac and the Coptic, and marks the Ps 132:5 allusion.
- NA28 prints *οἴκῳ*, the reading of 𝔓⁷⁴, the first hand of Sinaiticus, B and D.
- The answer's statement that "modern scholars (Metzger, Comfort, Omanson, NA27) note the Psalm 132:5 allusion supports *θεῷ*" mixes two things: the allusion is noted by all; **the printed text (NA27 and NA28) is *οἴκῳ***. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* is not owned, so this is reported second-hand through the Lexham notes.

**Effect:** The audit's note stands. NA28 prints *οἴκῳ* (the harder reading, early and weighty witnesses); the SBLGNT prints *θεῷ*; and the Ps 131:5 (Greek) echo holds on either reading and is verbatim only on *θεῷ*. History `[S: Comfort 360; WH Appendix 92; Lexham]`. The 6:8–7:60 dig should give both readings under Textual Variants and say that the argument of 7:46–50 stands under either.

## 9. Follow-up: the lexicons on ἐκβολή and κουφίζω (A18)

**Method:** the Exegetical Guide at Acts 27:18 and 27:38, opening the lexicon links (no AI credits).

**Which lexicons are available:**
- For *ἐκβολή* the Guide lists LSJ, Louw–Nida, DBL Greek, EDNT, the NASB dictionaries, Mounce and several smaller lexicons.
- **BDAG and Moulton–Milligan do not appear in either word's list.** They are probably not owned; confirm in the library before treating that as certain.

**LSJ, *ἐκβολή*, sense I.2:** "*jettisoning* of cargo".
- Classical citations: Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 769 (lyric); Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1110a9.
- Then Acts 27:18.
- LSJ also records a papyrus, Sammelbuch 1207, where the word means simply *unloading*.

**LSJ, *κουφίζω*, sense II.2:** "*lighten of* a load". Used absolutely, "*lighten* ships *of their cargo*", with these citations:
- Thucydides 6.34 (*κουφίσαντες*);
- Polybius 20.5.11 (*κουφισθεισῶν τῶν νεῶν*), compare 1.60.8.

**Assessment:**
- **Both words are standard nautical Greek outside the Bible.** *ἐκβολή* for jettisoning in a storm goes back to Aeschylus and Aristotle. *κουφίζω* of lightening ships is in Thucydides and Polybius.
- So the idiom rival to the Jonah 1 contact is now **documented for both words**, in the standard classical lexicon. The two words Acts 27 shares with Jonah 1:5 are what any Greek writer would use for this manoeuvre.
- What Jonah 1:5 still has, and the classical examples do not, is the two together in one storm scene with *σκεῦος* and *διασῴζω*. That is a narrative resemblance, not a lexical marker.

**Effect:** **A18 falls from moderate (uncertain) to low–moderate** `[S: LSJ s.vv.]`. Jonah 1 should be described as a reader's comparison drawn by some commentators (Thompson, Laney), not as an allusion Luke signals by vocabulary. This settles the audit's "Uncertain — flag for research".

## 10. Follow-up: named commentators on 28:18 (A13)

**Asked**, with the scope left at "All books" because the web Study Assistant's scope menu offers no Collections option: What do Craig Keener (*Acts: An Exegetical Commentary*), Darrell Bock (BECNT), David Peterson (PNTC) and Ben Witherington (*Socio-Rhetorical Commentary*) say about Acts 28:18? Does any of them compare it with Pilate's verdicts on Jesus (Luke 23:4, 14–16, 20–22) or with Acts 13:28? Does any of them comment on how this summary relates to Felix (24:26) and Festus (25:9)? With page references.

**Cited:**
- **Keener, *Acts: An Exegetical Commentary*, vol. 4 (Baker Academic, 2015), 3740.**
  - Paul's statement echoes the passion narrative, where Pilate wished to release Jesus. For neither Jesus nor Paul was there a "cause" for death (13:28; 26:31).
  - Read within Luke's narrative, the detention after Festus' recognition of innocence is political. Festus' willingness to release is not narrated before the Jerusalem leaders objected; it is possibly implied at 25:9.
- **Keener, vol. 2, 2067 (on 13:28).**
  - "Found" echoes Pilate's finding no guilt (Luke 23:4, 14), "especially" no "cause for death" (23:22).
  - The authorities sought the "cause" of the charges against Paul (22:24; 23:28; 25:27) and found them baseless (25:18; 28:18).
- **Peterson, *The Acts of the Apostles* (PNTC, 2009), 711.**
  - "The Jews objected" (28:19), as they did when Pilate declared his intention to release Jesus (Luke 23:18).
  - Felix delayed for a bribe (24:26) and as a favour to the Jews (24:27). Festus wished to do them a favour (25:9).
- **Witherington, *The Acts of the Apostles: A Socio-Rhetorical Commentary* (Eerdmans, 1998), 797.**
  - The narrative earlier does **not** say that Felix or Festus wanted to release Paul. Luke 23:15–18 says that Pilate wanted to release Jesus, so there "may be a deliberate echoing of the language of Jesus' trial".
  - The "they" may include Agrippa (26:32). He compares 26:31 with 28:18b: "no reason for death" is said in both, as Pilate said it of Jesus (Luke 23:15).
- **Bock (BECNT):** nothing specific on 28:18 surfaced.

**Caution:** As in question 3, the body of the answer gave different page numbers from the footnotes (Keener 1313 and 1038–1039; Peterson 644–645; Witherington 802–803). The footnote figures are used above. Check them in the volumes before citing.

**Assessment:**
- **History is now strong, and it comes from three critical or major commentaries.**
  - Keener and Witherington both read 28:18 as echoing Jesus' trial before Pilate.
  - Witherington calls it possibly deliberate. He adds the point the 27:1–28:18 dig made under Book-Overview Tensions: Paul's summary goes beyond what the narrative had reported.
  - Keener ties the "cause of death" formula through Luke 23:22 → Acts 13:28 → Paul's hearings.
  - Peterson extends the parallel into 28:19 ("the Jews objected" ~ Luke 23:18).
- **Where they point.** The commentators place the source across **Luke 23:13–22**: 23:4 and 14 (Keener), 23:15–18 (Witherington), 23:18 (Peterson) and 23:22 (Keener). That matches auditor B's finding that Luke 23:13–14 is the best Gospel block and that the motif is spread through the scene rather than tied to 23:20–22 alone.

**Effect:**
- **A13 — revised after the ownership check (see the final section).** The echo of Jesus' trial in Paul's summary was raised **from moderate–high to high** on the three commentaries above, but only Witherington is owned. It stands at **moderate–high**, as a series parallel extending into 28:19 `[T]` + `[S: Keener 2067, 4:3740; Witherington 797; Peterson 711]`.
- The specific source is **Luke 23:13–22 as a scene**, at **moderate–high**. No commentator singles out 23:20–22, so the 27:1–28:18 dig's wording, which did, still needs the audit's correction.
- Witherington's observation that 28:18 says more than the narrative had reported supports the dig's Book-Overview Tension 2.

## 11. Owned commentaries on 28:18–19 and 27:23–25 (A13, A14, A16)

**Scope:** "Your books". Two questions, each naming Barrett (ICC), Marshall (TNTC), Parsons (Paideia), Stott (BST) and Hamilton–Vickers (ESV Expository). Hamilton–Vickers returned nothing on either passage. **All the sources below are owned.**

### On 28:18–19

**Barrett, ICC 2:1239.** Barrett sets Pilate's finding of innocence (Luke 23:4, 15, 22) beside the Roman findings on Paul (23:29; 25:18, 25; 26:31–32).

**Stott, BST 337.** "The same had been true of Jesus. Luke finds a third parallel here." Luke is at pains to show that the Romans could find no fault in either Jesus or Paul.
