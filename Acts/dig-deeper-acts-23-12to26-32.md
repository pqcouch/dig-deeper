### 23:12–22 — The oath and the boy

> **23:12** Γενομένης δὲ ἡμέρας ποιήσαντες συστροφὴν οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι ἀνεθεμάτισαν ἑαυτοὺς λέγοντες μήτε φαγεῖν μήτε πιεῖν ἕως οὗ ἀποκτείνωσιν τὸν Παῦλον. **13** ἦσαν δὲ πλείους τεσσεράκοντα οἱ ταύτην τὴν συνωμοσίαν ποιησάμενοι· **14** οἵτινες προσελθόντες τοῖς ἀρχιερεῦσιν καὶ τοῖς πρεσβυτέροις εἶπαν· Ἀναθέματι ἀνεθεματίσαμεν ἑαυτοὺς μηδενὸς γεύσασθαι ἕως οὗ ἀποκτείνωμεν τὸν Παῦλον. **15** νῦν οὖν ὑμεῖς ἐμφανίσατε τῷ χιλιάρχῳ σὺν τῷ συνεδρίῳ ὅπως καταγάγῃ αὐτὸν εἰς ὑμᾶς ὡς μέλλοντας διαγινώσκειν ἀκριβέστερον τὰ περὶ αὐτοῦ· ἡμεῖς δὲ πρὸ τοῦ ἐγγίσαι αὐτὸν ἕτοιμοί ἐσμεν τοῦ ἀνελεῖν αὐτόν. **16** Ἀκούσας δὲ ὁ υἱὸς τῆς ἀδελφῆς Παύλου τὴν ἐνέδραν παραγενόμενος καὶ εἰσελθὼν εἰς τὴν παρεμβολὴν ἀπήγγειλεν τῷ Παύλῳ. **17** προσκαλεσάμενος δὲ ὁ Παῦλος ἕνα τῶν ἑκατονταρχῶν ἔφη· Τὸν νεανίαν τοῦτον ἄπαγε πρὸς τὸν χιλίαρχον, ἔχει γὰρ ἀπαγγεῖλαί τι αὐτῷ. **18** ὁ μὲν οὖν παραλαβὼν αὐτὸν ἤγαγεν πρὸς τὸν χιλίαρχον καὶ φησίν· Ὁ δέσμιος Παῦλος προσκαλεσάμενός με ἠρώτησεν τοῦτον τὸν νεανίαν ἀγαγεῖν πρὸς σέ, ἔχοντά τι λαλῆσαί σοι. **19** ἐπιλαβόμενος δὲ τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ ὁ χιλίαρχος καὶ ἀναχωρήσας κατ’ ἰδίαν ἐπυνθάνετο· Τί ἐστιν ὃ ἔχεις ἀπαγγεῖλαί μοι; **20** εἶπεν δὲ ὅτι Οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι συνέθεντο τοῦ ἐρωτῆσαί σε ὅπως αὔριον τὸν Παῦλον καταγάγῃς εἰς τὸ συνέδριον ὡς μέλλον τι ἀκριβέστερον πυνθάνεσθαι περὶ αὐτοῦ· **21** σὺ οὖν μὴ πεισθῇς αὐτοῖς, ἐνεδρεύουσιν γὰρ αὐτὸν ἐξ αὐτῶν ἄνδρες πλείους τεσσεράκοντα, οἵτινες ἀνεθεμάτισαν ἑαυτοὺς μήτε φαγεῖν μήτε πιεῖν ἕως οὗ ἀνέλωσιν αὐτόν, καὶ νῦν εἰσιν ἕτοιμοι προσδεχόμενοι τὴν ἀπὸ σοῦ ἐπαγγελίαν. **22** ὁ μὲν οὖν χιλίαρχος ἀπέλυσε τὸν νεανίσκον παραγγείλας μηδενὶ ἐκλαλῆσαι ὅτι ταῦτα ἐνεφάνισας πρὸς ἐμέ.

### 23:23–35 — The transfer and the letter

> **23:23** Καὶ προσκαλεσάμενός τινας δύο τῶν ἑκατονταρχῶν εἶπεν· Ἑτοιμάσατε στρατιώτας διακοσίους ὅπως πορευθῶσιν ἕως Καισαρείας, καὶ ἱππεῖς ἑβδομήκοντα καὶ δεξιολάβους διακοσίους, ἀπὸ τρίτης ὥρας τῆς νυκτός, **24** κτήνη τε παραστῆσαι ἵνα ἐπιβιβάσαντες τὸν Παῦλον διασώσωσι πρὸς Φήλικα τὸν ἡγεμόνα, **25** γράψας ἐπιστολὴν ἔχουσαν τὸν τύπον τοῦτον· **26** Κλαύδιος Λυσίας τῷ κρατίστῳ ἡγεμόνι Φήλικι χαίρειν. **27** τὸν ἄνδρα τοῦτον συλλημφθέντα ὑπὸ τῶν Ἰουδαίων καὶ μέλλοντα ἀναιρεῖσθαι ὑπ’ αὐτῶν ἐπιστὰς σὺν τῷ στρατεύματι ἐξειλάμην, μαθὼν ὅτι Ῥωμαῖός ἐστιν, **28** βουλόμενός τε ἐπιγνῶναι τὴν αἰτίαν δι’ ἣν ἐνεκάλουν αὐτῷ, κατήγαγον εἰς τὸ συνέδριον αὐτῶν. **29** ὃν εὗρον ἐγκαλούμενον περὶ ζητημάτων τοῦ νόμου αὐτῶν, μηδὲν δὲ ἄξιον θανάτου ἢ δεσμῶν ἔχοντα ἔγκλημα. **30** μηνυθείσης δέ μοι ἐπιβουλῆς εἰς τὸν ἄνδρα ἔσεσθαι ἐξαυτῆς ἔπεμψα πρὸς σέ, παραγγείλας καὶ τοῖς κατηγόροις λέγειν πρὸς αὐτὸν ἐπὶ σοῦ. **31** Οἱ μὲν οὖν στρατιῶται κατὰ τὸ διατεταγμένον αὐτοῖς ἀναλαβόντες τὸν Παῦλον ἤγαγον διὰ νυκτὸς εἰς τὴν Ἀντιπατρίδα· **32** τῇ δὲ ἐπαύριον ἐάσαντες τοὺς ἱππεῖς ἀπέρχεσθαι σὺν αὐτῷ ὑπέστρεψαν εἰς τὴν παρεμβολήν· **33** οἵτινες εἰσελθόντες εἰς τὴν Καισάρειαν καὶ ἀναδόντες τὴν ἐπιστολὴν τῷ ἡγεμόνι παρέστησαν καὶ τὸν Παῦλον αὐτῷ. **34** ἀναγνοὺς δὲ καὶ ἐπερωτήσας ἐκ ποίας ἐπαρχείας ἐστὶν καὶ πυθόμενος ὅτι ἀπὸ Κιλικίας, **35** Διακούσομαί σου, ἔφη, ὅταν καὶ οἱ κατήγοροί σου παραγένωνται· κελεύσας ἐν τῷ πραιτωρίῳ τοῦ Ἡρῴδου φυλάσσεσθαι αὐτόν.

> *NASB95:* **23:23** And he called to him two of the centurions and said, “Get two hundred soldiers ready by the third hour of the night to proceed to Caesarea, with seventy horsemen and two hundred spearmen.” **24** They were also to provide mounts to put Paul on and bring him safely to Felix the governor. **25** And he wrote a letter having this form: **26** “Claudius Lysias, to the most excellent governor Felix, greetings. **27** “When this man was arrested by the Jews and was about to be slain by them, I came up to them with the troops and rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman. **28** “And wanting to ascertain the charge for which they were accusing him, I brought him down to their Council; **29** and I found him to be accused over questions about their Law, but under no accusation deserving death or imprisonment. **30** “When I was informed that there would be a plot against the man, I sent him to you at once, also instructing his accusers to bring charges against him before you.” **31** So the soldiers, in accordance with their orders, took Paul and brought him by night to Antipatris. **32** But the next day, leaving the horsemen to go on with him, they returned to the barracks. **33** When these had come to Caesarea and delivered the letter to the governor, they also presented Paul to him. **34** When he had read it, he asked from what province he was, and when he learned that he was from Cilicia, **35** he said, “I will give you a hearing after your accusers arrive also,” giving orders for him to be kept in Herod’s Praetorium.

### 24:1–23 — Felix: accusation and defence

ὁ Τέρτυλλος λέγων· Πολλῆς εἰρήνης τυγχάνοντες διὰ σοῦ καὶ διορθωμάτων γινομένων τῷ ἔθνει τούτῳ διὰ τῆς σῆς προνοίας **3** πάντῃ τε καὶ πανταχοῦ ἀποδεχόμεθα, κράτιστε Φῆλιξ, μετὰ πάσης εὐχαριστίας. **4** ἵνα δὲ μὴ ἐπὶ πλεῖόν σε ἐγκόπτω, παρακαλῶ ἀκοῦσαί σε ἡμῶν συντόμως τῇ σῇ ἐπιεικείᾳ. **5** εὑρόντες γὰρ τὸν ἄνδρα τοῦτον λοιμὸν καὶ κινοῦντα στάσεις πᾶσι τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις τοῖς κατὰ τὴν οἰκουμένην πρωτοστάτην τε τῆς τῶν Ναζωραίων αἱρέσεως, **6** ὃς καὶ τὸ ἱερὸν ἐπείρασεν βεβηλῶσαι, ὃν καὶ ἐκρατήσαμεν, **8** παρ’ οὗ δυνήσῃ αὐτὸς ἀνακρίνας περὶ πάντων τούτων ἐπιγνῶναι ὧν ἡμεῖς κατηγοροῦμεν αὐτοῦ. **9** συνεπέθεντο δὲ καὶ οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι φάσκοντες ταῦτα οὕτως ἔχειν. **10** Ἀπεκρίθη τε ὁ Παῦλος νεύσαντος αὐτῷ τοῦ ἡγεμόνος λέγειν· Ἐκ πολλῶν ἐτῶν ὄντα σε κριτὴν τῷ ἔθνει τούτῳ ἐπιστάμενος εὐθύμως τὰ περὶ ἐμαυτοῦ ἀπολογοῦμαι, **11** δυναμένου σου ἐπιγνῶναι, ὅτι οὐ πλείους εἰσίν μοι ἡμέραι δώδεκα ἀφ’ ἧς ἀνέβην προσκυνήσων εἰς Ἰερουσαλήμ, **12** καὶ οὔτε ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ εὗρόν με πρός τινα διαλεγόμενον ἢ παρεγενόμην καὶ προσφοράς, **18** ἐν αἷς εὗρόν με ἡγνισμένον ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ, οὐ μετὰ ὄχλου οὐδὲ μετὰ θορύβου, **19** τινὲς δὲ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἀσίας Ἰουδαῖοι, οὓς ἔδει ἐπὶ σοῦ παρεῖναι καὶ κατηγορεῖν εἴ τι ἔχοιεν πρὸς ἐμέ, **20** ἢ αὐτοὶ οὗτοι εἰπάτωσαν τί εὗρον ἀδίκημα στάντος μου ἐπὶ τοῦ συνεδρίου **21** ἢ περὶ μιᾶς ταύτης φωνῆς ἧς ἐκέκραξα ἐν αὐτοῖς ἑστὼς ὅτι Περὶ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν ἐγὼ κρίνομαι σήμερον ἐφ’ ὑμῶν. **22** Ἀνεβάλετο δὲ αὐτοὺς ὁ Φῆλιξ, ἀκριβέστερον εἰδὼς τὰ περὶ τῆς ὁδοῦ, εἴπας· Ὅταν Λυσίας ὁ χιλίαρχος καταβῇ διαγνώσομαι τὰ καθ’ ὑμᾶς· **23** διαταξάμενος τῷ ἑκατοντάρχῃ τηρεῖσθαι αὐτὸν ἔχειν ### 24:24–27 — Felix and Drusilla: two years

> **24:24** Μετὰ δὲ ἡμέρας τινὰς παραγενόμενος ὁ Φῆλιξ σὺν Δρουσίλλῃ τῇ ἰδίᾳ γυναικὶ οὔσῃ Ἰουδαίᾳ μετεπέμψατο τὸν Παῦλον καὶ ἤκουσεν αὐτοῦ περὶ τῆς εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν πίστεως. **25** διαλεγομένου δὲ αὐτοῦ περὶ δικαιοσύνης καὶ ἐγκρατείας καὶ τοῦ κρίματος τοῦ μέλλοντος ἔμφοβος γενόμενος ὁ Φῆλιξ ἀπεκρίθη· Τὸ νῦν ἔχον πορεύου, καιρὸν δὲ μεταλαβὼν μετακαλέσομαί σε· **26** ἅμα καὶ ἐλπίζων ὅτι χρήματα δοθήσεται αὐτῷ ὑπὸ τοῦ Παύλου· διὸ καὶ πυκνότερον αὐτὸν μεταπεμπόμενος ὡμίλει αὐτῷ. **27** Διετίας δὲ πληρωθείσης ἔλαβεν διάδοχον ὁ Φῆλιξ Πόρκιον Φῆστον· θέλων τε χάριτα καταθέσθαι τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις ὁ Φῆλιξ κατέλιπε τὸν Παῦλον δεδεμένον.

> *NASB95:* **24:24** But some days later Felix arrived with Drusilla, his wife who was a Jewess, and sent for Paul and heard him speak about faith in Christ Jesus. **25** But as he was discussing righteousness, self-control and the judgment to come, Felix became frightened and said, “Go away for the present, and when I find time I will summon you.” **26** At the same time too, he was hoping that money would be given him by Paul; therefore he also used to send for him quite often and converse with him. **27** But after two years had passed, Felix was succeeded by Porcius Festus, and wishing to do the Jews a favor, Felix left Paul imprisoned.

### 25:1–12 — Festus and the appeal

## Headline Findings

1. **Luke tells Paul's Caesarean trials in the vocabulary of Jesus' trial before Pilate and Herod (Luke 23).** In a chance comparison against every chapter of Luke, Luke 23 is the closest Gospel chapter to the trial narrative at most settings. The links are verbally exact. ***ἀναπέμπω*** ("to send up / send back") occurs in five New Testament verses: three are the Herod episode (Luke 23:7, 11, 15), and the fourth is **Festus at 25:21**, "until I send him up to Caesar". ***ἄξιος* + *θάνατος* + *πράσσω*** ("worthy of death" + "do") occurs in five verses: **Luke 23:15**, the verdict Pilate reports from Herod, then Acts 25:11, 25:25 and 26:31, plus Rom 1:32. ***ἄτοπος*** ("wrong, out of place") is the penitent thief's word for Jesus (Luke 23:41), and Festus uses it of Paul (25:5). ***ἀνακρίνω*** ("to examine judicially") is Pilate's verb (Luke 23:14) before it is Tertullus's (24:8) and Festus's noun (25:26, NT hapax). A Herodian king and a Roman governor then sit together and acquit (26:30–31). `[T]` on every distribution, verified by lemma; `[I]`, **high**, that the parallel is designed. *Surfaced by:* Tools 10 and 11 (Move 4, cross-volume), the Luke-chapter baseline, and Tool 2. The sweep saw the acquittal pattern conceptually; this run grounds it in Luke 23's wording.

2. **Paul's defence before Agrippa (26:2–23) is written in the vocabulary of Luke 1–2.** Against all twenty-four chapters of Luke, at seven rarity settings, **Luke 1 or Luke 2 ranks first every time**, and the two take first and second place at the four strictest settings. These are the links:
   - *φῶς* ("light") + *ἔθνος* ("nation") + *λαός* ("people") in one verse: **Luke 2:32 (Simeon) and Acts 26:23** only in Luke–Acts.
   - *λατρεύω* ("to serve in worship") + "night and day": **Luke 2:37 (Anna) and Acts 26:7**.
   - *περιλάμπω* ("to shine around"): **Luke 2:9 and Acts 26:13**, the only two in the New Testament.
   - *ἀπειθής* ("disobedient") beside *ἐπιστρέφω ἐπὶ θεόν* ("to turn to God"): **Luke 1:16–17**, the Baptist's commission, **and Acts 26:18–20** only.
   - *ὀπτασία* ("vision"): Luke 1:22, 24:23 and Acts 26:19.
   - *ὑπηρέτης* ("servant") with what has been seen: the preface's "eyewitnesses and servants of the word" (Luke 1:2) and Acts 26:16.

   **The hope Paul says he is on trial for is the hope the first temple scenes of volume one were waiting for.** `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, on design, since the baseline is crude (see Open Questions). *Surfaced by:* Tool 10, Tool 11 Move 4, the baseline, and Original Audience. This is new to the Acts library.

3. **The commission in 26:16–18 is told in the commissioning words of the three major prophets, and it is spoken by a man in chains.**
   - ***στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου*** ("stand on your feet") stands **once in Swete, at Ezek 2:1**. The sequence there matches Acts 26:14–17: the prophet falls on his face, hears a voice, is told to stand, and is sent.
   - ***ἐξαιρούμενός σε … εἰς οὓς ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε*** ("rescuing you … to whom I send you") is Jeremiah's call: *πρὸς πάντας οὓς ἐὰν ἐξαποστείλω σε … τοῦ ἐξαιρεῖσθαί σε* (Jer 1:7–8). *ἐξαιρεῖσθαί σε* stands only in Jer 1:8, 1:17, 1:19 and 15:21.
   - ***ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμούς*** ("to open eyes") stands **once in Swete, at Isa 42:7**. The same verse holds the Greek Old Testament's only ***ἐκ δεσμῶν δεδεμένους*** ("those bound in chains"), and **Isa 42:16** holds its only ***τὸ σκότος εἰς φῶς*** ("darkness into light").

   So the Servant's task of opening eyes and bringing prisoners out of their chains is recited by a prisoner who ends his speech "except for these chains" (26:29). The manuscripts noticed: E, 096 and some Vulgate manuscripts read ***τυφλῶν*** ("of the blind") for *αὐτῶν* at 26:18, which supplies Isaiah's word. `[T]` on the Swete and apparatus data; **high** on Ezekiel and Isaiah 42, **moderate–high** on Jeremiah 1. *Surfaced by:* Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Tool 8 (apparatus), and Tool 9. This raises the overview's Isaiah 42 row and adds two prophets to it.

4. **Two "musts" and two claims of "alive" contest the unit, and the narrator settles neither aloud.**
   - The Lord's ***δεῖ*** (23:11, "you must testify at Rome") stands against the crowd's ***μὴ δεῖν αὐτὸν ζῆν μηκέτι*** ("he must not live any longer", 25:24) and against the persecutor's old, self-generated *ἔδοξα ἐμαυτῷ … δεῖν* ("I thought to myself that I must", 26:9). Paul's own *οὗ με δεῖ κρίνεσθαι* ("where I must be judged", 25:10) stands between them.
   - The present infinitive ***ζῆν*** ("to live") stands in five verses of Luke–Acts. Twice it is Jesus alive (Luke 24:23; **Acts 25:19**). Twice it is Paul's enemies saying he must not live (22:22; **25:24**). Once it is the Maltese saying Justice will not let him live (28:4).
   - Jesus is said to be alive (*ζάω*) twice in Acts: by the narrator at **1:3** and by Festus, quoting Paul, at **25:19**.

   `[T]` on every distribution, with *δεῖ* separated from *δέω* ("to bind") by parse; `[I]`, **high**, that the contest is the unit's spine. *Surfaced by:* Tools 3, 10 and 11 (Move 4, M1→M8) and Christological Reading.

5. **The officials' own accounts of the case are shown to be wrong, and the reader is given Theophilus's place in the governor's chair.**
   - Lysias writes that he rescued Paul "having learned that he was a Roman" (23:27). The narrative showed him learning it only when he was about to flog him (22:25–29).
   - Festus tells Agrippa he was "at a loss" (25:20). The narrator had said he wanted to grant the Jews a favour (25:9).
   - Paul's two accounts, by contrast, agree with what the narrative showed (24:17–18 recites three lemmata of 21:24–34).
   - ***κράτιστος*** ("most excellent") stands **four times in the New Testament: Luke 1:3 (Theophilus), then Felix twice (23:26; 24:3) and Festus (26:25)**.
   - Festus confesses he has nothing ***ἀσφαλές*** ("certain") to write (25:26), the preface's own word (Luke 1:4).

   The governors hold the reader's title and lack the reader's promised certainty. `[T]`, **high**, on the distributions and the discrepancies; `[I]`, **moderate–high**, on the Who-Am-I reading. *Surfaced by:* Tools 6, 7, 10 and 15.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** Four things have been set up that this unit now provides.
1. **The Lord's word at 23:11**: *ὡς γὰρ διεμαρτύρω τὰ περὶ ἐμοῦ εἰς Ἰερουσαλὴμ οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην μαρτυρῆσαι* ("as you have testified to the things about me in Jerusalem, so you must also testify at Rome"). That word requires a route from a Jerusalem barracks to Rome, and the book has given Paul no means of travel he controls.
2. **The commission's audience list at 9:15**: *ἐνώπιον ἐθνῶν τε καὶ βασιλέων υἱῶν τε Ἰσραήλ* ("before Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel"). It has never had its middle term. **Agrippa is the only king Paul addresses in Acts** (*βασιλεῦ*, 26:2, 7, 13, 19, 27) `[T]`.
3. **Jesus' warning in Luke 21:12–15**, that his followers would be brought *ἐπὶ βασιλεῖς καὶ ἡγεμόνας* ("before kings and governors") and need not prepare *ἀπολογηθῆναι* ("to make a defence"). The 21:1–36 run found its first half executed at 21:27; its second half has been waiting.
4. **The trial of Jesus in Luke 23**, which 21:1–23:11 has begun to re-enact: the crowd's *Αἶρε* (21:36; 22:22) and Pilate's acquittal formula in Pharisees' mouths (23:9). Three verdicts and a Herodian hearing remain.

**Necessity answer.** The unit exists *here* because the Jerusalem witness is over (23:11 puts it in the past tense, *διεμαρτύρω*). The movement to Rome needs a legal vehicle, and Luke makes that vehicle out of Paul's own appeal (25:11), routed through three Roman hearings that each fail to convict. Without it, 27:24's *Καίσαρί σε δεῖ παραστῆναι* ("you must stand before Caesar") would have no grounding in the narrative, and 28:31's *ἀκωλύτως* ("unhindered") would have no legal sense, because a man under unresolved capital charges is not "unhindered" in any ordinary way. **The unit converts a divine *δεῖ* into a Roman procedure, and on the way it puts on record three of the four verdicts that make the voyage a transfer and not an extradition** `[I]`, high. It also holds the last and fullest account of the Damascus commission, kept for the one audience that includes a king.

**Implication for this passage.** A sermon cannot treat 23:12–26:32 as an interval before the voyage. It is where the book's two necessities, the Lord's and the crowd's (Headline 4), are adjudicated, and where the reader is shown three powerful hearers responding to the gospel. Each of them falls short of what Theophilus is offered (Headline 5).

**Revisit after Tool 11.** The book-usage work sharpens the answer. Isa 49:6 was cited at 13:47 for the turn to the Gentiles, and 26:23's *φῶς … τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* ("light … to the Gentiles") recalls it at the moment the Gentile turn is defended before a king. Isa 60 was used at 20:28 for the overseers God gives; here it supplies the light that kings and nations come to (60:3). **The unit is where the Isaianic Gentile programme of the earlier chapters is put on trial and defended** `[I]`, moderate–high.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book level.** The overview's statement stands: to give a mixed church certainty that the risen Jesus is still acting by his Spirit, through witnesses, under a purpose no opposition can obstruct `[S: overview]`. Two features of this unit serve that purpose most directly. One is the cumulative acquittal: three verdicts, plus 26:32. The other is the repeated failure of the officials to obtain the *ἀσφαλές* the reader is promised (21:34; 22:30; **25:26**). The preface's vocabulary (*κράτιστος*, *ἀσφαλής*, *ἀκριβ-*, *ὑπηρέτης*) gathers in this stretch more densely than anywhere else in Acts `[T]`; see Tool 7.

**Passage level.** Three purposes, in order of weight:
1. **To show the gospel's innocence before Rome on Rome's own terms.** Each official in turn finds nothing *ἄξιον θανάτου* ("worthy of death"). The last verdict is given jointly by a king and a governor (26:30–31) `[T]`.
2. **To show the powerful hearing the gospel, and declining it** in three distinct ways: Felix defers (24:25), Festus dismisses ("you are mad", 26:24), and Agrippa deflects (26:28) `[T]`. The narrator does not comment. The reader is left to see the difference between the hearers and himself.
3. **To bring the commission story to its fullest form.** Only this telling carries the prophetic-call wording (Ezekiel, Jeremiah, Isaiah 42) and the Greek proverb (26:14), and only this telling is told to a king `[T]` (lemma comparison with 9:1–19 and 22:6–16).

**Narrative pacing** `[T]`. Luke spends **one verse on two years** (24:27) and **forty-one verses on one hearing** (25:23–26:32). The plot and the transfer get twenty-four verses; the Felix years get four (24:24–27). The deceleration falls on the speech before the king, and that is where the author wants the reader to stop.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check *(see Phase 0.6 above)*

**Immediately before (21:37–23:11).** The defence to the crowd ends in a demand for Paul's death (22:22). The Sanhedrin divides over resurrection (23:6–10). The night word comes (23:11). The previous run found the unit retrospective by genre, a speech recapitulating earlier narrative, and correspondingly poor in near-range echo `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`.

**Immediately after (27:1–28:16).** The voyage: the first person returns at 27:1, the angel's *δεῖ* comes at 27:24, and the shipwreck follows. **This unit plants heavily into that one** (see Tool 11, Move 4): *διασῴζω* ("bring safely", 23:24) next appears at 27:43–44 and 28:1, 4. The *εὐθυμ-* family ("courage", 24:10) reappears at 27:22, 25, 36. *χαρίζομαι* ("grant", 25:11, 16) reappears at 27:24. Luke 21:18's hair of the head reappears at 27:34. The unit is a hinge as well as a waiting-room.

**Section and book.** The overview's register data puts chapter 21 as the first chapter in Acts where forensic vocabulary overtakes mission vocabulary. This unit is the forensic peak. *ἀπολογέομαι* ("to make a defence") stands in eight Luke–Acts verses, and five of them are in 24:10–26:24 `[T]`. *κατήγορος* ("accuser"): all four NT occurrences are in this unit (23:30, 35; 25:16, 18) `[T]`. *ἔγκλημα* ("charge"): both NT occurrences (23:29; 25:16) `[T]`.

**Widely read out of context.** 24:25 (KJV "a convenient season") and 26:28 (KJV "Almost thou persuadest me") are among the best-known evangelistic texts in the English tradition, and both are usually read without the trial they belong to. See Pitfalls 1 and 2.

**The content of the failure that precedes the unit.** At 23:6–10 the Sanhedrin could not agree on whether there is resurrection, spirit or angel. The specific thing missed is named at 23:6, *περὶ ἐλπίδος καὶ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν* ("concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead"). It becomes Paul's refrain through the unit: *ἀνάστασις νεκρῶν* ("resurrection of the dead") in the genitive stands at 23:6, **24:21** and **26:23**, one in each hearing `[T]`.

**The device is a triptych of hearings with a prologue and a coda** `[I]`, high:

- **Prologue** (23:12–35): a plot and a rescue, with no hearing.
- **Panel 1** (24:1–27): an accuser speaks, Paul speaks, the judge defers. Then a private hearing.
- **Panel 2** (25:1–12): accusers speak without proof (25:7), Paul speaks in one sentence (25:8), the judge proposes Jerusalem, and Paul appeals.
- **Panel 3** (25:13–26:32): no accuser at all. The judge speaks for the accusers, Paul speaks at length, and the court acquits.

**Across the three panels the accusation shrinks and the defence grows** `[T]` by word count. Tertullus has 92 words. The Jerusalem Jews' charges in 25:7 are reported in a single clause with no direct speech. In 25:13–26:32 no accuser speaks at all.

**A second device: the unit is built of retellings, and the retellings are graded** `[T]` (see Tool 6).

| Retelling | By | Faithful to the narrative? |
|---|---|---|
| 23:20–21 | the nephew | **Yes**, matches 23:12–15 point for point, including "more than forty" |
| 23:26–30 | Lysias's letter | **No**, inverts the order of rescue and discovery of citizenship (22:25–29) |
| 24:2–8 | Tertullus | **No**, "tried to desecrate the temple" against 21:28–29's mistaken supposition |
| 24:10–21; 26:2–23 | Paul | **Yes**; 24:17–18 recites three lemmata of 21:24–34 `[S: 21:1–36 run; T re-verified]` |
| 25:14–21; 25:24–27 | Festus | **Partly**; omits the favour-motive the narrator gave (25:9), substitutes "at a loss" (25:20), and inflates the complainants to *ἅπαν τὸ πλῆθος τῶν Ἰουδαίων* ("the whole multitude of the Jews", 25:24) |

**Inclusio within the commission** `[T]`. *ἐκ τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ ἐκ τῶν ἐθνῶν* ("from the people and from the Gentiles", 26:17) is answered by *τῷ τε λαῷ καὶ τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* ("both to the people and to the Gentiles", 26:23). These are the only two *λαός* in the unit, and the speech is closed between them.

**Clause-flow layout.** Not run on the narrative. For the speech, the main clauses carry the structure: *ἥγημαι ἐμαυτὸν μακάριον* ("I consider myself fortunate", 26:2), *ἕστηκα κρινόμενος* ("I stand being judged", 26:6), *οὐκ ἐγενόμην ἀπειθής* ("I was not disobedient", 26:19), and *ἕστηκα μαρτυρόμενος* ("I stand testifying", 26:22). **The speech is hinged on two *ἕστηκα* ("I stand"), on trial (26:6) and testifying (26:22)** `[T]`, and between them stands the Lord's *στῆθι* ("stand", 26:16). *ἵστημι* ("to stand") occurs seven times in the unit (24:20, 21; 25:10, 18; 26:6, 16, 22) `[T]`.

### 4. Linking Words

- **23:15 *νῦν οὖν*** ("now therefore") → the plot's logic: oath, therefore request. **23:21 *σὺ οὖν μὴ πεισθῇς*** ("so do not be persuaded") repeats the *οὖν* against the plotters, and the nephew turns their inference round.
- **23:16 *Ἀκούσας δέ*** ("but hearing"). The *δέ* is the whole providence of the episode. A boy hears, and the forty never kill. There is no other connective to God in the scene (Tool 6).
- **24:14 *ὁμολογῶ δὲ τοῦτό σοι ὅτι*** ("but this I admit to you, that"). Paul's one concession is the Way, and he converts it into worship: *οὕτως λατρεύω* ("thus I serve").
- **24:17 *δι' ἐτῶν δὲ πλειόνων*** ("after several years"). The *δέ* moves from creed to conduct.
- **24:26 *ἅμα καὶ ἐλπίζων … διὸ καὶ*** ("at the same time also hoping … therefore also"). **The narrator's *διό* ("therefore") makes money the reason for Felix's frequent conversations** `[T]`. NASB95 keeps "therefore"; ESV renders "so", which is acceptable.
- **25:11 *εἰ μὲν οὖν … εἰ δέ*** ("if then … but if"). A two-branch conditional. The first branch is real and not rhetorical: Paul accepts death if guilty.
- **25:26 *διό … ὅπως*** ("therefore … so that"). Festus convenes the hearing in order to have something to write; the hearing exists for the letter.
- **26:19 *Ὅθεν*** ("wherefore, so then"). The only *ὅθεν* in the speech. The obedience flows from the commission.
- **26:22 *ἐπικουρίας οὖν τυχὼν τῆς ἀπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ*** ("having obtained help from God, therefore"). The *οὖν* infers Paul's survival from God's help, and it is **the only causal clause in the unit that names God as acting** `[T]`. It is in Paul's mouth, not the narrator's (Tool 6).
- **26:32 *εἰ μὴ ἐπεκέκλητο Καίσαρα*** ("if he had not appealed to Caesar"). A contrary-to-fact condition. It states that Paul could have been released; it does not state that the appeal was a mistake.

### 5. Parallels

**N/A as Hebrew poetry.** Three prose parallels matter.

- **Paired hapaxes for Felix and Festus** `[T]`. *Ἀνεβάλετο* ("he adjourned", 24:22; the verb *ἀναβάλλομαι* is an NT hapax) is answered by *ἀναβολὴν μηδεμίαν ποιησάμενος* ("making no adjournment", 25:17; *ἀναβολή* is an NT hapax). Two governors are set against each other by a cognate pair found nowhere else in the New Testament. Festus is the brisker of the two, and he is just as political: the same *χάριτα/χάριν καταθέσθαι* ("to grant a favour") is used of both (24:27; 25:9), and *κατατίθημι* + *χάρις* occurs only in these two verses in the New Testament `[T]`.
- **The two "lefts"** `[T]`. *κατέλιπε τὸν Παῦλον δεδεμένον* ("he left Paul bound", 24:27, narrator) is followed by *καταλελειμμένος ὑπὸ Φήλικος δέσμιος* ("left as a prisoner by Felix", 25:14, Festus). Festus repeats the narrator's verb.
- **The two promises** `[T]`. *προσδεχόμενοι τὴν ἀπὸ σοῦ ἐπαγγελίαν* ("waiting for the promise from you", 23:21) is set against *ἐπ' ἐλπίδι τῆς εἰς τοὺς πατέρας ἡμῶν ἐπαγγελίας* ("for the hope of the promise made to our fathers", 26:6). *ἐπαγγελία* stands eight times in Acts, and **23:21 is the only one that is not God's promise**. The assassins wait on a tribune's promise; the twelve tribes wait on God's. *προσδέχομαι* ("to wait for, expect") is then used of the accusers' own hope at 24:15. See Tool 10.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

This is the unit's most revealing tool.

**(a) What the narrator says.** Eight interventions, all brief, all about motive or capacity:
- the number of the plotters (23:13);
- Felix's accurate knowledge of the Way (24:22);
- Felix's fear (24:25);
- Felix's hope of money (24:26);
- Felix's and Festus's wish to grant a favour (24:27; 25:9);
- the ambush plan (25:3);
- the accusers' inability to prove (25:7, *ἃ οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀποδεῖξαι*, "which they could not prove");
- the pomp (25:23, *μετὰ πολλῆς φαντασίας*, "with great pomp", *φαντασία* an NT hapax).

**Every one is about a human motive, and none is about God** `[T]`.

**(b) What the narrator does not say: a pattern-break absence, with its pattern cited.** In 23:12–26:32:
- ***θεός* ("God") stands nine times, and every one is in speech**: Paul's at 24:14, 15, 16; 26:6, 8, 20, 22, 29, and Jesus' as quoted by Paul at 26:18.
- ***κύριος* ("lord") stands twice: Festus of the emperor (*τῷ κυρίῳ*, 25:26) and Paul quoting the risen Jesus (26:15).**
- ***πνεῦμα* ("spirit") does not occur at all.**

The established pattern that makes this visible:
- The narrator's last *θεός* before the unit is 21:20, and his next is **27:35** (*εὐχαρίστησεν τῷ θεῷ*, "he gave thanks to God").
- The narrator's last *κύριος* is **23:11** (*ὁ κύριος*), and his next is **28:31**.
- *πνεῦμα* occurs in every chapter of Acts from 1 to 21 except 3, 12 and 14, and occurs at 23:8–9 in the Sadducee debate. **Chapters 24–27 are the only run of more than one consecutive chapter in Acts without the word**. The Holy Spirit is last named at 21:11 and next at 28:25.

All of these were verified by lemma with positive controls: the same searches return 24:14, 23:11 and 23:8 respectively. `[T]` on the data; **moderate** (the cap for an absence) on its significance.

**What it may mean**, offered, not asserted. M8 opens with the narrator's last sight of the Lord standing by Paul (23:11). For three and a half chapters God is then present only in Paul's mouth, while the narration shows a nephew who happens to overhear, a tribune who happens to act, a governor's greed and another's haste. **It is a hidden-providence narrative of Esther's kind, with 23:11 carrying the unit's whole divine weight** `[I]`, moderate. The tradition noticed the gap: the Harclean Syriac margin at 26:1 adds that Paul spoke *"confident and receiving consolation in the Holy Spirit"* (NA28 apparatus). Scribes put back the Spirit Luke had left out, which is honesty note 5 working as a commentary.

**(c) The narrator lets officials be caught out.** This is the comment by juxtaposition that Luke favours here.
- **Lysias's letter (23:27)**: *ἐξειλάμην, μαθὼν ὅτι Ῥωμαῖός ἐστιν* ("I rescued him, having learned that he was a Roman"). In fact he learned it while preparing to examine Paul by scourging (22:24–29). The reader has seen both. `[T]`, high. 𝔓⁴⁸ and gig read *κράζοντα καὶ λέγοντα εἶναι Ῥωμαῖον* ("crying out and saying that he was a Roman"), which gives Lysias a better alibi. That looks like a scribe smoothing the lie.
- **Festus (25:20)**: *ἀπορούμενος δὲ ἐγώ* ("being at a loss") against the narrator's *θέλων τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις χάριν καταθέσθαι* ("wishing to grant the Jews a favour", 25:9). `[T]`, high.
- **Festus (25:24)**: *ἅπαν τὸ πλῆθος τῶν Ἰουδαίων* ("the whole multitude of the Jews"), where the narration had "the chief priests and the leading men" (25:2). `[T]`.

**Character speech versus narrator.** Tertullus's flattery (24:2–3) is character speech. The narrator never confirms Felix's "much peace" or his reforms; 24:26–27 tells against them.

Triage: most are scenery. **Candidates for canonical freight:** forty, Saul, Satan, Herod's praetorium, Caesarea, the twelve tribes, Christian.

- **Forty (*τεσσεράκοντα*, 23:13, 21)** `[T]`. Nine Luke–Acts verses: Luke 4:2; Acts 1:3; 4:22; 7:30, 36, 42; 13:21; 23:13, 21. Forty is the number of testing, of wilderness and of Moses' years. Here it is the size of a murder pact that fails. ***πλείους/πλειόνων τεσσεράκοντα*** ("more than forty") stands at **4:22**, the age of the man healed at the Beautiful Gate whose healing started the first hearing before the chief priests, **and at 23:13, 21** `[T]`. *Uncertain, consider verifying*: an attractive inversion, but "more than forty" is ordinary Greek. Not load-bearing.
- **Saul (26:14)** `[T]`. *Σαοὺλ Σαούλ* is the Hebrew form, kept in all three tellings (9:4; 22:7; 26:14), and here it is explicitly said to be spoken *τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ* ("in the Hebrew dialect"). The name of Israel's rejected king, spoken in Hebrew, to a man who then hears a Greek proverb. *Dossier* (load-the-story rule; 1 Sam 9–31 re-read in outline): the tall Benjaminite chosen, disobedient, rejected, hunting David. **Paul is of Benjamin** (Rom 11:1; Phil 3:5 `[S: Pauline letters, not Acts]`; in Acts only 13:21 names Benjamin, of King Saul). *Moderate*; the overview's Tool 7 example already holds this.
- **Satan (26:18)** `[T]`. *Σατανᾶς* stands twice in Acts: **5:3**, Satan filling Ananias's heart inside the church, and **26:18**, Satan's *ἐξουσία* ("authority") from which the Gentiles turn. *ἐξουσία* stands seven times in Acts, and **26:10, 26:12 and 26:18 use it in one speech for the chief priests' authority Paul carried and for Satan's**. 9:14 is the fourth instance, again the chief priests'. `[T]`; the juxtaposition `[I]`, moderate–high. Both NASB95 ("dominion") and ESV ("power") break the chain at 26:18 (Tool 8).
- **"Herod's praetorium" (23:35)** `[T]`. Paul is kept in a building named for the family whose current head will hear him at 25:23. *Ἡρῴδης* in Acts: 4:27 (with Pilate, against Jesus, the Ps 2 reading), 12:1–21, 13:1, 23:35. Agrippa's family relation to the Herod of ch. 12 is **not stated in Acts** `[S: Josephus]`.
- **Caesarea** `[T]`. Fifteen Acts verses. **Cornelius's city** (10:1, 24; 11:11), and the scene of Felix's fear. See Tool 11, Move 4, for the Cornelius/Felix inversion.
- **The twelve tribes (*τὸ δωδεκάφυλον*, 26:7)** `[T]`. An NT hapax. Paul speaks of Israel as still twelve tribes, worshipping night and day. Anna of the tribe of Asher (Luke 2:36), a northern tribe, is the canonical antecedent in Luke–Acts (Headline 2).
- **Christian (*Χριστιανός*, 26:28)** `[T]`. Three NT occurrences: 11:26 (Antioch's coinage), 26:28 (Agrippa), 1 Pet 4:16 (suffering "as a Christian"). In both Acts uses the name is an outsider's.

**Big Bible words and familiar words redefined.**

- ***ἐπικαλέομαι*** ("to call upon; to appeal to") `[T]`. In the middle voice in Acts it is used five times of calling on the Lord's name (2:21; 7:59; 9:14; 9:21; 22:16) and then six times of appealing to Caesar (25:11, 12, 21, 25; 26:32; 28:19). **The last "call on the name" in Acts is Paul's own baptism (22:16), and the next middle *ἐπικαλέομαι* is *Καίσαρα ἐπικαλοῦμαι*** (25:11). The sweep found this `[S: sweep]`, and this run re-derived it independently before reading the sweep. *Moderate*, as the sweep said, on whether Luke means anything by it. It could be pure legal idiom: *ἐπικαλέομαι* + Caesar is the technical term for appeal `[S: standard]`. What the text supports is narrower: **Luke does not censure the appeal**, since 27:24 confirms "you must stand before Caesar". The pun, if it is one, is irony at the expense of a world in which a man must call on Caesar to reach the place where he will call on Christ's name. `[I]`, moderate.
- ***χάρις*** ("grace, favour") `[T]`. Seventeen Acts verses. The last three are **24:27, 25:3 and 25:9**, all political favour: Felix and Festus "granting a favour" to the Jews, and the chief priests "asking a favour against him". Before 24:27 the word is God's grace (4:33; 11:23; 13:43; 14:3, 26; 15:11, 40; 18:27; **20:24, 20:32**, "the gospel of the grace of God", "the word of his grace") or favour before God and people (2:47; 7:10, 46). **Acts' grace-word ends as a currency of patronage** `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, that the irony is felt. The related verb ***χαρίζομαι*** ("to grant, hand over as a favour") runs a matching course `[T]`:
  - **3:14**: *ᾐτήσασθε ἄνδρα φονέα χαρισθῆναι ὑμῖν* ("you asked for a murderer to be granted to you"), Barabbas;
  - **25:11**: *οὐδείς με δύναται αὐτοῖς χαρίσασθαι* ("no one can hand me over to them");
  - **25:16**: Roman custom forbids it;
  - **27:24**: *κεχάρισταί σοι ὁ θεὸς πάντας τοὺς πλέοντας μετὰ σοῦ* ("God has granted you all who sail with you").

  Four Acts verses. Jerusalem had a murderer granted to it; it asks for Paul granted against him; Rome refuses; and **God grants Paul two hundred and seventy-six lives**. `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate–high. Both English versions lose the root at 25:11 and 25:16 (Tool 8).
- ***ἀσφαλής*** ("certain, secure") at 25:26 `[T]`. The third Roman failure to get "the certain thing" (21:34; 22:30; 25:26). The thread is settled in the library at moderate–high `[S: logos assessment, 11 Oct]`; this unit supplies its last term.
- ***κράτιστος*** ("most excellent") `[T]`, see Headline 5. Four NT occurrences. **Not previously noted in the Acts library** (checked at Phase 5.5).
- ***ἀκριβέστερον*** ("more accurately"; the comparative adverb is its own lemma) `[T]`. Four NT occurrences, all Acts: 18:26, 23:15, 23:20 and 24:22. **18:26 and 24:22 are the only two that join it to *ὁδός* ("the Way")**. Priscilla and Aquila *ἀκριβέστερον αὐτῷ ἐξέθεντο τὴν ὁδὸν τοῦ θεοῦ* ("explained the way of God to him more accurately", Apollos), and Felix is *ἀκριβέστερον εἰδὼς τὰ περὶ τῆς ὁδοῦ* ("knowing more accurately the things concerning the Way"). **Apollos was taught the Way more accurately and preached it; Felix knew it more accurately and adjourned** `[T]`; `[I]`, high. The superlative *ἀκριβεστάτην* ("strictest", 26:5) is Paul's word for the Pharisees, and *ἀκριβής* occurs only here in the New Testament. With 22:3's *ἀκρίβεια*, the family runs through the preface (Luke 1:3 *ἀκριβῶς*) and into the trials.
- ***μαίνομαι* / *μανία* / *ἐμμαίνομαι*** ("to be mad" / "madness" / "to rage against") `[T]`. *μαίνομαι* stands in Acts only at **12:15** and **26:24–25**. At 12:15 the household tells Rhoda *Μαίνῃ* ("you are mad") when she reports that Peter, freed from his chains, is at the gate. At 26:24 Festus tells Paul *Μαίνῃ, Παῦλε* ("you are mad, Paul") when he reports a man raised from the dead. **The same second-person form, *Μαίνῃ*, in both** `[T]`. And **26:11's *περισσῶς ἐμμαινόμενος*** ("being furiously enraged", NT hapax) is Paul's own confession that he was the madman, against the saints. Festus's charge comes twelve verses after Paul's admission, so the mania has changed sides. `[T]`; `[I]`, high. The overview's existing row (*ἀποφθέγγομαι* "to speak out" 2:13/26:25, the hearers calling the speaker deranged) is confirmed and extended by 12:15.
- ***ὑπηρέτης*** ("servant, assistant") at 26:16 `[T]`. Luke–Acts: Luke 1:2; 4:20; Acts 5:22, 26; 13:5; 26:16. **Luke 1:2's *αὐτόπται καὶ ὑπηρέται … τοῦ λόγου*** ("eyewitnesses and servants of the word") is the only other place where the word joins seeing. Paul is appointed *ὑπηρέτην καὶ μάρτυρα ὧν τε εἶδές με* ("servant and witness of the things in which you have seen me"), the preface's guarantor-role given to a man who was not with Jesus from the beginning. `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate–high. ESV and NASB95 cross each other here: each renders Luke 1:2 and Acts 26:16 differently (Tool 8).
- ***ἐξαιρέω*** ("to rescue, deliver") `[T]`. Five Acts verses: 7:10 (God rescues Joseph), **7:34** (*κατέβην ἐξελέσθαι αὐτούς· καὶ νῦν δεῦρο ἀποστείλω σε*, "I have come down to rescue them; and now come, I will send you", the burning bush), 12:11 (the Lord rescues Peter from Herod), **23:27** (Lysias: "I rescued him"), **26:17** (Jesus: "rescuing you … to whom I send you"). **Only 7:34 and 26:17 join *ἐξαιρέω* to *ἀποστέλλω* ("to send") in Acts**, Moses' commission and Paul's. In between, Lysias claims the verb falsely. `[T]`; `[I]`, high.
- ***διετία*** ("a period of two years") `[T]`. Two NT occurrences: **24:27**, *Διετίας δὲ πληρωθείσης … κατέλιπε τὸν Παῦλον δεδεμένον* ("when two years had passed … he left Paul bound"), and **28:30**, *Ἐνέμεινεν δὲ διετίαν ὅλην* ("he stayed two whole years"), followed by *ἀκωλύτως* ("unhindered"). **Two years bound in Caesarea, two years unhindered in Rome** `[T]`; `[I]`, high. A new bracket between M8 and the book's last verse.

**Name wordplay.** 26:26 *οὐ γάρ ἐστιν ἐν γωνίᾳ πεπραγμένον τοῦτο* ("this has not been done in a corner"). *γωνία* stands twice in Acts, 4:11 (*κεφαλὴν γωνίας*, "the head of the corner", Ps 118:22) and 26:26 `[T]`. *Uncertain*: an idiom (Plato, *Gorgias* 485d, is usually cited `[S: recall, unverified]`) and probably not an echo. Recorded so it is not proposed again without a test.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Dominant register: forensic irony, with a vein of farce, turning at 26:24 into urgency.**

- **Farce in the prologue** `[I]`, high. More than forty men swear an oath of fasting, and a boy undoes it. The tribune takes the boy *by the hand* (23:19, *ἐπιλαβόμενος … τῆς χειρός*). The response is **470 troops** (200 soldiers, 70 horsemen, 200 spearmen; *δεξιολάβος* is an NT hapax) for one prisoner, at the third hour of the night. Luke never reports what became of the oath-takers. The silence is the joke, and it is also a mercy.
- **Rhetorical pomp, deflated.** Tertullus's *captatio* (24:2–4) is overblown: *πολλῆς εἰρήνης … διορθωμάτων … προνοίας … πάντῃ τε καὶ πανταχοῦ … μετὰ πάσης εὐχαριστίας* ("much peace … reforms … providence … in every way and everywhere … with all thankfulness"). Paul's exordium (24:10) is one plain clause. Agrippa and Bernice enter *μετὰ πολλῆς φαντασίας* ("with great pomp") to hear a prisoner. The register satirises display the way Luke 23:11 does Herod's *ἐσθῆτα λαμπράν* ("splendid clothing") `[I]`, moderate.
- **Pathos without self-pity.** 24:27 is the unit's quietest sentence: two years, a successor, a favour, "left Paul bound". No complaint is recorded.
- **The turn to urgency (26:24–29).** Festus shouts (*μεγάλῃ τῇ φωνῇ*, "with a loud voice"). Paul turns from defence to appeal: *πιστεύεις, βασιλεῦ Ἀγρίππα, τοῖς προφήταις; οἶδα ὅτι πιστεύεις* ("do you believe the prophets, King Agrippa? I know that you do"). Then comes a prayer, *εὐξαίμην ἂν τῷ θεῷ* ("I would pray to God"), with *εὔχομαι* occurring only here and 27:29 in Acts, and the closing lightness of *παρεκτὸς τῶν δεσμῶν τούτων* ("except for these chains"). **The prisoner wishes his judges his own condition, minus the chains.** That is the emotional peak of the unit, and its tone is warmth rather than defiance `[I]`, high.
- **Soundtrack.** A courtroom drama scored like a chamber piece: one voice, steady, while the room changes around it.

**For the preacher.** Do not preach this unit as tension and escape. Its feel is a calm man in a chaotic system, and its comedy (the 470, the forty, the governors' motives) is part of the theology of providence. Let the congregation smile at 23:23.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Live sources for this book** `[S: overview v2.5]`: Luke's Gospel; Isaiah (12 uses in 9 texts); the Psalter; Exodus; Deuteronomy; Genesis; Amos. **There is no formal citation anywhere in 23:12–26:32**: *γέγραπται* last stands at 23:5 `[S: overview v2.5]`. Honesty note 3 governs: absence of a formula is not absence of a source.

**Independent candidate-finding.** Before the sweep was opened, the passage's distinctive vocabulary was searched in Swete. The scan tool's New Testament mode (six runs) was run on 26:16–18 and on 26:12–23, with sense-checks on Isa 60, Isa 42, Jer 1 and Ezek 2.
- **For 26:16–18**, Isaiah 49 is the only partner stable across runs (median rank 5). Isa 42 (median 11), Isa 58 (median 9) and Isa 43 (median 16) follow.
- **For 26:12–23 as a whole**, 1–2 Maccabees dominate every run: royal, epistolary and juridical vocabulary. **None of the prophetic texts below ranks in the top ten of the wider field.**

**So the claims below rest on rare phrases, Hays's "volume", not on field density, and the report says so.** The search was then run phrase by phrase, accent-stripped and augment-aware.

**Isaiah 42:6–7, 16 → 26:18** *(high, raised from the overview's moderate–high)*

*Source context.* The first Servant passage (Isa 42:1–9). God holds the Servant's hand and gives him *εἰς διαθήκην γένους* ("as a covenant for the people", 42:6). His task is *ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμοὺς τυφλῶν, ἐξαγαγεῖν ἐκ δεσμῶν δεδεμένους καὶ ἐξ οἴκου φυλακῆς καθημένους ἐν σκότει* ("to open the eyes of the blind, to bring out from chains those who are bound, and from the prison house those who sit in darkness", 42:7). At 42:16, *ποιήσω αὐτοῖς τὸ σκότος εἰς φῶς* ("I will make darkness into light for them"), the promise of a God who leads the blind by a way they did not know (*ἐν ὁδῷ ᾗ οὐκ ἔγνωσαν*). The register is announcement and comfort, against idols (42:8, 17).

**Rarity, all in Swete:**
- ***ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμούς*** ("to open eyes"): **one verse, Isa 42:7**.
- ***ἐκ δεσμῶν δεδεμένους*** ("those bound in chains"): **one verse, Isa 42:7**.
- ***τὸ σκότος εἰς φῶς*** ("darkness into light"): **one verse, Isa 42:16**.
- **Acts 26:18 has the first exactly** (*ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμοὺς αὐτῶν*, the same aorist infinitive of purpose), and the third reordered with *ἀπό* (*ἀπὸ σκότους εἰς φῶς*). The Servant's object *τυφλῶν* ("of the blind") becomes *αὐτῶν* ("their").
- **The manuscripts restore it**: E, 096 and some Vulgate manuscripts read *τυφλῶν* (NA28 apparatus), which is honesty note 5 in its plainest form.

*Book usage.* The sweep placed this as the third Servant text in Acts: Isa 53 at 8:32, Isa 49:6 at 13:47, Isa 42 here `[S: sweep]`. That is confirmed. Note too that **Isa 49:9** has *λέγοντα τοῖς ἐν δεσμοῖς Ἐξέλθατε, καὶ τοῖς ἐν τῷ σκότει* ("saying to those in chains, 'Come out', and to those in darkness"), so the Servant's commission at 13:47 already contained the prisoner motif three verses later in its own chapter `[T]`.

*OT-to-OT.* Isa 42:6 and 49:6 are a pair in Isaiah itself: *εἰς διαθήκην γένους* stands in both, and Isa 49:6 adds *εἰς φῶς ἐθνῶν* ("as a light to the nations"). In Swete, ***φῶς ἐθνῶν*** stands at **Isa 49:6 and 51:4** only `[T]`; Swete's 42:6 lacks the phrase. **So 26:23's *φῶς … τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* draws on Isa 49:6, already cited at 13:47**, and 26:18 draws on 42:7, 16. Paul's last speech takes up the Servant commission he had applied to himself at Pisidian Antioch.

*What it adds.* **The man who recites the Servant's task of freeing the bound is himself bound** (*τῶν δεσμῶν τούτων*, "these chains", 26:29; *δεσμός* at 23:29; 26:29, 31) `[T]`. He was formerly the one who *ἐν φυλακαῖς κατέκλεισα* ("shut up in prisons", 26:10), and Isa 42:7 has *ἐξ οἴκου φυλακῆς* ("from the prison house") `[T]`. **Jailer, then prisoner, then the Servant's agent for prisoners.** The control applies: the Servant frees prisoners; Paul is sent *to open their eyes* (26:18). Release is Christ's to give through the message, not Paul's own achievement.

**Ezekiel 2:1–3 → 26:16–17** *(high)*

*Source context.* Ezekiel's call. After the vision of the glory he falls on his face (*πίπτω ἐπὶ πρόσωπόν μου*) and hears a voice (*ἤκουσα φωνὴν λαλοῦντος*). He is told *Υἱὲ ἀνθρώπου, στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου* ("son of man, stand on your feet", 2:1). The Spirit sets him on his feet (2:2), and he is sent: *ἐξαποστέλλω ἐγὼ σὲ πρὸς τὸν οἶκον τοῦ Ἰσραήλ, τοὺς παραπικραίνοντάς με* ("I send you to the house of Israel, those who embitter me", 2:3). His audience is rebellious and the outcome will not be acceptance (2:4–7).

*Rarity.* ***στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου*** stands in **one verse of Swete, Ezek 2:1** `[T]`. A positive control confirms the search: the phrase returns its own verse, and the New Testament search returns Acts 26:16, plus Acts 14:10 with *ἀνάστηθι* ("rise") in place of *στῆθι*. **The sequence matches**: all fell to the ground (*καταπεσόντων ἡμῶν εἰς τὴν γῆν*), I heard a voice (*ἤκουσα φωνήν*), stand on your feet, I send you (*ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε*). And **𝔓⁷⁴ and C read *ἐξαποστέλλω σε* at 26:17** (NA28 apparatus), Ezekiel's own form `[T]`. *Rival*: Dan 10:11, *στῆθι ἐπὶ τοῦ τόπου σου* ("stand in your place"), is weak, as it is not "on your feet".

*Book usage.* No Ezekiel 2 row in the overview. Ezekiel's only Acts rows are 34 and 22:27, behind 20:28–29.

*OT-to-OT.* See Jeremiah below. The three prophetic calls are already a canonical conversation.

**Jeremiah 1:5–10 → 26:16–17** *(moderate–high)*

*Source context.* Jeremiah's call. He is consecrated before birth and appointed *προφήτην εἰς ἔθνη* ("a prophet to the nations", 1:5). He objects that he is young. The answer is *πρὸς πάντας οὓς ἐὰν ἐξαποστείλω σε πορεύσῃ … μὴ φοβηθῇς … ὅτι μετὰ σοῦ ἐγώ εἰμι τοῦ ἐξαιρεῖσθαί σε* ("to all to whom I send you, you shall go … do not fear … for I am with you to rescue you", 1:7–8). God touches his mouth (1:9) and sets him *ἐπὶ ἔθνη καὶ βασιλείας* ("over nations and kingdoms", 1:10). At 1:19: *πολεμήσουσίν σε … διότι μετὰ σοῦ ἐγώ εἰμι τοῦ ἐξαιρεῖσθαί σε* ("they will fight against you … for I am with you to rescue you").

*Rarity.* ***ἐξαιρεῖσθαί σε*** stands in **four verses of Swete, all Jeremiah**: 1:8, 1:17, 1:19 and 15:21 `[T]`. Acts has the participle, *ἐξαιρούμενός σε* ("rescuing you"), with the sending relative *εἰς οὓς ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε* ("to whom I send you"), matching Jer 1:7's *πρὸς πάντας οὓς ἐὰν ἐξαποστείλω σε* `[T]`. **The rival for the exact form:** *ἐξαιρούμενός σε* stands **once in Swete, Isa 60:16**, which is treated next. *Moderate–high*: the commission context is Jeremiah's, and the exact participle is Isaiah's.

*Book usage.* No Jeremiah row in the overview.

*OT-to-OT.* **Jeremiah's call is itself shaped on Moses'**: the objection of inadequacy (Exod 4:10; Jer 1:6), and *Ἰδοὺ δέδωκα τοὺς λόγους μου εἰς τὸ στόμα σου* ("behold, I have put my words in your mouth", Jer 1:9) answering Exod 4:12 and Deut 18:18 `[S: standard; Deut 18:18 not re-checked in this run]`. And ***προχειρίζομαι*** ("to appoint"), Acts 26:16's verb, stands in **six verses of Swete, and only one is a commissioning: Exod 4:13**, Moses' *προχείρισαι δυνάμενον ἄλλον ὃν ἀποστελεῖς* ("appoint another who is able, whom you will send") `[T]`. So the prophetic chain runs Moses (Exod 3–4), Jeremiah (1), Ezekiel (2). Acts 7:34 has already given Moses' call in Acts' own words, with *ἐξαιρέω* + *ἀποστέλλω* (Tool 7). **26:16–17 re-uses the Moses commission as Stephen told it, through Jeremiah's and Ezekiel's calls.** `[I]`, moderate–high. The Exodus 4:13 link is **moderate**: one verb, six Swete verses.

**Isaiah 60:1–3, 16, 19 → 26:13–18, 23** *(moderate)*

*Source context.* The dawn of Zion's glory. *Φωτίζου φωτίζου … ἥκει γάρ σου τὸ φῶς* ("shine, shine … for your light has come", 60:1). *ἰδοὺ σκότος καλύψει γῆν … ἐπὶ δὲ σὲ φανήσεται Κύριος* ("behold, darkness will cover the earth … but the Lord will appear upon you", 60:2), with *ὀφθήσεται* ("will be seen", 60:2). *καὶ πορεύσονται βασιλεῖς τῷ φωτί σου, καὶ ἔθνη τῇ λαμπρότητί σου* ("and kings will come to your light, and nations to your brightness", 60:3). *ἐγὼ Κύριος ὁ σώζων σε, καὶ ἐξαιρούμενός σε* ("I the Lord am the one who saves you and rescues you", 60:16). God gives *ἐπισκόπους* ("overseers", 60:17). *οὐκ ἔσται σοι ἔτι ὁ ἥλιος εἰς φῶς ἡμέρας … ἀλλ' ἔσται σοι Κύριος φῶς αἰώνιον* ("the sun will no longer be your light by day … but the Lord will be your everlasting light", 60:19).

*Rarity.*
- ***λαμπρότης*** ("brightness"), an NT hapax at 26:13, stands in **six verses of Swete**, and **Isa 60:3 is the only one with kings and nations** `[T]`.
- ***ἐξαιρούμενός σε***: **Isa 60:16 only** `[T]`.
- 26:13's *ὑπὲρ τὴν λαμπρότητα τοῦ ἡλίου … φῶς* ("light … brighter than the sun") meets Isa 60:19's light that surpasses the sun.

The cluster is: light, darkness, "will appear / be seen", kings, nations, brightness, the sun surpassed, "rescuing you". **Spoken by a man before a king, announcing light to the nations.** *Rival:* Dan 12:3 (Theodotion), *λάμψουσιν ὡς ἡ λαμπρότης τοῦ στερεώματος* ("will shine like the brightness of the firmament"), comes immediately after the double resurrection of 12:2, which 24:15 reflects.

*Book usage.* **Isa 60 is already a live source in Acts**: 60:17 behind 20:28 `[S: overview v2.5]`. Recurrence raises the prior.

*OT-to-OT.* Isa 60:3 picks up 49:6's light to the nations and the kings of 49:7 (*βασιλεῖς ὄψονται αὐτόν, καὶ ἀναστήσονται ἄρχοντες*, "kings shall see him, and rulers shall rise") `[T]`. The Isaiah texts here are in conversation with one another.

*What it adds.* If the allusion holds, **Agrippa is a king called to come to the light**, which is what 26:27–29 attempts. *Moderate*: two unique items and a thematic cluster, with no history-of-interpretation check. **The first Logos query of the next round should be whether anyone reads 26:13–23 against Isaiah 60.**

**Luke 2:29–38 and Luke 1:16–17, 68–79 → 26:6–23** *(cross-volume; moderate–high on design)*. See Headline 2 for the data. *Source context.* The infancy narrative: the temple, the hope of Israel, and the inclusion of the nations announced by aged Israelites. Simeon is *προσδεχόμενος παράκλησιν τοῦ Ἰσραήλ* ("waiting for the consolation of Israel", Luke 2:25) and sees *φῶς εἰς ἀποκάλυψιν ἐθνῶν καὶ δόξαν λαοῦ σου Ἰσραήλ* ("a light for revelation to the Gentiles and glory to your people Israel", 2:32). Anna is *νηστείαις καὶ δεήσεσιν λατρεύουσα νύκτα καὶ ἡμέραν* ("serving night and day with fastings and prayers", 2:37) and speaks *πᾶσι τοῖς προσδεχομένοις λύτρωσιν Ἰερουσαλήμ* ("to all those waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem", 2:38). Paul preaches John's message. In a unit whose refrain is "nothing *worthy* of death", the only thing called *ἄξιος* ("worthy") in Paul's mouth is repentance's deeds `[I]`, moderate.

**Luke 23:2–25 → 24:1–26:32** *(cross-volume; high on distribution, high on design)*. See Headline 1. Further items:

- **Pilate and Herod became friends** (Luke 23:12); **Agrippa and Bernice greet Festus** (25:13).
- **Herod "had long wanted to see him"** (Luke 23:8, *θέλων ἰδεῖν αὐτόν*); **Agrippa "would like to hear the man myself"** (25:22). The Harclean margin at 24:24 makes Drusilla ask to see Paul (NA28 apparatus), which is the scribes extending the Herodian pattern.

The trajectory is **contrast, not typology**: the same machinery, the opposite outcome. Pilate *παρέδωκεν τῷ θελήματι αὐτῶν* ("handed him over to their will", Luke 23:25); Festus does not, because *οὐκ ἔστιν ἔθος Ῥωμαίοις χαρίζεσθαί τινα ἄνθρωπον* ("it is not the custom of the Romans to hand over any man", 25:16). `[I]`, high.

**Deuteronomy 13:12–18 (Swete 13:15) → 23:12–14** *(moderate; register rather than allusion, with a named rival)*.
- *Data.* The cognate construction ***ἀναθέματι ἀναθεματ-*** ("to devote with a curse"; the Greek's rendering of the Hebrew infinitive absolute of חָרַם, "to devote to destruction") stands in **two verses of Swete: Deut 13:15 and 20:17** `[T]`. In the New Testament it stands only at **Acts 23:14** (*Ἀναθέματι ἀνεθεματίσαμεν ἑαυτούς*, "we have bound ourselves under a curse") `[T]`.
- *Deut 13:15 (= WLC 13:16)* also has ***ἀναιρῶν ἀνελεῖς*** ("you shall surely kill"), and *ἀνελεῖν* is the plotters' verb at 23:15 `[T]`. Its context is the law of the town that has been led into apostasy by *ἄνδρες παράνομοι* ("lawless men", 13:13), after the law of the prophet who leads Israel *ἀπὸ τῆς ὁδοῦ* ("from the way", 13:5).
- *Rival:* Deut 20:17, the Canaanite nations.
- *What it adds.* **The plotters speak the language of the ban on apostates**, and Paul has been charged with *ἀποστασία* (21:21). They have in effect made themselves *ἀνάθεμα* until they carry out Deut 13 on him. That they bind *themselves* under the ban turns the law on its users `[I]`, moderate. The Hebrew of WLC Deut 13:16 has the infinitive absolute (הַחֲרֵם) and the doubled verb (הַכֵּה תַכֶּה), so the Greek idiom is a faithful rendering. There is no translation loss of substance (triage category 1, nil).

**Deuteronomy 32:15 → 26:14** *(uncertain)*. *ἀπολακτίζω* ("to kick away, kick against") stands in Swete only at **Deut 32:15** (and its repetition in the Odes): *ἀπελάκτισεν ὁ ἠγαπημένος … καὶ ἀπέστη ἀπὸ θεοῦ σωτῆρος αὐτοῦ* ("the beloved kicked … and fell away from God his Saviour") `[T]`. The simple *λακτίζω* is absent from Swete; the stem's other matches are *γαλακτ-* ("milk") words, dropped on a sense-check. **Deut 32 is a live source in Acts (2:40; 17:26)**, and its kicking is apostasy, the very charge brought against Paul. The risen Lord's proverb would then tell the persecutor that *he* was the one kicking like Jeshurun. *Uncertain, consider verifying*: different compound, and **the proverb is Greek** (*πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν*, "to kick against the goads", is usually traced to Euripides' *Bacchae* 794–95, Pindar and Aeschylus `[S: recall, unverified]`). The Greek proverb is the primary frame; Deut 32:15 is a possible secondary resonance for a Jewish hearer.

**Daniel 12:2 → 24:15** *(moderate)*. *ἀνάστασιν … δικαίων τε καὶ ἀδίκων* ("a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous"). *δίκαιος* + *ἄδικος* + *ἀνάστασις* in one verse: **Acts 24:15 only** in the New Testament `[T]`. Dan 12:2 is the Old Testament's one explicit double resurrection, some to life and some to shame, in both Greek texts `[T]` (Swete OG and Theodotion read). Luke 14:14 has *ἐν τῇ ἀναστάσει τῶν δικαίων* ("at the resurrection of the righteous"). *Moderate*: conceptual, no verbal match beyond "resurrection" and "rise".

### 12. Genre

**Historical narrative with three embedded forensic speeches and two embedded documents**: Lysias's letter (23:26–30, introduced as *ἐπιστολὴν ἔχουσαν τὸν τύπον τοῦτον*, "a letter having this form") and Festus's reported briefing.

**Reading rules:**
- The speeches are defences (*ἀπολογία*) and must be read against the charges they answer (24:5–6; 25:7–8).
- The documents are characterised speech and may be wrong (Tool 6).
- The narrator's motive-comments are authoritative.

**The letter's genre-formula** is *Κλαύδιος Λυσίας τῷ κρατίστῳ ἡγεμόνι Φήλικι χαίρειν* ("Claudius Lysias to his Excellency the governor Felix, greetings"), with *χαίρειν* elsewhere in Acts only at 15:23, the Council's letter `[T]`. That makes it an official Roman *elogium*, and Luke's presenting of one is an implicit claim to documentary knowledge `[S: standard]`.

**N/A for apocalyptic and poetry.** The prophetic-call form (Ezek 2; Jer 1) is a sub-genre embedded in the speech (Tool 11).

| Paul's preaching to Felix: righteousness, self-control, judgement (24:25) | **Normative pattern** | The gospel addressed to the hearer's actual life: a governor with a wife taken from another man `[S: Josephus]` hears *ἐγκράτεια* ("self-control") |
| Felix's delay, greed and favour-seeking (24:25–27) | **Negative example** | — |
| Festus's favour-seeking (25:9) | **Negative example** | — |
| Paul's appeal (25:11) | **Unique event**, read by 23:11 and 27:24 as God's route | Do not preach "always appeal" or "never appeal" |
| Paul's willingness to die if guilty (25:11) | **Normative pattern** | Submission to just penalty; no claim to be above law |
| Paul's personal appeal to Agrippa and prayer for all hearers (26:27–29) | **Normative pattern** | Evangelistic boldness with courtesy (*κράτιστε Φῆστε*, "most excellent Festus", 26:25) |
| The heavenly vision (26:13–18) | **Unique event** | Paul's commission; the apostle's call is not the believer's template (Trap 1) |

**Manner-of-the-act check.** The deliverances in this unit are all given passively. Paul does not discover the plot, arrange the escort or secure the verdicts. Even the help of 26:22 is *ἐπικουρίας … τυχών* ("having obtained help"; *τυγχάνω* is "to happen upon, obtain"). The posture the unit implies is **steadfast witness while others act**, not strategy.

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage is.** Inaugurated new covenant; Christ ascended and reigning. The period is roughly AD 57–59/60 by conventional chronology, from Felix's procuratorship to Festus's arrival `[S: standard; moderate]`.

**Where the reader is.** The same age, between ascension and return. Paul's "judgement to come" (24:25) and "resurrection of righteous and unrighteous" (24:15) are still future for us. The reader faces the same choice as Felix, Festus and Agrippa.

**What has happened between.** Rome burned and Nero persecuted (AD 64); the temple fell (AD 70) `[S]`. **A first reader after AD 70 hears 26:21's last *ἱερόν* with the temple gone** `[I]`, moderate. Nothing in the text depends on a date, and the overview's caution applies.

**N/A for Tanak position**, as this is an NT passage. The OT sources cited are Nevi'im (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel: the three Latter Prophets) and Torah (Deut 13; 32; Exod 4). **The three prophets stand in 26:16–18 in the reverse of their Tanak order**: Ezekiel (26:16), Jeremiah (26:17), Isaiah (26:18) `[T]`. This is recorded as an observation only; no claim is made for it.

### 15. Who Am I?

| Character / group | Function |
|---|---|
| **Paul** | **Witness, not type.** His road runs where Jesus' ran (Luke 23), but he does not do Jesus' work; the escalation test fails (Christological Reading). An example of faithfulness under process where the text commends it (24:16; 26:19) |
| **The risen Jesus** (26:14–18, 23) | **The acting subject**: he appears, appoints, rescues, sends, and *himself* proclaims light (26:23) |
| **The forty; the chief priests** | Foil. Their oath collapses without notice |
| **The nephew; the tribune; the centurions** | Instruments of providence; incidental |
| **Felix, Festus, Agrippa** | **The hearers, and the reader's mirror.** Each is addressed with honour (*κράτιστε*, "most excellent"; *βασιλεῦ*, "O king"). Each hears the gospel. Each answers it in a way the reader is meant to see through: delay, dismissal, deflection |
| **"All who hear me today" (26:29)** | **The reader**, and Paul's prayer includes him |

**The sharpest Who-Am-I finding** `[I]`, moderate–high. **The reader is not first of all Paul; he is the "most excellent" outsider in the judge's seat.** Theophilus, the one other *κράτιστος* in the New Testament (Luke 1:3), has been promised *ἀσφάλεια* ("certainty", Luke 1:4). Festus, given the same case, has nothing *ἀσφαλές* to write (25:26). Agrippa, given the same prophets, will not say whether he believes them (26:27–28). **Luke seats his reader where the governors sat and asks him to do better.** Christians reading it are placed with Paul's prayer: that all hearers become *τοιούτους ὁποῖος καὶ ἐγώ εἰμι* ("such as I am").

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1. The author's intended response.** Certainty about the gospel's innocence and truth, against the reputational and political pressure that keeps the powerful from believing. The response is a decision about Jesus "whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19) that does not wait for a convenient season, does not dismiss resurrection as madness, and does not deflect by irony.

**Stage 2.**
- **Worldview.** God's purposes travel through procedures, protocols, letters and adjournments, and the narrator can tell three and a half chapters without naming God while every turn serves 23:11. **Providence is not suspended when God is not mentioned.**
- **Stop.** Treating the gospel as a matter for later (Felix); treating resurrection as an embarrassment of learning (Festus); hiding behind half-serious engagement (Agrippa); seeking favour by compromise (both governors).
- **Start.** Speaking plainly before people with power over us (24:10; 26:2); praying for our hearers' conversion (26:29); keeping a clear conscience before God and people (24:16).
- **Motive.** Not success. Paul's hearers are not converted. The motive is that *Christ* proclaims light through his witness (26:23) and that the help is God's (26:22).

**Four audiences.**
- **For me:** Where am I Felix: informed (*ἀκριβέστερον*, "more accurately"), afraid (*ἔμφοβος*), and deferring?
- **For a Christian friend under false accusation:** Paul's calm, and the Lord's *θάρσει* ("take courage") at 23:11 standing behind it.
- **For the church:** A movement repeatedly found innocent by its judges should not behave as though it were guilty, nor as though it needed the judges' favour.
- **For the unbeliever:** "This has not been done in a corner" (26:26). The claims are public and historical, and examinable.

**Prayer.** Thank you that the risen Christ still proclaims light to the people and to the nations. Forgive us our convenient seasons. Give us Paul's plainness and his prayer, that all who hear might become what we are, except for these chains.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting**
- **Time period:** c. AD 57–59/60 `[S: standard chronology; moderate]`.
- **Location:** Jerusalem's Antonia barracks, then by night to Antipatris (about 35–40 miles), then Caesarea Maritima, the Roman provincial capital (*πραιτώριον τοῦ Ἡρῴδου*, Herod the Great's palace) `[S]`.
- **Political context:**
  - **Felix**, a freedman procurator, notorious for brutality `[S: Tacitus, *Hist.* 5.9; *Ann.* 12.54; recall]`.
  - **Drusilla**, a daughter of Agrippa I, who left her husband Azizus of Emesa for Felix `[S: Josephus, *Ant.* 20.141–44]`.
  - **Festus**, an apparently brisker administrator who died in office `[S: Josephus]`.
  - **Agrippa II**, son of Agrippa I (the Herod of Acts 12), and **Bernice**, his sister, whose companionship attracted scandal `[S: Josephus; Juvenal]`.
  - **Ananias** the high priest (24:1), a man of violent reputation `[S: Josephus]`.

**What the original audience knew**
1. **Roman citizens' rights**: a citizen was protected from summary flogging, and could ask for transfer to the emperor's jurisdiction. The precise procedure in the 50s (*provocatio* vs *appellatio*) is debated `[S: Sherwin-White, recall; uncertain]`. "I appeal to Caesar" was a known, weighty step.
2. **Forum domicilii**: 23:34's question *ἐκ ποίας ἐπαρχείας* ("from what province") reflects whether a case could be referred to the defendant's home province `[S: Sherwin-White; moderate]`. Cilicia, then attached to Syria, was not worth the trouble.
3. **The Herodians**: a reader would know Agrippa as a Herodian, and the family's record (Herod the Great; Antipas and John the Baptist; Agrippa I and James) `[S]`. Acts itself names only Herod in 4:27, 12 and 23:35, so the reader brings the genealogy.
4. ***κύριος* of the emperor** (25:26): the title was coming into use for Nero `[S: standard; moderate]`. Christians confessed Jesus as Lord.
5. **Rhetorical convention**: Tertullus's *captatio benevolentiae* is textbook, and Paul's brevity would be noticed `[S]`.
6. **Bribery of governors** was common enough to be expected (24:26) `[S]`.

**What this changes about how we read**
- Drusilla's presence makes *ἐγκράτεια* ("self-control", 24:25) pointed preaching, not abstract ethics. The original hearers knew the story; we need to be told it, and it must be marked `[S]`.
- The appeal is not a desperate gamble but an established right, which supports reading it as the lawful route by which 23:11 is fulfilled.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience**
- **Canonical section:** NT, volume two of Luke–Acts, read after the Gospel.
- **What the canonical reader knows:** Luke 23's trials before Pilate and Herod; Luke 1–2's temple hopes; Luke 21's warnings. A reader of the Gospel hears all three throughout this unit (Headlines 1–2).
- **Canonical register:** the Greek Old Testament, especially Isaiah and the prophetic calls.

**The first hearers**
- **Who they were:** Theophilus, a "most excellent" person, probably of standing `[I]`, and a mixed Jewish–Gentile church `[I]`.
- **Their situation:** a movement repeatedly before courts, accused of sedition (*στάσις*), sectarianism (*αἵρεσις*) and temple desecration.
- **What they brought:** anxiety about legality and respectability; perhaps a sense of shame at a founder executed and a leading apostle long in chains.

**Where the passage fits their world**
1. **Officials of high rank find nothing.** This is the evidence a person of standing would weigh.
2. **The gospel is preached to the powerful without flattery**, and they decline it for reasons the reader can see: money, favour, embarrassment.
3. **The hope is Israel's own**: the twelve tribes, the prophets, Moses.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances**
- **Shocking:** a Herodian king and a Roman governor agree that the man is innocent, and the system still keeps him in chains (26:32).
- **Surprising:** the most powerful hearer is the most evasive; the prisoner is the freest man in the room.
- **Comforting:** "having obtained help from God, I stand to this day" (26:22); the verdicts; 23:11 behind it all.
- **Disturbing:** "a convenient season" is a real choice with real consequences. Felix is never heard of again in Acts after 25:14.

**What we bring that they didn't:** Individualist conversion-testimony expectations (Trap 4). The KJV's "almost persuaded". A modern assumption that legal process is neutral, where they knew it ran on favour.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

**1. The prophetic commission and the Servant's light to the nations**
- **Where this passage sits:** the commissioning of a witness who extends the Servant's mission (Isa 42:6–7, 16; 49:6) in the prophetic-call form (Exod 3–4; Jer 1; Ezek 2).
- **Before:** Moses sent to Pharaoh, a king; Jeremiah set over nations and kingdoms; Ezekiel sent to a rebellious house; the Servant given as light to the nations.
- **After:** the word unhindered in Rome (28:31); Isaiah 6 at 28:26–27.
- **What it contributes:** Paul's call is a prophet's call, so resistance (26:21) is expected, and the result belongs to God.
- **Confidence:** high.

**2. The hope of Israel and the resurrection**
- **Where:** the hope of the promise to the fathers (26:6–7), focused in the resurrection of the dead (23:6; 24:15, 21; 26:8, 23), and in Christ as the first to rise (26:23).
- **Before:** Dan 12:2; the infancy hopes of Luke 1–2 (Simeon, Anna, Zechariah).
- **After:** 28:20, *ἕνεκεν τῆς ἐλπίδος τοῦ Ἰσραήλ* ("for the sake of the hope of Israel"), the chain; consummation.
- **What it contributes:** the Christian claim is the fulfilment of Israel's hope, not a deviation. That is the apologetic the trials require.
- **Confidence:** high.

**3. The kingdoms of the world and the Lord's purpose (trial before kings)**
- **Where:** God's servant before governors and a king (Luke 21:12; Acts 9:15), with the rulers of Ps 2 (Acts 4:25–27: Herod and Pilate) now acquitting rather than conspiring.
- **Before:** Ps 2; Dan 1–6 (servants before kings); Luke 23.
- **After:** Caesar (27:24).
- **What it contributes:** the powers cannot convict the gospel and cannot stop it. Pilate–Herod collusion (Luke 23:12; Acts 4:27) is answered by Festus–Agrippa acquittal (26:30–31) `[I]`, moderate–high.
- **Confidence:** moderate–high.

**Confluence: (c) climactic convergence.** At 26:23 the three themes meet. *ὁ χριστός*, the Christ (the hope), is *πρῶτος ἐξ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν* ("first from the resurrection of the dead", the resurrection), and *φῶς μέλλει καταγγέλλειν τῷ τε λαῷ καὶ τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* ("will proclaim light to the people and to the Gentiles", the Servant's light). This is spoken before a king and a governor (the trial). **The risen Christ is the Servant whose light reaches the nations through a prisoner's testimony before their rulers.**

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection.** For an NT passage: **trajectory and contrast**, not typology, in the Luke 23 parallel. **Christ as acting subject** in the commission and in 26:23.

**How the passage points to Christ.**
1. **The risen Jesus acts in the unit's centre.** He appears (*ὤφθην*), appoints (*προχειρίσασθαι*), rescues (*ἐξαιρούμενος*) and sends (*ἀποστέλλω*) in 26:16–17. He is the one in whom sanctification is had: *πίστει τῇ εἰς ἐμέ* ("by faith in me", 26:18) `[T]`.
2. **At 26:23 the Christ himself is the subject of *καταγγέλλειν*** ("to proclaim"). Paul is the instrument; the proclaimer is Christ (Original Language Observations). This continues 1:1's *ἤρξατο* ("he began"): Jesus still teaches.
3. **"A certain Jesus, dead, whom Paul asserted to be alive" (25:19).** The outsider's summary is the gospel in eleven words, and it is the book's second statement that Jesus is alive (1:3).
4. **Union.** *Ἐγώ εἰμι Ἰησοῦς ὃν σὺ διώκεις* ("I am Jesus whom you are persecuting", 26:15). The book's sharpest Christological datum, the third time.

**Typology tests on the Paul–Jesus trial parallel.**
- Theological category: passes (the righteous sufferer before rulers).
- NT precedent: passes, in kind (Luke 21:12; John 15:20).
- **Escalation: fails.** Paul does not surpass Jesus; his trial ends in acquittal, and Jesus' in crucifixion.
- Authorial pattern: passes (lexical design).

**Three of four, but escalation fails, so this is trajectory, not typology.** The overview's control holds: *the servant's road runs where the master's ran, and the servant does not do the master's work.* The control is visible in the text itself: Pilate handed Jesus over (Luke 23:25); Festus may not hand Paul over (25:16). **Jesus was handed over so that his servant need not be** `[I]`, moderate. That is a preacher's line, offered for /point-purpose to weigh, not an exegetical claim.

**Trajectory.**
- **Anticipates:** the hope of Israel; the Servant's light to the nations.
- **Christ fulfils:** as the first raised and as the proclaimer of light.
- **Still remains:** the judgement to come (24:25); the resurrection of the just and unjust (24:15); Paul's chains, which are not lifted in Acts.

**Moralism check.**
- **The "be like X" temptation:** "Be bold like Paul before kings"; "Don't be a Felix."
- **The gospel grounding:** "Having obtained help from God" (26:22); the Lord stood by him (23:11); Christ proclaims (26:23).
- **Christ as hero:** the one who rescues the witness and speaks through him, and who was himself not rescued from Pilate.

**Confidence:** high on Christ as acting subject; moderate on the "handed over so that" line.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**23:12–14, 21 — the oath of the forty**
- **Category:** ethical; apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** Did they starve? Did Luke invent a detail?
- **Rhetorical function:** a foil for providence. The silence about the oath-takers' fate is part of the point.
- **Options:** (i) they were released from the vow by rabbinic provision `[S: m. Ned. 3:3, cited in the commentaries; date and applicability uncertain]`; (ii) Luke is uninterested.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text says nothing, and nothing turns on it.
- **Handling:** sidestep with a note.

**24:6b–8a — the longer text.** See Textual Variants. **Handling:** acknowledge in passing if preaching from NASB95.

**25:9–11 and 26:32 — was the appeal a mistake?**
- **Category:** interpretive; pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** Agrippa says Paul could have been freed but for the appeal (26:32). Did Paul's self-protection prolong his imprisonment?
- **Rhetorical function:** 26:32 is the fourth verdict's coda. It is a verdict about Paul's innocence, not a judgement on his decision.
- **Options:**
  - (i) The appeal was a lapse of faith, and God overruled it.
  - (ii) The appeal was the only safe course, since Festus proposed Jerusalem (25:9), where an ambush was already planned (25:3), and so it was faithful prudence.
  - (iii) The appeal was the means of 23:11's *δεῖ*, confirmed by 27:24.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text supports (ii) and (iii) together. Luke narrates the ambush plan (25:3) before Festus's Jerusalem proposal (25:9), so the reader knows what Paul is refusing. 27:24 makes Caesar the destination God has set. **No line of the text faults the appeal** `[T]`. Option (i) has to be supplied from outside.
- **Handling:** address briefly. This is a frequent question.

**26:28 — *Ἐν ὀλίγῳ με πείθεις Χριστιανὸν ποιῆσαι***
- **Category:** interpretive; commonly misread (KJV).
- **The difficulty:** Is Agrippa nearly persuaded, mocking, or deflecting?
- **Options:**
  - (i) KJV "Almost thou persuadest me", a near-conversion.
  - (ii) "In so short a time you would persuade me…?", ironic or rhetorical (ESV construes it as a question).
  - (iii) "With little [effort] you are trying to make me play the Christian" (*ποιῆσαι* "to make/act", with the Byzantine *γενέσθαι* "to become" the smoother reading).
- **Honest exegesis:** "almost" is not the sense of *ἐν ὀλίγῳ*. Paul's answer *καὶ ἐν ὀλίγῳ καὶ ἐν μεγάλῳ* ("whether in little or in great") treats it as a matter of measure or time, not proximity `[T]`. The unusual *ποιῆσαι* and the scribal correction to *γενέσθαι* suggest the original was awkward. **Deflection by irony is the likeliest reading** `[I]`, moderate–high.
- **Handling:** address directly; see Pitfall 2.

**26:14 — "kick against the goads"**
- **Category:** weaponised / psychologised.
- **The difficulty:** Often preached as Paul's long-suppressed guilt before Damascus.
- **Rhetorical function:** a proverb of futility: a mortal cannot resist a god.
- **Honest exegesis:** the text gives no prior inner struggle; 26:9 says he thought he was right (*ἔδοξα ἐμαυτῷ*, "I thought to myself"). **The proverb says resistance is futile, not that it was painful** `[I]`, moderate–high.
- **Handling:** correct briefly.

**26:10 — *κατήνεγκα ψῆφον*, "I cast my vote"**
- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** Was Paul a Sanhedrin member? Were there many executions?
- **Options:** literal vote, or figurative "gave my approval" (cf. 8:1's *συνευδοκῶν*, "approving").
- **Handling:** sidestep with a note.

**26:20 — "throughout all the region of Judea"**
- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** Gal 1:22 says he was unknown by face to the churches of Judea `[S: Galatians]`.
- **Honest exegesis:** 26:20 is a summary of his message's range on the 1:8 map, not a chronology. Commentators propose several harmonisations, and none is needed for preaching.
- **Handling:** sidestep with a note.

**24:24–25 — Drusilla and self-control**
- **Category:** pastoral (marriage and divorce).
- **Handling:** preach the boldness of naming sin to the powerful, with care about remarried hearers. The text does not dwell on it, and nor should the sermon.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Felix as the patron saint of procrastination

- **What it looks like:** "Don't wait for a more convenient season!", the KJV phrase lifted out of 24:25 for an evangelistic appeal.
- **Why it's wrong:** The appeal is not false, but it lifts the line out of the trial. Felix's delay is driven by money and politics (24:26–27, *διό*, "therefore"). He has more accurate knowledge than most (24:22). And **Paul, not Felix, is the one who waits two years**. The unit's interest is the powerful hearer's corruption and the witness's patience.
- **The corrective:** Preach Felix as Luke frames him: informed, frightened and venal, an inverted Cornelius in the same city. Keep the evangelistic edge, and add the second edge: the witness who keeps faith through two years of someone else's convenient seasons.

### Pitfall 2: "Almost persuaded"

- **What it looks like:** Agrippa as the tragic near-convert, the climax of a sermon on decision (and the hymn).
- **Why it's wrong:** *Ἐν ὀλίγῳ* does not mean "almost". Paul's reply turns it into "in little or in great". Agrippa's line is most likely ironic deflection (Difficult Verses).
- **The corrective:** Preach 26:27–29 as Paul pressing a king who will not answer the question about the prophets, and Paul's prayer for *all* who hear. The tragedy is not nearness but evasion.

### Pitfall 3: Paul the brilliant litigant

- **What it looks like:** "Paul knew his rights and used them. Be wise as serpents." The appeal as legal genius.
- **Why it's wrong:** The unit's providence is passive (the manner-of-the-act check). The nephew, the tribune and the governors' motives do the moving. The appeal is lawful prudence (25:3, 9) used by God (23:11; 27:24), not a strategy to imitate (Trap 2).
- **The corrective:** Preach who is acting: the Lord who stood by him (23:11) and "help from God" (26:22).

### Pitfall 4: Treating 26:2–23 as a testimony template

- **What it looks like:** "Here is how to give your testimony: before, the encounter, after."
- **Why it's wrong:** It is an *ἀπολογία* ("defence", 26:1, 2, 24), answering charges, in prophetic-call language unique to an apostle (Tool 11). The overview's Trap 4 (second form) applies more strongly here than at 22:1.
- **The corrective:** Preach it as defence of the hope of Israel and as commission, and draw the believer's application from 26:29, not from 26:12–18's structure.

### Pitfall 5: "Kicking against the goads" as guilty conscience

- **What it looks like:** Paul had been fighting his conscience since Stephen died.
- **Why it's wrong:** The text says he thought he was right (26:9). The proverb says resistance to a god is futile.
- **The corrective:** The point is the Lord's sovereignty over a resistant man, not a psychology of suppressed guilt.

### Pitfall 6: Skipping 23:12–35 as logistics, or allegorising it

- **What it looks like:** Either moving straight to Felix, or finding meaning in the 470 soldiers.
- **Why it's wrong:** The prologue carries the providence of the unit and its humour (Tool 9), and it plants *διασῴζω* for the shipwreck. But the numbers mean "a lot", not a code (Trap 6).
- **The corrective:** Preach the boy, the tribune and the oath that failed, with a smile, and let 23:24's "bring him safely" ring forward to 27:44's "all were brought safely".
