## Scope note — what kind of run this is, and why it matters

This is a **synoptic run with three members inside one book**, and that shape changes one thing fundamentally.

Synoptic Mode's first and hardest question is the **direction of dependence**: which text is prior, and may a difference therefore be narrated as one author's deliberate change to another's work? **Here that question does not arise at all.** Three tellings, one author, one book, one fixed order. Nobody is editing anybody. There is no earlier text to be revised and no later hand to revise it. Every difference between Acts 9, 22 and 26 is a difference *the same writer made on purpose within a single composition*.

That is not a technicality. It is the reason the study can be what Patrick has asked for — a study of **authorial intent** — rather than a study of sources. Where the direction of dependence is open (Isaiah 36–39 ‖ 2 Kings 18–20), a difference is evidence in an unresolved question and may not be read as intention. Here a difference *is* intention, or it is nothing.

**Extents, set at each telling's own seams rather than at the overlap:**

| Telling | Extent | Working core | Why the seam is there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 9:1–31 | 9:1–19a | 9:31 is one of Luke's own summary-notices and closes the panel that began at 6:8 |
| **2** | 21:40–22:22 | 22:3–21 | The speech begins when Paul is stood on the steps and ends when the crowd stops him mid-sentence |
| **3** | 26:1–32 | 26:2–23 | *ἀπολογέομαι* ("to make a defence") frames the whole hearing at 26:1, 2 and 24 |

**The alternative extent, rejected and named.** The tidy option was to set all three at the conversion-narrative overlap — 9:1–19a ‖ 22:6–16 ‖ 26:12–18. It would have excluded 9:15–16 (the commission, which in telling 1 is not spoken to Saul at all), 22:17–21 (the temple vision), and 26:19–23 (the summary of the ministry). **Those are the three places where the tellings most differ**, which is to say, the whole evidence base. An extent drawn at the overlap systematically deletes what a synoptic run exists to see.

## A note on authorial intent — Sailhamer, Schnittjer, and the PT frameworks

Patrick's point, added mid-run, is the right frame, and three accounts of one event turn out to be the sharpest test case the New Testament offers for it.

**Sailhamer's distinction between History-as-event and History-as-text.** The event behind Acts 9, 22 and 26 is one event. It happened once. Therefore **every single difference between the three accounts is located in the text and nowhere else** — in what Luke selects, what he omits, who he lets speak, what words he chooses, where he puts it. There is no event-level explanation available for *any* of them, because the event does not vary. A reader who asks first "what actually happened on the road?" has asked a question about the one thing in the vicinity that cannot account for a single feature of the three texts in front of him.

This is why the harmonising instinct, however devout, starts in the wrong place. It is not that the question of what happened is illegitimate; it is that it is not the question these texts are shaped to answer, and asking it first makes the differences into problems to be dissolved rather than into the author's argument.

**Schnittjer's categories give the working vocabulary**, and two of them do heavy lifting below: **narrative selectivity** (what is included and dropped) and **narrative braking** (where the author slows down, because that is where he is applying weight). Both are measurable here, and the measurements are the study's strongest evidence.

**The PT framework (Cooper, Davies, Styles)** supplies what Sailhamer does not: the *purpose* question. Cooper's distinction between the **point** (what the author teaches) and the **purpose** (what he intends to happen as a result) is exactly the distinction Patrick's three questions turn on — not just *what* Luke says three times, but *what he is trying to achieve* by saying it three times. Davies' "the Bible comes pre-applied" is the same instinct: the question "what is this telling *for*?" is not a homiletical add-on, it is part of the exegesis.

**One honest caveat, because the frameworks themselves flag it.** Sailhamer's method is built for the Pentateuch and the Tanak, and does not straightforwardly transfer to New Testament narrative; the PT material says so explicitly, and recommends the PT framework alone for NT texts. What transfers cleanly is (a) the text/event distinction, which is general, and (b) Schnittjer's reading habits — selectivity, pace, leading word, framing, first-versus-second reading — which the Sailhamer material itself says transfer to narrative anywhere in the canon. What does *not* transfer is Sailhamer's distinctive claim that the messianic meaning is already the literal authorial-intended meaning of the OT text, which is a thesis about the Tanak's composition and has no purchase on a question about Luke's second volume. This study uses (a) and (b) and leaves the rest alone.

## Headline Findings

**1. The commission grows as its human mediator disappears, and the two movements are exactly inverse.** `[T]` on every count, SBLGNT, bracketed sigla removed:

| | Ananias material | Commission proper |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Telling 1** (9:10–19a / 9:15–16) | **186 words** | **38 words** |
| **Telling 2** (22:12–16 / 22:14–15) | **82 words** | **36 words** |
| **Telling 3** (—/ 26:16–18) | **0 words** | **71 words** |

Ananias is given more space than the Damascus road itself in Luke's own telling (186 words against 144) `[corrected: 20 Nov 2026 — recounted; see the note under *Repetition as Luke's own device*]`, then half that in Paul's Jerusalem defence, then nothing at all before Agrippa. Across the same three tellings the commission nearly doubles. **Luke is not abbreviating a story as it becomes familiar; he is transferring weight from the mediator to the mandate.** Surfaced by narrative selectivity, narrative braking, Structure and Vocabulary converging.

**Checking stage — the pattern is established and this study did not find it.** `[revised: logos-research]` Parsons states plainly that Ananias's role diminishes as Saul's expands across the three accounts, and that his role in the first account is unparalleled in the other two; Tannehill sets out the same three-way movement in the terms used above — the Lord speaking to Ananias at 9:15–16, Ananias speaking to Saul at 22:14–15, the Lord speaking directly to Saul at 26:16–18 `[S: Logos round 2 — Parsons, *Acts* (Paideia), 130; Tannehill, *Narrative Unity* 2:116]`. **What this study adds is the measurement, not the reading.** That is a smaller contribution than the first draft of this document implied, and it is worth having for one reason only: it converts an impression about emphasis into two quantities moving in opposite directions, which is the form in which a claim of this kind can be checked, replicated, or refuted. **Tannehill also draws the interpretive consequence, which this study had not reached**: the direct transmission of the commission in ch. 26 tells *against* reading the narrative as subordinating Paul to the apostles and the Jewish church through mediation.

**2. Each telling names the commissioning agent differently, and each naming is fitted to the bench.** `[T]` on the distributions. In telling 1 the commissioning speaker is **ὁ κύριος** throughout (9:10, 11, 15, 17), identified at 9:17 as *Ἰησοῦς ὁ ὀφθείς σοι* ("Jesus, who appeared to you"). In telling 2 Ananias attributes the commission to **ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν** ("the God of our fathers", 22:14) — to a Jerusalem crowd that has just accused Paul of teaching against the people, the law and the place. In telling 3 **Jesus commissions Paul in his own voice with no intermediary at all** (26:15–18), and the goal is stated as turning people *ἐπὶ τὸν θεόν* ("to God", 26:18). The same commission, attributed to the God of the patriarchs before Israel and spoken by the risen Christ before a king. `[I]`, high, that the fitting is deliberate.

**3. Luke re-words the light for the third telling — and re-words it toward his own infancy narrative.** `[T]`, verified across all twenty-seven books. Tellings 1 and 2 describe the light with **περιαστράπτω** ("to flash around"), which stands in **two New Testament verses, Acts 9:3 and 22:6, and nowhere else**, each time with **ἐξαίφνης** ("suddenly"). Telling 3 drops both and uses **περιλάμπω** ("to shine around") with **λαμπρότης** ("brightness"), the latter a New Testament hapax. And **περιλάμπω also stands in exactly two New Testament verses: Luke 2:9 and Acts 26:13** — the glory of the Lord shining round the shepherds, and the light on the Damascus road as Paul describes it to Agrippa. The prior run on 23:12–26:32 established that Paul's defence before Agrippa is written in the vocabulary of Luke 1–2 `[S: 23:12–26:32 run]`; **what this comparison adds is that Luke did not merely reach for that vocabulary — he displaced the wording the other two tellings had used in order to reach it.**

**Checking stage — the data are old news, and the explanation has a live rival.** `[revised: logos-research]` Robertson already records both distributions: *λαμπρότης* unique to Acts 26:13 in the New Testament, and *περιλάμπω* "only here and Luke 2:9" `[S: Logos round 2 — Robertson, *Word Pictures*]`. **Licona explains the change semantically rather than allusively**: *περιαστράπτω* means "to flash around", like lightning, while *περιλάμπω* means "to shine around" and most likely denotes a more static effect `[S: Logos round 2 — Licona, in *Raised on the Third Day*, 311]`. **That is a genuine competitor and the distribution does not answer it**, since a writer reaching for the apter verb for a steady radiance would arrive at *περιλάμπω* whether or not Bethlehem was in his mind. What keeps the design reading alive is that the *displaced* verb occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, and that the third telling is demonstrably built from Luke 1–2 vocabulary at five further points `[S: 23:12–26:32 run]`. **Held at moderate, not high, and the rival is named in the sermon if the point is preached.**

**4. Luke's own preface vocabulary is distributed across the act of retelling.** `[T]` on every distribution, verified by lemma. **διήγησις** ("narrative", Luke 1:1) is a New Testament hapax, and its cognate verb **διηγέομαι** describes, in Acts, exactly two narrated acts of recounting a deliverance: **Barnabas telling the apostles how Saul saw the Lord (9:27)** and Peter telling the brothers how the Lord brought him out of prison (12:17). **καθεξῆς** ("in order", Luke 1:3) stands in five New Testament verses, all Luke–Acts, and in Acts it describes **Peter's retelling of the Cornelius episode (11:4)**. **αὐτόπται** ("eyewitnesses", Luke 1:2) is a hapax whose content is supplied by the seeing-chain that runs 9:17, 9:27, 22:14–15, 26:16. **ὑπηρέτης** ("servant", Luke 1:2) is what Christ makes Paul at 26:16. **τὸ ἀσφαλές** ("the certainty", Luke 1:4) is what two Roman officials seek and fail to obtain (22:30; 25:26). **Luke has described his own task in the preface, and then shown characters inside the book performing it.** `[I]`, moderate–high, on the design; the individual distributions are secure.

**5. In Acts, "persecute" is almost entirely a word about Saul — and the one exception tells the reader what it means.** `[T]`. **διώκω** stands in **nine verses of Acts**: 7:52, and then 9:4, 9:5, 22:4, 22:7, 22:8, 26:11, 26:14, 26:15. **Eight of the nine are in the three conversion accounts.** The ninth is Stephen's question to the council — *"which of the prophets did your fathers not persecute?"* — spoken moments before the coats are laid at Saul's feet. Luke defines the word in a martyr's last speech and then spends it, every remaining time, on the man standing over the coats.

# Part One — Why Luke told it himself, and why at Acts 9

## The positional question

**Q1 — what has the preceding movement set up that this passage now provides?**

Saul has been in the narrative since 7:58, where he is a young man minding coats, and 8:1–3, where his violence scatters the church and so produces the mission to Samaria and the Ethiopian. The movement that runs from 6:8 has been a sequence of boundaries crossed: the Hellenist fracture, Stephen's temple argument, the scattering, Samaria, a man the law excluded from the assembly. **The persecutor is the next boundary**, and he is the one boundary that is a person rather than a category.

**Q2 — why here, and not elsewhere?**

Because Luke needs the Gentile apostle *named, called and commissioned* before the Gentile mission opens — and then needs him off the stage while it opens through somebody else. 9:30 puts Saul on a boat to Tarsus and he does not return until 11:25–26, by which time Peter has been to Caesarea, the Spirit has fallen on uncircumcised Gentiles without anyone's permission, and Jerusalem has said *"then to the Gentiles also God has granted repentance unto life"* (11:18).

**This is the single most important positional fact in the three-account question, and it is an apologetic fact.** When Paul stands before a Jerusalem crowd in ch. 22 and before a king in ch. 26, the charge against him is innovation — that he teaches against the people, the law and the place, and has invented a lawless mission. Luke has already answered that charge **structurally**, thirteen chapters earlier, by the simple expedient of refusing to let Paul start it. The Gentile breakthrough is Peter's; the Jerusalem church ratifies it; Paul is in Tarsus.

**Checking stage.** This reading is **established, not new**. Bird argues the placement is designed to attribute the Gentile breakthrough to Peter, to give the mission apostolic precedent, and to introduce Paul beforehand; Greene describes a "rule of two" by which Luke affirms divine sponsorship of the Gentile mission through both apostles `[S: Logos, 19 Nov 2026 — Bird, *Jesus and the Origins of the Gentile Mission*, LNTS 331:5; Greene, *Imagine Paul*]`. It is recorded here as confirmation, not discovery.

## What Luke does with his one telling, that neither retelling does

**He slows down for Ananias.** Narrative braking is where the author applies weight, and in telling 1 the brake goes on not at the Christophany but after it. The road is 144 words; the Ananias scene is 186. The reader gets a two-way vision, an argument with the Lord, a stated objection with evidence (*"I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he did to your saints"*, 9:13), a direct answer, an act of obedience against the man's own judgement, and the word *ἀδελφέ* ("brother") spoken to the persecutor by one of his intended victims. **None of this is available in either retelling**, and in the third it is gone entirely.

**He gives the reader the commission the convert does not hear.** 9:15–16 is spoken to Ananias *about* Saul, in the third person. Saul is blind in a house on Straight Street and is told nothing of it. The reader is therefore given, from the start and on the narrator's authority, a piece of information that the character acquires only later and that the Jerusalem crowd and the king will each receive in a different form. This is the reader's privileged perspective in Sailhamer's sense — a vantage point the participants did not have, granted by the author on purpose.

**Checking stage adds two things here.** `[revised: logos-research]` Tannehill accounts for the placement at the level of the scene: putting the commission inside the dialogue with Ananias serves the narrator's interest in Ananias's **resistance and its overcoming** — the disclosure of Saul's future is what persuades a frightened disciple to go to the man with warrants in his pocket `[S: Logos round 2 — Tannehill, *Narrative Unity* 2:116]`. That is a more economical explanation of 9:15–16's position than "the reader needs to know early", and it is probably the primary one; the two are compatible, since the same speech does both jobs. Schnabel adds the function of such commission statements in Acts generally: they preview what follows, mark the narrative's central features, and supply **"norms for judging whether the character is fulfilling the Lord's will"** `[S: Logos round 2 — Schnabel, *Acts*, ZECNT, 449]`. **On that reading 9:15–16 is not only a programme but a measuring stick** — and it is handed to the reader, not to Paul.

**And what the reader is given is a programme.** *σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς* ("a chosen instrument", 9:15) — **ἐκλογή stands once in the Gospels and Acts together, here** `[T]` — *"to carry my name before **Gentiles and kings and the sons of Israel**"*, with suffering named as the content of the call rather than its risk (*ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματός μου παθεῖν*, 9:16). The three-part audience list is the agenda for the rest of the book, and **βασιλεύς ("king") stands in twenty verses of Acts, of which eleven are in chapters 25–26** `[T]`.

**Checking stage.** That 9:15 is programmatic is also established — Tannehill reads it as indicating a mission of unusual scope comprehending Gentiles, Jews and officials of the highest rank; Keener reads it as foreshadowing most of Acts 13–28 `[S: Logos — Tannehill, *Narrative Unity* 2:118–119; Keener, *Acts* 1:1597]`. **But the library also pushed back on the tidy version of the claim**, and the pushback is recorded in full under Critical Assessment below.

**He closes the panel with the church at peace.** 9:31 — *ἡ ἐκκλησία … εἶχεν εἰρήνην οἰκοδομουμένη … ἐπληθύνετο* — is one of Luke's own summary-notices, and the subject is the church. The man whose violence opened the panel by scattering the church is the man whose conversion closes it with the church at rest. **The first telling is placed inside a frame that interprets it: this is what happened to the persecution.** Neither retelling has, or could have, a frame of that kind.

## Answering the first question

**Why did Luke record it this way, and why there?** Because the first telling has a job the other two cannot do. It establishes, on the narrator's own authority and before any accusation has been made, four things: that the initiative was entirely Christ's; that the man was received into the church through a Jewish disciple who had every human reason to refuse; that the mandate to the Gentiles was issued by the Lord *about* Saul before Saul knew of it; and that it was issued *before* the Gentile mission began, to a man who was then removed from the narrative while somebody else began it.

**Point and purpose, in Cooper's terms.** The *point* of telling 1 is that the risen Jesus took hold of his chief persecutor and appointed him to the nations. The *purpose* is to put that beyond dispute before the disputes begin — so that when the charge of apostasy is brought in chapters 21–26, the reader has already watched the commission issued, and watched the church to which it was given grow quiet.

# Part Two — Why Paul retells it in Jerusalem, and in that manner

## What changes first is the genre

**22:1 names the speech ἀπολογία ("defence"), and the word's only other occurrence in Acts is 25:16, where Festus explains Roman trial procedure** `[T]` `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`. Telling 1 is narrative. Telling 2 is a legal defence, and must be read as one. The prior run on this unit established that the speech is built point by point against the threefold charge of 21:28 — *the people, the law, and this place* — and answers each in that order `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`. That finding is consumed here rather than re-derived, and it governs everything below.

## Every addition and every omission serves the bench

**The credentials are rebuilt for a Jerusalem audience.** Telling 1 said only *"a certain disciple named Ananias"* (9:10). Telling 2 says *ἀνὴρ εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον, μαρτυρούμενος ὑπὸ πάντων τῶν κατοικούντων Ἰουδαίων* — "a devout man according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews living there" (22:12). **The man who baptised Paul is produced as a character witness, with his Torah-observance and his standing among local Jews entered into evidence.** Nothing about Ananias has changed; the audience has.

**The commission is attributed to the God of the fathers.** 22:14's *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν προεχειρίσατό σε* ("the God of our fathers appointed you") is the single most audience-fitted sentence in the three accounts. The verb **προχειρίζομαι stands in three New Testament verses, all in Acts: 3:20, 22:14 and 26:16** `[T]`. At 3:20 it is used of the appointing of the Christ. To a crowd that believes he has abandoned the God of Israel, Paul says that the God of Israel appointed him, using the verb Peter used of God's appointment of the Messiah.

**The content of the commission is given in the language of Israel's own expectation**: *ἰδεῖν τὸν δίκαιον* ("to see the Righteous One", 22:14). **ὁ δίκαιος used absolutely of Jesus stands three times in Acts — 3:14, 7:52 and 22:14** `[T]` `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`, and 7:52 is Stephen's, in the sentence that got him killed while Paul watched. Paul tells the crowd that he was commissioned to see the one Stephen was killed for naming.

**The temple vision is added.** 22:17–21 appears in no other telling: Paul praying *ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ* ("in the temple"), falling into a trance, and being sent *εἰς ἔθνη μακράν* ("to the Gentiles far away"). He is accused of defiling the temple. **He answers that the Gentile mission was given to him inside it, at prayer.** The prior run showed that after the five-fold temple cluster of 21:26–30 — entering, being seen, being accused, being dragged out, the doors shut — the next occurrence of *ἱερόν* in the book is this one `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`. It is the strongest single move in the speech, and the crowd listens *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου*, "up to this word", and no further (22:22).

**Note also ἔκστασις ("trance"), which stands in three Acts verses: 10:10, 11:5 and 22:17** `[T]`. **Both of Acts' commissions to the Gentiles are given in a trance** — Peter's on the roof at Joppa, Paul's in the temple — and Luke has used the same word for each.

## What the Jerusalem telling contributes to Luke's purpose

Three things, and the third is the one usually missed.

**(a) It puts the defence of the Gentile mission into the mouth of the accused**, in Hebrew, in Jerusalem, to Israel, in the vocabulary of the fathers and the law. Luke has already told the reader the mission is God's; here he shows it being argued as continuous with Israel's own God by a man who claims, truthfully, to have been trained in the strictest accuracy of the ancestral law.

**(b) It establishes Paul as an eyewitness in the preface's own terms.** *ἔσῃ μάρτυς αὐτῷ πρὸς πάντας ἀνθρώπους ὧν ἑώρακας καὶ ἤκουσας* — "you shall be a witness for him to all men of what you have seen and heard" (22:15). Luke's preface rests his whole project on those who were *αὐτόπται* ("eyewitnesses") from the beginning (Luke 1:2). Paul was not one of the Twelve and had not followed Jesus; 22:15 is the sentence that makes him an eyewitness anyway, and it is spoken by a law-observant Jew rather than claimed by Paul himself.

**(c) It fails, and Luke lets it fail.** Twenty-one verses produce a demand for his execution (22:22), in a phrase — *αἴρω* with *ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* — that stands in only two New Testament verses, the other being the Isaiah 53 citation at 8:33 `[T]` `[S: 21:37–23:11 run]`. **This is not a model testimony and it is not meant to be read as one.** The purpose is not to show a technique that works. It is to put Israel's refusal on the record, in the Servant's own words, before any Roman hears the case — and then to have the Lord himself, nine verses later, call the failed defence *testimony given* (*ὡς διεμαρτύρω*, 23:11).

# Part Three — The third telling, before a king

## The audience is the commission's own middle term

9:15 named Gentiles, **kings**, and the sons of Israel. **Agrippa is the only king Paul addresses in Acts**, and he is addressed as *βασιλεῦ* at 26:2, 7, 13, 19 and 27 `[T]`. Eleven of Acts' twenty occurrences of *βασιλεύς* stand in chapters 25–26 `[T]`. The third telling is the one delivered to the term of the programme that had never yet been discharged.

## What is removed, and what that removal does

**Ananias is gone. So is the baptism, so is the washing away of sins — and so is the blindness.** In the account Paul gives to a king there is no incapacity, no three days of darkness, no dependence on a stranger, and no Jewish intermediary of any kind. There is a light, a voice, a commission, and an obedience. **The omission is noticed in the literature and read in the same direction as here**: Squires lists the disappearance of the command to go into the city and of Ananias together, and concludes that there is no need for a disciple to mediate between Paul and God because Luke has Paul stress that he received his commission directly `[S: Logos round 2 — Squires, "Acts", *Eerdmans Commentary on the Bible*, 1261]`. `[revised: logos-research]`

**What takes their place is the commission at its longest and from its highest speaker** — 71 words, more than the other two combined, spoken by Jesus in the first person. And it is phrased as a prophetic call. The prior run on this unit established the three sources, each verified against Swete: *στῆθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου* ("stand on your feet") stands once in Swete, at **Ezekiel 2:1**; *ἐξαιρούμενός σε … εἰς οὓς ἐγὼ ἀποστέλλω σε* matches **Jeremiah 1:7–8**; *ἀνοῖξαι ὀφθαλμούς* ("to open eyes") stands once in Swete, at **Isaiah 42:7**, whose verse also holds the Greek Old Testament's only *ἐκ δεσμῶν δεδεμένους* ("those bound in chains") `[S: 23:12–26:32 run]`. **Paul's call is narrated as a prophet's call, to a rebellious house, by a man in chains who is reciting the Servant's mandate to free prisoners.**

## The details added only here, and what each is for

**The voice spoke in Hebrew (26:14).** **Ἑβραΐς stands in three verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 21:40, 22:2 and 26:14** `[T]`. The first two are Paul addressing the Jerusalem crowd in Hebrew; the third is the heavenly voice. **Luke withholds the detail in the Hebrew speech, where the hearers were already hearing Hebrew, and supplies it in the Greek speech before a Roman governor and a Herodian king, where nobody present would have understood a word of it.** The information is therefore not for the people in the room; it is for Theophilus. `[I]`, moderate–high.

**The goads proverb (26:14).** *σκληρόν σοι πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν* — a Greek proverb, in a Greek speech, to a Greek-speaking court. The prior run considered and rated *uncertain* a secondary resonance with Deuteronomy 32:15 `[S: 23:12–26:32 run]`; the primary frame is the classical proverb, and the point for this study is simply that **it appears in the one telling whose audience would recognise it.**

**All of them fell (26:14).** In telling 1 Saul falls and the men stand; in telling 2 Paul falls alone; in telling 3 *πάντων … καταπεσόντων ἡμῶν* — the whole party goes down. Before a king, the epiphany prostrates the entire company.

**The light outshines the sun (26:13), in new words.** See Headline 3. *ὑπὲρ τὴν λαμπρότητα τοῦ ἡλίου*, at *ἡμέρας μέσης* ("midday") — the brightest light at the brightest hour, described with the verb Luke used of the glory at Bethlehem.

**Moses and the prophets (26:22–23).** *οὐδὲν ἐκτὸς λέγων ὧν τε οἱ προφῆται ἐλάλησαν μελλόντων γίνεσθαι καὶ Μωϋσῆς* — "saying nothing beyond what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass". The charge is innovation; the answer is that there is no new content. And the content is then given as two things the prophets said: a suffering Christ, and *φῶς … τῷ λαῷ καὶ τοῖς ἔθνεσιν* ("light to the people and to the Gentiles", 26:23).

**That last phrase closes a chain this study can now state in full.** **φῶς stands in ten verses of Acts, and seven of them are in the three conversion accounts** `[T]` — 9:3; 22:6, 9, 11; 26:13, 18, 23. Of the remaining three, 12:7 is the angel's light in Peter's prison and 16:29 is lamps at Philippi; **the only other theological use is 13:47, Paul citing Isaiah 49:6, "I have set you as a light for the Gentiles."** So in Acts the word "light" belongs almost entirely to two things: the light that struck Paul down, and the light he was made for the nations. **26:23 is where Luke finally says both in one sentence.**

**"Not done in a corner" (26:26).** *οὐ γάρ ἐστιν ἐν γωνίᾳ πεπραγμένον τοῦτο*. Paul appeals to public verifiability, before a king who is said to know these things, having opened by appealing to living witnesses of his own early life *"if they are willing to testify"* (26:4–5). **This is the preface's own epistemology — a case built on what can be checked — spoken by a character inside the book.**

## What the third telling contributes

It converts the whole Damascus event from a biography into **the hope of Israel on trial**. Paul says he stands accused *"for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers"* (26:6), the thing the twelve tribes serve night and day to attain, and asks *"why is it judged incredible among you that God raises the dead?"* (26:8). The conversion is then narrated as the evidence. **The question before the court is no longer whether Paul is a troublemaker; it is whether the resurrection happened** — which is exactly the question Luke wrote both volumes to settle for Theophilus.

And the verdict is the one Luke has been building to: *"this man could have been released"* (26:32) — the last of four official acquittals, after which the only thing carrying Paul to Rome is his own appeal.

# The design across the three tellings

## The invariant core

Three things, and only three, stand in all three tellings in near-identical Greek `[T]`, verified by phrase search across all twenty-seven books:

1. **Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις;** — "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (9:4; 22:7; 26:14)
2. **Τίς εἶ, κύριε;** — "Who are you, Lord?" (9:5; 22:8; 26:15)
3. **Ἐγώ εἰμι Ἰησοῦς ὃν σὺ διώκεις** — "I am Jesus whom you are persecuting" (9:5; 22:8; 26:15), with *ὁ Ναζωραῖος* added only at 22:8

Everything else moves. **What Luke refuses to vary is the identification of the risen Christ with his persecuted people** — the overview's judgement that this is the book's sharpest Christological datum, and the only part of the event that is spoken rather than narrated in every telling `[S: overview v2.5]`.

## The movements

| | Telling 1 | Telling 2 | Telling 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genre** | narrative | defence (*ἀπολογία*) | defence (*ἀπολογέομαι* ×3) |
| **Speaker** | the narrator | the convert | the convert |
| **Audience** | Theophilus | Israel, in Hebrew | a king and a governor, in Greek |
| **Mediator** | Ananias, 186 words | Ananias, 82 words | none |
| **Commission** | 38 words, about Saul | 36 words, to Paul | 71 words, to Paul |
| **Commissioner named** | ὁ κύριος | the God of our fathers | Jesus, directly |
| **Light vocabulary** | ἐξαίφνης, περιαστράπτω | ἐξαίφνης, περιαστράπτω | περιλάμπω, λαμπρότης |
| **Ends with** | the church at peace | "Away with him!" | "He could have been released" |

Read down the last row and the three tellings are a single argument: **God did it; Israel refused it; Rome could find nothing against it.** That is the shape of the book Luke's presenting situation says he wrote — a mixed church needing certainty that the Gentile mission is God's purpose and that it is safe to belong to this movement `[S: overview v2.5]`.

## Repetition as Luke's own device

**The term in use, defined.** `[added: 20 Nov 2026]` A **retelling** here means *the repetition of a large proportion of the original narration* — the event narrated again, not merely referred to. A clause or two reporting that the event happened is a **report**; an appeal to its outcome in the course of an argument is a **citation**. The distinction is not only a convenience: measured against the SBLGNT it falls out of the text with a gap in it. Paul's two accounts run at 109% and 92% of the first telling's length (361 and 302 words against 330); Peter's Jerusalem account runs at 30% of Acts 10 (248 against 836). Every other reference to either event runs at or below 12% and narrates nothing: Barnabas at 9:27, 32 words; Peter at 15:7–11, 98 words of which only 63 touch the event, and those name no place, no scene and no sequence; Barnabas and Paul at 15:12, 23 words; James on Peter at 15:14, 14 words. **Retellings cluster at 30–109%; everything else at 12% and below.** With five instances the threshold is observed rather than established, but the gap is wide and the qualitative test agrees with it: a retelling *narrates the sequence again*, and nothing below the gap does.

**One correction to this document's own figures, made in the course of setting the base.** `[corrected: 20 Nov 2026]` The first telling is **330 words (9:1–19a)**, not the 339 implied by the Ananias figure previously tabulated, and the Ananias scene is **186 words (9:10–19a)**, not 195. The earlier count ran to the end of 9:19, where the pericope closes at 19a — the second half of the verse opens the Damascus-preaching section. Four independent tokenisations of the SBLGNT agree on 186. **The road figure of 144 is unaffected, and so is every conclusion drawn from the movement**: the Ananias scene still outweighs the road (186 against 144), still halves in the second telling (82) and still vanishes in the third, while the commission still runs 38 → 36 → 71.

**Only two events in Acts are told more than once**: the Cornelius episode, narrated at 10:1–48 and retold by Peter at 11:5–17 (with later appeals at 15:7–9 and 15:14, which refer to it rather than narrate it), and Saul's conversion, narrated three times. **Saul's is the only event in the book told three times** `[T]` on the two sets; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the exhaustiveness, which rests on a survey of the book rather than on a mechanical check — see Critical Assessment.

**And a case was made for a fourth.** `[revised: logos-research]` Barnabas's report at 9:27 — *he saw the Lord on the road; the Lord spoke to him; he preached boldly at Damascus* — is treated in at least one commentary as a fourth, compressed telling, which also notes that the verb Luke uses for it, **διηγέομαι**, is the cognate of the **διήγησις** of Luke 1:1 `[S: Logos round 2 — Harvey and Gentino, *Acts*, Kerux, 240–241]`. The same source observes that the aorist backgrounds Barnabas's act of telling, so that what stands in the foreground is what he told. **This matters in one of the two ways first supposed, not both.** `[revised: 20 Nov 2026 — definition adopted]` It does mean the Luke 1:1 link reported at Headline 4 is attested rather than this project's own, and that stands. It does **not** require restating the three-fold count: on the definition now in use, Barnabas's thirty-two words are a *report that the event happened* — three verbs, no road, no light, no voice, no Ananias, no commission — and not a retelling of it. The “fourth telling” label is `[S]`, thinly attested, and **not adopted**; the lexical datum behind it is `[T]`, secure, and retained on its own merits, since *διηγέομαι* at 9:27 tells us what Luke calls the giving of an account whether or not the account is itself a retelling. **The count stands: one telling and two retellings, in selected detail.**

**The two multiply-told events in Acts are the conversion of the persecutor and the conversion of the Gentile.** Both are the moments on which the legitimacy of the Gentile mission rests, and Luke has given each the treatment he gives nothing else. That he marks Peter's retelling with **καθεξῆς** — *"Peter began and explained it to them in order"* (11:4), using the word he used of his own method in Luke 1:3 — makes the device hard to read as anything but deliberate.

## Critical assessment — where this reading could be wrong

Recorded at length, per the standing preference, because the risk in a study like this is that a pattern found by the person looking for it is confirmed by the library answering the question he asked.

> **The chief correction, after Logos round 2, and it applies to the whole document.** `[revised: logos-research]` **This study is markedly less original than its first draft implied.** Of its five headline findings, the central one — the mediator diminishing as the mandate grows — is set out by Parsons and by Tannehill, the latter in almost the terms used here; the Luke 2:9 link behind Headline 3 is in Robertson, who died in 1934; the *διήγησις* link behind Headline 4 is in a 2023 commentary; and the two positional claims of Part One were already known to be established from round 1. **What survives as this project's own is a method rather than a thesis**: the decision to measure what others had described, which yields the word-counts of Headline 1, the three-way naming of the commissioning agent, the *διώκω* concentration, and the observation that the third telling *displaces* vocabulary rather than merely adopting it. Those are real and they are checkable. They are not a new reading of Acts, and the document should not be preached as though they were. **Two rounds of questions turned a study that looked novel into a study that is mostly corroborative — which is the correct outcome and the reason the rounds are run.**

**1. The library frames the differences more conservatively than this study does, and the difference is real.** Keener explains the variation by ancient historiographical convention — authors less concerned with consistency of minute detail than modern readers expect, and rhetorically free to select material suited to the audience `[S: Logos — Keener, *Acts* 1:1600–1601]`. **That explains the differences as something Luke was permitted to do. This study claims something stronger — that the differences are doing work.** Both can be true, but they are not the same claim, and a reader who finds Keener's account sufficient will think this one over-reads. The honest statement of the gap: convention explains *that* the accounts may differ; it does not explain *why these particular things* move in these particular directions, and the inverse 195→82→0 against 38→36→71 is the hardest datum for a purely conventional account to absorb.

**2. The 9:15 triad should not be mapped mechanically, and the library said so.** The tidy version — ch. 22 discharges "sons of Israel", ch. 26 discharges "kings", Rome discharges "Gentiles" — is attractive and **should be resisted**. The library's answer, having confirmed that 9:15 is programmatic, added that the three-part formula functions as an overarching framework for the whole mission rather than being mechanically fulfilled by specific individuals. **That caveat carried no footnote in the answer, so it has the standing of a reasonable caution rather than a sourced finding** — but it is the right caution, and the text supports it: Paul preaches to Gentiles from ch. 13, to Israel throughout, and the "kings" term is discharged once. The defensible claim is the narrow one: **9:15 names an audience that includes kings, Acts contains exactly one royal hearing, and Luke puts the fullest telling of the conversion into it.**

**3. The preface-vocabulary finding is a distribution, not a demonstration.** That *καθεξῆς*, *διηγέομαι*, *ὑπηρέτης* and *τὸ ἀσφαλές* all recur at retelling-moments in Acts is verified and striking. But these are ordinary words doing ordinary jobs, and a writer with a settled vocabulary will reuse it without meaning anything by it. **ἐπιχειρέω is the warning case**: it stands in three New Testament verses, Luke 1:1, Acts 9:29 and 19:13 `[T]` — the preface's "many have *attempted* to compile a narrative" and, inside our own passage, the Hellenists who *attempted* to kill Saul. The distribution is as tight as any above and plainly means nothing. **The finding is reported at moderate–high confidence on the cluster and should not be rested on any single member of it.** `[revised: logos-research]` **Round 2 strengthened exactly one member**: the *διηγέομαι* link at 9:27 is noticed in a commentary, which raises that strand above coincidence. The other members remain untested, and the *ἐπιχειρέω* warning above still governs the cluster as a whole.

**4. The companions' discrepancy has not been resolved here, and should not be smoothed.** 9:7 has the men standing, hearing the voice, seeing no one; 22:9 has them seeing the light and not hearing the voice; 26:14 has them all on the ground. The standard grammatical resolution (the genitive *φωνῆς* at 9:7 for hearing a sound, the accusative *τὴν φωνήν* at 22:9 for understanding speech) is available and is not nothing, but it does not cover the standing-versus-falling difference at all. **This study's position is that the differences are audience-shaped, which is an account of their purpose and not of their reconciliation.** A preacher who needs the harmonisation should get it from a commentary and should be told honestly that the text-first reading does not supply one.

**5. One claim rests on a survey rather than a mechanical check.** "Saul's conversion is the only event in Acts told three times" was reached by reading the book, not by an exhaustive procedure, and an exhaustive procedure for "the same event narrated again" is not obvious to design. It is capped at moderate–high and flagged. `[revised: 20 Nov 2026 — definition adopted]` **The complication Round 2 raised is now closed; the survey weakness is not.** With *retelling* defined as the repetition of a large proportion of the original narration, Barnabas's clause at 9:27 does not qualify, and the “three times” formulation stands as written. The definition also makes the claim testable in a way it was not before — the test is whether any other event in Acts is narrated again at length — but that test has still not been run as a procedure, so the flag stays at moderate–high.

**6. The Sailhamer frame is being used outside its home.** Stated plainly above and repeated here because it bears on how much weight the frame can carry: his method is built for the Pentateuch and the Tanak, and the PT material recommends the PT framework alone for New Testament texts. The text/event distinction transfers; the rest of Sailhamer's programme is not in play and no conclusion here depends on it.

## Preaching pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching any of the three as a conversion paradigm

- **What it looks like:** "Every Christian needs a Damascus road", or a testimony-shaped sermon on 22:3–21.
- **Why it's wrong:** all three are **commissioning** narratives, and two of them are legal defences. 9:15–16 is about a mission and its cost. Luke's own conversion paradigm is 2:38. The second telling is named *ἀπολογία* by the speaker, and it ends in a demand for his death.
- **The corrective:** preach the commission and the Commissioner. If the sermon needs a conversion text, Luke has supplied better ones.

### Pitfall 2: Harmonising the three before reading any of them

- **What it looks like:** opening with a reconciliation of 9:7 and 22:9 and then preaching a composite account.
- **Why it's wrong:** the composite text exists nowhere, and the differences are where Luke's meaning is. Reconciling them first dissolves the evidence.
- **The corrective:** preach the one Luke put in front of that congregation's passage, whole, in its own setting, and let the comparison serve rather than replace it.

### Pitfall 3: Skipping Ananias

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on 9:1–31 that ends at the light.
- **Why it's wrong:** Luke gives Ananias more words than the road. He has the harder obedience, no vision of glory, a stated objection with real evidence behind it, and the only use of "brother" in the chapter.
- **The corrective:** let the braking set the emphasis. And note that the man Luke lingers on longest is the one the third telling drops — which is a sermon in itself about who gets remembered.

### Pitfall 4: Making Paul the hero of ch. 26

- **What it looks like:** Paul the brilliant advocate, running rings around Festus and nearly landing Agrippa.
- **Why it's wrong:** the speech's own claim is that it contains nothing new (26:22), the acting subject of the commission is Christ, and the speech ends with Paul still in chains and the king walking out.
- **The corrective:** ask who the subject of the main verbs is — the overview's standing test.
