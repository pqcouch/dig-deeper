Headline Findings

1. Paul’s last speech is assembled out of the vocabulary of his first mission. Five distinct lexical threads run from Acts 13–14 into this pericope and nowhere else in between: δρόμος (13:25 / 20:24 — the only two in Acts); βουλή + ὑπηρετέω (13:36 / 20:27 + 20:34 — ὑπηρετέω occurs three times in the NT, all in Acts); διαστρέφω of a deceiver (13:8, 10 / 20:30); ὁ λόγος τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ (14:3 / 20:32 — verbatim, and only these two); and the four-element cluster of 14:22–23 (θλῖψις + βασιλεία; πρεσβύτεροι; προσευξάμενοι; παρέθεντο αὐτοὺς τῷ κυρίῳ) answered at 20:23 + 25, 20:17, 20:36 and 20:32. [T] on every count, lemma-verified; [I] at moderate–high confidence on the design. The end of the Pauline mission speaks in the words of its beginning.

2. 20:22 is the hinge on which Acts turns Paul from binder into bound. δέω in the sense “bind” occurs twelve times in Acts (9:2, 9:14, 9:21, 12:6, 20:22, 21:11 ×2, 21:13, 21:33, 22:5, 22:29, 24:27 — separated from the 24 occurrences of δεῖ, which MorphGNT files under the same lemma). In the first four Saul is the one doing the binding; from 20:22 to the end Paul is the one bound — and 20:22, the pivot, gives the binding to the Holy Spirit before any human being lays a hand on him. Luke lets Paul himself recite the earlier role at 22:5 while he is in chains. [T], high confidence on the distribution; [I], moderate–high on the reversal being designed.

3. Acts 20:28 stands inside a cluster of Isaianic redemption texts, not (chiefly) inside Ezekiel 34. περιποιέομαι with God as subject and his people as object occurs once in the LXX: Isaiah 43:21, λαόν μου ὃν περιεποιησάμην — same verb, same middle aorist, same subject and object as ἣν περιεποιήσατο (20:28). Three verses of Isaiah 63:9–11 supply, in order, φείδομαι (cf. 20:29 μὴ φειδόμενοι), τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον twice, τὸν ποιμένα τῶν προβάτων, and ὁ θεὶς ἐν αὐτοῖς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον — τίθημι governing τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον, exactly as at 20:28 (τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον ἔθετο). Isaiah 63:10 is already a live source in Acts at 7:51. And Isaiah 60:17 is the LXX’s only text in which God gives his restored people ἐπισκόπους. High confidence on Isa 43:21; moderate–high on Isa 63:9–11 as a cluster; moderate on Isa 60:17. [T] on all the lexical data; [I] on the allusions.

4. The wolves are not from Ezekiel 34 — Ezekiel 34 LXX contains no wolf. Its predators are τὰ θηρία τοῦ ἀγροῦ (34:5, 8). The prophetic topos of Israel’s own leaders as wolves is Ezekiel 22:27 (οἱ ἄρχοντες αὐτῆς ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῆς ὡς λύκοι ἁρπάζοντες … ὅπως πλεονεξίᾳ πλεονεκτῶσιν) and Zephaniah 3:3, with Jeremiah 5:6. Ezekiel 22:27’s two motives — shedding blood and covetous gain — are precisely what Paul disclaims at 20:26 and 20:33. This corrects the sweep and the overview’s intertextual map, which assign 20:28–29 to Ezekiel 34 alone. Ezekiel 34 remains a real conceptual source (see §11), but on narrower verbal ground than claimed.

5. Judas and Paul are Acts’ two men who “received a ministry”, and the book sets their acquisitions against each other. διακονία + λαμβάνω occurs at 1:25 (λαβεῖν τὸν τόπον τῆς διακονίας ταύτης) and 20:24 (τὴν διακονίαν ἣν ἔλαβον), and nowhere else in Acts. ἐπισκοπή occurs once (1:20, Judas’s vacated office); ἐπίσκοπος once (20:28). All three of Acts’ κτάομαι are acquisitions with money (1:18 Judas’s field; 8:20 Simon; 22:28 the tribune’s citizenship); the one περιποιέομαι is God’s acquisition with blood (20:28). Judas acquired a field of blood with the wages of unrighteousness; God acquired a church with blood — and Paul, who received the same kind of ministry, ends by disclaiming silver and gold. [T] on every distribution; [I], moderate–high, on the antitype.

6. 20:37–38 is a Genesis reunion type-scene, inverted. ἐπιπίπτω ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον + κλαυθμός occurs once in the LXX: Genesis 46:29, Joseph meeting Jacob — followed at 46:30 by “now let me die, since I have seen your face”. Acts 20:37 has all three elements and 20:38 has the face; but where Genesis has a reunion that makes death bearable, Acts has a parting after which the face will not be seen. Luke has already used the idiom once, at Luke 15:20 (the father and the prodigal), which is where the kiss (καταφιλέω) comes from. [T] on the lexical data; [I], high, on the type-scene; moderate on the inversion being pointed.

7. The melancholy of 20:25 is answered by the last sentence of the book. κηρύσσω governing βασιλεία occurs twice in Acts: 20:25 (ἐν οἷς διῆλθον κηρύσσων τὴν βασιλείαν) and 28:31 (κηρύσσων τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ … ἀκωλύτως). Paul says the preaching among them is over; the book’s final clause says the preaching itself is not. [T] on the distribution; [I], moderate–high, on the arrangement.

Phase 0.5 — Book-Overview Active Layer

Four threads from book-overview-acts.md v2.1, front-loaded before the tools and held in view throughout.

Presenting situation. The book is written to give a mixed Jewish-and-Gentile church ἀσφάλεια that the risen Jesus is still acting and that the Gentile mission is God’s purpose, not a deviation. Bearing on this passage: the Miletus speech is addressed to a church that will shortly have neither Paul nor an apostle. If the book’s anxiety is is it safe to belong to this?, then the one passage in Acts that tells a congregation how it will be governed after the founders are gone is doing load-bearing work for that anxiety, not merely rounding off Paul’s biography.

Structural arc. The passage sits in the seventh and last movement, 19:21–28:31, “The road to Rome”, whose header is δεῖ με καὶ Ῥώμην ἰδεῖν (19:21). It is inside the βασιλεία inclusio (1:3 … 28:31): 20:25 is the sixth of the eight occurrences and the last before Rome. It is also inside the last of the four “we” sections’ opening stretch (20:5–15 ends at Miletus; the speech itself is not narrated in the first person, and 21:1 resumes “we”).

Intertextual map. Isaiah is the book’s most-used source (seven uses across four texts, live). The map assigns 20:28–29 to Ezekiel 34, moderate, “behind” — the only entry for this pericope. The map also warns that the Ephesus panel has no OT citation anywhere in it, and that runs on the seams should lean on Move 4 (internal echo) rather than Move 2. Both warnings proved right and one proved incomplete: there is indeed no citation formula here, and the internal-echo yield is very high; but the Isaianic material at 20:28 is denser than “no citation” suggests, and the Ezekiel 34 entry needs correcting.

Christological trajectory. Acts preaches a living, reigning, acting Christ; the acting subjects of the main verbs are God, the Lord, the Spirit or the word, rarely the apostles. Bearing on this passage: in 20:28 the actors are the Holy Spirit (ἔθετο) and God (περιεποιήσατο); the elders’ verbs are προσέχετε and ποιμαίνειν. Even in the book’s most office-conscious sentence the office-holders are the objects of divine action before they are the subjects of anything. The moralism check applies with unusual force here (§13).

Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

Q1. What has to have happened before this passage for it to make sense?

Four things, all narrated. (a) A three-year Ephesian ministry (19:1–20:1; 19:8’s three months plus 19:10’s two years, plus the unspecified stay of 19:22). Without it, 20:18–21 and 20:31 have no referent. (b) The Ephesian riot (19:23–41), which is why a farewell to Ephesus happens on a beach at Miletus and not in the city. (c) The Jerusalem resolve (19:21), which 20:22 is executing. (d) The plot at Corinth (20:3), which forced the land route through Macedonia, which is why Paul is late and hurrying past Ephesus (20:16). Luke has built the geography that makes the scene necessary: Paul is close enough to summon the elders and too pressed to visit them.

Q2. Why does this passage exist here rather than anywhere else?

Because it must come before the arrest and after the ministry. Luke needs a place where Paul is still free — free enough to describe a completed work and to hand it over — and where the work is genuinely complete. That window is exactly one chapter wide. Put the speech after 21:33 and it becomes a prisoner’s apologia, which is what chapters 22–26 already are, four times over. Put it before 19:20 and there is no finished ministry to describe.

There is a second answer, structural. This is Luke’s only sustained address to Christians — every other speech in Acts is evangelistic (2, 3, 13, 14, 17), forensic (4, 5, 7, 22–26) or deliberative (15). If Acts is to say anything about the internal life and government of the church, it has to say it once, somewhere, and it says it here. The placing is therefore not sentimental but architectural: the church’s constitution is given at the moment the founder leaves.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Luke’s purpose in the pericope is stated by the pericope’s own asymmetry. The speech is 18 verses; three of them (20:22–24) are about Paul’s future and fifteen are about what the Ephesians already know or must now do. The dominant grammatical person is second plural: ἐπίστασθε (18), ὑμῖν … ὑμᾶς (20), ὄψεσθε (25), ὑμῖν (26, 27), προσέχετε ἑαυτοῖς (28), ὑμᾶς … ὑμῶν αὐτῶν (28, 30), γρηγορεῖτε, μνημονεύοντες (31), παρατίθεμαι ὑμᾶς (32), γινώσκετε (34), ὑπέδειξα ὑμῖν (35). Luke is not writing Paul’s obituary; he is writing the Ephesians’ charge, with Paul’s past as its warrant and Paul’s departure as its occasion.

The two imperatives are the spine: προσέχετε (28) and γρηγορεῖτε (31). Everything before 28 supplies the credentials of the man giving them; everything after 31 supplies the resources (32) and the example (33–35). [T] on the distribution of persons and moods.

A second purpose is apologetic, and it is easy to miss. 20:26’s καθαρός εἰμι ἀπὸ τοῦ αἵματος πάντων and 20:33’s ἀργυρίου ἢ χρυσίου … οὐδενὸς ἐπεθύμησα are both defences, not devotions. Luke has just narrated two riots caused by Paul damaging somebody’s income (16:19; 19:24–27) and one accusation that he teaches against the law (21:28 is coming). A speech that puts on record, before witnesses, that Paul took no money and withheld no doctrine is doing the same work as the four acquittals of chapters 23–26. [I], moderate–high.

2. Context

Immediately before (20:1–16). A travel sequence with one long scene inside it: Eutychus at Troas (20:7–12). Luke’s own interest there is stated — παρεκλήθησαν οὐ μετρίως (20:12). Three details of that scene reappear here: Paul talking μέχρι μεσονυκτίου and ἄχρι αὐγῆς (20:7, 11) prepares 20:31’s νύκτα καὶ ἡμέραν; ἐπέπεσεν αὐτῷ of Paul falling on the boy (20:10) is answered by ἐπιπεσόντες ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον τοῦ Παύλου (20:37); and ἡ ψυχὴ αὐτοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ ἐστιν (20:10) sits seven verses from οὐδενὸς λόγου ποιοῦμαι τὴν ψυχὴν (20:24). [T] on the lexical repetitions.

Immediately after (21:1–16). The oracle at Tyre (21:4) and Agabus’s belt (21:11) execute 20:23’s δεσμὰ καὶ θλίψεις με μένουσιν, and 21:13 (ἑτοίμως ἔχω … ἀποθανεῖν) executes 20:24. 21:5 repeats 20:36 almost exactly — θέντες τὰ γόνατα ἐπὶ τὸν αἰγιαλὸν προσευξάμενοι — so that the kneeling farewell happens twice in fifteen verses, once with elders and once with whole families. The verb προπέμπω likewise runs 20:38 → 21:5. Luke is not repeating himself; he is establishing a pattern of leave-taking that the reader will carry into the arrest.

Boundaries. The pericope is sealed at both ends by the same movement: μετεκαλέσατο (17, he sends for them) and προέπεμπον … εἰς τὸ πλοῖον (38, they send him off). The unit runs 17–38 and not 18–35: without v.17 the addressees are unidentified, and without vv.36–38 the οὐκέτι ὄψεσθε of v.25 has no reaction and no narrator’s confirmation (v.38 supplies both).

3. Structure

The binding device is a threefold νῦν. καὶ νῦν ἰδού (22), Καὶ νῦν ἰδοὺ ἐγώ (25), καὶ τὰ νῦν (32). Each opens a panel and each turns from retrospect to what is now the case; the third drops ἰδού and takes the plural article (τὰ νῦν, “as for the present”), which is a Lukan formula (Acts 4:29; 5:38; 17:30; 27:22 — all in Acts, none in the Gospel). [T], lemma-checked on τὰ νῦν.

The panels are not of equal weight. Panels 1 and 3 (18–21, 25–27) are both about what Paul did not withhold, and both use ὑποστέλλω — a verb that occurs four times in the NT and twice here (20:20, 27) and nowhere else in Luke–Acts. It brackets the two retrospective panels, so that the charge in 28–31 stands on a doubly-stated claim: I held nothing back. [T], high confidence.

Not a chiasm. The temptation is to read 18–21 / 22–24 / 25–27 / 28–31 / 32–35 as a five-part chiasm with the charge at the centre. It does not survive inspection: 18–21 and 32–35 do not correspond (past ministry / future resource), and 22–24 and 28–31 do not correspond (Paul’s fate / the elders’ duty). What the passage actually has is a linear build with a bracket: two ὑποστέλλω panels enclosing the announcement of departure, then the charge, then the handover. Say so rather than manufacturing a symmetry.

4. Linking Words

  • διό (31) — the only inferential conjunction in the speech, and it draws the watchfulness imperative from the wolves, not from the general charge. Watch is a response to a named threat.
  • γάρ (27) — grounds the innocence claim of v.26 in the completeness of v.27. The logic is forensic: I am clean of the blood of all, for I did not shrink from declaring the whole purpose of God. The Ezekiel watchman idea is doing work here without being quoted (see §11).
  • διότι (26) — “for this very reason”, the strongest of Luke’s causal connectives, drawing the innocence claim out of the οὐκέτι ὄψεσθε of v.25. The claim is made because this is the last chance to make it. NA28 marks the whole clause with ⸂⸃; SBLGNT and NA28 both read διότι.
  • πλήν (23) — “except that”, the one qualification on μὴ εἰδώς. Paul does not know what awaits him; he does know it includes bonds. The adversative measures exactly how much revelation he has, and it is not much.
  • ὡς (24) — SBLGNT’s ὡς τελειῶσαι; NA28 marks a variant here (⸀ὡς). The construction is difficult in any reading (see §Textual Variants).
  • οὕτως (35) — points backwards to the demonstrated example (ταῖς χρείαις μου … ὑπηρέτησαν αἱ χεῖρες αὗται), not forwards to a principle. The manner is the argument.

5. Parallels

Within Acts — the farewell scene. 20:36–38 and 21:5–6 are the same scene twice: kneeling (θεὶς τὰ γόνατα / θέντες τὰ γόνατα), prayer, weeping or embracing, and προπέμπω to the ship. γόνυ occurs four times in Acts (7:60; 9:40; 20:36; 21:5) and always in the idiom τίθημι τὰ γόνατα, which is also Luke 22:41 (Gethsemane) and is not the usual Greek phrase. [T]. The company Luke keeps this idiom in is worth naming: Stephen dying, Peter raising the dead, Jesus in Gethsemane, and Paul leaving Ephesus.

Within Acts — Peter and Paul. χρυσίον occurs exactly twice in Acts: 3:6 (Ἀργύριον καὶ χρυσίον οὐχ ὑπάρχει μοι) and 20:33 (ἀργυρίου ἢ χρυσίου … οὐδενὸς ἐπεθύμησα). Peter’s first recorded miracle-word and Paul’s last recorded pastoral word both begin from the same disclaimer. [T], high confidence on the distribution; [I], moderate, that Luke intends the pairing — but the overview’s list of Peter–Paul parallels (3:2/14:8, 12:6/21:33) shows he does this deliberately elsewhere, which raises the prior.

Within Acts — the two Ephesian “you know” speeches. ἐπίσταμαι occurs nine times in Acts and never in Luke’s Gospel. Three of the nine are Ephesian: 19:15 (the evil spirit — τὸν Ἰησοῦν γινώσκω καὶ τὸν Παῦλον ἐπίσταμαι), 19:25 (Demetrius — Ἄνδρες, ἐπίστασθε ὅτι ἐκ ταύτης τῆς ἐργασίας ἡ εὐπορία ἡμῖν ἐστιν), and 20:18 (Paul — Ὑμεῖς ἐπίστασθε). Demetrius and Paul both open an address to an Ephesian body with “you know”, and the subject of Demetrius’s is where his money comes from. Paul’s answer arrives at 20:33–34: these hands. [T] on the distribution; [I], moderate, on the contrast being intended. Note also ἐργασία, four occurrences in Acts (16:16, 19; 19:24, 25), every one of them money made out of religion, and both times ending in a riot against Paul.

Outside Acts — the Pauline letters. 2 Timothy 4:6–8 shares δρόμος + τελ- (τὸν δρόμον τετέλεκα), and the resemblance to 20:24 is close enough that it is regularly noticed [S]. Treat with care: the letters are not available as controls for Acts’ own usage, and the run’s discipline is that Acts’ vocabulary is measured against Acts. The observation belongs in a footnote, not an argument.

6. Narrator’s Comment

Luke comments four times, and every comment is in vv.36–38.

  • v.36 θεὶς τὰ γόνατα αὐτοῦ σὺν πᾶσιν αὐτοῖς προσηύξατο — the narrator supplies the posture and the fact of prayer, and no content. Contrast 1:24 and 4:24–30, where he gives the words. Here the prayer is a gesture, not a text.
  • v.37 ἱκανὸς δὲ κλαυθμὸς ἐγένετο πάντων — a rare narratorial report of collective emotion. ἱκανός is a Lukan quantifier (Acts 20:8 λαμπάδες ἱκαναί; 20:11 ἐφ’ ἱκανόν) and here understates: “there was a good deal of weeping”.
  • v.38 ὀδυνώμενοι μάλιστα ἐπὶ τῷ λόγῳ ᾧ εἰρήκει — the narrator tells us which sentence hurt. Of the eighteen verses, the one that landed was v.25. This is Luke’s only comment in Acts identifying the emotional effect of a specific clause of a speech. [T], and it is the strongest indicator we have of where Luke wants the weight.
  • v.38b προέπεμπον δὲ αὐτὸν εἰς τὸ πλοῖον — the scene closes on movement, not on words. Compare 21:6 and 21:16.

What the narrator conspicuously does not do: he never says whether v.25 came true. See Open Questions.

7. Vocabulary

Every count below is against SBLGNT via the MorphGNT lemma index; where a surface search was used it was run accent-insensitively over NFD-stripped text.

A methodological result, stated because it is negative. The intuition that this speech is lexically exotic — that its density of rare vocabulary marks it out — is false. Counting lemmata that occur exactly once in Acts, normalised per 1,000 words across the 33-unit map, 20:17–38 scores 49.5 and ranks 17th of 33, almost exactly the median. (For comparison: 28:1–15 scores 116.4, 27:1–44 scores 111.7, 17:16–34 scores 102.4, and 20:2–16 — the Eutychus travelogue immediately before — scores 98.5, twice this passage’s rate.) The speech’s distinctiveness is not that its words are rare but which rare words they are, and how much of its common vocabulary is borrowed from earlier Acts. [T], computed.

The nineteen once-in-Acts lemmata here: γρηγορέω, καταφιλέω, κλαυθμός, κοπιάω, λύκος, νουθετέω, πειρασμός, περιποιέομαι, ποιμαίνω, ταπεινοφροσύνη, τελειόω, τριετία, φείδομαι, ἀντιλαμβάνομαι, ἄφιξις, ἐπίσκοπος, ἐπιθυμέω, ἱματισμός, ὀδυνάομαι. Note what they cluster around: oversight (ἐπίσκοπος, ποιμαίνω, γρηγορέω, νουθετέω), money (ἐπιθυμέω, ἱματισμός, κοπιάω), and grief (κλαυθμός, ὀδυνάομαι, καταφιλέω). Acts had not needed these words before because Acts had not before described a church being handed over.

ὑποστέλλω (20:20, 27). Four times in the NT; twice here, once at Galatians 2:12, once at Hebrews 10:38 (quoting Hab 2:4 LXX). Nowhere else in Luke–Acts. It means to draw back, furl, keep something out of view. Its two occurrences bracket the speech’s retrospective panels (§3). Both are negated and both govern τοῦ μὴ ἀναγγεῖλαι — the same infinitive construction twice, which is why the second reads as a formal repetition rather than a fresh thought. [T], high.

ἀναγγέλλω (20:20, 27). Five in Acts: 14:27, 15:4, 19:18, 20:20, 20:27. The first two are missionaries reporting to a church what God has done; 19:18 is Ephesian converts confessing their practices. Paul’s use is the reporting sense: he has told them everything, as a returning missionary reports everything.

διαμαρτύρομαι (20:21, 23, 24). Nine in Acts, one in Luke, fifteen in the NT — a decidedly Lukan word. Three of the nine are in this speech, and the three distribute the testimony across all three parties: Paul testifies to Jews and Greeks (21); the Holy Spirit testifies to Paul (23); Paul’s remaining purpose is to testify to the gospel (24). Add μαρτύρομαι at 20:26 (two in Acts: 20:26; 26:22) and the speech has four testimony-verbs in six verses. [T], high. The forensic register is not accidental: Paul is putting a record before witnesses.

δρόμος (20:24). Two in Acts, three in the NT. 13:25 is John the Baptist: ὡς δὲ ἐπλήρου Ἰωάννης τὸν δρόμον. Luke gives the forerunner and the apostle the same word for a life measured as a distance to be completed. [T], high — and note it comes from the Pisidian Antioch sermon, which supplies two further threads to this passage (§1, §11 Move 4).

βουλή (20:27). Seven in Acts (2:23; 4:28; 5:38; 13:36; 20:27; 27:12; 27:42), two in Luke. Five of the seven are God’s purpose or a human plan; two (27:12, 42) are a decision taken on a ship. 20:27 is the only occurrence in which the βουλή τοῦ θεοῦ is a preacher’s subject matter rather than an agent’s determination. The phrase πᾶσαν τὴν βουλὴν τοῦ θεοῦ therefore means something specific in Acts: the whole of what 2:23 and 4:28 call God’s determinate purpose — Jesus delivered up by it and killed, raised, enthroned. Not “the whole Bible” and not “the whole system of doctrine”. [T] on the distribution; [I], high, on the referent.

ὑπηρετέω (20:34). Three in the NT, all in Acts: 13:36, 20:34, 24:23. 13:36 is Δαυὶδ … ὑπηρετήσας τῇ τοῦ θεοῦ βουλῇ ἐκοιμήθη — David served God’s βουλή in his own generation and died. Acts 20 has both halves of that sentence, split: βουλή at v.27 and ὑπηρετέω at v.34, with the death expected at vv.24–25. [T] on the two lemmata; [I], moderate–high, that the Pisidian sentence is being replayed. This is the single most economical piece of evidence for Headline Finding 1.

ποίμνιον / ποιμαίνω / ἐπίσκοπος / πρεσβύτερος (20:17, 28, 29). ποίμνιον twice in Acts, both here; ποιμαίνω once; ἐπίσκοπος once; πρεσβύτερος eighteen times, of which 20:17 is the twelfth. Three office-words for one body of men in twelve verses. Two further data sharpen it:

  • ἐπισκοπή occurs once in Acts, at 1:20 — Judas’s vacated office, from Psalm 108:8 LXX. The book’s office-noun and its office-holder therefore stand at the two ends of the narrative, and the first instance is of an office forfeited from within. 20:30 says men will arise ἐξ ὑμῶν αὐτῶν. [T] on the distribution.
  • ἀπόστολος occurs 28 times in Acts and the last is 16:4 — twelve chapters before the end. In the book’s fullest treatment of church order, the apostles are not mentioned and the offices named are elder and overseer. [T]. The overview lists the disappearance of ἀπόστολος as an open question; this pericope is where it comes due.

περιποιέομαι (20:28). Once in Acts, once in Luke (17:33), once elsewhere in the NT (1 Tim 3:13). See §11 for the LXX work. Note in passing that Luke 17:33 is ὃς ἐὰν ζητήσῃ τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ περιποιήσασθαι — whoever seeks to acquire his life will lose it — and that Paul at 20:24 has just said he holds his ψυχή of no account. Suggestive; the verb is not repeated at 20:24, so hold it at low–moderate and do not preach it as a link.

καθαρός (20:26). Two in Acts: 18:6 (Τὸ αἷμα ὑμῶν ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν ὑμῶν· καθαρὸς ἐγώ, at Corinth) and 20:26. Both pair καθαρός with αἷμα. 18:6 is Paul disclaiming responsibility for those who reject; 20:26 is Paul disclaiming responsibility for those he has taught. The formula is the same and the target has moved from outsiders to insiders. [T], high.

αἷμα (20:26, 28). Eleven in Acts. Two of them are here, twelve words apart, and they mean opposite things: the blood Paul is clean of (26) and the blood God acquired with (28). Luke has used the word once before in a purchase context — 1:19’s Χωρίον Αἵματος, the field Judas bought. [T].

δάκρυον / κλαυθμός. δάκρυον twice in Acts, both here (19, 31), bracketing the two panels that describe Paul’s ministry; κλαυθμός once, at v.37, where the elders return the tears. [T].

μνημονεύω (20:31, 35). Twice in Acts, both here — remember the three years, remember the words of the Lord Jesus. The speech’s two acts of memory are the apostle’s example and the Lord’s saying, in that order, and the second is given the last word. [T].

ὑποδείκνυμι (20:35). Twice in Acts: 9:16 (ἐγὼ γὰρ ὑποδείξω αὐτῷ ὅσα δεῖ αὐτὸν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματός μου παθεῖν) and 20:35 (πάντα ὑπέδειξα ὑμῖν ὅτι οὕτως … δεῖ). Both are followed by δεῖ. The verb by which the risen Christ announced he would show Saul what he must suffer is the verb Paul uses of what he has shown the Ephesians they must do. [T], high on the distribution, and the shared δεῖ makes coincidence less likely; [I], moderate–high.

ἁγιάζω / κληρονομία (20:32). ἁγιάζω twice in Acts: 20:32 (ἐν τοῖς ἡγιασμένοις πᾶσιν) and 26:18 (κλῆρον ἐν τοῖς ἡγιασμένοις πίστει τῇ εἰς ἐμέ). The phrase ἐν τοῖς ἡγιασμένοις occurs twice in the NT and both are on Paul’s lips in Acts — once handing the Ephesians their inheritance, once reciting the commission Christ gave him. What he was commissioned to obtain for others is what he now hands over. [T], high. κληρονομία twice in Acts: 7:5 (God gave Abraham οὐκ … κληρονομίαν ἐν αὐτῇ οὐδὲ βῆμα ποδός) and 20:32. Stephen’s point is that the patriarch owned no ground; Paul’s is that the sanctified are given an inheritance. [T] on the distribution; the theological relation (land-promise transposed) is [I], moderate.

ἐπιβουλή (20:19). Four in the NT, all in Acts: 9:24 (Damascus), 20:3 (Corinth), 20:19, 23:30. Half of them are in chapter 20 — 20:3 narrates the plot, 20:19 has Paul cite the plots as the condition of his service. [T].

τριετία (20:31). NT hapax. Against the narrative: 19:8 three months, 19:10 two years, 19:22 an unspecified further stay — twenty-seven months plus. “Three years” is a round figure and Luke’s own numbers do not quite make it. [T], and worth saying from the pulpit only to observe that ancient reckoning rounds; it is not a discrepancy worth a paragraph.

γρηγορέω (20:31) and ὕπνος (20:9). γρηγορέω once in Acts; ὕπνος twice, both in 20:9, of Eutychus sinking into deep sleep while Paul talked on. The only sleep-words in Acts are in this chapter, and the imperative stay awake follows the story of a man who could not, by twenty-two verses. Luke does not use the same root, so this is [I] at moderate confidence, not [T] — but it is worth a sentence in the pulpit, lightly.

9. Tone and Feel

Three registers, and they do not blend.

Forensic (18–21, 25–27, 33–34). ἐπίστασθε … γινώσκετε … μαρτύρομαι ὑμῖν ἐν τῇ σήμερον ἡμέρᾳ … καθαρός εἰμι. This is the language of a man putting evidence on a record before competent witnesses, on a stated date. The tone is not warm. It is careful.

Prophetic (22–24, 29–30). δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι … τὸ πνεῦμα … διαμαρτύρεταί μοι … ἐγὼ οἶδα ὅτι εἰσελεύσονται. Two futures known and one unknown, held together. The ἰδού of 22 and 25 is the prophetic presentative.

Pastoral, and only at the end (31–32, 35–38). μετὰ δακρύων νουθετῶν ἕνα ἕκαστον — the diminutive precision of one by one — and then the kneeling, the weeping, the kissing.

The movement across the three is the feel of the passage: a man defends himself, warns, and only then weeps. Preaching it in the reverse order — starting from the tears — inverts what Luke built.

One tonal datum easy to miss. The speech contains no thanksgiving, no greeting, no benediction and no commendation of anyone by name. For a farewell it is remarkably unsentimental until v.36. The emotion in the scene is supplied by the elders, not by Paul: the narrator gives the weeping to them (37) and the grief to them (38). Paul’s own tears are all in the past tense (19, 31).

10. Repetition

  • ὑποστέλλω ×2 (20, 27), both negated, both with τοῦ μὴ ἀναγγεῖλαι. The speech’s most formal repetition; see §3.
  • καὶ νῦν ×3 (22, 25, 32). Structural; see §3.
  • οἶδα ×2 with ἰδοὺ ἐγώ (25, 29) — “I know that you will not see me”; “I know that wolves will come”. The two certainties are a loss and a threat, in that order, and both are introduced identically. Against them stands μὴ εἰδώς at v.22: what Paul does not know is his own future; what he does know is theirs. [T], and it is the sharpest ironic structure in the speech.
  • ποίμνιον ×2 (28, 29) — the flock he charges them to shepherd is the flock the wolves will not spare. The repetition is what makes the wolf-clause a threat rather than an aside.
  • μνημονεύω ×2 (31, 35). See §7.
  • πᾶς ×8 (18 τὸν πάντα χρόνον; 19 πάσης ταπεινοφροσύνης; 25 ὑμεῖς πάντες; 26 πάντων; 27 πᾶσαν τὴν βουλήν; 28 παντὶ τῷ ποιμνίῳ; 32 ἡγιασμένοις πᾶσιν; 35 πάντα ὑπέδειξα; also 36 σὺν πᾶσιν αὐτοῖς, 37 πάντων). The speech’s real leading word is πᾶς. Every claim Paul makes is totalising: the whole time, all humility, all of you, all men’s blood, the whole purpose, the whole flock, all the sanctified, all things shown. A ministry described as complete is described in the vocabulary of completeness. [T], high — and this is the one repetition an English congregation can hear without help, because every version keeps it.
  • ἐγώ expressed ×4 (22 δεδεμένος ἐγώ, 25 ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ οἶδα, 26 καθαρός εἰμι, 29 ἐγὼ οἶδα) — in a language that does not need the pronoun. The emphasis falls on the speaker precisely in the three claims that only he can make.

11. Quotation / Allusion

There is no citation formula in the pericope — no γέγραπται, no καθὼς λέγει, nothing. [T]. The overview predicted this for the Ephesus material. What the passage has instead is one agraphon, a dense Isaianic substratum, a prophetic topos, a Genesis type-scene, and an unusually heavy internal-echo load.

The agraphon (20:35)

Μακάριόν ἐστιν μᾶλλον διδόναι ἢ λαμβάνειν, introduced as τῶν λόγων τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ ὅτι αὐτὸς εἶπεν. This is the only saying of Jesus quoted in Acts that appears in no Gospel, and the only place in Luke–Acts where Luke’s own Gospel is insufficient to supply a dominical word he wants. [T] on the datum. Two observations:

  1. The introduction is μνημονεύειν … τῶν λόγων — plural words — followed by one saying. Paul is pointing at a remembered body of teaching and quoting a specimen.
  2. The saying’s verbs are διδόναι and λαμβάνειν, and λαμβάνω is the verb of 20:24 (τὴν διακονίαν ἣν ἔλαβον) and of 1:20/1:25 (Judas’s office to be received). The speech ends by subordinating receiving to giving, having begun by saying that what Paul has is received. [I], moderate.

Move 1 — the sources in their own context

Isaiah 43:21. λαόν μου ὃν περιεποιησάμην τὰς ἀρετάς μου διηγεῖσθαι (Swete). Its context is the new-exodus oracle: 43:19 ἰδοὺ ἐγὼ ποιῶ καινὰ … ποιήσω ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ὁδόν, 43:20 water in the wilderness for τὸ γένος μου τὸ ἐκλεκτόν. God acquires a people for himself in a second exodus so that they may recount his praises.

An accent- and augment-insensitive sweep of the whole Swete corpus returns 34 forms of περιποιέομαι / περιποίησις. In the great majority the subject is human and the object is livestock, plunder, or a person spared in war (Gen 12:12; 31:18; 36:6; Exod 1:16; Josh 6:17; 9:26; Judg 21:11; 1 Sam 15:9, 15; 25:39; 2 Sam 12:3; 1 Kgs 18:5). God is the subject in five places: Exodus 32:14 (περιποιῆσαι τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ, after the golden calf), Psalm 78[79]:11, Isaiah 31:5, Malachi 3:17 (εἰς περιποίησιν), and Isaiah 43:21. Of these, only Isaiah 43:21 has God as subject, his people as direct object, and the middle aorist — which is precisely the form at Acts 20:28. [T] on the search; [I], high, on the allusion.

Isaiah 63:9–11. Read consecutively in Swete: v.9 διὰ τὸ ἀγαπᾷν αὐτοὺς καὶ φείδεσθαι αὐτῶν· αὐτὸς ἐλυτρώσατο αὐτούς; v.10 παρώξυναν τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον αὐτοῦ; v.11 ποῦ ὁ ἀναβιβάσας ἐκ τῆς θαλάσσης τὸν ποιμένα τῶν προβάτων; ποῦ ἐστιν ὁ θεὶς ἐν αὐτοῖς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον; Its context is the exodus remembered as the paradigm of redemption, and the complaint that the redeemed grieved the Spirit that redeemed them.

Isaiah 60:17. ἀντὶ χαλκοῦ οἴσω σοι χρυσίον, ἀντὶ δὲ σιδήρου οἴσω σοι ἀργύριον … καὶ δώσω τοὺς ἄρχοντάς σου ἐν εἰρήνῃ, καὶ τοὺς ἐπισκόπους σου ἐν δικαιοσύνῃ. Its context is restored Zion re-provisioned and re-governed. A full-corpus search returns ἐπίσκοπος fourteen times in Swete (Num 4:16; 31:14; Judg 9:28; 4 Rgns 11:15, 18; 2 Chr 34:12; Neh 11:9, 14, 22; Job 20:29; Wis 1:6; 7:23; Isa 60:17; 1 Macc 1:51). All but Wisdom’s two are civil, military or Levitical supervisors. Isaiah 60:17 is the only text in which God himself gives ἐπίσκοποι to his restored people. [T] on the search.

Ezekiel 22:27. οἱ ἄρχοντες αὐτῆς ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῆς ὡς λύκοι ἁρπάζοντες ἁρπάγματα τοῦ ἐκχέαι αἷμα, ὅπως πλεονεξίᾳ πλεονεκτῶσιν. Its context is the catalogue of Jerusalem’s failed classes — princes, priests, officials, prophets, people — each corrupt in its own office.

Genesis 46:29–30. Joseph goes up to meet Israel; ἐπέπεσεν ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον αὐτοῦ, καὶ ἔκλαυσεν κλαυθμῷ πίονι; and Israel says Ἀποθανοῦμαι ἀπὸ τοῦ νῦν, ἐπειδὴ ἑώρακα τὸ πρόσωπόν σου. Its context is the resolution of the Joseph novella: the reunion that makes the patriarch’s death acceptable.

This is the decisive column for the passage. Ezekiel, by contrast, is nowhere else used in Acts. That asymmetry does not disprove an Ezekiel 34 allusion, but it means an Isaianic reading has a much higher prior, and it is the reason the overview’s single Ezekiel entry for this pericope is inadequate rather than merely thin.

Move 3 — OT to OT

Isaiah 63:11’s ὁ ποιμὴν τῶν προβάτων brought up out of the sea is Moses, read through the shepherd category; Ezekiel 34:23’s ποιμένα ἕνα … τὸν δοῦλόν μου Δαυείδ is the same category applied to the coming king. The shepherd-of-Israel line therefore already runs Moses → David → the one God will raise, before Acts touches it. Isaiah 60:17’s ἐπίσκοποι and Ezekiel 34:11’s ἐπισκέψομαι (God himself will visit and oversee his sheep) are cognate: in the prophets, oversight is first something God does and then something he delegates. Acts 20:28 has exactly that order — the Spirit ἔθετο, and only then are men ἐπίσκοποι.

12. Genre

A farewell discourse — the form is well attested in Jewish and Greco-Roman literature (Jacob, Moses, Joshua, Samuel, David, the Testaments), and its conventions are visible here: the summons of successors, the retrospect on the speaker’s conduct, the prediction of trouble after the speaker’s death, the exhortation, the commendation, the emotional parting [S] on the form-critical classification.

Two things follow for reading, and one warning.

Reading. (a) The retrospect is a convention, which means Paul’s self-defence is not self-congratulation but the form’s required credentialing; a farewell speech that did not review the speaker’s conduct would be defective. (b) The prediction of post-mortem apostasy is a convention too, which is why 20:29–30’s specificity matters: Luke does not say heresy in general will come, but that it will come from inside this eldership.

Warning. The form’s conventionality is the argument most often used to say the speech tells us about Luke’s church rather than Paul’s [S]. That is a historical judgement the text cannot settle, and it is not needed either way for preaching: the speech is in Acts, addressed to elders, and Acts is what is being preached. Name the issue if the congregation is likely to meet it; do not stake anything on either side.

Sub-genre note. The narrative frame (17, 36–38) is Lukan travel narrative, and the speech proper is the only piece of internal church instruction in the book. The genre mixture is itself the point: Luke embeds a testament inside an itinerary.

13. Copycat

This is the most normative passage in Acts, and therefore the one where the Copycat discipline matters most, not least.

Normative — safe to transfer directly:

  • 20:28’s charge. The Spirit appoints overseers to shepherd the church; the church is God’s and was acquired at cost. Every clause of this is doctrinal, not circumstantial.
  • 20:29–30’s warning. That danger arises from within a leadership is stated as a general expectation, not a local forecast — 20:30’s ἐξ ὑμῶν αὐτῶν is specific in address but the pattern is prophetic.
  • 20:31’s remedy. Watchfulness plus persistent individual instruction (ἕνα ἕκαστον).
  • 20:32’s resource. God and the word of his grace, which builds up and gives the inheritance. Note the grammar: τῷ δυναμένῳ agrees with what precedes; the power is God’s, or the word’s, not the elders’.
  • 20:35’s principle. δεῖ makes it obligatory: hard work in order to help the weak. And the dominical saying.

Descriptive — do not transfer:

  • Paul’s tears, his three years, his night-and-day labour. These are his credentials in a form-critical convention, not a job description. A minister who preaches this as the standard of pastoral intensity has turned a defence into a rod.
  • Working with his hands (20:34). Acts records what Paul did at Ephesus. It does not legislate tentmaking; 1 Cor 9 (unavailable here as a control) shows he argued the other case elsewhere [S]. Preach it as freedom from the charge of profiteering, which is what the context makes it, not as a rule about paid ministry.
  • The prediction of 20:25. Circumstantial, and see Open Questions.
  • The Spirit’s city-by-city testimony (20:23). Descriptive. It is not a promise of comparable guidance.

The specific trap. Because 20:28 is genuinely normative, preachers slide from it into treating the whole speech as normative, and the biography goes across with the charge. Ask of each clause: is Paul saying “this is what a shepherd is” or “this is what I did”? The first is transferable; the second is evidence for the first’s sincerity, and nothing more.

14. Bible Timeline

The passage sits at the end of the third missionary journey, on the return to Jerusalem for Pentecost (20:16), some weeks before the arrest of 21:33. In Acts’ own reckoning: three months plus two years plus a further stay at Ephesus (19:8, 10, 22), then Macedonia and three months in Greece (20:3), then the voyage.

In redemptive-historical terms the passage sits at a specific joint: the point at which the apostolic generation begins to hand over to a non-apostolic one. 20:28 is the only place in Acts where that transfer is described rather than narrated. Everything after it in Acts is trial, voyage and Rome; the church’s internal future is settled here or nowhere.

15. Who Am I?

The Ephesian elders, in the story, are men who have known Paul for three years, who are being told that danger will come out of their own number, and who weep at v.25 rather than at v.29 — the loss of the man moves them more than the warning about themselves. That is a true and unflattering detail Luke does not comment on.

The modern hearer is not Paul and should not be invited to be. If the congregation contains elders, they are addressed at 20:28–31 with unusual directness — but the address is to the whole eldership as the flock’s guardians, and the first object of προσέχετε is ἑαυτοῖς. If the congregation does not contain elders, it is the ποίμνιον: the ones about whom the warning is given, who are told, in effect, what their leaders have been charged to do and what they must not do to them.

God’s part. The Spirit appoints (28), testifies (23) and binds (22). God acquired the church (28), and God with the word of his grace is what Paul falls back on when he goes (32). In a speech dominated by first-person claims, every decisive verb belongs to God.

16. So What?

The passage says three things a congregation can carry.

First: a ministry can be finished. Not successful, not vindicated — finished. Paul’s measure is τελειῶσαι τὸν δρόμον and οὐδὲν ὑπεστειλάμην: he ran the distance and he withheld nothing. He does not claim results. Whatever the Ephesian church becomes, and 20:29–30 says some of it will be bad, that is not the measure by which the ministry is assessed.

Second: the church is not the minister’s. τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ θεοῦ, ἣν περιεποιήσατο — it was acquired, by God, at cost, before any overseer was appointed to it. The elders’ relation to it is custodial. The wolves’ error and the insiders’ error are the same error: drawing disciples ὀπίσω ἑαυτῶν, after themselves, when the flock is somebody else’s.

Third: what remains when the apostle goes is God and the word of his grace. Not a succession, not a structure, not the apostle’s memory — παρατίθεμαι ὑμᾶς τῷ θεῷ καὶ τῷ λόγῳ τῆς χάριτος αὐτοῦ, τῷ δυναμένῳ οἰκοδομῆσαι. The building verb is not given to the elders.

And the shape of the whole: the last thing Paul is recorded saying to a church is that it is more blessed to give than to receive — from a man who has just said that everything he has, he received.

Original Language Observations

δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι (20:22). The dative is ambiguous and every translation must choose. It may be instrumental/agentive (“bound by the Spirit”, NASB95, ESV “constrained by the Spirit”), or it may be locative/respect (“bound in my spirit”), in which case πνεύματι is Paul’s own. Three considerations: (a) Luke writes τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον explicitly in the very next verse, which slightly favours a different referent at 22; (b) 19:21’s ἔθετο ὁ Παῦλος ἐν τῷ πνεύματι is the closest parallel construction in Acts and is likewise disputed; (c) the passive participle sits at the head of the δέω chain that runs to 24:27, and its whole force depends on someone other than Paul doing the binding. On balance the divine reference is better, but the ambiguity is real and a hearer with a study Bible will see it. [T] on the data; [I], moderate.

οὐδενὸς λόγου ποιοῦμαι τὴν ψυχὴν τιμίαν ἐμαυτῷ (20:24). A tangled clause, and NA28 marks two variation units inside it. As it stands: οὐδενὸς λόγου is a genitive of value with ποιοῦμαι (“I make my life of no account”), and τιμίαν ἐμαυτῷ is a second predicate (“nor precious to myself”). The difficulty is that τιμίαν has no connective. Both NASB95 and ESV smooth it in different directions (§8). The variants are best explained as ancient attempts to smooth the same difficulty, which is itself an argument that the harder text is original.

διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου (20:28) — the word order. This is worth doing carefully because it is checkable and the results cut both ways.

  • The NT’s other two “through his own blood” phrases both use the first attributive position: Hebrews 9:12 διὰ … τοῦ ἰδίου αἵματος, Hebrews 13:12 διὰ τοῦ ἰδίου αἵματος. [T], full-NT search. Acts 20:28 has the second attributive position (article–noun–article–adjective), which is not what the NT does elsewhere with this exact phrase.
  • But Luke uses the second attributive position with ἴδιος elsewhere: Acts 1:25 εἰς τὸν τόπον τὸν ἴδιον, where the adjective is plainly attributive. [T]. So the construction is available to Luke as an ordinary attributive.
  • And Luke uses ἴδιος substantivally of persons: Acts 4:23 ἦλθον πρὸς τοὺς ἰδίους; 24:23 τῶν ἰδίων αὐτοῦ; cf. 21:6 εἰς τὰ ἴδια. [T]. So “the blood of his Own” is available too.

Conclusion: the word order neither settles the question nor is neutral. It removes the claim that “his own blood” is simply what the Greek says — the NT’s normal way of saying that is not what stands here — while Acts 1:25 removes any claim that the order forbids it. Luke supplies precedent for both readings, three verses apart in the same book. Say that from the pulpit if the question comes up; do not adjudicate it.

τοῦ μὴ ἀναγγεῖλαι (20:20, 27). Articular infinitive with redundant μή after a verb of hindering/withholding — normal Greek, and the μή should not be pressed. NA28 marks °μή as omitted in some witnesses at both verses, which is what one expects of scribes tidying a construction they found awkward.

ἐν ᾧ … ἔθετο ἐπισκόπους (20:28). The relative is neuter, agreeing with ποιμνίῳ: the Spirit placed them as overseers within the flock, not over it. English “made you overseers of the flock” (which no version to hand actually prints, but which is how it is generally heard) loses the preposition. The elders are inside what they guard — which is exactly the premise of 20:30.

ὀπίσω ἑαυτῶν (20:30). SBLGNT reads ἑαυτῶν, NA28 marks a variant (⸁αὐτῶν). The reflexive sharpens it: after themselves, in explicit contrast to the flock that is God’s.

Historical and Cultural Background

Only what the text needs.

Miletus and Ephesus. About 30 miles apart by road; a summons and a journey of two or three days each way, which is why Luke notes Paul was in a hurry (20:16) and yet waited. The delay is itself a datum about how much this meeting mattered.

Elders. The term is drawn from synagogue and civic usage; Luke has used it of Jewish elders eleven times before using it of Christian ones (2:17 aside, the Christian uses are 11:30; 14:23; 15:2, 4, 6, 22, 23; 16:4; 20:17; 21:18) [T]. A first-century hearer would have found nothing novel in a body of elders; what is novel is 20:28’s derivation of their appointment from the Holy Spirit rather than from the congregation or the founder — even though 14:23 says Paul and Barnabas appointed them. Both are said; Luke does not reconcile them, and neither should a sermon.

Working with one’s hands. Manual labour carried low status in the Greek city, and a teacher who worked was making a visible statement about his relation to patronage. 20:33–35 is therefore a claim about independence from patrons, which in Ephesus — where Paul had already cost the silversmiths money (19:24–27) — is pointed. [S] on the social convention; [T] on the Ephesian setting.

Kissing and the neck-embrace. The gesture is Semitic and biblical (Gen 33:4; 45:14; 46:29; Tob 11:9) rather than Greek; Luke’s readers meet it in the Greek Bible. See §11.

Original Audience Reception

In the narrative world, the Ephesian elders hear a man they have known for three years tell them that they will not see him again, that some of them will become the danger, and that he is handing them to God. Luke tells us which part landed (20:38): the first.

For Theophilus and the book’s first readers, the passage does something else. They are reading a church that has already lost its founding generation. The speech tells them (a) that this was foreseen and provided for, (b) that the provision is the Spirit’s appointment of overseers and the word of God’s grace, and (c) that internal corruption was expected — so that its appearance is not evidence that the movement was false. Given the book’s stated purpose (ἀσφάλεια), that third point is doing real work: a church that has seen leaders go bad can read 20:30 and know it was predicted. [I], moderate–high.

One thing they would have heard that we do not. The speech’s forensic vocabulary — ἐπίστασθε, γινώσκετε, μαρτύρομαι … ἐν τῇ σήμερον ἡμέρᾳ, καθαρός εἰμι — is the register of a deposition. To a reader who has just watched Paul be acquitted nowhere yet and is about to watch him acquitted four times, the register would be audible. [I], moderate.

Biblical-Theological Themes

  • The flock of God. Israel as God’s flock; leaders as shepherds accountable for it; the failure of the shepherds and God’s promise to shepherd them himself and then to raise one shepherd (Ezek 34:11, 23; Isa 63:11; Jer 23; Zech 11). Acts 20:28 places Christian elders inside that line — as the delegated form of an oversight that is first God’s own.
  • Acquisition. God acquires a people for himself: Exodus 19:5 (λαὸς περιούσιος), Exodus 32:14, Isaiah 43:21, Malachi 3:17. Acts 20:28 says the church is that acquisition and names its cost.
  • Inheritance among the sanctified. From land (Gen 15; Deut) through Stephen’s “not even a foot of it” (7:5) to τὴν κληρονομίαν ἐν τοῖς ἡγιασμένοις — a possession that is no longer territorial and is held among persons.
  • The finished course. A life as a measured distance (13:25; 20:24), with the completion, not the outcome, as the measure.
  • Blood and innocence. The watchman’s blood-guilt (Ezek 3, 33), Pilate’s hand-washing, τὸ αἷμα ὑμῶν ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν ὑμῶν (18:6), and here.

Schnittjer Pass

Schnittjer’s concern is how later Scripture uses earlier Scripture — the direction, the transformation, and what the later use does that the earlier text did not. Three observations in that key, none requiring the volume in hand:

  1. Isaiah 43:21 is used without being quoted. Acts does not say “as it is written”; it takes a rare verb in a distinctive form and applies it to a new object (ἐκκλησία for λαός). This is the allusive end of Schnittjer’s spectrum, and its effect is to make the identification without arguing it. [I].
  2. Isaiah 60:17’s oversight-language is re-attributed. In Isaiah, God gives Zion ἐπίσκοποι in righteousness; in Acts the Holy Spirit places them in the flock. The giver becomes specifically the Spirit — a Lukan transformation of an Isaianic promise, consistent with what the book does elsewhere with the Servant Songs (transfer of role, not merely of prediction). [I], moderate.
  3. Ezekiel 34’s judgement becomes a charge. Ezekiel indicts shepherds who fed themselves; Acts 20:28–31 does not indict anyone — it charges men before the failure and predicts that some will fail anyway. The oracle’s retrospective judgement is turned into prospective warning. [I], moderate.

Christological Reading

The passage does not preach Christ by naming him often. κύριος Ἰησοῦς appears twice (24, 35) and κύριος alone once (19). What it does is put Christ at three structural points:

  • Origin: τὴν διακονίαν ἣν ἔλαβον παρὰ τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ (24). Paul’s ministry is not his own initiative; it was received from a living person who gives commissions.
  • Cost: ἣν περιεποιήσατο διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου (28). Whichever way the grammar goes, blood is the means of the church’s acquisition, and the blood is God’s own or God’s Own’s. This is Acts’ most concentrated statement of the atonement, and it is made in passing, as the ground of a pastoral charge.
  • Authority: τῶν λόγων τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ ὅτι αὐτὸς εἶπεν (35). The speech’s final appeal is to a word of Jesus, and the emphatic αὐτός insists on the speaker.

The trajectory. The overview’s Christological thread is a living, acting, reigning Christ whose commission passes outward — to the missionaries, to Paul, and (26:18) to those Paul is sent to. This passage is the next step in that line and its most institutional form: what Christ gave Paul, Paul hands to elders, and the handing over is done by putting them in God’s hands, not his own. The Servant’s commission has become an office.

Moralism check. The temptation this passage invites is precise and severe: be a pastor like Paul — tireless, tearful, unmercenary, unflinching. Three text-level guards. (a) Every decisive verb in the charge belongs to the Spirit or to God: ἔθετο, περιεποιήσατο, τῷ δυναμένῳ οἰκοδομῆσαι καὶ δοῦναι. (b) Paul’s résumé is form-critical convention, not a standard (see §12, §13). (c) The speech’s last word is a saying of Jesus, not a summary of Paul — and it commends giving, which is what God has just been said to have done at the cost of blood. Christ as the one who acquired the flock, not Paul as the model of how to keep it.

Difficult / Contested Verses

20:22 — “bound by the Spirit” or “bound in spirit”? See Original Language Observations. Do not settle it from the pulpit; the point of the clause survives either way, because 20:23 makes the Spirit the source of what Paul knows.

20:22–23 against 21:4. At Tyre disciples tell Paul διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος not to go up. The sweep’s reading — that the Spirit’s information (bonds await) is consistently given and the inference (therefore do not go) is the disciples’ — is the honest one and is supported here: 20:23 says the Spirit testifies that bonds await, and says nothing about whether to go. [T] on what 20:23 attributes to the Spirit. Name the difficulty; do not smooth it.

20:25 — did Paul see them again? Acts never says. See Open Questions.

20:28 — the two questions. See Textual Variants and Original Language Observations. The pastoral force of the verse does not depend on either: the church is God’s, it was acquired, and the acquisition cost blood. Say the questions exist, in one sentence, and move to the charge.

20:30 — does this predict a specific apostasy? The text says men will arise from among you yourselves. Acts does not narrate the fulfilment. Anything more specific is imported.

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Preaching 20:28 as a proof-text for the deity of Christ

How it sounds: “God purchased the church with his own blood — here is the deity of Christ in one clause.” Why it is wrong: two questions stand in the clause, one textual (θεοῦ / κυρίου) and one grammatical (τοῦ ἰδίου attributive or substantival), and every study Bible in the pews has a footnote on at least one of them. Building the doctrine here makes it look as though the doctrine needs a contested clause. Instead: say in one sentence that the clause has two well-known questions, that neither affects the charge, and preach the charge. The doctrine is secure elsewhere and does not need rescuing.

Pitfall 2: Turning Paul’s résumé into the pastor’s job description

How it sounds: “Three years, night and day, with tears, house to house — is that your ministry?” Why it is wrong: the retrospect is a required feature of the farewell form (§12) and its function in the speech is to credential the charge that follows, not to set a standard. Luke gives the tears twice in the past tense (19, 31) and gives the present tears to the elders (37). Instead: preach the charge (28–32), and use the résumé for what the speech uses it for — as the reason these men should take the charge seriously.

Pitfall 3: Making 20:29–30 a warning about other people

How it sounds: “Wolves out there; watch for false teachers.” Why it is wrong: v.29 has wolves coming in, but v.30 immediately relocates the threat: ἐξ ὑμῶν αὐτῶν. And the first object of προσέχετε in v.28 is ἑαυτοῖς. The passage’s own order is self, then flock, then external threat, then internal threat — ending inside. Instead: preach it in Luke’s order. The sermon that ends with “and it could be one of us” is the one the text wrote.

Pitfall 4: Preaching 20:32’s “build up” as something the elders do

How it sounds: “God has given you elders who can build you up and give you an inheritance.” Why it is wrong: τῷ δυναμένῳ is singular and attaches to God and the word of his grace, not to the elders — and it is the reason Paul can leave. The whole point of the commendation is that what is being handed over is handed to God, not to men. Instead: let the grammar carry it. Paul goes; God and the word remain; the building is theirs to do.

Pitfall 5: Sentimentalising 20:36–38

How it sounds: a closing anecdote about what a beloved pastor Paul was. Why it is wrong: Luke tells us the grief was about v.25 — the loss of the face — and the passage’s argument is that the loss of the face does not matter as much as it feels, because God and the word remain (32) and because the kingdom is still being preached at 28:31. Instead: preach the tears as real and as answered. They are the last thing Luke says about the Ephesians and they are not rebuked; nor are they the last word.

Pitfall 6: Using 20:34 to settle a question about paid ministry

How it sounds: either “real ministers work for a living” or “Paul only did this because he had to”. Why it is wrong: the clause is in a paragraph about covetousness and independence from patrons (33–35), in a city where Paul had already been accused of damaging an industry (19:24–27). Acts is not addressing remuneration policy, and the letters that do are not available as controls in a text-first run. Instead: preach it as what the context makes it — a public disclaimer of financial interest, offered as evidence for the ministry’s integrity.

Open Questions / Uncertainties

  1. Did the Ephesians see Paul again? 20:25 says they will not; 20:38 says the narrator knows they took it hard; Acts never returns to it. The book neither confirms nor falsifies the prediction, and the translation question at v.25 (§8) affects how absolute the prediction is. Note that on the standard reconstruction of the Pastorals Paul returns to the region [S] — which is precisely why the silence matters: Luke had the chance to close the loop and did not.
  2. The ἐπίσκοπος / Isaiah 60:17 link. Is it noticed in the literature, and does anyone connect Acts 20:28 with 1 Clement 42:5’s use of Isaiah 60:17 for ἐπίσκοποι? Flagged for a Logos query. The claim is stated at moderate confidence and should not be preached as established until checked.
