## The Passage

NA28 above, NASB95 beneath. Where the ESV diverges in a way that bears on a finding, the wording is given under Tool 8 rather than here.

**6:8**  Στέφανος δὲ πλήρης χάριτος καὶ δυνάμεως ἐποίει τέρατα καὶ σημεῖα μεγάλα ἐν τῷ λαῷ.

> And Stephen, full of grace and power, was performing great wonders and signs among the people.

**6:9**  ἀνέστησαν δέ τινες τῶν ἐκ τῆς συναγωγῆς τῆς λεγομένης Λιβερτίνων καὶ Κυρηναίων καὶ Ἀλεξανδρέων καὶ τῶν ἀπὸ Κιλικίας καὶ Ἀσίας συζητοῦντες τῷ Στεφάνῳ,

> But some men from what was called the Synagogue of the Freedmen, including both Cyrenians and Alexandrians, and some from Cilicia and Asia, rose up and argued with Stephen.

**6:10**  καὶ οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀντιστῆναι τῇ σοφίᾳ καὶ τῷ πνεύματι ᾧ ἐλάλει.

> But they were unable to cope with the wisdom and the Spirit with which he was speaking.

**6:11**  τότε ὑπέβαλον ἄνδρας λέγοντας ὅτι ἀκηκόαμεν αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος ῥήματα βλάσφημα εἰς Μωϋσῆν καὶ τὸν θεόν.

> Then they secretly induced men to say, “We have heard him speak blasphemous words against Moses and against God.”

**6:12**  συνεκίνησάν τε τὸν λαὸν καὶ τοὺς πρεσβυτέρους καὶ τοὺς γραμματεῖς καὶ ἐπιστάντες συνήρπασαν αὐτὸν καὶ ἤγαγον εἰς τὸ συνέδριον,

> And they stirred up the people, the elders and the scribes, and they came up to him and dragged him away and brought him before the Council.

**6:13**  ἔστησάν τε μάρτυρας ψευδεῖς λέγοντας· ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὗτος οὐ παύεται λαλῶν ῥήματα κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου τούτου καὶ τοῦ νόμου·

> They put forward false witnesses who said, “This man incessantly speaks against this holy place and the Law;

**6:14**  ἀκηκόαμεν γὰρ αὐτοῦ λέγοντος ὅτι Ἰησοῦς ὁ Ναζωραῖος οὗτος καταλύσει τὸν τόπον τοῦτον καὶ ἀλλάξει τὰ ἔθη ἃ παρέδωκεν ἡμῖν Μωϋσῆς.

> for we have heard him say that this Nazarene, Jesus, will destroy this place and alter the customs which Moses handed down to us.”

**6:15**  καὶ ἀτενίσαντες εἰς αὐτὸν πάντες οἱ καθεζόμενοι ἐν τῷ συνεδρίῳ εἶδον τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ ὡσεὶ πρόσωπον ἀγγέλου.

> And fixing their gaze on him, all who were sitting in the Council saw his face like the face of an angel.

**7:1**  Εἶπεν δὲ ὁ ἀρχιερεύς· εἰ ταῦτα οὕτως ἔχει;

> The high priest said, “Are these things so?”

**7:2**  ὁ δὲ ἔφη· Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες, ἀκούσατε. Ὁ θεὸς τῆς δόξης ὤφθη τῷ πατρὶ ἡμῶν Ἀβραὰμ ὄντι ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ πρὶν ἢ κατοικῆσαι αὐτὸν ἐν Χαρρὰν

> And he said, “Hear me, brethren and fathers! The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia, before he lived in Haran,

**7:3**  καὶ εἶπεν πρὸς αὐτόν· ἔξελθε ἐκ τῆς γῆς σου καὶ ἐκ τῆς συγγενείας σου, καὶ δεῦρο εἰς τὴν γῆν ἣν ἄν σοι δείξω.

> and said to him, ‘LEAVE YOUR COUNTRY AND YOUR RELATIVES, AND COME INTO THE LAND THAT I WILL SHOW YOU.’

**7:4**  τότε ἐξελθὼν ἐκ γῆς Χαλδαίων κατῴκησεν ἐν Χαρράν. κἀκεῖθεν μετὰ τὸ ἀποθανεῖν τὸν πατέρα αὐτοῦ μετῴκισεν αὐτὸν εἰς τὴν γῆν ταύτην εἰς ἣν ὑμεῖς νῦν κατοικεῖτε,

> “Then he left the land of the Chaldeans and settled in Haran. From there, after his father died, God had him move to this country in which you are now living.

**7:5**  καὶ οὐκ ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ κληρονομίαν ἐν αὐτῇ οὐδὲ βῆμα ποδὸς καὶ ἐπηγγείλατο δοῦναι αὐτῷ εἰς κατάσχεσιν αὐτὴν καὶ τῷ σπέρματι αὐτοῦ μετʼ αὐτόν, οὐκ ὄντος αὐτῷ τέκνου.

> “But He gave him no inheritance in it, not even a foot of ground, and yet, even when he had no child, He promised that HE WOULD GIVE IT TO HIM AS A POSSESSION, AND TO HIS DESCENDANTS AFTER HIM.

**7:6**  ἐλάλησεν δὲ οὕτως ὁ θεὸς ὅτι ἔσται τὸ σπέρμα αὐτοῦ πάροικον ἐν γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ καὶ δουλώσουσιν αὐτὸ καὶ κακώσουσιν ἔτη τετρακόσια·

> “But God spoke to this effect, that his DESCENDANTS WOULD BE ALIENS IN A FOREIGN LAND, AND THAT THEY WOULD BE ENSLAVED AND MISTREATED FOR FOUR HUNDRED YEARS.

**7:7**  καὶ τὸ ἔθνος ᾧ ἐὰν δουλεύσουσιν κρινῶ ἐγώ, ὁ θεὸς εἶπεν, καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα ἐξελεύσονται καὶ λατρεύσουσίν μοι ἐν τῷ τόπῳ τούτῳ.

> “ ‘AND WHATEVER NATION TO WHICH THEY WILL BE IN BONDAGE I MYSELF WILL JUDGE,’ said God, ‘AND AFTER THAT THEY WILL COME OUT AND SERVE ME IN THIS PLACE.’

**7:8**  καὶ ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ διαθήκην περιτομῆς· καὶ οὕτως ἐγέννησεν τὸν Ἰσαὰκ καὶ περιέτεμεν αὐτὸν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ τῇ ὀγδόῃ, καὶ Ἰσαὰκ τὸν Ἰακώβ, καὶ Ἰακὼβ τοὺς δώδεκα πατριάρχας.

> “And He gave him the covenant of circumcision; and so Abraham became the father of Isaac, and circumcised him on the eighth day; and Isaac became the father of Jacob, and Jacob of the twelve patriarchs.

**7:9**  Καὶ οἱ πατριάρχαι ζηλώσαντες τὸν Ἰωσὴφ ἀπέδοντο εἰς Αἴγυπτον. καὶ ἦν ὁ θεὸς μετʼ αὐτοῦ

> “The patriarchs became jealous of Joseph and sold him into Egypt. Yet God was with him,

**7:10**  καὶ ἐξείλατο αὐτὸν ἐκ πασῶν τῶν θλίψεων αὐτοῦ καὶ ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ χάριν καὶ σοφίαν ἐναντίον Φαραὼ βασιλέως Αἰγύπτου καὶ κατέστησεν αὐτὸν ἡγούμενον ἐπʼ Αἴγυπτον καὶ ἐφʼ ὅλον τὸν οἶκον αὐτοῦ.

> and rescued him from all his afflictions, and granted him favor and wisdom in the sight of Pharaoh, king of Egypt, and he made him governor over Egypt and all his household.

**7:11**  ἦλθεν δὲ λιμὸς ἐφʼ ὅλην τὴν Αἴγυπτον καὶ Χανάαν καὶ θλῖψις μεγάλη, καὶ οὐχ ηὕρισκον χορτάσματα οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν.

> “Now a famine came over all Egypt and Canaan, and great affliction with it, and our fathers could find no food.

**7:12**  ἀκούσας δὲ Ἰακὼβ ὄντα σιτία εἰς Αἴγυπτον ἐξαπέστειλεν τοὺς πατέρας ἡμῶν πρῶτον.

> “But when Jacob heard that there was grain in Egypt, he sent our fathers there the first time.

**7:13**  καὶ ἐν τῷ δευτέρῳ ἀνεγνωρίσθη Ἰωσὴφ τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς αὐτοῦ καὶ φανερὸν ἐγένετο τῷ Φαραὼ τὸ γένος τοῦ Ἰωσήφ.

> “On the second visit Joseph made himself known to his brothers, and Joseph’s family was disclosed to Pharaoh.

**7:14**  ἀποστείλας δὲ Ἰωσὴφ μετεκαλέσατο Ἰακὼβ τὸν πατέρα αὐτοῦ καὶ πᾶσαν τὴν συγγένειαν ἐν ψυχαῖς ἑβδομήκοντα πέντε.

> “Then Joseph sent word and invited Jacob his father and all his relatives to come to him, seventy-five persons in all.

**7:15**  καὶ κατέβη Ἰακὼβ εἰς Αἴγυπτον καὶ ἐτελεύτησεν αὐτὸς καὶ οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν,

> “And Jacob went down to Egypt and there he and our fathers died.

**7:16**  καὶ μετετέθησαν εἰς Συχὲμ καὶ ἐτέθησαν ἐν τῷ μνήματι ᾧ ὠνήσατο Ἀβραὰμ τιμῆς ἀργυρίου παρὰ τῶν υἱῶν Ἐμμὼρ ἐν Συχέμ.

> “From there they were removed to Shechem and laid in the tomb which Abraham had purchased for a sum of money from the sons of Hamor in Shechem.

**7:17**  Καθὼς δὲ ἤγγιζεν ὁ χρόνος τῆς ἐπαγγελίας ἧς ὡμολόγησεν ὁ θεὸς τῷ Ἀβραάμ, ηὔξησεν ὁ λαὸς καὶ ἐπληθύνθη ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ

> “But as the time of the promise was approaching which God had assured to Abraham, the people increased and multiplied in Egypt,

**7:18**  ἄχρι οὗ ἀνέστη βασιλεὺς ἕτερος ἐπʼ Αἴγυπτον ὃς οὐκ ᾔδει τὸν Ἰωσήφ.

> until THERE AROSE ANOTHER KING OVER EGYPT WHO KNEW NOTHING ABOUT JOSEPH.

**7:19**  οὗτος κατασοφισάμενος τὸ γένος ἡμῶν ἐκάκωσεν τοὺς πατέρας ἡμῶν τοῦ ποιεῖν τὰ βρέφη ἔκθετα αὐτῶν εἰς τὸ μὴ ζῳογονεῖσθαι.

> “It was he who took shrewd advantage of our race and mistreated our fathers so that they would expose their infants and they would not survive.

**7:20**  Ἐν ᾧ καιρῷ ἐγεννήθη Μωϋσῆς καὶ ἦν ἀστεῖος τῷ θεῷ· ὃς ἀνετράφη μῆνας τρεῖς ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ πατρός,

> “It was at this time that Moses was born; and he was lovely in the sight of God, and he was nurtured three months in his father’s home.

**7:21**  ἐκτεθέντος δὲ αὐτοῦ ἀνείλατο αὐτὸν ἡ θυγάτηρ Φαραὼ καὶ ἀνεθρέψατο αὐτὸν ἑαυτῇ εἰς υἱόν.

> “And after he had been set outside, Pharaoh’s daughter took him away and nurtured him as her own son.

**7:22**  καὶ ἐπαιδεύθη Μωϋσῆς ἐν πάσῃ σοφίᾳ Αἰγυπτίων, ἦν δὲ δυνατὸς ἐν λόγοις καὶ ἔργοις αὐτοῦ.

> “Moses was educated in all the learning of the Egyptians, and he was a man of power in words and deeds.

**7:23**  Ὡς δὲ ἐπληροῦτο αὐτῷ τεσσερακονταετὴς χρόνος, ἀνέβη ἐπὶ τὴν καρδίαν αὐτοῦ ἐπισκέψασθαι τοὺς ἀδελφοὺς αὐτοῦ τοὺς υἱοὺς Ἰσραήλ.

> “But when he was approaching the age of forty, it entered his mind to visit his brethren, the sons of Israel.

**7:24**  καὶ ἰδών τινα ἀδικούμενον ἠμύνατο καὶ ἐποίησεν ἐκδίκησιν τῷ καταπονουμένῳ πατάξας τὸν Αἰγύπτιον.

> “And when he saw one of them being treated unjustly, he defended him and took vengeance for the oppressed by striking down the Egyptian.

**7:25**  ἐνόμιζεν δὲ συνιέναι τοὺς ἀδελφοὺς αὐτοῦ ὅτι ὁ θεὸς διὰ χειρὸς αὐτοῦ δίδωσιν σωτηρίαν αὐτοῖς· οἱ δὲ οὐ συνῆκαν.

> “And he supposed that his brethren understood that God was granting them deliverance through him, but they did not understand.

**7:26**  τῇ τε ἐπιούσῃ ἡμέρᾳ ὤφθη αὐτοῖς μαχομένοις καὶ συνήλλασσεν αὐτοὺς εἰς εἰρήνην εἰπών· ἄνδρες, ἀδελφοί ἐστε· ἱνατί ἀδικεῖτε ἀλλήλους;

> “On the following day he appeared to them as they were fighting together, and he tried to reconcile them in peace, saying, ‘Men, you are brethren, why do you injure one another?’

**7:27**  ὁ δὲ ἀδικῶν τὸν πλησίον ἀπώσατο αὐτὸν εἰπών· τίς σε κατέστησεν ἄρχοντα καὶ δικαστὴν ἐφʼ ἡμῶν;

> “But the one who was injuring his neighbor pushed him away, saying, ‘WHO MADE YOU A RULER AND JUDGE OVER US?

**7:28**  μὴ ἀνελεῖν με σὺ θέλεις ὃν τρόπον ἀνεῖλες ἐχθὲς τὸν Αἰγύπτιον;

> ‘YOU DO NOT MEAN TO KILL ME AS YOU KILLED THE EGYPTIAN YESTERDAY, DO YOU?’

**7:29**  ἔφυγεν δὲ Μωϋσῆς ἐν τῷ λόγῳ τούτῳ καὶ ἐγένετο πάροικος ἐν γῇ Μαδιάμ, οὗ ἐγέννησεν υἱοὺς δύο.

> “At this remark, MOSES FLED AND BECAME AN ALIEN IN THE LAND OF MIDIAN, where he became the father of two sons.

**7:30**  Καὶ πληρωθέντων ἐτῶν τεσσεράκοντα ὤφθη αὐτῷ ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ τοῦ ὄρους Σινᾶ ἄγγελος ἐν φλογὶ πυρὸς βάτου.

> “After forty years had passed, AN ANGEL APPEARED TO HIM IN THE WILDERNESS OF MOUNT Sinai, IN THE FLAME OF A BURNING THORN BUSH.

**7:31**  ὁ δὲ Μωϋσῆς ἰδὼν ἐθαύμαζεν τὸ ὅραμα, προσερχομένου δὲ αὐτοῦ κατανοῆσαι ἐγένετο φωνὴ κυρίου·

> “When Moses saw it, he marveled at the sight; and as he approached to look more closely, there came the voice of the Lord:

**7:32**  ἐγὼ ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων σου, ὁ θεὸς Ἀβραὰμ καὶ Ἰσαὰκ καὶ Ἰακώβ. ἔντρομος δὲ γενόμενος Μωϋσῆς οὐκ ἐτόλμα κατανοῆσαι.

> ‘I AM THE GOD OF YOUR FATHERS, THE GOD OF ABRAHAM AND ISAAC AND JACOB.’ Moses shook with fear and would not venture to look.

**7:33**  εἶπεν δὲ αὐτῷ ὁ κύριος· λῦσον τὸ ὑπόδημα τῶν ποδῶν σου, ὁ γὰρ τόπος ἐφʼ ᾧ ἕστηκας γῆ ἁγία ἐστίν.

> “BUT THE LORD SAID TO HIM, ‘TAKE OFF THE SANDALS FROM YOUR FEET, FOR THE PLACE ON WHICH YOU ARE STANDING IS HOLY GROUND.

**7:34**  ἰδὼν εἶδον τὴν κάκωσιν τοῦ λαοῦ μου τοῦ ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ καὶ τοῦ στεναγμοῦ αὐτῶν ἤκουσα, καὶ κατέβην ἐξελέσθαι αὐτούς· καὶ νῦν δεῦρο ἀποστείλω σε εἰς Αἴγυπτον.

> ‘I HAVE CERTAINLY SEEN THE OPPRESSION OF MY PEOPLE IN EGYPT AND HAVE HEARD THEIR GROANS, AND I HAVE COME DOWN TO RESCUE THEM; COME NOW, AND I WILL SEND YOU TO EGYPT.’

**7:35**  Τοῦτον τὸν Μωϋσῆν ὃν ἠρνήσαντο εἰπόντες· τίς σε κατέστησεν ἄρχοντα καὶ δικαστήν; τοῦτον ὁ θεὸς καὶ ἄρχοντα καὶ λυτρωτὴν ἀπέσταλκεν σὺν χειρὶ ἀγγέλου τοῦ ὀφθέντος αὐτῷ ἐν τῇ βάτῳ.

> “This Moses whom they disowned, saying, ‘WHO MADE YOU A RULER AND A JUDGE?’ is the one whom God sent to be both a ruler and a deliverer with the help of the angel who appeared to him in the thorn bush.

**7:36**  οὗτος ἐξήγαγεν αὐτοὺς ποιήσας τέρατα καὶ σημεῖα ἐν γῇ Αἰγύπτῳ καὶ ἐν ἐρυθρᾷ θαλάσσῃ καὶ ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ ἔτη τεσσεράκοντα.

> “This man led them out, performing wonders and signs in the land of Egypt and in the Red Sea and in the wilderness for forty years.

**7:37**  οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ Μωϋσῆς ὁ εἴπας τοῖς υἱοῖς Ἰσραήλ· προφήτην ὑμῖν ἀναστήσει ὁ θεὸς ἐκ τῶν ἀδελφῶν ὑμῶν ὡς ἐμέ.

> “This is the Moses who said to the sons of Israel, ‘GOD WILL RAISE UP FOR YOU A PROPHET LIKE ME FROM YOUR BRETHREN.’

**7:38**  οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ γενόμενος ἐν τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ μετὰ τοῦ ἀγγέλου τοῦ λαλοῦντος αὐτῷ ἐν τῷ ὄρει Σινᾶ καὶ τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν, ὃς ἐδέξατο λόγια ζῶντα δοῦναι ἡμῖν,

> “This is the one who was in the congregation in the wilderness together with the angel who was speaking to him on Mount Sinai, and who was with our fathers; and he received living oracles to pass on to you.

**7:39**  ᾧ οὐκ ἠθέλησαν ὑπήκοοι γενέσθαι οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν, ἀλλʼ ἀπώσαντο καὶ ἐστράφησαν ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις αὐτῶν εἰς Αἴγυπτον

> “Our fathers were unwilling to be obedient to him, but repudiated him and in their hearts turned back to Egypt,

**7:40**  εἰπόντες τῷ Ἀαρών· ποίησον ἡμῖν θεοὺς οἳ προπορεύσονται ἡμῶν· ὁ γὰρ Μωϋσῆς οὗτος, ὃς ἐξήγαγεν ἡμᾶς ἐκ γῆς Αἰγύπτου, οὐκ οἴδαμεν τί ἐγένετο αὐτῷ.

> SAYING TO AARON, ‘MAKE FOR US GODS WHO WILL GO BEFORE US; FOR THIS MOSES WHO LED US OUT OF THE LAND OF EGYPT—WE DO NOT KNOW WHAT HAPPENED TO HIM.’

**7:41**  καὶ ἐμοσχοποίησαν ἐν ταῖς ἡμέραις ἐκείναις καὶ ἀνήγαγον θυσίαν τῷ εἰδώλῳ καὶ εὐφραίνοντο ἐν τοῖς ἔργοις τῶν χειρῶν αὐτῶν.

> “At that time they made a calf and brought a sacrifice to the idol, and were rejoicing in the works of their hands.

**7:42**  ἔστρεψεν δὲ ὁ θεὸς καὶ παρέδωκεν αὐτοὺς λατρεύειν τῇ στρατιᾷ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καθὼς γέγραπται ἐν βίβλῳ τῶν προφητῶν· μὴ σφάγια καὶ θυσίας προσηνέγκατέ μοι ἔτη τεσσεράκοντα ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ, οἶκος Ἰσραήλ;

> “But God turned away and delivered them up to serve the host of heaven; as it is written in the book of the prophets, ‘IT WAS NOT TO ME THAT YOU OFFERED VICTIMS AND SACRIFICES FORTY YEARS IN THE WILDERNESS, WAS IT, O HOUSE OF ISRAEL?

**7:43**  καὶ ἀνελάβετε τὴν σκηνὴν τοῦ Μόλοχ καὶ τὸ ἄστρον τοῦ θεοῦ ὑμῶν Ῥαιφάν, τοὺς τύπους οὓς ἐποιήσατε προσκυνεῖν αὐτοῖς, καὶ μετοικιῶ ὑμᾶς ἐπέκεινα Βαβυλῶνος.

> ‘YOU ALSO TOOK ALONG THE TABERNACLE OF MOLOCH AND THE STAR OF THE GOD ROMPHA, THE IMAGES WHICH YOU MADE TO WORSHIP. I ALSO WILL REMOVE YOU BEYOND BABYLON.’

**7:44**  Ἡ σκηνὴ τοῦ μαρτυρίου ἦν τοῖς πατράσιν ἡμῶν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ καθὼς διετάξατο ὁ λαλῶν τῷ Μωϋσῇ ποιῆσαι αὐτὴν κατὰ τὸν τύπον ὃν ἑωράκει·

> “Our fathers had the tabernacle of testimony in the wilderness, just as He who spoke to Moses directed him to make it according to the pattern which he had seen.

**7:45**  ἣν καὶ εἰσήγαγον διαδεξάμενοι οἱ πατέρες ἡμῶν μετὰ Ἰησοῦ ἐν τῇ κατασχέσει τῶν ἐθνῶν, ὧν ἐξῶσεν ὁ θεὸς ἀπὸ προσώπου τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν ἕως τῶν ἡμερῶν Δαυίδ,

> “And having received it in their turn, our fathers brought it in with Joshua upon dispossessing the nations whom God drove out before our fathers, until the time of David.

**7:46**  ὃς εὗρεν χάριν ἐνώπιον τοῦ θεοῦ καὶ ᾐτήσατο εὑρεῖν σκήνωμα τῷ οἴκῳ Ἰακώβ.

> “David found favor in God’s sight, and asked that he might find a dwelling place for the God of Jacob.

**7:47**  Σολομὼν δὲ οἰκοδόμησεν αὐτῷ οἶκον.

> “But it was Solomon who built a house for Him.

**7:48**  ἀλλʼ οὐχ ὁ ὕψιστος ἐν χειροποιήτοις κατοικεῖ, καθὼς ὁ προφήτης λέγει·

> “However, the Most High does not dwell in houses made by human hands; as the prophet says:

**7:49**  ὁ οὐρανός μοι θρόνος, ἡ δὲ γῆ ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν μου ποῖον οἶκον οἰκοδομήσετέ μοι, λέγει κύριος, ἢ τίς τόπος τῆς καταπαύσεώς μου;

> ‘HEAVEN IS MY THRONE,

**7:50**  οὐχὶ ἡ χείρ μου ἐποίησεν ταῦτα πάντα;

> ‘WAS IT NOT MY HAND WHICH MADE ALL THESE THINGS?’

**7:51**  Σκληροτράχηλοι καὶ ἀπερίτμητοι καρδίαις καὶ τοῖς ὠσίν, ὑμεῖς ἀεὶ τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ ἀντιπίπτετε ὡς οἱ πατέρες ὑμῶν καὶ ὑμεῖς.

> “You men who are stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears are always resisting the Holy Spirit; you are doing just as your fathers did.

**7:52**  τίνα τῶν προφητῶν οὐκ ἐδίωξαν οἱ πατέρες ὑμῶν; καὶ ἀπέκτειναν τοὺς προκαταγγείλαντας περὶ τῆς ἐλεύσεως τοῦ δικαίου, οὗ νῦν ὑμεῖς προδόται καὶ φονεῖς ἐγένεσθε,

> “Which one of the prophets did your fathers not persecute? They killed those who had previously announced the coming of the Righteous One, whose betrayers and murderers you have now become;

## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools. **Revised in place at v1.1** (25 November 2026) from Acts claim audits 2 and 3 and Logos round 3. The v1.0 wording is preserved in the backup; what changed and why is set out in *What Changed in v1.1* below.

1. **Stephen's opening paraphrase of Genesis 15 brings in wording from three other texts, and the third turns Scripture's "this mountain" into "this place".** `[T]`
   - **7:5.** *οὐδὲ βῆμα ποδός* with a negated *δίδωμι*, a string found once in the Greek Old Testament (Deut 2:5, of Edom's land) and once in the New. `[S: audit 2, A1 — moderate–high; wording high]`
   - **7:6.** Stephen replaces Gen 15:13's *ἐν γῇ οὐκ ἰδίᾳ* with *πάροικον ἐν γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ*. *πάροικος* with *γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ* stands in Swete and in Rahlfs 1979 only at Exod 2:22 and 18:3, where Moses names Gershom, and Stephen returns to that moment at 7:29. `[S: audit 3, A20 — moderate–high; no history of interpretation found]`
   - **7:7.** Exod 3:12 is spliced onto Gen 15:14, and *ἐν τῷ ὄρει τούτῳ* becomes *ἐν τῷ τόπῳ τούτῳ*. Splice and substitution **high**.

   The substitution has two hooks in texts Stephen is using. The Greek of Gen 15:14 adds *ὧδε* ("here"), and Exod 3:5, quoted at 7:33, already calls the mountain *ὁ τόπος*. So "this place" answers the charge's *ὁ τόπος οὗτος* (6:13–14). The reading is **moderate** `[S: audit 2, A3]`.

   v1.0 called this "the hinge of the whole speech". That overstated it. *τόπος* stands only three times in the speech itself (7:7, 33, 49), two of them inside quotations, and it clusters no more than chance would give `[S: audit 3, A26]`. The finding is the run of three splices in three verses, not a *τόπος* thread.

   Surfaced by: Phase 0.6, Tool 7, Tool 11 (Moves 1 and 4); audits 2–3.

2. **One verb of compulsory resettlement brackets the speech, and it occurs twice in the New Testament — both times here.** `[T]` *μετοικίζω* ("to remove, resettle") stands at 7:4, where God moves Abraham into the land, and at 7:43, where God says *μετοικιῶ ὑμᾶς ἐπέκεινα Βαβυλῶνος* ("I will remove you beyond Babylon"). The second is lifted out of the Amos citation, whose own wording Stephen then alters from Damascus to Babylon. Surfaced by: Tools 3, 10, 11. The speech's frame is God moving a landless man in and a settled people out, in one word. *(Unchanged at v1.1; not audited — a lemma chain.)*

3. **Isaiah's question (7:49–50) is set against the Davidic–Solomonic temple tradition, not against Psalm 132 alone.** `[T]`
   - **7:46 and Ps 131:5.** 7:46 echoes Ps 131:5 (Greek), *εὑρεῖν σκήνωμα*. The echo is verbatim only on the SBLGNT's *θεῷ Ἰακώβ*. **NA28 prints *οἴκῳ Ἰακώβ***, with 𝔓⁷⁴, the first hand of Sinaiticus, B and D, the harder reading. `[S: Comfort, NTTTC 360; Westcott–Hort, Appendix 92]`
   - **7:48–49 and Ps 131:14.** These verses set *κατοικέω* beside the noun *κατάπαυσις*. In Swete the two stand together only at Ps 131:14 ("here I will dwell"). `[S: audit 3, A33]`
   - **Isa 57:15** (*ὁ ὕψιστος … κατοικῶν*) shares two of 7:48's three content words.
   - **The dedication tradition.** The fullest Old Testament form of the sequence David → Solomon → "will God indeed dwell?" is 1 Kgs 8 // 2 Chr 6, and 2 Chr 6:41–42 itself quotes Ps 132. `[S: Walton, citing Sylva; audit 3, A23 — moderate]`
   - ***χειροποίητος* with *κατοικέω*** stands in the New Testament only at 7:48 and 17:24. In the Greek Old Testament *χειροποίητος* is used of idols, and once of Moab's sanctuary (Isa 16:12). `[S: audit 3, A31 — moderate–high]`

   Two readings of the speech's stance are on record: transcendence, which does not reject Solomon's temple (Walton, Sylva), and critique (Beale, NSBT 17; Squires, ECB). **Moderate.**

   *v1.0's Headline 3, the absence of ἱερόν, is withdrawn.* Its counts were wrong. *ἱερόν* stands in 24 Acts verses: twelve in chs 2–5, one at 19:27 (Artemis) and eleven from 21:26. The word is absent from 5:42 to 19:27, so its absence here is one part of an ordinary gap `[S: audit 2, A8; audit 3, A26]`.

   Surfaced by: Tool 11 (Moves 1–3), Textual Variants; audits 2–3; Logos round 3.

4. **Stephen is introduced with the rare order "wonders and signs", which Luke set when he added "signs" to Joel at 2:19.** `[T]`
   - **How rare it is.** In the Greek Bible "wonders and signs" stands in six verses: five in Acts 2–7 (2:19, split across clauses; 2:22; 2:43; 6:8; 7:36) and Wis 10:16, of Moses. Elsewhere the New Testament has "signs and wonders" seven times out of seven, and Swete has it in 29 verses.
   - **Acts alternates.** The order is not confined to one stretch. 4:30, 5:12, 14:3 and 15:12 have "signs and wonders", a chain that begins with the church's prayer.
   - **The order does not track the agent.** The apostles are given both orders (2:43 against 5:12).
   - **Its meaning is moderate**, and three readings are on record: Luke's sources (Longenecker, EBC 9:317), typology (Rengstorf, TDNT) and no significance (Remus, AYBD 4:857). `[S: audit 3, A25]`
   - **The triad.** *δύναμις* + *τέρας* + *σημεῖον* at 2:22 and 6:8 is a stock formula, and at 6:8 *δύναμις* is Stephen's endowment, not a deed. **Low–moderate.** `[S: audit 2, A6]`
   - ***σοφία*** occurs four times in Acts, all in this complex (6:3, 10; 7:10, 22). This stands as a verified lemma chain.

   Surfaced by: Tools 1, 7, 10, Tool 11 (Move 4); audits 2–3.

5. **Stephen stands inside Israel for the whole speech, and steps outside it exactly twice — and the first time is about the land.** `[T]` In his own voice (that is, excluding the pronouns inside his quotations) he says "our/us" eleven times and "you" three.
   - The first "you" is at **7:4**: *εἰς τὴν γῆν ταύτην εἰς ἣν **ὑμεῖς** νῦν κατοικεῖτε* ("into this land in which **you** now dwell").
   - The other two are at **7:51 and 7:52**, in the indictment.

   Before the indictment, the one thing the man accused of speaking against the place will not say "we" about is the place. Surfaced by: Tools 4, 7, 9, 16. *(Unchanged at v1.1; not audited.)*

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Preceding movement.** The overview's Arc Map puts the seam at **6:7** and opens M3 ("the pivot outward") at **6:8**. What 3:1–6:7 has established, and what this unit now requires:

- **A council that has twice tried and twice released.** 4:1–22 and 5:17–42 are two hearings before the same body, and both end in release. The second ends with Gamaliel: *ἐὰν ᾖ ἐξ ἀνθρώπων … **καταλυθήσεται*** ("if it is of men, it will be overthrown", 5:38) and *μήποτε καὶ θεομάχοι εὑρεθῆτε* ("lest you be found fighting against God", 5:39), after which *ἐπείσθησαν δὲ αὐτῷ* — "they were persuaded by him". **The pattern established is: the council is cut to the heart, wants to kill, and is talked out of it.** `[T]`
- **An unresolved charge.** Nothing in chapters 3–5 has accused the movement of anything to do with the temple or the law. The apostles are charged with teaching in the name (4:17–18; 5:28). The temple-and-law charge arrives for the first time at 6:13.
- **A fracture just healed by appointing men over a task.** 6:1–6 resolves the Hellenist complaint by having the Twelve say *καταστήσομεν* ("we will appoint", 6:3) seven men *πλήρεις πνεύματος καὶ **σοφίας*** over it. `[T]`
- **A word that has begun to grow.** 6:7 is the first of the four growth-notices, in the vocabulary of Exodus 1:7.

**Necessity answer — why here and not elsewhere.** Three answers, and the third is the one the text makes and commentary most often misses.

1. **Because the appointing is what makes the charge possible.** The unit opens with one of the seven men just appointed, and the verb of his appointing is the verb that will be thrown at Moses twice in his own speech. *καθίστημι* ("to appoint, set over") stands five times in Acts: **6:3** (the Seven), **7:10** (God appointed Joseph governor), **7:27** and **7:35** (*τίς σε κατέστησεν ἄρχοντα καὶ δικαστήν;* — "who appointed you a ruler and a judge?"), and 17:15. **Four of the five are in this unit's complex, and the question "who appointed you?" is asked of Moses in the vocabulary in which the church has just appointed Stephen.** `[T]` *High confidence* on the distribution, verified by lemma against the SBLGNT. The speech is therefore not a digression: the man under examination has been appointed by a community, and he answers by telling the story of two men God appointed whose brothers asked them who had.

2. **Because the council's restraint has to be shown to fail, and Luke has built the apparatus for showing it.** *διαπρίομαι* ("to be sawn through, cut to the quick") stands in **two verses of the New Testament, both in Acts: 5:33 and 7:54** `[T]` — verified by lemma across all twenty-seven books, after the search for it under the active form *διαπρίω* returned a nil (the index files middle-only verbs under the middle; see the Text-First Declaration). At 5:33 they are cut to the quick, want to kill, and Gamaliel stops them. At 7:54 they are cut to the quick, and nobody speaks. **The same reaction twice, and the difference is that the second time there is no Gamaliel** — whose pupil, as Paul will say at 22:3, is the young man at the coats two verses later. *High confidence* on the distribution; the reading of the contrast is `[I]`, *moderate–high*.

3. **Because the temple question has to be opened before the mission can move, and only this unit opens it.** The programme of 1:8 requires Judaea, Samaria and the ends of the earth. What the book has not yet asked is whether the God of Israel is locatable — and the answer at 7:48 (*οὐχ ὁ ὕψιστος ἐν χειροποιήτοις κατοικεῖ*) is the premise of everything after 8:1. **The sentence Stephen is killed for is the sentence Paul will say to Athens** — *κατοικέω* ("to dwell") with *χειροποίητος* ("made by hands") stands in exactly two verses of the New Testament, **7:48 and 17:24** `[T]`, verified by collocation. The overview carries *χειροποίητος* alone; the pair is tighter, because it is the verb that makes it a claim about where God lives rather than about what hands make.

**Implication for this passage.** The speech is not a potted history that happens to end badly. It exists at this point because the book cannot proceed to Samaria, to a eunuch, or to a Gentile household while God has a postcode. The scattering of 8:1 is caused by the speech, and the speech's content is what makes the scattering theologically survivable rather than merely a disaster.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 sharpens answer 3 and adds a fourth. Deuteronomy 18:15 has already been used, by Peter, at 3:22 — **with** the command to listen and **with** the penalty for not listening. Stephen uses the same verse at 7:37 and quotes **only the promise**, dropping *αὐτοῦ ἀκούσεσθε* ("him you shall hear"), which much of the manuscript tradition then restores (Tool 11, Move 2). The book-usage pattern therefore shows a deliberate division of labour: **Peter cited Moses' command to listen; Stephen makes the charge of not listening in his own words** (7:51–53) rather than in Moses'. That is positional too — the second use of a text can only do this because the first use established it.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** As the overview has it: to give a mixed church certainty that the risen Jesus is still acting, by his Spirit, through witnesses, under a purpose no opposition can obstruct — and that the Gentile mission is that purpose rather than a deviation from it. `[S: overview]`

**Passage-level.** Three contributions, in descending order of confidence.

**(a) To establish that God has never been locatable, so that the mission's movement is not apostasy.** This is the unit's own argument and it is made structurally: every section of the speech puts God's decisive act somewhere other than the temple's site — Mesopotamia (7:2), Haran (7:4), Egypt (7:9–10), Midian (7:29–33), Sinai (7:38), the wilderness tent (7:44). `[T]` The one building in the speech gets one verse (7:47) and is immediately qualified (7:48).

**(b) To put the first of the book's deaths in the vocabulary of the Lord's own, and so to show what a witness is.** The unit introduces *μάρτυς* in a sense the book has not yet used. The word has been the apostolic office (1:8, 1:22, 2:32, 3:15, 5:32); here it is used twice, and **both times of people testifying against Stephen** — *μάρτυρας ψευδεῖς* ("false witnesses", 6:13) and *οἱ μάρτυρες* at the stoning (7:58). `[T]` Paul will call Stephen *ὁ μάρτυς σου* ("your witness") at 22:20. See Tool 7.

**(c) To put Saul where the reader can see him.** 7:58's *νεανίου καλουμένου Σαύλου* is Luke's narration, not Stephen's speech, and it is the only thing in the unit that serves no purpose inside the unit. `[T]`

**Passage-level purpose in one sentence:** *to show that the God who has never had an address has now taken his seat, and his standing — and that the first man to say so dies saying what Israel's own Scriptures say.*

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check

*See Phase 0.6 above — recorded there in full rather than repeated.*

**Immediately before (6:1–7).** The Hellenist complaint, the appointment of the Seven, and the first growth-notice. Stephen is named second of the seven (6:5) and is the only one of them described twice over — *πλήρης πίστεως καὶ πνεύματος ἁγίου* (6:5) and then *πλήρης χάριτος καὶ δυνάμεως* (6:8). `[T]`

**Immediately after (8:1–4).** *Σαῦλος δὲ ἦν συνευδοκῶν τῇ ἀναιρέσει αὐτοῦ* ("and Saul was consenting to his execution"), then the great persecution, then the scattering, then — the crucial clause — *οἱ μὲν οὖν διασπαρέντες διῆλθον εὐαγγελιζόμενοι τὸν λόγον* ("those who were scattered went about preaching the word", 8:4). **The unit's consequence is the mission.** `[T]`

**The section.** M3 of the Arc Map, 6:8–9:31: the pivot outward. The unit is the first panel of it and supplies the cause of the rest.

**Don't trust the chapter break.** The chapter division at 7:1 falls inside a single scene — the council has been assembled at 6:12 and does not disperse until 7:58 — and the division at 8:1 cuts a sentence-group in half: 8:1a (*Σαῦλος δὲ ἦν συνευδοκῶν*) belongs with 7:58 and is where Paul finds it when he quotes the scene in the first person at 22:20. **The real unit is 6:8–8:1a**, and the extent given here (6:8–7:60) follows the overview's preaching-unit map, which is the right pastoral boundary but one verse short of the narrative one. Flagged for the preacher rather than altered.

**A levelling to resist.** This passage is not widely read out of context in the way Jeremiah 29:11 is, but it is widely read *in part*: the speech is treated as a long preamble and the sermon starts at 7:51. Tool 3 shows why that cannot work.

### 3. Structure

The unit is not a speech with a frame; it is **a frame with a speech inside it**, and the frame is written so that its two halves answer each other.

| Verses | Section | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 6:8–10 | **The man and the wisdom they cannot withstand** | *πλήρης χάριτος καὶ δυνάμεως*; the synagogue disputes; *οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀντιστῆναι* |
| 6:11–14 | **The charge, in three stages** | suborned men (blasphemy against Moses and God) → the crowd, elders and scribes stirred → false witnesses before the council (this holy place and the law) |
| 6:15 | **The face** | *ὡσεὶ πρόσωπον ἀγγέλου* — the council stares |

| 7:17–29 | **Moses rejected: the first attempt** | the people multiply; the king who did not know Joseph; Moses' three forties begin; "who appointed you?"; flight to Midian |

| 7:51–53 | **The indictment** | stiff-necked; your fathers; the Righteous One; you received the law and did not keep it |
| 7:54–58 | **The response, and the seeing** | cut to the quick; full of the Holy Spirit; the heavens opened; the Son of Man standing; the stoning; the coats |
| 7:59–60 | **Two prayers** | "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit"; "Lord, do not hold this sin against them"; *ἐκοιμήθη* |

**The device is a double bookend, and both halves of it are verbal.**

- **Outer:** *πλήρης* ("full") + *ἀτενίζω* ("to gaze fixedly"). At 6:8 Stephen is *πλήρης χάριτος καὶ δυνάμεως* and at 6:15 the council *ἀτενίσαντες* at him; at 7:55 he is *πλήρης πνεύματος ἁγίου* and *ἀτενίσας* into heaven. `[T]` *πλήρης* stands in eight verses of Acts and four are here (6:3, 6:5, 6:8, 7:55); *ἀτενίζω* stands in ten and two are here (6:15, 7:55). Both verified by lemma. **The staring is reversed: they look at his face, he looks past them.**
- **Inner:** *μετοικίζω* at 7:4 and 7:43 (Headline 2).

**And a third frame, in the speech's own geography.** *κατοικέω* ("to dwell") occurs four times in the unit — 7:2 (before Abraham dwelt in Haran), 7:4 (he dwelt in Haran; *you* now dwell in this land), 7:48 (the Most High does not dwell in handmade things) `[T]`, twenty occurrences in nineteen verses of Acts. **The speech opens with a man who has nowhere to live and closes with a God who will not be housed.**

**How the sections relate.** Not chronologically — though the order is chronological — but by a repeated two-beat: **God acts outside the land, and the brothers refuse the man he acts through.** Abraham gets the promise and no ground. Joseph is sold and becomes the saviour of those who sold him. Moses is refused twice and sent anyway. Then the pattern is named in the present tense at 7:51–53. The structure is the argument, which is why starting at 7:51 preaches a conclusion without its premises.

### 4. Linking Words

Narrative, so the connectors are fewer than in an epistle and three of them carry real weight.

**6:10 → 6:11: *τότε*.** *οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀντιστῆναι* → ***τότε*** *ὑπέβαλον ἄνδρας*. The suborning happens **because** the disputing failed. `[T]` The ESV's "Then they secretly instigated men" keeps it; the causal force is in the sequence, not in a conjunction, and the preacher should say it out loud: they did not lose an argument and change their minds, they lost an argument and bought witnesses.

**6:14: *γάρ*.** ←*for*← grounding the charge of 6:13 in a report of Stephen's own words: *ἀκηκόαμεν **γὰρ** αὐτοῦ λέγοντος ὅτι Ἰησοῦς ὁ Ναζωραῖος οὗτος καταλύσει τὸν τόπον τοῦτον*. The witnesses offer evidence, and the evidence is a claim about Jesus. `[T]` **The charge is Christological before it is cultic**, which is worth noticing, because the speech never mentions Jesus until 7:52 and then only as *ὁ δίκαιος*.

**7:7: the citation's own connectives.** *καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα ἐξελεύσονται **καὶ** λατρεύσουσίν μοι ἐν τῷ τόπῳ τούτῳ* — the second *καί* is the splice point. In Genesis 15:14 the clause that follows "after that they shall come out" is *ὧδε μετὰ ἀποσκευῆς πολλῆς* ("here, with much baggage"). Stephen's *καί* joins Exodus 3:12 instead. `[T]` **The most consequential connective in the speech is one whose second half comes from a different book.**

**7:35: *τοῦτον … τοῦτον*.** Resumptive, not a connector in the usual sense, but doing a connector's work: the accusative is stated, the quotation interrupts, and the accusative is stated again so the sentence can land. In English this needs a dash or a colon; both the NASB95 ("This Moses whom they disowned… is the one whom God sent") and the ESV ("This Moses, whom they rejected… — this man God sent") supply one, and both keep the repetition. `[T]`

**7:48: *ἀλλά*.** The hinge of the last movement. Solomon built him a house (7:47) — ***ἀλλ'*** *οὐχ ὁ ὕψιστος ἐν χειροποιήτοις κατοικεῖ*. `[T]` **Strong adversative, and it is the only strong adversative in the speech.** Everything before it is narrative; the *ἀλλά* is where Stephen stops narrating and starts arguing. Note what it does *not* say: not "but the house was wrong", not "but Solomon should not have". The contrast is between a house being built and God being housed. The preacher who makes 7:48 a condemnation of the temple has read the adversative as denying 7:47, which it does not.

**7:51: asyndeton.** No connector at all. *Σκληροτράχηλοι καὶ ἀπερίτμητοι…* follows the Isaiah citation with nothing between. `[T]` The abruptness is the device (Tool 9); both English versions supply a paragraph break, and the ESV supplies an opening quotation mark, which is as close as punctuation gets to the Greek's silence.

**One ←*for*← worth naming inside a citation: 7:33.** *ὁ **γὰρ** τόπος ἐφ' ᾧ ἕστηκας γῆ ἁγία ἐστίν*. The *γάρ* is Exodus 3:5's own, and it grounds a command to take off sandals in a statement about a place. Stephen has reproduced the grounding intact `[T]` — which matters, because the sentence he leaves standing is *the only time in his speech that Scripture says a place is holy*, and it is said in Midian.

### 5. Parallels

Not Hebrew poetry, but two formal parallelisms are present and one of them is load-bearing.

**(a) 7:51 — a tricolon of accusation, antithetical in its last member.** `[T]`

```
Σκληροτράχηλοι                       stiff-necked
  καὶ ἀπερίτμητοι καρδίαις            and uncircumcised in hearts
  καὶ τοῖς ὠσίν                       and in the ears
ὑμεῖς ἀεὶ τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ ἀντιπίπτετε   you always resist the Holy Spirit
  ὡς οἱ πατέρες ὑμῶν                  as your fathers
  καὶ ὑμεῖς                           and you
```

The last two words are the point. *ὡς οἱ πατέρες ὑμῶν **καὶ ὑμεῖς*** is not "like your fathers, so you" tidily balanced; it is a clause that has already finished and then adds "and you" on the end. The NASB95 smooths it to "you are doing just as your fathers did" and the ESV to "As your fathers did, so do you" — **both make a comparison out of what in the Greek is an afterthought that will not let go.** *Moderate confidence* on the rhetorical reading; `[T]` on the wording.

**(b) 7:49–50 — the citation's own parallelism, which Stephen keeps whole.** Isaiah 66:1–2a is four cola: heaven my throne // earth my footstool, then what house will you build // what place is my rest, then the hand that made. Synonymous in the first pair, synonymous in the second, and the fourth colon is a rhetorical question that collapses both. `[T]` Keeping all four is itself a decision: Stephen could have quoted the first pair and made a point about transcendence. Quoting the second pair is what makes it a point about a *place*.

**(c) A near-parallel worth flagging as a non-parallel.** 7:2's *ὁ θεὸς τῆς δόξης ὤφθη* and 7:55's *εἶδεν δόξαν θεοῦ* bracket the speech with *δόξα* ("glory"), but they are not parallel in construction — the first is God appearing to a man in Mesopotamia, the second a man seeing God's glory from Jerusalem. `[T]` The relation is an inclusio of vocabulary, not a parallelism, and reporting it as the latter would overstate it.

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Narrative, so the tool applies, and Luke's intrusions here are few and all of them interpretive.

| Verse | The comment | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **6:10** | *καὶ οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀντιστῆναι τῇ σοφίᾳ καὶ τῷ πνεύματι ᾧ ἐλάλει* | **The verdict on the dispute, before the trial begins.** Luke tells the reader the opposition lost on the merits. Without this the suborning of 6:11 would be ambiguous. `[T]` |
| **6:13** | *ἔστησάν τε μάρτυρας **ψευδεῖς*** | **The verdict on the witnesses.** Luke does not leave the charge's truth open. Everything said at 6:13–14 is marked false before it is said. `[T]` This is the single most important narrator's comment in the unit, and it governs how 7:48 must be read: whatever Stephen said, he did not say what they said he said. |
| **6:15** | *εἶδον τὸ πρόσωπον αὐτοῦ ὡσεὶ πρόσωπον ἀγγέλου* | **Inside-information, reported of the council.** Luke tells us what *they* saw. He does not tell us what it meant; *ὡσεί* ("as if") keeps it a simile. `[T]` |
| **7:55** | *ὑπάρχων δὲ πλήρης πνεύματος ἁγίου* | **The verdict on the vision.** Stephen's claim at 7:56 could be read as a dying man's hallucination; Luke forecloses that by attributing the seeing to the Spirit before the seeing is reported. `[T]` |
| **7:58** | *νεανίου καλουμένου Σαύλου* | **Foreshadowing.** The name is given and nothing is done with it. `[T]` |
| **7:60** | *ἐκοιμήθη* | **A theological verdict in one word.** Not *ἀπέθανεν*. *κοιμάομαι* ("to fall asleep") stands three times in Acts: here, 12:6 (Peter sleeping in prison, literal) and 13:36 (David *ἐκοιμήθη* and saw corruption). `[T]` Verified by lemma. The euphemism is the narrator's and it is the last word of the unit. |

**What Luke does *not* comment on.** He does not say whether Stephen's history is accurate (relevant at 7:16, see Difficult Verses); he does not evaluate the speech; and he does not say that Stephen forgave. 7:60 reports a prayer, not a disposition. `[T]` The absence is worth the preacher's attention: it is a restraint the sermon should match.

### 7. Vocabulary

#### Proper-noun inventory

Run before triage, as the tool requires. **Peoples and places:** Freedmen (Λιβερτῖνοι), Cyrenians, Alexandrians, Cilicia, Asia (6:9); Mesopotamia, Haran (7:2, 7:4); Chaldaeans (7:4); Egypt and the Egyptians (7:9–40, passim); Canaan (7:11); Shechem (7:16); Midian (7:29); Mount Sinai (7:30, 7:38); the Red Sea (7:36); Babylon (7:43). **Persons and divine names:** Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, the twelve patriarchs, Pharaoh, Hamor, Moses, Aaron, Joshua (Ἰησοῦς), David, Solomon, Moloch, Rephan, Saul. **Numbers:** four hundred years (7:6), the eighth day (7:8), twelve patriarchs (7:8), seventy-five persons (7:14), three months (7:20), two sons (7:29), forty ×4 (7:23, 7:30, 7:36, 7:42).

Most are routine scenery inside a historical recital and get no further check. **Four carry canonical freight and are worked below; one number is a textual question and is worked under Textual Variants.**

#### (a) Cilicia — the one place name in the frame

*Κιλικία* stands in seven verses of Acts: **6:9**, 15:23, 15:41, 21:39, 22:3, 23:34, 27:5. `[T]` Verified by lemma. **The first mention of Cilicia in the book is the synagogue that disputed with Stephen; the man who twice names Cilicia as his birthplace is standing at Stephen's execution.** *High confidence* on the distribution.

**The load-the-story rule, applied, and a limit stated.** Reading 21:39 and 22:3: Paul says *Ταρσεὺς τῆς Κιλικίας* and then *ἀνατεθραμμένος δὲ ἐν τῇ πόλει ταύτῃ παρὰ τοὺς πόδας Γαμαλιήλ* — he names Cilicia as birth and Jerusalem as upbringing, in the same breath. **So Luke's own data make Saul a Cilician Jew in Jerusalem, which is precisely the description of the men in 6:9.** But Luke never says Saul was in that synagogue, and the inference is `[I]`, *moderate* — attractive, widely drawn, and not stated. The preacher may say the stones were thrown by men from his home province; he may not say Stephen's opponents included Saul.

#### (b) Joshua, and the name the reader already knows

At 7:45 the tent is brought into the land *μετὰ Ἰησοῦ*. **This is the one place in Acts where *Ἰησοῦς* does not mean Jesus** — the name stands in sixty-eight verses of Acts and sixty-seven of them are the Lord's. `[T]` The other New Testament instance of the name meaning Joshua is Hebrews 4:8, *εἰ γὰρ αὐτοὺς Ἰησοῦς κατέπαυσεν* ("for if Joshua had given them rest"). `[T]`

**What this adds, stated carefully.** Acts 7:45 and Hebrews 4:8 are the New Testament's two Joshua texts, and **both of them stand in an argument about whether the land was the rest**: Hebrews says it was not because another day remains; Stephen, four verses later, quotes the question *ἢ τίς τόπος τῆς καταπαύσεώς μου;* `[T]` on the two facts; the connection between them is `[I]`, *moderate* — a convergence of two writers on one problem, not evidence that either knew the other. **No claim is made that Stephen puns on the name.** He does not, and the preacher who makes him do so is inventing.

#### (c) Shechem

Named once (7:16), and the burial there is the unit's hardest textual-historical crux (Difficult Verses). The canonical freight: Shechem is where Abram first stops in the land (Genesis 12:6, *ἕως τοῦ τόπου Συχεμ* — and the word is *τόπος*), where Jacob buys a field (Genesis 33:19), and where Joseph's bones are finally laid (Joshua 24:32). `[T]` Verified by reading the three source passages. **In a speech about a people with no ground, the one purchase of ground that gets named is a grave.** *Moderate confidence* on the significance; the facts are `[T]`.

#### (d) Babylon

Stephen's own substitution for the Amos citation's Damascus (Tool 11). Worth noting here only that *Βαβυλών* occurs once in Acts, at 7:43. `[T]`

#### Words that need work

**1. *τόπος* ("place") — the unit's Leitwort.** `[T]` *High confidence.*

*τόπος* stands in sixteen verses of Acts (eighteen occurrences), and **five of those verses are in this unit**: 6:13, 6:14, 7:7, 7:33, 7:49. Verified by lemma against the SBLGNT. Its career inside the unit is an argument:

| Verse | Phrase | Whose word, and what it means |
|---|---|---|
| 6:13 | *κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου* | The **witnesses'** — the temple, called holy |
| 6:14 | *καταλύσει τὸν τόπον τοῦτον* | The **witnesses'** report of Stephen — the temple, to be destroyed |
| 7:7 | *λατρεύσουσίν μοι ἐν τῷ τόπῳ τούτῳ* | **God's**, in Stephen's citation — and the source says *mountain* |
| 7:33 | *ὁ γὰρ τόπος … γῆ ἁγία ἐστίν* | **God's**, at the bush — holy ground, in Midian |
| 7:49 | *ἢ τίς τόπος τῆς καταπαύσεώς μου;* | **God's**, through Isaiah — a question |

**The argument is not that the temple is nothing; it is that "place" is God's word before it is theirs, and that every time God uses it the referent is somewhere else.** This is the finding the overview's sweep flagged as "a Leitwort — *τόπος*" and rated `[S: sweep]`; the count and the career are now verified and the claim is `[T]`.

A further datum: *τόπος* with *ἅγιος* in one verse stands in Acts at **6:13, 7:33** and **21:28** `[T]` — the charge against Stephen, the bush, and the charge against Paul. (Also Acts 4:31, in the unrelated sense of the room being shaken, and elsewhere Matthew 24:15 and 1 Corinthians 1:2.) **The only two places Acts calls holy are the temple, in a false witness's mouth, and a hillside in Midian, in God's.**

**2. The temple, named four ways and never by its name.** `[T]` *High confidence.*

*ἱερόν* stands in twenty-four verses of Acts: **2:46; 3:1, 2, 3, 8, 10; 4:1; 5:20, 21, 24, 25, 42** — twelve verses in the four chapters before this unit — and then **21:26, 27, 28, 29, 30; 22:17; 24:6, 12, 18; 25:8; 26:21**, with 19:27 of Artemis's temple. **None in 6:8–7:60.** *ναός* stands twice in Acts (17:24, 19:24) and never of the Jerusalem temple. Both verified by lemma.

This is an **absence**, and it is admissible on the tool's own terms because the pattern that makes it visible can be cited: twelve verses in chapters 2–5, ten from 21:26. It is rated **moderate**, per the hard gate's ceiling on absence claims, and flagged for library verification. What stands instead:

- *τόπος* ×5 (above);
- *σκηνή* ("tent") at 7:43 and 7:44 — the tent of Moloch and the tent of witness, two verses apart; three occurrences in Acts, the third at 15:16;
- ***σκήνωμα*** ("dwelling") at 7:46 — **its only occurrence in Acts** `[T]`, and it is Psalm 132:5's word;
- *οἶκος* ("house") at 7:47 and 7:49, with 7:42's *οἶκος Ἰσραήλ* from Amos and 7:10's and 7:20's domestic sense.

**3. *σοφία* ("wisdom") — four in Acts, all four here.** `[T]` *High confidence.*

6:3 (the Seven are to be full of it), 6:10 (they could not withstand it), 7:10 (God gave Joseph grace and wisdom before Pharaoh), 7:22 (Moses was instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians). Verified by lemma. **The word occurs nowhere else in the book.** And the two men Stephen calls wise are the two men his speech says their brothers refused.

**4. *χάρις* ("grace, favour") — the chain the Byzantine text breaks.** `[T]` *High confidence* on the distribution; the reading of it *moderate–high*.

*χάρις* stands in seventeen verses of Acts; three are in this unit: **6:8** (Stephen *πλήρης χάριτος*), **7:10** (God gave Joseph *χάριν καὶ σοφίαν* before Pharaoh), **7:46** (David *εὗρεν χάριν* before God). Verified by lemma. Two things follow.

- **7:10 holds both of 6:8–10's words at once.** *χάρις* and *σοφία* stand together in five verses of the New Testament: **Luke 2:40, Luke 2:52, Acts 7:10**, 2 Corinthians 1:12 and Colossians 3:16. `[T]` Verified by collocation across all twenty-seven books. **The three Luke–Acts instances are the boy Jesus twice and Joseph in Stephen's speech** — and the pair is split across Stephen at 6:8 and 6:10. *Moderate–high* that this is a Lukan register rather than coincidence; the distribution is `[T]`.
- **The Byzantine tradition replaces *χάριτος* at 6:8 with *πίστεως* ("faith"), and E reads both.** NA28's apparatus: *πιστεως* 1241. 1505 𝔪 syʰ; GrNy ¦ *χαριτος και πιστεως* E ¦ txt 𝔓⁸ 𝔓⁴⁵ᵛⁱᵈ 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B D 0175. 33. 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1739 lat syᵖ co. The easier reading assimilates 6:8 to 6:5 (*πλήρης πίστεως*) — and in doing so **it severs the only link between Stephen and the two men he calls favoured.** An eighth instance of the overview's standing instruction (honesty note 5). See Textual Variants.

**5. *μετοικίζω* ("to remove, resettle") — twice in the New Testament, both here.** `[T]` *High confidence.* 7:4 and 7:43; verified by collocation across the whole New Testament. Headline 2.

**6. *κατάσχεσις* ("possession") — twice in the New Testament, both here.** `[T]` 7:5 (promised to Abraham as a possession) and 7:45 (in the dispossession of the nations). Verified across all twenty-seven books. **The word enters the speech as a promise and returns as a conquest**, and between the two it is never said that the promise was kept.

**7. *λυτρωτής* ("redeemer") — a New Testament hapax.** `[T]` *High confidence.* 7:35, verified across the whole New Testament. And its placing is exact: the question *τίς σε κατέστησεν **ἄρχοντα καὶ δικαστήν**;* stands in two verses of the New Testament, **7:27 and 7:35** `[T]` (verified by collocation of *ἄρχων* and *δικαστής*), and the second time Stephen answers it by **keeping *ἄρχων* and replacing *δικαστής* with *λυτρωτής***. **God did appoint him a ruler — not a judge but a redeemer.** This is the sharpest single move in the speech and it turns on one substituted noun.

**8. Four more New Testament hapaxes in the unit, which is a high density for sixty-eight verses.** `[T]` *σκληροτράχηλος* ("stiff-necked") and *ἀπερίτμητος* ("uncircumcised"), both 7:51; *ἀντιπίπτω* ("to resist, strike against"), 7:51; *μοσχοποιέω* ("to make a calf"), 7:41. All verified across the whole New Testament. **Three of the four are in one verse**, 7:51 — the verse where Stephen stops narrating and accuses. *Note:* *διαπρίομαι* (7:54) is not a hapax but stands in only two verses, both Acts (Phase 0.6); *λόγιον* ("oracle", 7:38) stands in four New Testament verses and only here in Acts; *διαταγή* ("ordinance", 7:53) in two, here and Romans 13:2; *βῆμα* with *πούς* (7:5) once.

**9. *μάρτυς* ("witness") — the office, and its inversion.** `[T]` *High confidence* on the distribution.

*μάρτυς* stands in thirteen verses of Acts. The programmatic uses are 1:8, 1:22, 2:32, 3:15, 5:32. **The two in this unit are 6:13 (*μάρτυρας ψευδεῖς*) and 7:58 (*οἱ μάρτυρες*)** — and the second has no adjective, so the men Luke called false at 6:13 are simply "the witnesses" when they lay down their coats. `[T]` Under Deuteronomy 17:7 the witnesses' hands are first in a stoning, which is what 7:58 is narrating. *Moderate–high* that the bare *οἱ μάρτυρες* is deliberate; `[T]` that it is bare. At 22:20 Paul will call Stephen *ὁ μάρτυς σου*. **The office is filled, in the one unit where the word means its opposite.**

**10. *πλήρης* ("full"), and what Stephen is full of, three times.** 6:5 faith and the Holy Spirit; 6:8 grace and power; 7:55 the Holy Spirit. Four of Acts' eight *πλήρης* verses are here. `[T]`

### 8. Translations

The findings rest on the Greek; this section reports where the English versions decide something.

**(a) 7:46 — the one place where both English versions follow a reading NA28 rejects.** This is the most consequential translation note in the unit and it is really a textual one; it is worked in full under Textual Variants and summarised here because the preacher meets it in the pulpit text.

- **NA28 prints:** *ᾐτήσατο εὑρεῖν σκήνωμα τῷ **οἴκῳ** Ἰακώβ* — "asked to find a dwelling for the **house** of Jacob" (𝔓⁷⁴ א* B D saᵐˢ).
- **SBLGNT prints:** *τῷ **θεῷ** Ἰακώβ* — "for the **God** of Jacob" (א² A C E Ψ 33 and the majority, with the Latin, Syriac and Coptic).
- **NASB95:** "asked that he might find a dwelling place for the God of Jacob." **ESV:** "asked to find a dwelling place for the God of Jacob."
- **The preacher's note:** on NA28's text, David asked for a dwelling **for the people**, not for God — which is the premise 7:48 then needs. **On the pulpit text, 7:48 contradicts 7:46; on NA28 it completes it.** Say which text you are preaching.

**(b) 7:51 — the ESV and NASB95 both make a comparison out of an afterthought.** Greek: *ὡς οἱ πατέρες ὑμῶν καὶ ὑμεῖς*. ESV: "As your fathers did, so do you." NASB95: "you are doing just as your fathers did." **Both are good English and both lose the trailing *καὶ ὑμεῖς*.** What the congregation will hear is a comparison; what Stephen said ends by pointing at them. No version available fixes this; the preacher can simply read the Greek order aloud in English — "as your fathers — and you".

**(c) 7:38 and 7:39 — "us" or "you", and the versions follow NA28 rather than the papyri.** NA28 prints *λόγια ζῶντα δοῦναι **ἡμῖν*** at 7:38 and *οἱ πατέρες **ἡμῶν*** at 7:39. The variant *ὑμῖν* at 7:38 is read by 𝔓⁷⁴ א B 453. 2818 p co; Irᵍᵃᵉᵛ, and *ὑμῶν* at 7:39 by Ψ 81 saᵐˢ mae; Irˡᵃᵗ. **NASB95 has "to pass on to you" at 7:38** — against NA28 and with the papyri — **while the ESV has "to give to us".** So the two English versions part company at exactly the verse where the pronoun pattern of Headline 5 is decided. *Significant for preaching:* if 7:38 reads *ὑμῖν*, Stephen's turn from "we" to "you" begins fourteen verses earlier and is gradual; if *ἡμῖν*, it is sudden and falls at 7:51. **On the ESV the turn is sudden; on the NASB95 it is not.** This divergence is not noted in the overview and belongs in it.

**(d) 6:13 — "this holy place", and a bracket.** NA28 prints *κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου **°[τούτου]*** — *τούτου* in brackets, omitted by 𝔓⁷⁴ א A D E Ψ 0175. 614. 1175. 1241. 1505 𝔪 lat and read by (𝔓⁸) B C 33. 323. 945. 1739. 2818 and others. **The ESV prints "this holy place" and the NASB95 "this holy place"** — both include it, as does the parallel charge against Paul at 21:28 (*τὸν ἅγιον τόπον τοῦτον*), which is not disputed. Nothing turns on it exegetically; it is worth knowing that the editors are not certain.

**(e) 7:20 — "lovely to God".** Greek: *ἦν ἀστεῖος τῷ θεῷ*. NASB95 "he was lovely in the sight of God"; ESV "he was beautiful in God's sight". Both supply "in the sight of", which the dative does not have. The phrase is a Semitic superlative and **the words *τῷ θεῷ* are Stephen's addition to Exodus 2:2, which reads only *ἀστεῖον*** (Swete; Rahlfs 1979 agrees). `[T]` *High confidence.* **The congregation hears a statement about God's opinion; the Greek may mean no more than "exceedingly beautiful".** Both readings are defensible; the preacher should not build on the first without saying so. *ἀστεῖος* stands in two New Testament verses, 7:20 and Hebrews 11:23 — both of the infant Moses. `[T]`

**(f) 7:60 — "do not hold this sin against them".** Greek: *μὴ στήσῃς αὐτοῖς ταύτην τὴν ἁμαρτίαν*, aorist subjunctive of *ἵστημι* — "do not set this sin to their account", a reckoning idiom. NASB95 and ESV both have "do not hold this sin against them", which is accurate and idiomatic. **No divergence bearing on the findings.** Noted only because *ἵστημι* in this verse is the same verb as 6:13's *ἔστησαν … μάρτυρας ψευδεῖς* ("they set up false witnesses") `[T]` — *they set witnesses against him; he asks that nothing be set against them.* *Moderate confidence* that the repetition is designed; `[T]` that it is there. The versions cannot carry it and should not be expected to.

**Ancient versions check.** Run, and it reports a split that is not a split about this passage.

Where the modern versions agree and the rendering carries weight, the check applies to **7:42–43** (the Amos citation) and **7:14** (the seventy-five). Both are places where the **Greek Old Testament differs from the Hebrew**, so the three-way triage governs, and both fall in **category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text** rather than in category 2:

- **7:43's *σκηνὴ τοῦ Μόλοχ*.** The Hebrew of Amos 5:26 reads *סִכּוּת מַלְכְּכֶם* ("Sikkuth your king") and *כִּיּוּן צַלְמֵיכֶם* ("Kiyyun your images") — two divine names. The Greek read the first as a tent (*σκηνή*) and the second as *Ῥαιφάν*. `[T]` Verified in the WLC and in both Greek editions. **So the *σκηνή* contrast on which 7:43–44 turns exists only in the Greek.** This is not a defect in the Septuagint and not a text-critical finding; it is a fact about which text Stephen argues from, and it is load-bearing whichever text is prior. The preaching consequence is concrete: **the contrast cannot be shown from an English Old Testament**, because an English Old Testament translates the Hebrew.
- **7:14's seventy-five.** Worked under Textual Variants.
- **7:7's *τόπος*.** *Not* an ancient-versions matter: Swete and Rahlfs agree on *ὄρει* at Exodus 3:12 and the Hebrew has *בָּהָר הַזֶּה* ("on this mountain"). **The change is Stephen's own, against every witness to his source** `[T]` — which is what makes it a finding rather than a variant.

**Pulpit divergence note.** Pulpit text: **ESV (Anglicised)**. Three divergences bear on findings: **7:46** (shared with the NASB95 — both follow *θεῷ* against NA28's *οἴκῳ*, and the congregation will hear 7:48 as a correction of 7:46 rather than its ground); **7:38** (the ESV's "to us" keeps NA28's reading and so keeps the pronoun turn sudden, where the NASB95's "to you" would make it gradual — here the ESV is the closer of the two to the printed text); **7:51** (the ESV's "As your fathers did, so do you" tidies the trailing *καὶ ὑμεῖς*). The overview's honesty note 6 — *check the repetition in both versions, and expect either of them to break it* — holds again, and again in both directions.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Three registers, in sequence, and the shifts are structural.**

**(a) 6:8–7:1 — forensic, gathering.** Short clauses, a rising sequence of actors (some men → the people, the elders, the scribes → the council), and three verbs of violence in one verse: *ἐπιστάντες συνήρπασαν αὐτὸν καὶ ἤγαγον* ("they came upon him, seized him and brought him", 6:12). `[T]` The pacing accelerates and then stops dead at 6:15 for a single still image — the face — and then one four-word question. **The stillness at 6:15 and the brevity of 7:1 are the tonal hinge**: everything before is noise, everything after is a man talking for fifty-two verses.

**(b) 7:2–50 — measured, patient, unhurried to the point of being risky.** This is the register that makes the speech hard to preach. There is no indignation in it. The vocative is courteous — *Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες* (7:2) — and it is the same vocative Paul will use at 22:1. `[T]` The verbs are mostly God's: *ὤφθη* (7:2), *ἐπηγγείλατο* (7:5), *ἐλάλησεν* (7:6), *ἔδωκεν* (7:8), *ἦν … μετ' αὐτοῦ* (7:9), *ἐξείλατο* (7:10), *κατέστησεν* (7:10). **Author's Purpose and Tone converge here on the overview's Trap 2: the subject of the main verbs is God, and the tone is what makes that audible.**

Within this long middle the temperature rises once, at **7:35–38**, where the fourfold demonstrative turns narrative into something nearer proclamation (*Τοῦτον … οὗτος … οὗτός ἐστιν … οὗτός ἐστιν*) `[T]`, and once at **7:48**, the only strong adversative.

**(c) 7:51–53 — abrupt, vocative, accusatory.** No connective (Tool 4), three New Testament hapaxes in one verse (Tool 7), a vocative plural of insult, and two rhetorical questions. The pronoun changes. **The shift is not a loss of temper but a change of genre**: narration ends and prosecution begins, and it takes three verses.

**(d) 7:54–60 — animal, then quiet.** *ἔβρυχον τοὺς ὀδόντας* ("they ground their teeth", 7:54); *κράξαντες … φωνῇ μεγάλῃ συνέσχον τὰ ὦτα* ("they cried out with a loud voice and covered their ears", 7:57) — **a courtroom stopping its ears, which is the ear-image of 7:51 enacted** `[T]`, *moderate–high* on the connection; `[T]` on both wordings. Then *ὥρμησαν ὁμοθυμαδόν* ("they rushed with one impulse"), the one use of *ὁμοθυμαδόν* in Acts that is not of the church at prayer or in agreement. And then, against all of it, three quiet clauses: he knelt, he spoke, he fell asleep.

**Soundtrack.** Strings, low and even, for fifty verses — unhurried, almost liturgical. Then at 7:54 the brass and the timpani come in at once and drown it. And the last bar is unaccompanied.

**Note to the preacher on emotional architecture.** The text's own shape is *long calm → short fury → quiet*. A sermon that is indignant from the first minute has inverted it. The indignation in this passage belongs to the council.

**(b) The *οὗτος* run at 7:35–40, and whose word it is.** Six demonstratives of Moses in six verses: *Τοῦτον τὸν Μωϋσῆν … τοῦτον ὁ θεός* (7:35), *οὗτος ἐξήγαγεν* (7:36), *οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ Μωϋσῆς* (7:37), *οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ γενόμενος* (7:38) — and then, at 7:40, inside the quotation of Exodus 32:1, Israel's own *ὁ γὰρ Μωϋσῆς **οὗτος***. `[T]` Verified by lemma, in order.

**This is the rhetorical centre of the speech.** The people's contemptuous "this Moses — we don't know what's become of him" is the phrase Stephen has spent five verses redeeming. And he has altered the citation to make the collision cleaner: **Exodus 32:1 reads *ὁ γὰρ Μωϋσῆς οὗτος ὁ ἄνθρωπος* ("this Moses, the man") in both Swete and Rahlfs 1979, and Stephen drops *ὁ ἄνθρωπος*** `[T]`, leaving the bare demonstrative that matches his own four. *High confidence* on the omission; *moderate–high* that it is for this reason.

**(c) *ἀπωθέομαι* ("to thrust away") — twice here, and the pair it makes.** 7:27 (the man thrust Moses aside) and 7:39 (the fathers thrust him aside); three occurrences in Acts, the third at 13:46. `[T]` **The rejection of Moses is told twice with the same verb, once by an individual and once by the nation.**

**(d) *στρέφω* ("to turn") — a turn answered by a turn, in consecutive paragraphs.** 7:39 *ἐστράφησαν … εἰς Αἴγυπτον* ("they turned … to Egypt") and 7:42 *ἔστρεψεν δὲ ὁ θεός* ("but God turned"). `[T]` Three occurrences in Acts; two are these. **Israel turns and God turns, and the second is the judgement for the first.** *High confidence*; the verb is the hinge of 7:39–43 and neither English version loses it ("turned back"/"turned away" in the NASB95; "turned"/"turned away" in the ESV).

**(e) Idea-level repetition: "and God was with him / and God sent him".** Not one word but one shape, five times: God appeared (7:2), God promised (7:5), God was with him (7:9), God sent him (7:35), God gave (7:8, 7:38). `[T]` Against it, five refusals: sold (7:9), did not know (7:18), did not understand (7:25), thrust aside (7:27, 7:39), were unwilling (7:39). **The speech alternates divine initiative and human refusal until the alternation itself becomes the accusation at 7:51.**

**(f) Near-repetition worth the preacher's eye.** 6:11 *ῥήματα βλάσφημα εἰς Μωϋσῆν **καὶ τὸν θεόν*** (suborned men: against Moses and God) → 6:13 *κατὰ τοῦ τόπου τοῦ ἁγίου **καὶ τοῦ νόμου*** (false witnesses: against the place and the law). `[T]` **The charge is restated once and in the restatement God becomes a building and Moses becomes a code.** *Moderate–high* that the shift is deliberate; `[T]` that it is there. And it is the second form that Paul inherits at 21:28, with "the people" added at the front.

### 11. Quotation/Allusion

**Book-level live sources, for Move 2** (from Phase 0.5): Luke's own Gospel; Isaiah (twelve uses, nine texts); the Psalter; Exodus (five); Deuteronomy (three); Genesis (three); Amos (two). Every one of these is used in this unit, and the unit is the densest citation block in Acts.

**Independent candidate-finding ran first.** Before any candidate the overview names was tested, the unit's own distinctive vocabulary was searched against the Greek Old Testament: *βῆμα ποδός*, *μετοικίζω*, *κατάσχεσις*, *σκήνωμα*, *κατάπαυσις*, *ἀστεῖος*, *χειροποίητος*, and the *τόπος*/*ὄρος* pair. **Two of the findings below (Deuteronomy 2:5 at 7:5, and the Psalm 132 / Isaiah 66 relation) came out of that search and not out of the overview**, which is the point of doing it first.

---

#### **Genesis 12:1 → 7:3** *(high confidence — direct quotation)* `[T]`

*Source context.* The call of Abram. In Genesis 11:31–32 Terah has already taken the family out of Ur toward Canaan and settled in Haran, and Terah dies there; 12:1 is God's word to Abram in Haran. The command has three members in the Greek — *ἐκ τῆς γῆς σου καὶ ἐκ τῆς συγγενείας σου **καὶ ἐκ τοῦ οἴκου τοῦ πατρός σου*** ("from your land and from your kindred and from your father's house") — and is immediately followed by the promise of a great nation and the blessing of all the families of the earth (12:2–3). `[T]` Verified in Swete and in Rahlfs 1979.

*Stephen's edit.* He keeps the first two members and **drops the third, "and from your father's house"** `[T]`, and he relocates the call: Genesis puts it in Haran, Stephen puts it *ὄντι ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ πρὶν ἢ κατοικῆσαι αὐτὸν ἐν Χαρράν* ("while he was in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran", 7:2). **Both changes do the same work.** Dropping "your father's house" removes the one domestic item from a list of things left behind; relocating the call pushes God's first word to Abraham further out — before even the staging-post. *High confidence* on both facts; *moderate–high* on the reading.

*Book usage.* Genesis 12 has been used once before in Acts, at **3:25**, where Peter quotes the *blessing* half (Genesis 12:3 / 22:18) to a temple crowd — the overview's row notes it announces the Gentile horizon in the second sermon. `[T]` **So Acts cites Genesis 12 twice: Peter takes the promise to the nations, Stephen takes the command to leave.** The two halves of one paragraph, divided between two speakers, and in the order the chapter has them. *Moderate–high* that this is design rather than coincidence.

*OT-to-OT.* Genesis 12:1's "go out" is reworked across the canon — most relevantly for this speech at Joshua 24:2–3, where Joshua's own recital begins with the fathers beyond the River and God taking Abraham from there. **Stephen's speech is in the genre of Joshua 24 and Nehemiah 9, and begins where Joshua 24 begins.** *Moderate confidence*; see Tool 12.

*What it adds.* The speech's first act of God is extraterritorial. Before there is a land, a law, a tent or a temple, there is a voice in Mesopotamia telling a man to leave.

---

#### **Deuteronomy 2:5 → 7:5** *(moderate–high — rare verbal allusion; history of interpretation unchecked)*

*The datum first.* ***βῆμα ποδός*** ("a foot's step") **stands in exactly one verse of the Greek Old Testament — Deuteronomy 2:5 — and one verse of the New Testament, Acts 7:5.** `[T]` Verified by ordered phrase search across all sixty books of Swete (accent-stripped and with the apparatus sigla of the U+2E00 block removed), with a positive control passed, and confirmed in Rahlfs 1979.

*Source context.* Deuteronomy 2:4–6: Israel is passing the territory of Esau's descendants and is forbidden to fight them, **because the land is not theirs to take** — *οὐ γὰρ μὴ δῶ ὑμῖν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς αὐτῶν οὐδὲ βῆμα ποδός, ὅτι **ἐν κλήρῳ** δέδωκα τοῖς υἱοῖς Ησαυ τὸ ὄρος τὸ Σηιρ* ("for I will not give you any of their land, not even a foot's step, because I have given Mount Seir to the sons of Esau as an inheritance"). The function of the verse in its own passage is a **restraint**: a boundary God sets on his own people's expansion, grounded in a grant he has made to someone else.

*The contact, stated precisely.* Acts 7:5 reads *καὶ οὐκ **ἔδωκεν** αὐτῷ **κληρονομίαν** ἐν αὐτῇ οὐδὲ **βῆμα ποδός***. Three elements of Deuteronomy 2:5 are present: the giving-verb, the two-word phrase, and the inheritance word (*κληρονομία* for Deuteronomy's *ἐν κλήρῳ*). `[T]` That is a rare phrase plus two contextual anchors — strong on Volume and Recurrence, strong on Availability, and the Thematic Coherence is sharp rather than merely compatible.

*Book usage.* Deuteronomy is a live source in Acts (18:15–19 at 3:22 and 7:37; 32:5 at 2:40; 32:8 behind 17:26). **This would be a fourth Deuteronomy text and the only one from its narrative rather than its preaching or its Song.** `[T]`

*OT-to-OT.* Deuteronomy 2 and Genesis 12–23 are already in conversation: Deuteronomy 2:5's grant to Esau presupposes the Genesis genealogies, and the Abraham narrative's own land-problem (Genesis 23, buying a grave) is the background against which Deuteronomy's territorial grants read. *Moderate.*

*What it adds, and the honest caveat.* **Stephen describes Abraham's holding in the land of promise in the phrase God used to tell Israel that Edom's land was not theirs.** Abraham stood to Canaan as Israel stood to Seir: the land was somebody else's to walk on and his only by promise. That is the sharpest possible way to make the speech's first point, and it is made with two words. **Rating: moderate–high, and deliberately not higher** — the phrase is rare and the context anchors are real, but **the history of interpretation has not been checked** and this is a cold-ish allusion in the sense that no commentator consulted in this project has been shown to make it. **First item for the next Logos round**, and queued for claim audit.

---

#### **Genesis 15:13–14 + Exodus 3:12 → 7:6–7** *(high confidence — direct quotation with a spliced ending)* `[T]`

This is the unit's most important citation and it is a composite.

*Source context — Genesis 15.* The covenant of the pieces. Abram has asked how he will know he will inherit the land (15:8); the answer is a ritual, a deep sleep, and a terror, and then this oracle: your seed will be a sojourner in a land not its own, enslaved and ill-treated four hundred years; and the nation that enslaves them, **I will judge**; and after that they shall come out — *ὧδε μετὰ ἀποσκευῆς πολλῆς* ("here, with much baggage", 15:14) — and in the fourth generation they shall return (15:16). **The function of the cited verses in their own passage is the answer to "how shall I know?": the land will be yours, four centuries late, after slavery.** `[T]`

*Source context — Exodus 3.* The bush. Moses objects ("who am I?"), and God answers with a promise of presence and a sign: *καὶ τοῦτό σοι τὸ σημεῖον … **καὶ λατρεύσετε τῷ θεῷ ἐν τῷ ὄρει τούτῳ*** ("and this shall be the sign to you … and you shall serve God on this mountain", 3:12). **"This mountain" is Horeb, in Midian, where Moses is standing** — some distance from any land of promise, and the sign God gives is that the exodus will terminate in worship at the place where the commission was given. `[T]` Verified in Swete and in Rahlfs 1979, which agree verbatim at the load-bearing clause; the Hebrew has *בָּהָר הַזֶּה*.

*Stephen's three edits.* `[T]` All verified against both Greek editions.

1. **Third person for second.** Genesis has *τὸ σπέρμα **σου***; Stephen has *τὸ σπέρμα **αὐτοῦ*** — necessary, since he is narrating God's speech to Abraham rather than reproducing it as address.
2. ***ἐν γῇ ἀλλοτρίᾳ*** **for** ***ἐν γῇ οὐκ ἰδίᾳ***. "In a foreign land" for "in a land not its own". Both editions have *οὐκ ἰδίᾳ*. A modest sharpening; the point is alienation either way.
3. **The ending replaced.** Genesis 15:14 ends *ἐξελεύσονται ὧδε μετὰ ἀποσκευῆς πολλῆς*; Stephen ends *ἐξελεύσονται **καὶ λατρεύσουσίν μοι ἐν τῷ τόπῳ τούτῳ***. **He cuts the baggage and splices in Exodus 3:12, changing *ὄρει* to *τόπῳ*.**

**A correction to an earlier reading of my own, made in the checking.** On Swete, Genesis 15:13 runs *κακώσουσιν … καὶ δουλώσουσιν* and Acts 7:6 runs *δουλώσουσιν … καὶ κακώσουσιν*, which looks like a fourth edit. **It is not: Rahlfs 1979 has the order Stephen has.** `[T]` The reversal is a divergence between Vaticanus and the eclectic text, not between Stephen and his source, and the finding is withdrawn before it was written. *This is what the corpus's observe-from-Swete, cite-from-Rahlfs rule is for.* Stephen also drops *ταπεινώσουσιν αὐτούς* ("and will humble them"), which both editions have `[T]`.

*Book usage.* **Exodus is live in Acts, and this is the fifth use — but it is the only one that is a quotation.** The overview's note 1 and note 3 are both in play: the four Exodus 1:7 seam-uses (6:7; 7:17; 12:24; 19:20) carry **no citation formula at all**, and the only formula-citation of Exodus in the book is Paul's at 23:5. **7:7 is therefore the one place in Acts where Exodus is quoted inside a speech without a formula and as the words of God** `[T]` — which is exactly the species of use honesty note 3 says a passage run must search for rather than conclude absent.

*OT-to-OT.* **Genesis 15 and Exodus 3 are already in conversation, and Exodus makes the link itself.** Exodus 2:24 — *καὶ ἐμνήσθη ὁ θεὸς τῆς διαθήκης αὐτοῦ τῆς πρὸς Ἀβραὰμ καὶ Ἰσαὰκ καὶ Ἰακώβ* — makes the bush narrative the answer to the patriarchal covenant, and Exodus 3:6's self-identification ("the God of your father, the God of Abraham…") is the same move. `[T]` **So Stephen is not fusing two unrelated texts; he is joining a promise to its own fulfilment-formula along a seam Exodus has already cut.** That is what licenses the splice and raises its confidence. *Moderate–high.*

*What it adds.* The full triad gives the speech its thesis in one verse. God told Abraham that the seed would come out **and worship in this place** — and the clause that supplies "this place" is spoken on a mountain in Midian. **When the council says "this place" they mean a building forty-odd verses of history away; when God said it, he was pointing at the ground under Moses' feet.** Stephen has not denied that God named a place. He has shown that God named a different one, and he has done it inside the council's own vocabulary.

#### **Exodus 2:14 → 7:27–28, and again at 7:35** *(high confidence — direct quotation, quoted twice, clipped the second time)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Exodus 2:11–15. Moses, grown, goes out to his brothers, sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, kills him and hides him in the sand; the next day he finds two Hebrews fighting and intervenes; the man answers with the question; Moses is afraid, concludes the thing is known, and flees. **The function of the question in its own passage is to expose Moses' assumed authority as unauthorised** — and the narrative's verdict is Moses' fear, not his vindication.

*The quotation.* Acts 7:27–28 reproduces Exodus 2:14 essentially verbatim in NA28: *τίς σε κατέστησεν ἄρχοντα καὶ δικαστὴν ἐφ' ἡμῶν; μὴ ἀνελεῖν με σὺ θέλεις ὃν τρόπον ἀνεῖλες ἐχθὲς τὸν Αἰγύπτιον;* `[T]`

*The second use, and what it does.* At 7:35 Stephen quotes the question again — **and NA28 prints it without *ἐφ' ἡμῶν*** ("over us"), while much of the tradition supplies it (א C D Ψ 81. 453. 1175. 2818 co read *εφ ημων*; E 33. 323. 945. 1505. 1739 read *εφ ημας*; txt 𝔓⁴⁵ 𝔓⁷⁴ A B). `[T]` **Clipped, the question is no longer a complaint about jurisdiction but a bare question about appointment — which is the question Stephen then answers.** *High confidence* on the printed text and the variant; *moderate–high* on the rhetorical reading. See Textual Variants: this is a tidying instance.

*Book usage.* First and only use of Exodus 2 in Acts. `[T]`

*OT-to-OT.* Exodus 2:14's question is itself answered inside the Pentateuch, at Numbers 16:13 (Dathan and Abiram: *ὅτι κατάρχεις ἡμῶν ἄρχων;*) and in the Korah complex generally — the challenge to Moses' authority is a recurring Pentateuchal form, and Stephen's reuse stands in that line. *Moderate.*

*What it adds.* The question is the speech's refrain and the answer is its Christology in miniature: *ruler* conceded, *judge* replaced by *redeemer* (Tool 7). And Luke has already used the shape: Peter said at 3:13–14 *ὃν ὑμεῖς … ἠρνήσασθε* twice; Stephen says at 7:35 *ὃν ἠρνήσαντο*. See Move 4.

#### **Exodus 3:2, 5, 6, 7–10 → 7:30–34** *(high confidence — sustained quotation and close paraphrase)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Exodus 3:1–10, the commission at the bush: the angel of the Lord appears in flame, the ground is holy, God names himself as the God of the fathers, Moses hides his face, and God says he has seen, has heard, has come down to deliver, and is sending Moses.

*Stephen's handling.* Compressed but close. **7:33 reproduces Exodus 3:5 almost word for word** — *λῦσον τὸ ὑπόδημα τῶν ποδῶν σου, ὁ γὰρ τόπος ἐφ' ᾧ ἕστηκας γῆ ἁγία ἐστίν*, against Swete and Rahlfs *λῦσαι τὸ ὑπόδημα ἐκ τῶν ποδῶν σου, ὁ γὰρ τόπος ἐν ᾧ σὺ ἕστηκας γῆ ἁγία ἐστίν* — the differences being the imperative form, *ἐφ'* for *ἐν*, and the dropping of *σύ*. `[T]` **7:34 fuses 3:7 and 3:8 and 3:10**, keeping the Hebraic *ἰδὼν εἶδον* ("I have surely seen"), and ending *δεῦρο ἀποστείλω σε εἰς Αἴγυπτον* where Exodus 3:10 has *δεῦρο ἀποστείλω σε πρὸς Φαραώ*. `[T]` **Stephen's Moses is sent to a country; Exodus' Moses is sent to a man.** *Moderate* that this is pointed; `[T]` that it is the wording.

*Two notes on the angel.* **NA28 prints a bare *ἄγγελος* at 7:30**, where Exodus 3:2 has *ἄγγελος Κυρίου* and where D E Ψ 33. 323. 614. 945. 1241. 1505. 1739 𝔪 p w sy mae boᵐˢ supply *κυρίου*. `[T]` **The scribes restored the genitive from Exodus.** And at 7:35 Moses is sent *σὺν χειρὶ ἀγγέλου τοῦ ὀφθέντος αὐτῷ ἐν τῇ βάτῳ* — the angel is the instrument, which is a step beyond Exodus. *ἄγγελος* occurs five times in this unit (6:15; 7:30, 35, 38, 53) `[T]`, and the frame is built on it: a face like an angel's at the start, the law by angels' ordinance at the end.

*Book usage.* Exodus 3 is used once elsewhere in Acts — **3:13**, where Peter opens *ὁ θεὸς Ἀβραὰμ καὶ Ἰσαὰκ καὶ Ἰακώβ, ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν*, which is Exodus 3:6's formula. `[T]` **So Peter opened his temple sermon with the bush's self-naming and Stephen narrates the bush itself** — the second use supplies the scene the first use quoted from. *Moderate–high.*

*OT-to-OT.* Exodus 3:6's formula is itself a citation of the patriarchal narratives and is the verse Jesus uses against the Sadducees at Luke 20:37 on the resurrection. **The formula is therefore already, in Luke's Gospel, a resurrection text** `[T]` — which is worth the preacher's notice, since Stephen narrates it fifteen verses before he sees the risen Lord. *Moderate* on the significance.

*What it adds.* The bush is the speech's structural answer to the charge. It is the one episode Stephen tells at length rather than in summary, and it contains the only *γῆ ἁγία* in his Scripture.

#### **Deuteronomy 18:15 → 7:37** *(high confidence — direct quotation, with the command dropped)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Deuteronomy 18:15–19, inside the law's regulation of prophecy and against the divination of the nations (18:9–14). The ground given is Horeb: the people asked not to hear the voice or see the fire again lest they die (18:16), and God's provision is a prophet like Moses, whose words carry God's own sanction — *καὶ ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὃς ἐὰν μὴ ἀκούσῃ … ἐγὼ ἐκδικήσω ἐξ αὐτοῦ* (18:19). **The function of the cited verse in its own passage is a concession to a people who could not bear direct revelation.** `[T]`

*Stephen's edit.* Deuteronomy 18:15 in both Greek editions ends ***αὐτοῦ ἀκούσεσθε*** ("him you shall hear"). **NA28's Acts 7:37 stops before it** — and C D\*·¹ E 33. 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1241. 1739. 2818 gig vgᶜˡ·ʷʷ sy mae bo supply it, **the NA28 apparatus itself labelling the addition "(Dt 18,15)"**. `[T]` Stephen also has *ὁ θεός* for *κύριος ὁ θεός σου*, and *ὑμῖν … ὑμῶν* for *σοι … σου*.

*Book usage — and this is the finding.* **Peter quoted this verse at 3:22, and he kept the command and added the penalty:** *αὐτοῦ ἀκούσεσθε κατὰ πάντα ὅσα ἂν λαλήσῃ πρὸς ὑμᾶς* (3:22), followed by 3:23's *πᾶσα ψυχὴ ἥτις ἂν μὴ ἀκούσῃ τοῦ προφήτου ἐκείνου ἐξολεθρευθήσεται ἐκ τοῦ λαοῦ* — Deuteronomy 18:19 in Levitical dress. `[T]` **Stephen quotes the promise and drops both.** *High confidence* on the two quotations; *moderate–high* on the reading: Stephen does not need Moses' command to listen, because he is about to make the charge of not listening in his own voice at 7:51 — *ὑμεῖς ἀεὶ τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ ἀντιπίπτετε* — and in his own hapax. The overview's phrase for this row, "planted in Peter's appeal, detonated in Stephen's indictment", is right and can now be made precise: **what is detonated is the half Peter quoted and Stephen left out.**

*OT-to-OT.* Deuteronomy 18:15–19 is anchored at Horeb (18:16), which is the same mountain as Exodus 3:12's *ἐν τῷ ὄρει τούτῳ* — the clause Stephen has already borrowed and altered at 7:7. `[T]` **Two of the speech's citations are grounded at the same mountain, and Stephen alters the place-word in one of them and keeps the mountain in the other** (7:38, *ἐν τῷ ὄρει Σινᾶ*). *Moderate.*

*What it adds.* The prophet-like-Moses promise arrives at the exact midpoint of the Moses section, immediately after Moses has been identified as *ἄρχων καὶ λυτρωτής*. Stephen never says who the prophet is. The speech's one Christological title is *ὁ δίκαιος* at 7:52, and the identification is left to the hearer — which is how 7:52 can be an accusation rather than an argument.

#### **Exodus 32:1 → 7:40** *(high confidence — direct quotation)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Exodus 32:1–6. Moses is on the mountain and is late; the people gather against Aaron; the calf is made, sacrificed to, and celebrated. **The function of the cited verse in its own passage is to show the golden calf as a response to Moses' absence, not to his teaching** — they do not disagree with him, they cannot find him.

*Stephen's edits.* He drops *ὁ ἄνθρωπος* (Tool 10b), has *ἐκ γῆς Αἰγύπτου* for *ἐξ Αἰγύπτου*, and *ἐγένετο* for *γέγονεν*. `[T]` Verified against both editions.

*Book usage.* First use of Exodus 32 in Acts, and the only one. `[T]`

*OT-to-OT.* **Exodus 32 and Amos 5:25–27 are already in conversation in the Greek**, and this is what licenses Stephen's next move: Amos 5:26's *τοὺς τύπους οὓς ἐποιήσατε* ("the images which you made") is in the Greek a reference back to wilderness image-making, and Exodus 32:4's calf is the canonical instance. `[T]` *Moderate–high.* **So 7:41 (the calf) and 7:42–43 (Amos) are not two complaints but one: Stephen cites the prophet who says the wilderness worship was already idolatrous, immediately after narrating the episode that proves it.**

*What it adds.* The calf is placed where the speech needs it: between the living oracles (7:38) and the tent of witness (7:44). **Israel had the words and made an image before it had a sanctuary.** That is the premise of 7:48.

#### **Amos 5:25–27 → 7:42–43** *(high confidence — direct quotation with formula, four edits)* `[T]`

*Source context.* Amos 5:21–27. The oracle rejects Israel's festivals and offerings (5:21–23), demands justice rolling down like water (5:24), and then asks the rhetorical question Stephen quotes — did you bring me sacrifices in the wilderness? — and announces exile beyond Damascus. **The function of the cited verses in their own passage is the climax of a cultic indictment: the oracle does not say the worship is insufficient, it says it was never offered to God at all.** `[T]` And in Amos' own setting "beyond Damascus" is Assyrian exile, for the northern kingdom.

*Stephen's four edits.* `[T]` All verified against Swete and Rahlfs 1979, which agree with each other at every point.

1. **The citation formula.** *καθὼς γέγραπται ἐν βίβλῳ τῶν προφητῶν* ("as it is written in the book of the prophets") — Stephen's own, not Amos'. *γέγραπται* stands at 1:20, 7:42, 13:33, 15:15 and 23:5 in Acts `[T]`; **7:42 is the second of the book's five written-text appeals and the only one in a prophet.**
2. **The forty years moved to the front.** Both editions read *ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ τεσσαράκοντα ἔτη*; Stephen reads *ἔτη τεσσεράκοντα ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ*. `[T]` **The citation's first words are now the number that has just organised Moses' life three times.** *Moderate* on the purpose; `[T]` on the reordering.
3. ***προσκυνεῖν*** **added.** Amos: *τοὺς τύπους αὐτῶν οὓς ἐποιήσατε ἑαυτοῖς* ("their images which you made for yourselves"). Stephen: *τοὺς τύπους οὓς ἐποιήσατε **προσκυνεῖν αὐτοῖς*** ("the images which you made **to worship** them"). `[T]` **He drops "for yourselves" and supplies an infinitive of worship.** *προσκυνέω* occurs four times in Acts and this is the only one of idolatry. The edit makes explicit what Amos implies, and it is the word the Gentile-mission scenes will use of the eunuch (8:27) and of Cornelius (10:25).
4. ***Βαβυλῶνος*** **for** ***Δαμασκοῦ***. `[T]` **Damascus stands in the Hebrew *and* in both Greek editions**, so the change is Stephen's own and not a *Vorlage*. The overview carries this row at high confidence and it is now confirmed against the Hebrew as well.

*Book usage.* **Amos is used twice in Acts — here and at 15:16–18 (Amos 9:11–12) — and both uses work only on the Greek, and both sit at structural hinges.** `[T]` The overview's honesty note 2 flags this as "the most attractive untested pattern in the book", clearing the two-member threshold but not the three-pericope macro gate, and rates the *σκηνή* link **moderate `[S: sweep]`, flagged for audit**. **This run leaves that rating where it is** and adds one datum in its favour and one against:

- *In favour:* the Amos 5 citation's own *σκηνή* is a **translation artefact** (Tool 8, ancient-versions check) — the Hebrew has a divine name — so **both Amos citations in Acts turn on words that exist only in the Greek**, which is a stronger and more specific version of the overview's claim than "both are LXX-dependent".
- *Against:* *σκηνή* stands in only three verses of Acts (7:43, 7:44, 15:16) `[T]`, and 7:43/7:44 are two verses apart while 15:16 is eight chapters away; a two-verse cluster plus one distant occurrence is thin evidence for a designed thread, and the word is the obvious one for a tent in any case.

*OT-to-OT.* Amos 5:25–27 and Exodus 32 (above). Also Deuteronomy 4:19 and 17:3 on worshipping the host of heaven, which is 7:42's *λατρεύειν τῇ στρατιᾷ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ* — Stephen's own phrase, not Amos'. *Moderate.*

*What it adds.* The citation does the speech's hardest work. It denies that Israel's wilderness worship was ever acceptable, which removes the premise that the cult has a golden age to be defended; and it names exile as the consequence, in the city the hearers' own history makes unforgettable. **Stephen has moved the sentence from Assyria to Babylon — from the exile that happened to the north to the one that happened to Jerusalem.**

#### **Psalm 132:5 → 7:46, and Isaiah 66:1–2 → 7:49–50** *(high confidence on both; the relation between them is the finding)* `[T]`

These are treated together because **Move 3 is where this pair pays.**

*Source context — Psalm 132.* A Song of Ascents, and the Psalter's fullest treatment of the temple's legitimacy. It opens by asking God to remember David and his oath, which is quoted: he will not enter his house or sleep *ἕως οὗ **εὕρω τόπον** τῷ κυρίῳ, **σκήνωμα** τῷ θεῷ Ἰακώβ* ("until I find a **place** for the Lord, a **dwelling** for the God of Jacob", 132:5). The psalm then recounts finding the ark (132:6–8), prays for the priests and the anointed (132:9–10), recites God's oath to David (132:11–12), and ends: *ὅτι ἐξελέξατο Κύριος τὴν Σιών … **αὕτη ἡ κατάπαυσίς μου** εἰς αἰῶνα αἰῶνος· **ὧδε κατοικήσω*** ("for the Lord has chosen Zion … **this is my resting place** for ever; **here I will dwell**", 132:13–14). `[T]` Verified in Swete, in Rahlfs 1979 and in the WLC. **The function of the psalm is to ground Zion's election in David's vow — and its last word on the subject is God saying he will dwell there.**

*Source context — Isaiah 66.* The last chapter of the book, opening the final oracle: *Ὁ οὐρανός μοι θρόνος, ἡ δὲ γῆ ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν μου· ποῖον οἶκον οἰκοδομήσετέ μοι; ἢ **ποῖος τόπος τῆς καταπαύσεώς μου**;* and then 66:2's answer — the hand that made all things, and the one God looks to, *τὸν ταπεινὸν καὶ ἡσύχιον καὶ τρέμοντα τοὺς λόγους μου* ("the humble and quiet and trembling at my words"). `[T]` **The function of the verse in its own passage is not to abolish the temple but to relocate God's regard**: 66:3–4 go on to condemn sacrifice offered by the lawless, and the chapter ends with the nations streaming in (66:18–21). The overview's row notes this: Isaiah 66 "answers the temple charge from the prophet who ends with the nations streaming in". `[S: overview]`, now confirmed by reading the chapter.

*Move 3 — the two texts are already in conversation, in both languages.* **"My rest" (*κατάπαυσις* with *μου*) stands in exactly three verses of the Greek Old Testament: Psalm 95:11, Psalm 132:14 and Isaiah 66:1.** `[T]` Verified by ordered phrase search across all sixty Swete books with a positive control passed, and the Hebrew confirms the pairing independently: **Isaiah 66:1 contains both of Psalm 132's two key nouns — *מָקוֹם* ("place", Psalm 132:5) and *מְנוּחָה* ("rest", Psalm 132:14)** `[T]`, so the relation is not an artefact of the Greek.

**Isaiah 66:1 asks the question to which Psalm 132:14 is the answer — and Stephen quotes them in that order.** He alludes to the psalm's vow at 7:46 and cites the prophet's question at 7:49. *High confidence* on the distribution and on both source identifications; **the claim that Stephen is deliberately setting one against the other is `[I]`, moderate–high.**

And the verbal join runs through 7:48: Psalm 132:14's *ὧδε **κατοικήσω*** ("here I will dwell") is answered by *οὐχ ὁ ὕψιστος ἐν χειροποιήτοις **κατοικεῖ***. `[T]` **Same verb. The psalm says "here"; Stephen says "not in things made by hands".**

*Book usage.* The Psalter is a live source in Acts (Psalms 16, 110, 2, 118, 69/109, and Psalm 16 given to both Peter and Paul). **Psalm 132 is new here and is the only Song of Ascents used in the book** `[T]` — and it is used by allusion, without a formula, exactly as honesty note 3 predicts. Isaiah is the book's most-used prophet (twelve uses, nine texts at v2.5) and **66:1–2 is its longest continuous Isaiah quotation.** `[T]`

*What it adds, and the variant that decides it.* On **NA28's text**, 7:46 says David asked to find a dwelling **for the house of Jacob** — and the psalm says **for the God of Jacob**. If *οἴκῳ* is right, Stephen has altered the psalm at exactly the point where the psalm grounds the temple, turning David's vow from *a place for God* into *a place for the people*; and 7:47's "but Solomon built him a house" then lands as a contrast rather than a completion, and 7:48's *ἀλλά* has something to be adversative to. **On the variant *θεῷ*, the allusion is exact and the argument has to be carried by 7:48 alone.** See Textual Variants; this is the one place in the unit where a text-critical decision changes the shape of the paragraph.

**Psalm 95:11 is named here as the third member of the field and not as an allusion.** Stephen does not quote it. But *κατάπαυσις* stands in the New Testament only at Acts 7:49 and seven times in Hebrews 3–4 `[T]`, and Hebrews' argument is built on Psalm 95:11 and on Joshua (Hebrews 4:8). **Acts 7:45–49 and Hebrews 3–4 are the New Testament's two sustained treatments of whether the land was the rest, and the Greek Old Testament gives them a field of exactly three verses to work in.** `[T]` on the two distributions; *moderate* on the convergence, which is a convergence and not a dependence.

**And two echoes of Luke's Gospel that are the unit's strongest, because they are consecutive verses of one oracle.** These belong under Move 4 on the overview's own ground that Luke's Gospel is "the controlling intertext of the whole work".

**Luke 11:47–49 → Acts 7:52, 22:20, and the event itself.** `[T]`

- **Luke 11:47** — *οὐαὶ ὑμῖν, ὅτι οἰκοδομεῖτε τὰ μνημεῖα τῶν προφητῶν **οἱ δὲ πατέρες ὑμῶν ἀπέκτειναν αὐτούς***. **The lemmata *πατήρ*, *ἀποκτείνω* and *προφήτης* stand together in exactly two verses of the New Testament: Luke 11:47 and Acts 7:52.** Verified by collocation across all twenty-seven books. And in Luke–Acts the prophets are said to have been killed in four verses — Luke 11:47, 11:49, 13:34 and **Acts 7:52** — **so 7:52 is the only such statement in Luke–Acts not spoken by Jesus.** *High confidence.*
- **Luke 11:48** — *ἄρα **μάρτυρές** ἐστε καὶ **συνευδοκεῖτε** τοῖς ἔργοις τῶν πατέρων ὑμῶν*. *μάρτυς* with *συνευδοκέω* stands in two New Testament verses: Luke 11:48 and **Acts 22:20**, where Paul says of this scene *ἤμην ἐφεστὼς καὶ **συνευδοκῶν***. `[T]` *High confidence* on the distribution; the overview carries the 22:20 end of it.
- **Luke 11:49** — *ἡ **σοφία** τοῦ θεοῦ εἶπεν· Ἀποστελῶ εἰς αὐτοὺς προφήτας καὶ ἀποστόλους, καὶ ἐξ αὐτῶν **ἀποκτενοῦσιν** καὶ **διώξουσιν*** — and *διώκω* with *προφήτης* stands in Luke–Acts at Luke 11:49 and **Acts 7:52** only (*τίνα τῶν προφητῶν οὐκ **ἐδίωξαν***). `[T]` **This third member is the weakest and is rated *moderate***: the *σοφία* link to 6:10 is thematic rather than collocational, and *διώκω* is a common verb.

**What the three together add.** Luke 11:47–49 is a single oracle in three verses, and **all three verses land in this unit's complex**: the first is Stephen's accusation at 7:52, the second is Paul's confession of this very scene at 22:20, and the third predicted it. **The oracle Jesus spoke against the lawyers is discharged on a man who repeats it.** *High confidence* on the first two, *moderate* on the third; and **the first two are only visible if Luke 11:47 and 11:48 are held together**, which is the case the move's "hold the opening open" discipline exists to produce.

**Luke 13:34 → 7:52 and 7:58–59.** *Ἰερουσαλὴμ Ἰερουσαλήμ, ἡ **ἀποκτείνουσα** τοὺς προφήτας καὶ **λιθοβολοῦσα** τοὺς ἀπεσταλμένους πρὸς αὐτήν.* `[T]` *ἀποκτείνω* + *λιθοβολέω* + *προφήτης* stand together in two New Testament verses, Matthew 23:37 and Luke 13:34; *λιθοβολέω* stands in seven New Testament verses, three of them in Acts and **two of those in this unit** (7:58, 7:59, and 14:5). **Luke 13:34's two verbs are both discharged here — the killing of prophets in Stephen's accusation, the stoning of the sent in the narrative of his death.** *High confidence* on the distributions; *moderate–high* that Luke intends the oracle's fulfilment, since he does not flag it.

**Nil returns, recorded.** No echo was found of 1:12–26 (the lot, the twelfth man) or of 2:42–47 and 4:32–5:11 (the community of goods) in this unit, despite the Stephen narrative beginning in a dispute about distribution at 6:1. *Checked and empty.*

### 12. Genre

**Historical narrative with an embedded forensic speech** — the "sermon/speech embedded in narrative" category, and the embedding matters more than usual here because the speech is fifty-two of the unit's sixty-eight verses.

Three reading rules follow.

1. **The speech is a defence, not a sermon, and it is also not quite a defence.** The council asks *εἰ ταῦτα οὕτως ἔχει;* and Stephen never answers it. He does not say "I did not say that"; he does not say "I did say it and here is why". **He answers a different question — not "did you say it?" but "where does God live?"** The genre-marker for a defence is *ἀπολογία*, which Acts uses at 22:1 and 25:16 and **not here** `[T]`; the overview's trap 4b warns against treating a defence as a testimony, and the converse warning applies to this unit: **do not treat this as an *ἀπολογία*, because Luke does not call it one.**
2. **The sub-genre of the speech is the historical recital.** Joshua 24:2–13, 1 Samuel 12:6–15, Nehemiah 9:6–37 and Psalms 78, 105 and 106 are the canonical form: God's acts rehearsed in order, usually turning into either covenant renewal or confession. `[T]` Stephen's follows the form and breaks it at the end — **a recital that normally ends in "therefore choose" or "therefore we confess" ends in "you killed him".** *Moderate–high* on the generic identification; the break is `[T]`.
3. **The martyrdom is reported, not dramatised.** Luke gives the stoning four words (*ἐκβαλόντες ἔξω τῆς πόλεως ἐλιθοβόλουν*) and the prayers two sentences. There is no description of suffering. `[T]` A sermon that supplies the physical detail is supplying a genre Luke declined.

**One embedded sub-genre inside the sub-genre:** 7:49–50 is poetry, set as verse in NA28, and should be read as such — four cola and a question (Tool 5b).

### 14. Bible Timeline

**Where the passage sits.** After the ascension and Pentecost, inside the first year or two of the church in Jerusalem, before any Gentile has been admitted, before the Council, and before a line of the New Testament was written. **The apostles are still worshipping in the temple daily (2:46; 3:1; 5:42)** `[T]`, so within Acts' own narrative it is standing and functioning `[I]` — which is the fact that makes 7:48 something other than a denunciation of a building.

**Where the reader is.** This side of the cross and the resurrection, and — for the first readers and for us alike — **after the temple's destruction**. That difference is the timeline fact most likely to distort the sermon, and it cuts both ways: we know the building went, which makes 7:48 sound like a prediction fulfilled; the first hearers did not, which is why it sounded like blasphemy.

**What has happened in between that changes the reading.** Three things. (i) **The temple is gone**, so the question Stephen's hearers thought was about loyalty we hear as a question about history. (ii) **The Gentile mission has succeeded**, so the premise Stephen died for is the air we breathe, and we are liable to think it obvious. (iii) **The New Testament has placed the argument elsewhere too** — the same case is made at 17:24, and at length in Hebrews and in 1 Corinthians 3:16 and Ephesians 2:21.

**Canonical order within the speech, which is worth a line.** Stephen narrates Torah (7:2–44), then Joshua (7:45), then David and Solomon (7:45–47), and then quotes a Psalm and a Prophet (7:46, 7:49). **His citation order tracks his narrative order**: he reaches David and quotes the psalm about David's vow, and then reaches the temple and quotes the prophet about the temple. `[T]` *Moderate* that this is designed; *high* that it is the sequence.

**The "Russian dolls" note.** The speech is itself an exodus-shaped recital — slavery, deliverance, wilderness, land — told to people who are in it again. Stephen does not draw the parallel. 7:51–53's present tense does it for him.

### 15. Who Am I?

**The honest answer is the uncomfortable one, and it is the overview's trap 2 in another form.**

| Figure | Function | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Stephen** | **Type of the witness; and secondarily an example** | He is the first of the *μάρτυρες* in the sense the word acquires. **But he is not the one we are placed with by the text's rhetoric** — see below. |
| **The council** | **Recipients of the message, and the ones the text addresses in the second person** | *ὑμεῖς … ὑμῶν … ὑμεῖς … ὑμῶν … ὑμεῖς* (7:51–52). **The only people directly addressed in this unit are the accused's accusers.** `[T]` |
| **Moses, in the speech** | **Type of Christ, explicitly** | 7:37 makes the typology the speech's own: a prophet like me. And the structure of 7:35 — rejected, then sent as ruler and redeemer — is the pattern Peter has already applied to Jesus at 3:13–15 and 5:30–31. |
| **Joseph** | **Type of Christ, by the speech's own logic** | Rejected by his brothers, given grace and wisdom, made the saviour of those who sold him. Stephen does not say so; the pattern he is building does. *Moderate.* |
| **The patriarchs who sold Joseph; the man who said "who appointed you?"; the fathers who turned to Egypt** | **Negative examples — and the ones Stephen says his hearers are** | `[T]` at 7:51. |
| **Saul** | **Negative example, and the book's best news** | |

**The reading to resist, named plainly.** The sermon's instinct is *be like Stephen*. The text's own second person is *you are like your fathers*. **A sermon on this passage that lets the congregation sit with Stephen has reversed the only direct address in the unit.** That does not make "be like Stephen" false — Luke does commend him — but it makes it the second thing to say, not the first. The first is that this speech was preached at people who thought they were defending God's house, and that it was spoken by a man they thought was attacking it.

**And the figure the preacher should not let the congregation be.** Not Moses, and not Stephen either, finally: the speech ends with a man seeing *τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου* standing. **The one standing is the one to identify as the actor; everyone else in the unit is either doing the killing, watching the coats, or dying.**

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response Luke seeks.** Not admiration of Stephen. **Confidence that the God of this book cannot be confined, and therefore cannot be stopped** — which is why Luke follows the death immediately with 8:4, *those who were scattered went about preaching the word*. The unit's intended effect is the overview's purpose sentence in narrative form: a purpose no opposition can obstruct.

**Stage 2 — worldview before behaviour.**

**Worldview.** Three shifts, in the order the text makes them.

1. **God's presence is not a location, and never was.** Not "the temple has been replaced" but "the God who filled it was never contained by it". The speech's evidence is the patriarchs' own history, which is why it is sixty per cent of the unit.
2. **Rejecting God's messenger is the normal response of God's people, and knowing that is protection against two errors** — the error of being scandalised when the church resists its own reformers, and the error of assuming one is always the reformer.
3. **The seat at God's right hand is occupied, and the one occupying it stands up.** 7:55–56 is the only place in the New Testament where the figure at God's right hand is standing (Tool 11, Textual Variants). Whatever else the standing means, it means that the dying of a witness is not unattended.

**Behaviour.**

- **Stop** reading institutional loyalty as faithfulness without testing it. The men who killed Stephen were defending the place God had given them, and they were not cynics: Luke says they thought they heard blasphemy.
- **Stop** supplying the physical detail the text declines (Tool 12), and stop preaching the vision as attainable (Tool 13).
- **Start** praying for those who oppose, in the terms 7:60 actually uses — *do not set this sin against them* — which is a request about God's reckoning, not a report of one's own feelings.
- **Start** reading one's own Scriptures the way Stephen read his: historically, at length, and toward a question rather than from a conclusion.

**Motivation, so that none of this is moralism.** The ground is not "Stephen managed it". It is 7:55–56. The man who prayed that prayer had just been shown the one who prayed *πάτερ, ἄφες αὐτοῖς* standing at God's right hand, and he addressed his own prayer to him. **The capacity to pray for one's executioners is derivative, not native** — Stephen prays to Jesus the prayer Jesus prayed to the Father, and the direction of the dependence is the gospel in the text.

**Four audiences.**

- *For me.* Where am I defending a place rather than following a person? And: whose stones am I holding the coats for — what am I consenting to by standing near it and doing nothing?
- *For a Christian friend.* The believer worn down by opposition inside the church needs 8:4: the scattering was the mission. Not a promise that it will feel like that, but a fact about what God did with it once.
- *For the church community.* **A congregation can be the Sanhedrin.** 7:51's "as your fathers did, and you" is addressed to the most religiously serious body in Jerusalem. The corporate application is not "be brave like Stephen" but "we are capable of this, and the symptom is that we stop our ears" (7:57).
- *For an unbeliever.* The claim is testable in the shape the book gives it: a movement with no temple, no territory and no protection outlived the institution that killed its first witness. And the one thing Stephen asks for is that the sin not be charged — which is what the Christian gospel offers to the people who committed it, Saul included.

**Prayer.** Lord, you are not housed, and you have never been absent. Give us the wisdom that cannot be withstood and the grace not to need to win. Where we have stopped our ears, open them; where we have held the coats, forgive us. And do not hold this sin against them — or against us.

#### Grammatical and syntactical features

- **7:51's present tense is the verse's whole force.** *ὑμεῖς ἀεὶ τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ **ἀντιπίπτετε*** — present indicative with *ἀεί* ("always"), so the accusation is not that they once resisted but that they are doing it now and habitually. `[T]` *High confidence.* Both English versions render it as a present ("you always resist"; "you are doing just as"), and the ESV's "you always resist the Holy Spirit" is the closer.
- **7:35's double accusative with an interposed quotation** — *Τοῦτον τὸν Μωϋσῆν, ὃν ἠρνήσαντο εἰπόντες· …, **τοῦτον** ὁ θεὸς … ἀπέσταλκεν* — is a resumptive construction that English must punctuate (Tool 4). `[T]`
- **7:35's perfect *ἀπέσταλκεν*.** Not the aorist. "God **has sent** him" — a completed sending whose effect stands. `[T]` *Moderate–high* on the significance; the perfect is doing work in a sentence otherwise full of aorists, and it is the verb of the sentence that answers the speech's refrain.
- **7:55–56's two perfect participles of standing.** *ἑστῶτα* twice (7:55, 7:56), perfect of *ἵστημι* — "having stood and standing", a state, not an action in progress. `[T]` **The Son of Man is not caught in the act of rising; he is standing.** *High confidence* on the morphology; the exegetical weight is under Difficult Verses.
- **7:59–60's two addressees.** *κύριε Ἰησοῦ* (7:59) and then *κύριε* alone (7:60), both vocatives, with a kneeling between them. `[T]` The second is naturally read as the same addressee as the first; **the Greek does not settle it**, and the ambiguity is worth leaving open rather than closed (Difficult Verses).
- **6:15's *ὡσεί*.** "As if", not "as" — a simile with a softener. `[T]` The NASB95's "like the face of an angel" and the ESV's "like the face of an angel" both carry it adequately.
- **7:2's genitive absolute chain** — *ὤφθη τῷ πατρὶ ἡμῶν Ἀβραὰμ **ὄντι** ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ **πρὶν ἢ κατοικῆσαι** αὐτὸν ἐν Χαρράν* — stacks a participle and an infinitival temporal clause to put the appearance as early as the syntax will allow. `[T]`

#### Wordplay and sound

**One, and it is modest.** 7:51's three accusations alliterate on the privative and the sibilant — *Σκληροτράχηλοι καὶ ἀπερίτμητοι … καὶ τοῖς ὠσίν* — and the two hapaxes are both long compounds, so the line is harder to say than anything else in the speech. *(Transliterated here because the sound is the point: sklērotrachēloi kai aperitmētoi kardiais kai tois ōsin.)* `[I]` *Moderate confidence* — the effect is real in reading aloud, and no canonical precedent licenses calling it designed.

**No paronomasia is claimed on *Στέφανος* ("crown").** The name is not punned on in the text and the Greek offers no trigger; the association belongs to later homiletics, not to Luke.

#### Recommended verification

- **BDAG on *λυτρωτής*** — whether the word carries the Exodus-redemption colouring (as *λυτρόω* does in Deuteronomy) strongly enough to be preached as "the one who ransoms". Patrick has BDAG as a PDF.
- **Wallace on the perfect *ἀπέσταλκεν* (7:35)** — whether the aspect bears the weight placed on it here, or is an extensive perfect doing nothing special. *Flagged as moderate until checked.*
- **A grammar on 7:46's dative *τῷ οἴκῳ***, if NA28's reading is preached: whether a dative of advantage ("a dwelling for the house of Jacob") is the natural construal, or whether *τῷ οἴκῳ* can be read in apposition. This is the hinge of the variant and a grammar should settle it.

### Textual Variants

The NA28 apparatus for the whole of Acts is in the corpus and was read for every verse of the unit. **Five variants bear on preaching, and three of them are instances of the overview's standing instruction.**

#### 7:46 — *οἴκῳ* or *θεῷ*: the one variant that changes a paragraph

- **The issue.** Did David ask to find a dwelling **for the house of Jacob** (*τῷ οἴκῳ*) or **for the God of Jacob** (*τῷ θεῷ*)?
- **Manuscript evidence.** NA28 prints ***οἴκῳ*** on **𝔓⁷⁴ א\* B D saᵐˢ**. *θεῷ* is read by **א² A C E Ψ 33. 81. 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1241. 1505. 1739 𝔪 lat sy co** — and the SBLGNT prints it, which is why both English versions have it. **The NA28 apparatus labels the variant "(Ps 132,5)".**
- **Most likely original: *οἴκῳ*, and the internal argument is strong.** Three reasons. (i) **It is the harder reading by a wide margin**, and the easier reading is not merely easier but *available*: a scribe who knew the psalm had its wording to hand. (ii) **No text of the psalm supports *οἴκῳ***: Swete and Rahlfs 1979 both read *σκήνωμα τῷ θεῷ Ἰακώβ*, and the Hebrew reads *מִשְׁכָּנוֹת לַאֲבִיר יַעֲקֹב* ("dwellings for the Mighty One of Jacob") `[T]`. **So *οἴκῳ* cannot be an assimilation to anything, and *θεῷ* can be — and is exactly what assimilation would produce.** (iii) א reads *θεῷ* only by correction, which is a scribe doing the assimilation where we can watch him. *High confidence* that NA28 is right.
- **Preaching implication.** Substantial. On *οἴκῳ*, 7:46–48 is a single argument: David asked for a dwelling for the people, Solomon built a house for God, **and God does not live in houses** — with 7:48's *ἀλλά* adversative to 7:47. On *θεῷ*, 7:46 and 7:47 say the same thing twice and 7:48 corrects both. **Both readings yield a sound sermon; they do not yield the same sermon.** The congregation will hear *θεῷ* from the ESV. The honest move is to preach from the pulpit text and say in one sentence that the oldest manuscripts read "house", and what difference it makes.
- **Confidence:** *high* on the text-critical judgement; *moderate* on the claim that Stephen is deliberately altering the psalm, since he is alluding rather than quoting and NA28's own apparatus treats the psalm as the variant's source rather than as Stephen's.

#### 6:8 — *χάριτος* or *πίστεως*

- **The issue.** Was Stephen full of **grace** or of **faith**?
- **Evidence.** NA28 prints *χάριτος* on **𝔓⁸ 𝔓⁴⁵ᵛⁱᵈ 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B D 0175. 33. 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1739 lat syᵖ co**. *πίστεως* is read by 1241. 1505 𝔪 syʰ (and Gregory of Nyssa); **E reads *χάριτος καὶ πίστεως*, which is the conflation that shows the two readings competing**; Ψ reads a triple.
- **Most likely original: *χάριτος*.** Overwhelming external support including three papyri, and the variant is explicable as assimilation to 6:5's *πλήρης πίστεως*. *High confidence.*
- **Preaching implication.** Minor for the sentence, real for the chain: ***πίστεως* severs 6:8 from 7:10 and 7:46**, the only other *χάρις* verses in the unit (Tool 7, finding 4). **A tidying instance: the easier reading breaks a link.**

#### 7:35 and 7:37 — two clippings the tradition restored

- **7:35.** NA28 prints the question without *ἐφ' ἡμῶν*; א C D Ψ 81. 453. 1175. 2818 co supply *εφ ημων* and E 33. 323. 945. 1505. 1739 supply *εφ ημας*, against 𝔓⁴⁵ 𝔓⁷⁴ A B. **The tradition is restoring Exodus 2:14 — and Acts 7:27, which has it.**
- **7:37.** NA28 prints Deuteronomy 18:15 without *αὐτοῦ ἀκούσεσθε*; C D\*·¹ E 33. 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1241. 1739. 2818 gig vgᶜˡ·ʷʷ sy mae bo supply it, **and the apparatus itself tags the addition "(Dt 18,15)"**.
- **7:30** belongs with these: NA28's bare *ἄγγελος* is given *κυρίου* by D E Ψ 33. 323. 614. 945. 1241. 1505. 1739 𝔪 p w sy mae boᵐˢ, **restoring Exodus 3:2**.
- **Most likely original: the shorter readings in all three.** *High confidence* — each longer reading is an assimilation to a named source, which is the commonest species of secondary reading in a citation-dense passage.
- **Preaching implication.** Direct, and the same in all three: **Stephen's citations are edited, and the manuscript tradition kept trying to un-edit them.** That is external evidence that the edits are real and that early readers noticed. **This takes the overview's honesty note 5 from seven instances to a count the overview should restate** — see Book-Overview Tensions.

#### 7:49 — the one place where a manuscript harmonises its New Testament to its own Old Testament

- **The issue.** NA28 prints *ὁ οὐρανός **μοι** θρόνος, **ἡ δὲ** γῆ ὑποπόδιον*; 𝔓⁷⁴ reads *μου*, D\* *μου ἐστιν*, D² *μοι ἐστιν*, and **B reads *καὶ ἡ*** (with h).
- **The observation.** **Rahlfs 1979's Isaiah 66:1 reads *μοι* and *ἡ δέ*; Swete's — that is, Vaticanus' — reads *μου* and *καὶ ἡ*.** `[T]` Verified in both editions. **So the two disputed points in Acts 7:49 are the two points at which Vaticanus and the eclectic text differ in Isaiah 66:1, and at 7:49 B reads what B reads in Isaiah.**
- **Most likely original: NA28's text.** *Moderate–high* — the reading agreeing with the eclectic Isaiah is not thereby secondary, and the explanation runs the other way: a scribe copying a citation conforms it to the Old Testament in front of him.
- **Preaching implication.** None directly. **Method implication, and it is a new kind:** the overview's instruction says to read the apparatus as a reader's commentary on internal echo. **Here it is a commentary on *external* citation — a single manuscript visibly harmonising its Acts to its own Isaiah**, which is the cleanest demonstration of the mechanism the project has yet found. *Confidence: high on the collation; moderate on the inference about the scribe.*

#### 7:14 — the seventy-five, which is not a variant in Acts at all

- **The issue.** Jacob's household numbers **seventy-five** at 7:14, and the Hebrew of Genesis 46:27 and Exodus 1:5 reads **seventy** (*שִׁבְעִים*). `[T]`
- **Where the difference is.** **Not in the manuscripts of Acts** — the apparatus records no variant at the number. It is between the Hebrew and the Greek: **Swete and Rahlfs 1979 both read ἑβδομήκοντα πέντε at Genesis 46:27**, and Exodus 1:5 reads *πέντε καὶ ἑβδομήκοντα*. `[T]`
- **Triage: category 3 — the Greek as the New Testament's own text**, and it stands whichever text is prior. Stephen's phrase *ἐν ψυχαῖς ἑβδομήκοντα πέντε* follows Genesis 46:27's *πᾶσαι ψυχαὶ … ἑβδομήκοντα πέντε*. `[T]`
- **And the Greek shows its working, which defuses the "error" charge honestly.** Genesis 46:27 in both Greek editions says Joseph's sons born in Egypt were ***ἐννέα*** ("nine") where the Hebrew says ***שְׁנָיִם*** ("two"). `[T]` **So the seventy-five is not an arithmetic slip but a different genealogical reckoning, and it is internally consistent in the Greek.** Deuteronomy 10:22's Greek, by contrast, reads *ἐν ἑβδομήκοντα ψυχαῖς* — seventy `[T]` — so the Greek Old Testament itself carries both figures.
- **Preaching implication.** Do not defend the figure by arithmetic and do not apologise for it. **Say that Stephen is reading the Greek Bible of the synagogues, that it counts Joseph's Egyptian-born descendants differently, and move on.** The Qumran evidence sometimes cited in support of seventy-five at Exodus 1:5 is **not verifiable from this corpus and is not claimed here** — flagged for the library.

**Not flagged:** 7:5's word-order variant, 7:11's Egypt readings, 7:12's *σιτία/σιτα/σιτον*, 7:13's *ἀνεγνωρίσθη/ἐγνωρίσθη*, 7:16's *μετετέθησαν/μετηχθησαν* and the Shechem genitive, 7:18's bracketed *ἐπ' Αἴγυπτον*, 7:26's *συνήλλασσεν/συνήλασεν*, 7:39's pronoun (reported under Tool 8c), 7:43's *Ῥαιφάν* spellings and bracketed *ὑμῶν*, 7:51's *καρδίαις/καρδίας/τῇ καρδίᾳ*, 7:56's *διηνοιγμένους/ἀνεωγμένους*, 7:58's *αὐτῶν/ἑαυτῶν*, 7:60's word order. **None affects a finding.** The Western text (D) is heavily active throughout the unit — seventeen distinct readings in the speech alone, including the substantial additions at 6:10–11 (*μὴ δυνάμενοι ἀντοφθαλμεῖν τῇ ἀληθείᾳ*) and 7:24 (*καὶ ἔκρυψεν αὐτὸν ἐν τῇ ἄμμῳ*, from Exodus 2:12) — and **D's habit here is expansion toward the Old Testament source**, which is the same phenomenon as the three clippings above, seen from the other side.

### Historical and Cultural Background

#### Setting

- **Time period.** Within a few years of the crucifixion; the temple built by Herod is standing, incomplete and operating. The Sanhedrin has the standing to try a capital charge and, on the ordinary reconstruction, not the standing to execute without the prefect — which is the background to the question of what 7:57–58 actually is.
- **Location.** Jerusalem. The council; then *ἔξω τῆς πόλεως* (7:58), which is where a stoning had to happen.
- **Political context.** Roman prefecture, local Sadducean high-priestly administration, a Pharisaic minority in the council (which 23:6–9 will exploit and which is silent here).

#### What the original audience knew

1. **"The Synagogue of the Freedmen" (6:9) is a diaspora synagogue, and its membership is the unit's social key.** *Λιβερτῖνοι* is a Latin loanword — *libertini*, freedmen — so these are Greek-speaking Jews, or their descendants, from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia and Asia, resident in Jerusalem. **The dispute that kills Stephen is not between the church and the temple establishment; it starts between Greek-speaking Jews and a Greek-speaking Jewish Christian**, and it has to be escalated deliberately (6:11–12) before the council is involved. *High confidence* on the identification; *moderate* on whether 6:9 names one synagogue or more (the Greek is ambiguous and the versions differ).
2. **The charge is a capital one and the hearers knew it.** Blasphemy and speaking against the temple and the law are the charges that carry death. Leviticus 24:16 prescribes stoning for blasphemy and specifies that the whole congregation stones. `[T]`
3. **The witnesses throw first.** Deuteronomy 17:7 — *the hand of the witnesses shall be first upon him* — is the law behind 7:58's *οἱ μάρτυρες ἀπέθεντο τὰ ἱμάτια αὐτῶν*. `[T]` **The coats come off because the men who testified have to do the throwing.** This is the single most useful background item in the unit, and it is what makes Saul's custody of the coats more than a detail: he is assisting the witnesses in a lawful execution, which is exactly how he will describe himself (26:9–11).
4. **The temple was the economy as well as the cult.** Pilgrimage, sacrifice, tithe, the treasury, and a very large workforce. A claim about the temple was a claim about Jerusalem's livelihood, not only its theology.
5. **"Made with hands" was polemical vocabulary.** The Greek Old Testament uses *χειροποίητος* of idols (Leviticus 26:1; Isaiah 2:18; 16:12; 46:6, and elsewhere). `[T]` *Moderate–high.* **So 7:48 is not a neutral observation that God is bigger than a building; it borrows the vocabulary used of idols.** That is a large part of why 7:54 happens, and the preacher should say so rather than wondering why they reacted as they did.
6. **Stoning outside the city, and the legal question.** Whether 7:57–60 is a judicial execution or a lynching is genuinely open on the text: *κράξαντες … ὥρμησαν ὁμοθυμαδόν* reads like a mob, while the witnesses' coats and the location outside the city read like procedure. `[T]` **Luke gives both and resolves neither.** *Flag as uncertain; do not assert "lynching" from the pulpit as though the text said it.*

#### What this changes about how we read

**First, it stops the sermon treating 7:54 as inexplicable fury.** On the background, the council has heard a man use idol-vocabulary of their sanctuary and then accuse them of their fathers' crime. Luke marks their reaction as animal; he does not make it unmotivated.

**Second, it makes Saul's role specific rather than atmospheric.** He is not a bystander who happened to be there. He is holding the clothes of the men whose legal duty it was to throw.

### Original Audience Reception

#### Canonical audience

- **Canonical section.** New Testament narrative; volume two of a two-volume work addressed to Theophilus.
- **What the canonical reader knows.** The whole Old Testament — including, crucially, **Joshua 24, 1 Samuel 12 and Nehemiah 9**, so that the *form* of the speech is recognised before its content is evaluated; and **Luke's Gospel**, so that 7:52's accusation is heard as Jesus' own words (Luke 11:47; 13:34) and 7:59–60 as a recapitulation of Luke 23:46.
- **Canonical register.** Acts' own preceding six chapters, in which the temple is where the apostles preach daily (5:42) and the council has twice let them go.

#### The first hearers

- **Who they were.** A mixed Jewish-and-Gentile Christian readership, under pressure in the synagogue and periodically before Roman courts, for whom the legality and the Jewishness of their movement were live anxieties.
- **Their situation.** By the time most of them read this, **the temple was either about to fall or had fallen.** Either way, a speech arguing that God was never housed read as consolation rather than attack.
- **What they brought.** The assumption, shared with their accusers, that God had chosen a place — Deuteronomy's "the place the Lord your God will choose" is the Torah's own phrase, repeated some twenty times. **Stephen is not arguing against a Jewish distortion; he is arguing against the plain sense of Deuteronomy**, and his hearers knew it. That is why he argues for fifty verses before he gets to the point.

#### Where the passage fits their world

**(i)** A diaspora Jew arguing with other diaspora Jews about the temple was an intramural argument of a familiar kind — and the novelty is the escalation, not the argument. **(ii)** The forty-year structure of Moses' life (7:23, 30, 36) is a known Jewish periodisation and would have sounded traditional, not inventive. **(iii)** The seventy-five would have sounded correct to anyone reading the Greek Bible, and odd only to someone reading the Hebrew.

#### Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

- **Shocking.** *χειροποίητος* of the temple (above). And **7:51's *ἀπερίτμητοι*** — calling the Sanhedrin uncircumcised, which is the one insult in the language that reverses a Jew's standing entirely. `[T]` The term is the prophets' own (Jeremiah 9:25; Ezekiel 44:7), which is what makes it arguable rather than mere abuse.
- **Surprising.** That the speech never defends Stephen. A first hearer braced for a rebuttal of 6:13–14 gets a history lesson. And that **Jesus is not named until 7:52, and then only as "the Righteous One"** — in a speech that gets its speaker killed for what he says about Jesus (6:14).
- **Comforting.** For a scattered church: that God's decisive acts happen in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Midian and a wilderness. **A congregation that has lost access to its holy place is being told that its God has a history of acting outside one.**
- **Disturbing.** 7:51–53 in the second person, and the fact that the people it is addressed to were the serious ones.
- **Funny — one dry note.** 7:57's *συνέσχον τὰ ὦτα αὐτῶν*. A court accused of uncircumcised ears stops its ears. `[T]` Luke does not comment.
- **Familiar.** The recital form; the patriarchal names; the Moses forty-years structure; the legal choreography of the coats.

#### What we bring that they didn't

| What we bring | How it distorts |
|---|---|
| **A destroyed temple** | Makes 7:48 obvious, and the hearers' reaction unintelligible |
| **A Gentile church** | Makes the speech's premise a platitude |
| **Individualism** | Turns a corporate second-person indictment into personal challenge |
| **Martyrdom as a category with centuries of hagiography behind it** | Supplies serenity the text does not describe and heroism the text does not praise |
| **"Church" as a building** | Makes 7:48 land as anticlerical rather than as a claim about God |

#### Candidate Fallen Condition Focus

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **What they felt** | That faithfulness and the defence of the holy place were the same thing — and that anyone loosening the second was attacking the first `[I]`, anchored in 6:13–14 and 7:54, and in the Torah's own place-language |
| **Candidate FCF (shared concern)** | **We bind God's presence to the things we have received him through, and we defend those things as though they were him — so that a true word about God can arrive sounding like blasphemy.** |
| **Shared / differs** | *Shared:* the instinct to locate God in what mediates him, and the reflex of stopping the ears. *Differs:* their place was divinely given and ours are not; we have a destroyed temple and a finished canon, so our version of the error is usually about a tradition, a building, a style or an institution rather than about a sanctuary God commanded. |
| **Confidence** | Anchored — derived from the charge, the speech's answer, and 7:57 |

#### Implication for the sermon

Recovering the reception changes the emphasis in one specific way: **the sermon's antagonists have to be made sympathetic before they are condemned, or 7:51–53 will land on nobody.** A congregation that cannot see why the council reacted will hear the indictment as addressed to someone else — which is the one hearing the text forecloses by putting it in the second person.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Three themes, and they converge.

#### 1. Temple and God's presence

- **Where this passage sits.** At the hinge. The canonical trajectory runs Eden → patriarchal altars → tabernacle → Solomon's temple → the glory departing (Ezekiel 10) → the second temple without the cloud → **Christ as temple** → the church as temple → no temple in the city. **Acts 7 is the point at which the question is asked out loud inside the Christian movement for the first time**, and it is asked historically rather than doctrinally.
- **What comes before that the passage assumes.** Solomon's own prayer: *ἀλλ' ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ὁ οὐρανὸς τοῦ οὐρανοῦ οὐκ ἀρκέσουσίν σοι* (1 Kings 8:27, Greek) — **the temple's builder said at its dedication what Stephen is killed for saying.** `[T]` *High confidence* on the datum; it is the strongest single defence of Stephen's orthodoxy available and the preacher should have it.
- **What comes after.** 17:24 says it to Athens; Hebrews argues it at length; 1 Corinthians 3:16 and Ephesians 2:21 relocate the dwelling into the people.
- **What tracing it contributes.** It stops 7:48 being anti-Jewish. **The claim is Israel's own, made by Israel's temple-builder, and Stephen's offence is not the content but the timing and the company he keeps.**

#### 2. The rejected deliverer

- **Where this passage sits.** The speech constructs the pattern and names it: Joseph sold and made ruler; Moses refused twice and sent as ruler and redeemer; the prophets persecuted; the Righteous One betrayed and murdered. `[T]` **This is the one theme the passage builds explicitly rather than participating in.**
- **Before.** Genesis 37–50; Exodus 2–4; Numbers 14 and 16; the prophets' own biographies.
- **After.** Peter has already made the move twice (3:13–15; 4:11; 5:30–31), and the pattern will be applied to the missionaries (13:46) and to Paul (22:22, where the crowd uses the Servant's own phrase).
- **What tracing it contributes.** The sequence is not "bad things happen to God's messengers" but **"the one Israel refuses is the one God sends, and refusing him is how the sending becomes visible"**. That is why Stephen can tell the story at this length without ever arguing for his conclusion.

#### 3. Exodus and new exodus

- **Where this passage sits.** The speech is mostly an exodus narrative, told to people in Jerusalem, in the vocabulary of a book (Acts) whose growth-notices are Exodus 1:7's.
- **Before and after.** The overview's Exodus 1:7 row is the book-level form of this; Isaiah's new exodus is the prophetic form; and Luke 9:31's *ἔξοδος* at the transfiguration is the Christological form.
- **What tracing it contributes.** 7:36's Moses *ἐξήγαγεν αὐτούς* with wonders and signs, set beside 6:8's Stephen doing wonders and signs *ἐν τῷ λαῷ*, makes the Christian movement the exodus continuing — which is what the growth-notices have been saying since 6:7.

#### Confluence

**Climactic convergence**, and it happens at 7:48–49. The temple theme asks where God lives; the rejected-deliverer theme asks who speaks for him; the exodus theme asks where his people are going. **All three meet in Isaiah's question — "what place is the place of my rest?" — and in the answer the unit gives four verses later: the place of God's rest is a throne at his right hand, and there is a man standing at it.** `[I]` *Moderate–high.* Naming the relation is the point: this is not three themes side by side but one question asked three ways and answered once.

### Christological Reading

NT passage, so this is brief and concerns the typology the text itself deploys.

#### Type of connection

**Three, and the text supplies all three.**

1. **Direct identification by title** — *ὁ δίκαιος* (7:52), *ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου* (7:56), *κύριος Ἰησοῦς* (7:59).
2. **Typology, authorised inside the passage** — Moses as *ἄρχων καὶ λυτρωτής* (7:35) and *προφήτην … ὡς ἐμέ* (7:37). **Four tests:** category — yes, the connection runs through prophetic and mediatorial office, not incidental detail. NT precedent — yes, explicit: Acts 3:22 makes it. Escalation — yes, and the speech leaves it implicit. Authorial pattern — yes: Deuteronomy 18 raises an expectation the Torah leaves unresolved. **Passes all four: *high confidence* typology.**
3. **Typology of Joseph, by the speech's construction rather than its statement** — rejected brother, given grace and wisdom, made the saviour of those who sold him. Category: yes. NT precedent: the same *kind* of connection, in the rejected-stone pattern Peter applies at 4:11. Escalation: implicit. Authorial pattern: Genesis itself raises it (50:20). **Passes one explicitly and two by analogy: *moderate*, and it should be preached as the speech's pattern rather than as the Bible's doctrine of Joseph.**

#### How the passage points to Christ

Not chiefly by typology, in the end, but by **sight**. The speech argues that God is not housed; the vision shows where he now is. And the two together are the answer to the charge: *they* say Jesus will destroy this place; *he* says Jesus is standing in the place Isaiah asked about.

**Three Christological data in three verses, and they escalate.** 7:52's *ὁ δίκαιος* is a title Peter has used. 7:56's *ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου* is **the only use of the title in Acts** `[T]` and the only use in the New Testament on anyone's lips but Jesus' own (with the partial exceptions of John 12:34 and Revelation 1:13; 14:14) — **and Stephen uses it in the presence of the court that condemned Jesus for using it** (Luke 22:69–71). 7:59's *κύριε Ἰησοῦ* is a prayer.

#### Trajectory

- **What the passage anticipates.** That the rest Isaiah asked about has a location after all.
- **What Christ fulfils.** The prophet like Moses; the rejected deliverer; and the temple question — by being where God is.
- **What remains.** The standing is not explained. The church still waits for what 1:11 promised.

#### Moralism check

- **The "be like Stephen" temptation.** Overwhelming, and the overview names it: *faithful like Stephen*. It takes two specific forms — *be bold under questioning* and *forgive like he did* — and both are preachable; both become moralism the moment they are the sermon's ground rather than its consequence.
- **The gospel grounding.** 7:55: *ὑπάρχων δὲ **πλήρης πνεύματος ἁγίου***. The grammar puts the Spirit before the seeing, the seeing before the prayer, and the prayer before the death. `[T]` **Stephen's courage is the fourth thing in the sentence, not the first.**
- **Christ as hero, not example.** The subject of the main verbs across the unit is God, the Spirit, or the Lord (Tool 9b), and the last thing the unit shows is not a man dying well but **a man seeing someone else standing up.**

### Difficult / Contested Verses

#### 7:16 — Abraham, Shechem and the sons of Hamor

- **Category:** apologetic, with a doctrinal edge.
- **The difficulty.** Acts 7:16 says the fathers were carried to Shechem and laid in the tomb *ὃ ὠνήσατο **Ἀβραάμ** τιμῆς ἀργυρίου παρὰ τῶν υἱῶν **Ἑμμὼρ** ἐν **Συχέμ***. The Old Testament has two purchases: **Abraham** buys a cave at **Machpelah near Mamre** from **Ephron the Hittite** for four hundred silver shekels (Genesis 23:16–20), and **Jacob** buys a field at **Shechem** from **Hamor** for a hundred lambs (Genesis 33:19; Joshua 24:32, where Joseph is buried in it). `[T]` All three source passages read in Swete for this run. **Stephen's sentence has Abraham's name, Jacob's vendors and location, and Abraham's medium of payment.** And 7:15–16's plural *καὶ μετετέθησαν* takes Jacob to Shechem, where Genesis 50:13 buries him at Machpelah.
- **Common mishandlings.** Asserting that there were two Abrahamic purchases, for which there is no evidence; emending the text, for which there is no manuscript support at the name; or treating it as proof that Luke invented the speech.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** This is a four-word subordinate clause in a sixteen-verse summary whose point is that the patriarchs died outside the promise with a grave for an inheritance. **Nothing in Stephen's argument depends on who bought the field.** Naming that first constrains the options sensibly.
- **The main interpretive options.** (i) **Deliberate telescoping** — the two purchases compressed into one summary clause, in a recital that is compressing centuries into verses. (ii) **A traditional Jewish harmonisation** that Stephen is reproducing and Luke reports. (iii) **A slip in the speech, faithfully recorded** — Luke reports what was said, and a speaker summarising from memory under trial conflated two purchases. (iv) A textual corruption, for which the apparatus gives no support here.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The clause is a conflation on any reading. *(i)* and *(iii)* are both consistent with a high view of Scripture, since what is inspired is Luke's accurate record of Stephen's speech, and neither requires the speech to be free of compression. **The text-critical evidence does not help and should not be invoked.** *Moderate confidence* that telescoping is the best account; the question is genuinely open.
- **Suggested handling.** **Acknowledge it in one sentence if the congregation reads attentively, and do not build on it.** A footnote in the service sheet does more good than three minutes in the sermon. If pressed: *Stephen is summarising, the summary compresses two purchases, and the point he is making — that the fathers' only holding in the land was a grave — is true of both of them.*

#### 7:55–56 — why is the Son of Man standing?

- **Category:** doctrinal, and the one crux a sermon on this passage cannot avoid.
- **The difficulty.** Everywhere else the exalted Christ **sits**. **In the New Testament the figure at God's right hand is seated in twelve verses and standing in two — and both standings are 7:55 and 7:56.** `[T]` Verified by collocation of *δεξιός* with *κάθημαι* (Matthew 22:44; 26:64; Mark 12:36; 14:62; 16:5; Luke 20:42; **22:69**; **Acts 2:34**; Colossians 3:1; Hebrews 1:13; Revelation 5:1, 5:7) and with *ἵστημι* (Matthew 25:33; Luke 1:11; **Acts 7:55, 7:56**; Revelation 10:5). **And Luke 22:69 is Jesus telling this same council that the Son of Man would be *καθήμενος*.** `[T]`
- **Rhetorical function in context.** It is the content of a vision reported by a man about to be executed, immediately after an indictment and immediately before a prayer. **It functions as vindication, not as instruction** — which narrows the plausible readings: whatever the standing means, it means something *for Stephen at that moment*.
- **The main interpretive options.** (i) **Rising as advocate** — the posture of a witness or counsel in court, answering the court that is condemning him; Luke 12:8's *ὁμολογήσει … ἐνώπιον τῶν ἀγγέλων* is the thematic support. (ii) **Rising to receive** — standing to welcome the first of the martyrs, which 7:59's *δέξαι τὸ πνεῦμά μου* fits. (iii) **Rising as judge** — about to act against the court. (iv) **No significance** — *ἵστημι* as a general verb of presence, the posture carrying no weight. (v) The Son of Man **as the one whose "coming" the council was told to expect**, seen in the act.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** **The distribution is real and it is not an accident of vocabulary** — Acts 2:34 has already quoted Psalm 110:1's *κάθου*, so Luke knows the seated formula and uses it. `[T]` That tells against option (iv). The perfect participle *ἑστῶτα* describes a state rather than a motion, which tells against reading it as *rising* in any literal sense — **the text says standing, not getting up** — and that is a real constraint on options (i)–(iii) as usually stated. Beyond that the text does not say, and **the preacher should resist the confident single answer.** *Confidence: high on the distribution; the explanation is genuinely contested and no option rises above moderate.*
- **Suggested handling.** **Make this the sermon's last movement and preach the fact before the explanation.** The fact — that the one at God's right hand is on his feet while his witness is being killed — preaches on its own. Then name the likeliest readings as readings. **The one thing not to do is to assert "he stood up to welcome him" as though the text said it.**

#### 7:51–53 — the indictment, and the charge of anti-Jewish preaching

- **Category:** pastoral and ethical, and it is the live one in a modern pulpit.
- **The difficulty.** Three verses of second-person accusation against a Jewish council, in a book with a long history of being misused against Jews.
- **Common mishandlings.** Preaching it as a description of Judaism rather than an address to a court; or, in the other direction, apologising it away until it says nothing.
- **Rhetorical function in context.** **It is a prophetic indictment in the prophets' own vocabulary** — *σκληροτράχηλος* is Exodus 32:9's and Deuteronomy 9:6's word for Israel, spoken by God through Moses; *ἀπερίτμητος* of the heart is Jeremiah 9:25 and Ezekiel 44:7; resisting the Spirit is Isaiah 63:10, which the overview records as already used at 7:51. `[T]` **Every term is one Israel's own Scriptures use of Israel, and the speaker is a Jew.**
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** This is intramural prophetic speech, and its closest formal parallel is Jesus' woes in Luke 11. `[T]` It is not a verdict on a people; it is an accusation against the men in front of him, and the proof of that is 7:51's *καὶ ὑμεῖς* — he is not saying "Jews are like this", he is saying "you are doing it now" (Tool 5a, Original Languages). **And the overview's trap 5 is in point: the book that contains this verse also reports *μυριάδες* of law-zealous Jewish believers (21:20).**
- **Suggested handling.** Preach it, in the second person, to the congregation. **The safeguard against misuse is not softening the words but getting the addressee right** — and the addressee the text models is the religiously serious insider, which in the room is us.

#### 7:59–60 — to whom does Stephen pray, and did he forgive?

- **Category:** doctrinal, and pastoral.
- **(a) The addressee.** 7:59's *κύριε Ἰησοῦ* is unambiguous and is **the New Testament's clearest instance of prayer addressed to Jesus** — which is a substantial Christological datum from the earliest period of the church. `[T]` *High confidence.* 7:60's *κύριε* alone is naturally the same addressee, but the Greek does not require it. **The variant at 7:55 is worth knowing: D h p saᵐˢˢ mae read *τὸν κύριον* for *δόξαν θεοῦ*** — the Western text makes the seeing more explicitly Christological still.
- **(b) The relation to Luke 23.** The parallel is real and it is not a copy. **Shared with Luke 23:46:** *φωνῇ μεγάλῃ*; *τὸ πνεῦμά μου*; and the closing formula *καὶ τοῦτο εἰπὼν* / *τοῦτο δὲ εἰπών* followed by a death verb. `[T]` **Differences, and they are the finding:** Jesus says *παρατίθεμαι* ("I entrust") to the Father; Stephen says *δέξαι* ("receive") to Jesus. **And the order is reversed** — Jesus prays for his executioners (Luke 23:34) and *then* commits his spirit (23:46); Stephen commits his spirit (7:59) and *then* prays for them (7:60). `[T]` *High confidence* on all of it.
- **The textual caveat the preacher needs.** **Luke 23:34's forgiveness prayer is itself textually disputed** — the SBLGNT brackets it, and it is omitted by a strong group of early witnesses. **So the closest parallel to Stephen's second prayer stands on a contested verse, while the parallel to his first (Luke 23:46) is secure.** If 23:34 is not original, the inversion-of-order finding weakens and the *πνεῦμα* parallel does not. *Moderate confidence* on the textual question; **say nothing from the pulpit that depends on Luke 23:34 without checking the pulpit text's footnote.**
- **(c) Did Stephen forgive?** The text reports a prayer: *μὴ στήσῃς αὐτοῖς ταύτην τὴν ἁμαρτίαν* — "do not set this sin to their account". **It does not report a feeling, and it does not say he forgave them.** `[T]` He asks God not to reckon it. *This is pastorally important* and is the fourth pitfall below.

#### 7:7 — "they shall serve me in this place": which place?

- **Category:** doctrinal, and it is the crux the Headline turns on.
- **The difficulty.** If Stephen's *τόπος* means the temple, the speech concedes the council's premise and 7:48 contradicts 7:7. If it means Sinai, the speech is coherent but Stephen has altered Scripture.
- **The main options.** (i) **Sinai**, from the source. (ii) **The land**, generalising. (iii) **The temple**, in which case Stephen grants the site and denies only its confinement of God. (iv) **Deliberate ambiguity** — the council's phrase used with the source's referent, so that the hearer who checks finds a mountain.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces.** The source has *ὄρει* and Stephen has *τόπῳ*, and both Greek editions and the Hebrew agree on the source. `[T]` **So the substitution is certain; the intention behind it is not.** Option (iv) accounts for the most: the phrase is the council's (6:13, 6:14), the referent is the source's, and the speech's later use of *τόπος* at 7:33 and 7:49 keeps pulling the word away from Jerusalem. **But (iii) is defensible**, and it has the merit of making 7:7 a concession that 7:48 then qualifies rather than contradicts. *Moderate* for (iv); *moderate* for (iii); the preacher should name the substitution and let it do its own work rather than adjudicating.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Starting at 7:51, or treating 7:2–50 as a preamble

- **What it looks like:** "Stephen gives a long summary of Israel's history, and then comes to the point in verse 51." The sermon covers the speech in two minutes and spends twenty on the indictment and the vision.
- **Why it's wrong:** the speech **is** the argument, and the indictment is its conclusion. 7:51's accusation has no force without the pattern 7:9, 7:25, 7:27 and 7:39 have established, and 7:48's *ἀλλά* — **the only strong adversative in the speech** — has nothing to be adversative to if 7:44–47 has not been preached. Structurally, the recital form (Tool 12) exists to earn the right to say the last thing.
- **The corrective:** preach the two-beat — **God acts outside the land; the brothers refuse the man he acts through** — from the Abraham section, so that 7:51 lands as the fourth instance rather than the first accusation. If time forces a choice, cut the historical detail, not the pattern.

### Pitfall 2: Making the sermon about Stephen's courage

- **What it looks like:** "Stephen shows us how to stand firm." The vision becomes the reward for faithfulness and the prayers become the proof of sanctity.
- **Why it's wrong:** the overview names this as the book's standing trap and names Stephen in it. The grammar of 7:55 puts the Spirit first and the courage fourth (Christological Reading, moralism check); and Luke 21:15's promise of *σοφία ᾗ οὐ δυνήσονται ἀντιστῆναι* is **a promise to believers on trial, not a technique** — which is to say the thing Stephen does well in 6:10 is the thing Jesus said he would be given.
- **The corrective:** ask the overview's own question of the unit — *who is the subject of the main verbs?* — and answer it out loud. Then let "be like Stephen" be the second thing the sermon says.

### Pitfall 3: Making 7:48 an attack on the temple, or on church buildings

- **What it looks like:** "Stephen says God does not live in buildings — so the temple was always a mistake"; or, in the modern register, a sermon against sanctuaries, architecture and institutions.
- **Why it's wrong:** three reasons, each from the text. (i) **Luke has marked the temple charge false** at 6:13 before it is made, so whatever Stephen said, it was not what they said he said. (ii) **The *ἀλλά* of 7:48 does not negate 7:47** — Solomon built him a house, *but* the Most High is not housed; the contrast is between building and containing. (iii) **Solomon said it first**, at the dedication (1 Kings 8:27, Greek). And in Acts' own narrative the apostles are in the temple daily (5:42) on the far side of this speech.
- **The corrective:** preach 7:48 as a claim about God, not about architecture — and name the vocabulary: *χειροποίητος* is the Greek Old Testament's word for idols, which is why 7:54 happens. The offence is real; it is just not the offence the sermon usually supplies.

### Pitfall 4: "Stephen forgave them"

- **What it looks like:** a sermon on forgiveness built on 7:60, with Stephen's serenity as the model, and often with an invitation to feel as he felt.
- **Why it's wrong:** **the text reports a prayer and not a disposition.** *μὴ στήσῃς αὐτοῖς ταύτην τὴν ἁμαρτίαν* asks God not to reckon the sin; it does not say Stephen had ceased to feel the injury, and Luke supplies no comment. Nor does Luke praise the prayer. **And pastorally this matters:** a hearer who has been seriously wronged, told that forgiveness looks like Stephen's composure, concludes that forgiveness is beyond them.
- **The corrective:** preach what the prayer asks. It is a request about God's books, and it can be prayed through clenched teeth. Then ground it where the text grounds it — in 7:55, and in the fact that the man who prayed it was looking at the one who prayed first.

### Pitfall 5: Asserting a single meaning for the standing Son of Man

- **What it looks like:** "He stood up to welcome Stephen home" — delivered as exegesis, usually with feeling.
- **Why it's wrong:** the distribution is a real finding (twelve seated, two standing, both here) but **the explanation is contested and the text does not give one.** The perfect participle describes a state, so even "stood up" is going beyond the Greek, which says *standing*. The welcome reading, the advocate reading and the judge reading are all defensible and none is stated.
- **The corrective:** preach the fact and name the readings as readings. The fact is enough: **the one at God's right hand is on his feet while his witness is being killed.**

### Pitfall 6: Treating the opponents as obviously wicked

- **What it looks like:** the council as cardboard villains, the reaction at 7:54 as motiveless malice.
- **Why it's wrong:** it is the quickest way to make 7:51–53 land on nobody (Original Audience, Implication for the sermon). These were the men charged with guarding what God had given, and Luke reports that they thought they had heard blasphemy. The sermon that cannot explain their anger cannot deliver their indictment.
- **The corrective:** make the case for the prosecution before answering it, and then put 7:51's second person where it belongs.
