## Headline Findings

The exegetically strongest claims that emerged across multiple tools. Every count and chain below was verified by lemma against the SBLGNT index, and the verification pass is recorded in the Text-First Declaration.

**1. The crowd's cry at 22:22 is the Servant Song's own phrase, and this closes a question the previous run had to leave open.** *αἴρω* with *ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* occurs **twice in the whole New Testament** `[T]`, verified by phrase search across all 27 books: **Acts 8:33**, inside the Isaiah 53:7–8 LXX citation that Philip read to the Ethiopian eunuch (*ὅτι **αἴρεται ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς** ἡ ζωὴ αὐτοῦ*), and **Acts 22:22** (*Αἶρε ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς τὸν τοιοῦτον*). The 21:1–36 run flagged this at moderate confidence and said explicitly that it "would be better tested in a run on 21:37–22:29"; it has now been tested and it holds. **The Jerusalem crowd demands Paul's removal in the words Isaiah used of the Servant whose life was taken away, and Luke has already put those words in the mouth of a man reading to a eunuch the Law excluded.** Surfaced by Tools 7, 10 and 11 (Moves 1 and 4) converging. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the design — the phrase is idiomatic Greek for judicial removal, which is the honest counterweight, but the two occurrences stand sixteen chapters apart in one author with no third instance anywhere in the NT.

**2. Three citizenships in three scenes, and the third is invisible in English.** Acts uses three words from the *πολι-* stem of civic membership, **each exactly once, and each in a different scene of this one pericope** `[T]`: *πολίτης* at **21:39** ("a **citizen** of no insignificant city" — Tarsus, offered to buy the right to speak), *πολιτεία* at **22:28** ("I acquired this **citizenship** with a large sum" — Rome, produced to stop the lash), and *πολιτεύομαι* at **23:1** (*πεπολίτευμαι τῷ θεῷ* — literally "I have discharged my citizenship toward God"). *πολίτης* occurs four times in the NT and once in Acts; *πολιτεία* twice in the NT (22:28; Eph 2:12); *πολιτεύομαι* twice (23:1; Phil 1:27). **Paul answers three different benches by naming three different citizenships, in ascending order, and the third one is the only one that is not a privilege.** Surfaced by Tools 3, 7 and 8. **Both NASB95 and ESV render 23:1 as "I have lived my life", which erases the word** — see Tool 8.

**3. Paul's self-portrait is assembled out of the Stephen material, by five independent lexical links.** `[T]` on every distribution:

- ***ἀνατρέφω*** ("to bring up") occurs **three times in the New Testament, all in Acts**: **7:20** and **7:21**, of Moses, in Stephen's speech, and **22:3**, of Paul. And the three-verb résumé Stephen gives Moses across 7:20–22 — *ἐγεννήθη* → *ἀνετράφη* → *ἐπαιδεύθη* — is the three-verb résumé Paul gives himself in a single clause at 22:3: *γεγεννημένος* → *ἀνατεθραμμένος* → *πεπαιδευμένος*. Those three lemmata stand together in one verse **once in the New Testament**, at 22:3.
- ***παρὰ τοὺς πόδας*** occurs in five Acts verses: money laid at the apostles' feet (4:35, 4:37, 5:2), the executioners' coats laid **at Saul's feet** (7:58), and Paul educated **at Gamaliel's feet** (22:3). **The only two men in Acts who have feet that things are laid at are Gamaliel and Saul**, and Paul puts both pairs into one speech seventeen verses apart.
- ***συνευδοκέω*** ("to consent, approve") occurs twice in Acts: **8:1**, Luke's narration of Saul at Stephen's killing, and **22:20**, Paul's first-person recollection of the same moment.
- ***μάρτυς*** with ***συνευδοκέω*** stands in **exactly two verses of the New Testament**: **Luke 11:48**, where Jesus tells the lawyers *μάρτυρές ἐστε καὶ **συνευδοκεῖτε*** to their fathers' killing of the prophets, and **Acts 22:20**, where Paul says of Stephen *τοῦ **μάρτυρός** σου* and of himself *ἤμην … **συνευδοκῶν***. The two terms have changed places: the man killed is now the witness, and the consenter is the one speaking.
- ***ὁ δίκαιος*** used absolutely of Jesus occurs three times in Acts — **3:14** (Peter), **7:52** (**Stephen**), **22:14** (Ananias, reported by Paul). **Paul's commission is to "see the Righteous One" — the title Stephen used in the sentence that got him killed, with Paul standing over the coats.**

Surfaced by Tools 7, 11 (Move 4) and 15 converging. `[I]`, high, that the assembly is deliberate: five links of this specificity in twenty verses is not coincidence, and 22:20 names Stephen outright, so the reader is being told where to look.

**4. The temple returns exactly once in this pericope, in Paul's own mouth, as the place where he was commissioned to the Gentiles.** *ἱερόν* occurs in 24 verses of Acts `[T]`. After the five-fold cluster of 21:26–30, in which Paul enters the temple, is seen in it, is accused of defiling it, is dragged out of it and the doors shut behind him, **the next occurrence in the book is 22:17** — *προσευχομένου μου ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ* — and it is the only one in this unit. The trance in which Paul is sent *εἰς ἔθνη μακράν* (22:21) happened **in the temple he is accused of profaning**, and the crowd listens *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* and no further (22:22). Surfaced by Tools 2, 3, 10, 11 and the Original Audience work. This also **corrects a claim in the neighbouring report** — see Book-Overview Tensions §3.

**5. The first of Luke's four official acquittals of Paul is spoken in the formula Pilate used twice of Jesus.** *οὐδέν* + *εὑρίσκω* + *ἐν τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ τούτῳ* occurs three times in Luke–Acts and nowhere else in the New Testament in that construction `[T]`, verified by collocation across all 27 books: **Luke 23:4** (*Οὐδὲν **εὑρίσκω** αἴτιον **ἐν τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ τούτῳ***), **Luke 23:14** (*οὐθὲν **εὗρον ἐν τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ τούτῳ** αἴτιον*), and **Acts 23:9** (*Οὐδὲν κακὸν **εὑρίσκομεν ἐν τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ τούτῳ***). The overview counts 23:9 as the first of the four verdicts of innocence on which the book's apologetic rests. **It is delivered by Pharisees, inside the Sanhedrin, in the Roman governor's own words from the Gospel.** Surfaced by Tools 7, 11 and the overview's presenting situation. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, that Luke intends the echo — the wording is not verbatim (Luke has *αἴτιον*, Acts has *κακόν*), and that difference should be stated rather than smoothed.

## Phase 0.6 — Positional Necessity Check

**Q1 — what has the preceding movement predicted, required, or set up that this passage now provides?**

Four things, and three of them are discharged inside this unit.

**(a) A speech from the steps.** 21:40 stands Paul *ἐπὶ τῶν ἀναβαθμῶν*. The 21:1–36 run established that *ἀναβαθμός* occurs **twice in the New Testament, at 21:35 and 21:40** `[T]` — the steps he was carried up become the steps he preaches from. The preceding pericope therefore ends one verse short of its own payoff, and this unit supplies it. **The place of his rescue is his pulpit**, and that is a positional fact, not a devotional one.

**(b) An answer to the charge of 21:28.** The crowd's accusation was threefold and specific: Paul teaches *κατὰ τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ νόμου καὶ τοῦ τόπου τούτου*. The speech answers all three in order — **the people** (he addresses them *τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ* and calls them *ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες*, 21:40–22:1), **the law** (*κατὰ ἀκρίβειαν τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου*, 22:3, and *κατὰ τὸν νόμον* of Ananias, 22:12), and **the place** (*προσευχομένου μου ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ*, 22:17). `[T]` on the three terms; `[I]`, high, that the speech is built to the shape of the charge. It is the strongest positional observation in the unit and the preacher should not miss it: **Paul's defence is not a testimony with an accusation nearby; it is a point-by-point rebuttal in the accusation's own vocabulary.**

**(c) The information the tribune could not get.** 21:34 left the tribune *μὴ δυνάμενος … γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλές διὰ τὸν θόρυβον*. 22:24 has him order a flogging *ἵνα ἐπιγνῷ δι' ἣν αἰτίαν*, and 22:30 has him convene the Sanhedrin *βουλόμενος γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλές*. **The whole second half of this unit is one Roman officer's three attempts to obtain the thing 21:34 said he could not obtain** — and 23:28–29 will show that what he finally writes to Felix is a summary of 23:9, not a finding of his own.

**(d) The *δέω* chain's next link.** 20:22's *δεδεμένος τῷ πνεύματι* ran through 21:11, 21:13 and 21:33 to this unit's 22:29 (*αὐτὸν ἦν δεδεκώς*) and 22:30 (*ἔλυσεν αὐτόν*). **22:29–30 is the only place in Acts where Paul is unbound**, and it happens because he is a Roman, three verses after he has been bound because nobody could tell who he was.

**Q2 — why does this passage exist here, and not somewhere else in the book?**

Because this is the last possible moment at which Paul can address **Israel** and **the Sanhedrin** as a free speaker, and Luke needs both on the record before any Roman magistrate hears the case. From 23:12 the narrative passes to a plot, a night march and a provincial court; Paul will speak four more times (24:10–21; 25:8–11; 26:2–23; 28:17–20) and **every one of them is to a Roman or a client king**. So 22:1–21 is the only speech in Acts addressed to a Jerusalem crowd by Paul, and 23:1–6 the only one addressed to the council. The unit is the hinge at which the audience changes permanently.

It also exists here because **the book's register has completed its change**. The overview records that chapter 21 is the first chapter in Acts where forensic vocabulary overtakes mission vocabulary and never properly reverses. This unit is where the forensic vocabulary arrives as a *system*: *ἀπολογία* (22:1), *ἀνετάζω* (22:24, 29), *ἀκατάκριτος* (22:25), *αἰτία* (22:24), *κατηγορέω* (22:30), *συνέδριον* (22:30; 23:1, 6), *κρίνω* (23:3, 6), *συνείδησις* (23:1), *στάσις* (23:7, 10). **Four of these are the word's first occurrence in Acts** `[T]`.

**Implication.** Reading the unit as "Paul's testimony, twice" misses its position. Positionally it is **the discharge of the accusation of 21:28 and of the tribune's unanswered question at 21:34** — and its last verse is the Lord settling, in the one word *δεῖ*, what the crowd and the council between them have failed to settle.

**Revisit after Tool 11.** Move 2 confirms and sharpens (b): Exodus is not a formula-source in Acts outside the seams, and the single citation here (23:5) functions as the **law** leg of the threefold answer. That the only OT quotation in the unit is a law protecting a ruler of the people, quoted by a man accused of teaching against the law and the people, is positional design rather than proof-texting.

## The Sixteen Tools

### 1. Author's Purpose

**Book-level.** Per the overview: to give a mixed church certainty that the risen Jesus is still acting under a purpose no opposition can obstruct, and that the Gentile mission is that purpose.

**Passage-level, and there are two purposes which pull against each other — as in the preceding unit, but differently.**

**The apologetic purpose dominates.** Luke puts on record, in this order: that Paul addressed Jerusalem in Hebrew and was heard in silence (21:40–22:2); that his Jewish credentials are impeccable and checkable (22:3–5, with the high priest and the whole *πρεσβυτέριον* named as witnesses); that his conversion was not a defection but a commission from *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν* (22:14); that the man who baptised him was *εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον* and attested by all the local Jews (22:12); that he is a Roman citizen by birth and was illegally bound (22:25–29); that he submits to the written law even under provocation (23:5); and that the only verdict actually pronounced in the unit is *οὐδὲν κακὸν εὑρίσκομεν* (23:9). **Every element is a rebuttal, and the sequence is cumulative.**

**The theological purpose is quieter and is carried by the speech's ending.** The Gentile commission is not presented as Paul's idea, a policy, or a consequence of Jewish rejection. It is presented as something the Lord said to him **in the temple**, against his own objection (22:19–20 — Paul argues back, citing his usefulness as a known persecutor), and as a command to **leave Jerusalem** (22:18). *οὐ παραδέξονταί σου μαρτυρίαν* — they will not accept your testimony — is the Lord's prediction, and 22:22 is its fulfilment inside the same scene. **Luke's theological claim is that the Gentile mission was commanded in the temple and refused at the temple steps, and that both the command and the refusal were foreseen.**

**The two purposes explain the pericope's one structural oddity:** Paul is never allowed to finish. The speech to the crowd is cut off at 22:22; the speech to the council is cut off at 23:2 after a single sentence; the second attempt (23:6) is a single sentence again, and the riot takes over. **Three truncations in forty-five verses** `[T]`. A purely apologetic writer would have given Paul a complete hearing somewhere. Luke gives him none until Agrippa.

### 2. Context

#### Positional Necessity Check *(see Phase 0.6 above)*

**Immediately before (21:1–36).** The ascent, the welcome, James's proposal, the unfinished rite, the riot, the arrest. The join is tight and verbal: *παρεμβολή* at 21:34 and 21:37; *ἀναβαθμός* at 21:35 and 21:40; the *λαός/ὄχλος* alternation of 21:35–36 continued below (Tool 7).

**Immediately after (23:12–35).** The plot of forty men, the nephew, the night ride to Caesarea, Claudius Lysias's letter. **The letter is the relevant join**: 23:29 has the tribune write *μηδὲν δὲ ἄξιον θανάτου … ἔχοντα ἔγκλημα* — which is the overview's second verdict of innocence, and it is a paraphrase of the Pharisees' 23:9, not of anything the tribune discovered. `[I]`, moderate–high.

**Boundaries.** The unit's opening boundary is secure: 21:37 begins with Paul's first words since 21:13 — he has been silent for twenty-four verses and is the object of nearly every verb in them. The closing boundary at 23:11 is **contestable and worth stating**. Three options:

| End | Case for | Case against |
|---|---|---|
| **23:10** | Closes the unit at the end of the Arc Map's M7; the riot-and-rescue pattern of 21:30–36 repeats exactly (crowd violence → tribune afraid → troops → *εἰς τὴν παρεμβολήν*), making a clean inclusio | Ends on a second failure, with the question of 21:34 still unanswered — a sermon with no resolution |
| **23:11** *(the unit as given)* | The night word answers everything the unit has raised: the crowd would not hear, the council could not judge, and the Lord says *δεῖ*. *μαρτυρῆσαι* answers the *μάρτυς* of 22:15 and 22:20 | Takes the first verse of M8, so the preaching unit and the structural panel overlap by one verse |
| **23:12** | Would include the plot, and the plot is what 23:11 is spoken against | Breaks the Sanhedrin scene's own closure and makes the unit a chapter-and-a-bit |

**This run endorses 23:11** and recommends saying why: Luke marks 23:11 with *Τῇ δὲ ἐπιούσῃ νυκτί*, a new-scene formula, and the verse looks **both ways** — *ὡς … διεμαρτύρω … εἰς Ἰερουσαλήμ* summarises the unit just ended, and *οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην* opens the one beginning. **It is a janus verse, which is exactly why a preaching unit and a structural panel can both legitimately claim it.** That observation resolves the apparent conflict rather than adjudicating it, and it belongs in the overview.

### 3. Structure

| Verses | Movement | Marker | Audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| 21:37–40 | **The tribune's mistake, and permission to speak** | *Μέλλων τε εἰσάγεσθαι* | the tribune |
| 22:1–5 | **Credentials: born, brought up, educated** | *Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες* | the people |
| 22:6–11 | **The road: light, voice, blindness** | *Ἐγένετο δέ μοι πορευομένῳ* | the people |
| 22:12–16 | **Damascus: Ananias, sight, baptism, commission** | *Ἁνανίας δέ τις* | the people |
| 22:17–21 | **The temple: the trance, the objection, the sending** | *Ἐγένετο δέ μοι ὑποστρέψαντι* | the people |
| 22:22–29 | **The explosion, and the citizenship** | *Ἤκουον δὲ αὐτοῦ ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* | the tribune |
| 22:30–23:10 | **The council: the wall, the law, the resurrection** | *Τῇ δὲ ἐπαύριον* | the Sanhedrin |
| 23:11 | **The night word** | *Τῇ δὲ ἐπιούσῃ νυκτί* | Paul alone |

**The binding device is a frame of four entries into the barracks.** *παρεμβολή* occurs **six times in Acts and all six fall between 21:34 and 23:32** `[T]` — 21:34, 21:37, 22:24, 23:10, 23:16, 23:32. Inside this unit it stands at 21:37 (about to be brought in), 22:24 (ordered in), and 23:10 (brought in). **The pericope opens with Paul on the threshold of the barracks and closes with him inside them, and each of his two speeches is bracketed by a march in that direction.** The structure is therefore not two speeches with incidents between; it is **two attempts to be heard, each ending in military custody, and then a word spoken in that custody.**

**Two chiastic frames sit inside that.**

The first is lexical and tight: *Ἑλληνιστὶ γινώσκεις;* (21:37) → *τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ* (21:40) → *τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ* (22:2) → the speech → the crowd's Greek-less roar (22:22–23) → *Ῥωμαῖος* (22:25–29). **Paul is taken for an Egyptian, speaks as a Hebrew, and is released as a Roman** — three identities in twenty-five verses, in the order of their usefulness.

The second is the *ἐφίστημι* triple, which is the unit's quietest structural signal. *ἐφίστημι* occurs in eleven Acts verses; **three of them are here** `[T]`: **22:13** (*Ἁνανίας … **ἐπιστὰς*** — standing over the blind Paul), **22:20** (*καὶ αὐτὸς ἤμην **ἐφεστώς*** — Paul standing over Stephen's killing), **23:11** (*ὁ κύριος **ἐπιστὰς** αὐτῷ* — the Lord standing over Paul in the barracks). **Three men stand over someone in this pericope: a disciple over a blind persecutor, the persecutor over a dying witness, and the Lord over a bound apostle.** `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the design. It is the sort of thing a preacher can hang a whole movement on, and no English version marks it, because all three are rendered differently.

**What the structure does not do.** It does not climax. The crowd scene fails, the council scene fails, and the resolution arrives from outside the room, at night, in one sentence. **Any structure a sermon imposes that builds to a triumph is working against this one.**

### 4. Linking Words

- ***δέ* (21:39) — the hinge of the whole exchange.** *Ἐγὼ ἄνθρωπος **μέν** εἰμι Ἰουδαῖος … **δέομαι δέ** σου.* The *μέν … δέ* sets the correction against the request: not an Egyptian but a Jew, **and therefore** let me speak to the people. Paul converts a misidentification into a platform in one sentence.
- ***ὅς* (22:4) — the relative that carries the whole argument.** *ζηλωτὴς ὑπάρχων τοῦ θεοῦ … **ὃς** ταύτην τὴν ὁδὸν ἐδίωξα.* The persecution is attached to the zeal by a relative pronoun, not by an adversative. **Paul does not say "though I was zealous, I persecuted"; he says the persecution was the zeal.** That is the sentence on which the whole speech's logic rests, and it is why 22:3's *καθὼς πάντες ὑμεῖς ἐστε σήμερον* is not flattery but diagnosis.
- ***διότι* (22:18)** — *ἔξελθε … **διότι** οὐ παραδέξονταί σου μαρτυρίαν.* The strong causal: the reason for leaving Jerusalem is stated as a fact about the hearers, not as a strategy. Luke then proves it at 22:22.
- ***ὅτι* (22:21)** — *Πορεύου, **ὅτι** ἐγὼ εἰς ἔθνη μακρὰν ἐξαποστελῶ σε.* The ground of the going is the Lord's own sending, in the emphatic *ἐγώ*. **The last word of the speech is a first-person divine verb**, and the crowd interrupts on it.
- ***γάρ* (22:22, 22:26, 23:8, 23:11)** — four explanatory grounds, and three of them explain a decision to act: *οὐ **γὰρ** καθῆκεν αὐτὸν ζῆν* (the crowd's warrant for killing), *ὁ **γὰρ** ἄνθρωπος οὗτος Ῥωμαῖός ἐστιν* (the centurion's warrant for stopping), *Σαδδουκαῖοι **μὲν γὰρ** λέγουσιν* (the narrator's warrant for the reader), *ὡς **γὰρ** διεμαρτύρω* (the Lord's warrant for Rome). **Every significant action in the second half of the unit is justified by a *γάρ*, and the last one is God's.**
- ***οὖν* (22:29)** — *εὐθέως **οὖν** ἀπέστησαν.* The one inferential connector in the unit, and it draws a legal conclusion: citizenship stated, therefore examination abandoned. Rome's logic is the only logic in the pericope that actually works.
- ***καί … καί* (23:3)** — *καὶ σὺ κάθῃ κρίνων με κατὰ τὸν νόμον, **καὶ** παρανομῶν κελεύεις.* The second *καί* is adversative in force and carries the charge: you sit **in accordance with** the law and command **in breach of** it. The whole rebuke is one antithesis built on a repeated conjunction.

### 5. Parallels

Not Hebrew poetic parallelism — this is narrative and reported speech. But the unit is built on **two sustained prose parallels**, and in both the second member inverts the first.

**(a) The two hearings.** Crowd and council are staged identically and fail differently:

| | The crowd (21:40–22:23) | The council (22:30–23:10) |
|---|---|---|
| Paul's posture | *ἑστὼς ἐπὶ τῶν ἀναβαθμῶν* (21:40) | *ἀτενίσας … τῷ συνεδρίῳ* (23:1) |
| Opening address | *Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες* (22:1) | *Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοί* (23:1) |
| First claim | what he **was** — born, reared, taught (22:3) | what he **has been** — *πεπολίτευμαι … ἄχρι ταύτης τῆς ἡμέρας* (23:1) |
| What stops him | a shout (22:22) | a blow (23:2) |
| Outcome | *εἰς τὴν παρεμβολήν* (22:24) | *εἰς τὴν παρεμβολήν* (23:10) |

**The parallel exists in order to show that the second body is worse than the first.** The crowd at least listens to twenty-one verses; the council strikes him in the mouth after one sentence. `[I]`, moderate–high.

**(b) Two men named Ananias, eleven verses apart.** See Tool 7. The parallel is exact in form — both are introduced with the name and a qualifier — and opposite in content: *ἀνὴρ εὐλαβὴς **κατὰ τὸν νόμον**, μαρτυρούμενος ὑπὸ πάντων* (22:12) against *ὁ ἀρχιερεὺς Ἁνανίας* who *κελεύεις* a blow **παρανομῶν** (23:2–3).

### 6. Narrator's Comment

Luke comments sparingly here — the unit is mostly speech — and every intrusion is doing work.

- **22:2** *Ἀκούσαντες δὲ ὅτι τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ προσεφώνει αὐτοῖς **μᾶλλον παρέσχον ἡσυχίαν***. The narrator tells the reader the *effect* of the language choice. *ἡσυχία* occurs four times in the NT and **once in Acts, here** `[T]`. He does not say the crowd was persuaded — only quieter.
- **22:9** *οἱ δὲ σὺν ἐμοὶ ὄντες τὸ μὲν φῶς ἐθεάσαντο τὴν δὲ φωνὴν οὐκ ἤκουσαν.* Strictly this is inside Paul's speech, so it is Paul's comment, not Luke's — which matters for the difficult-verse discussion, since the apparent contradiction with 9:7 is between **the narrator** and **the character**.
- **22:29** *καὶ ὁ χιλίαρχος δὲ **ἐφοβήθη** ἐπιγνοὺς ὅτι Ῥωμαῖός ἐστιν καὶ ὅτι αὐτὸν ἦν δεδεκώς.* The narrator supplies the officer's inner state and the legal reason for it. **The only fear reported in the unit belongs to the man in charge** — and it is the mirror of 16:38, where the Philippian magistrates *ἐφοβήθησαν* on hearing the same word.
- **23:8** *Σαδδουκαῖοι μὲν γὰρ λέγουσιν μὴ εἶναι ἀνάστασιν μήτε ἄγγελον μήτε πνεῦμα.* A decoder aside for a reader who might not know the parties. **This is the clearest case in Acts of Luke explaining a Jewish division to an audience that needs it explained**, and it is evidence about his readership rather than about the scene.
- **23:9** the sentence breaks off: *εἰ δὲ πνεῦμα ἐλάλησεν αὐτῷ ἢ ἄγγελος—* **Luke leaves the Pharisees' apodosis unwritten.** The aposiopesis is almost certainly deliberate, and the manuscript tradition proves that readers felt it: the Byzantine text supplies *μὴ θεομαχῶμεν* ("let us not fight against God") — **Gamaliel's own word from 5:39, where *θεομάχος* is a New Testament hapax** `[T]`. See Textual Variants (c).

**What the narrator does not do:** he does not tell us whether Paul knew Ananias was high priest; does not evaluate the Pharisee claim at 23:6; does not say whether the companions heard the voice; and does not report a single word of the crowd's reaction to anything in 22:3–21 until the last clause. **Four silences, and all four are the points a preacher most wants answered.** The same pattern the 21:1–36 run found, in the same proportion.

### 7. Vocabulary

Counts against the SBLGNT via the MorphGNT lemma index. Every chain below was re-verified in a single machine-checked pass (Text-First Declaration).

**πολίτης / πολιτεία / πολιτεύομαι (21:39; 22:28; 23:1).** See Headline 2. Each occurs **once in Acts** `[T]`. The ascent is the finding: a provincial city's franchise, an imperial citizenship bought for a large sum, and a citizenship exercised *τῷ θεῷ* with a clear conscience. **Note what Paul does *not* do with the second one.** At Philippi he produced his citizenship *after* the rods had fallen (16:37, *δείραντες ἡμᾶς δημοσίᾳ ἀκατακρίτους, ἀνθρώπους Ῥωμαίους ὑπάρχοντας*); at Jerusalem he produces it *before* the lash (22:25, the same two words, *ἄνθρωπον Ῥωμαῖον καὶ ἀκατάκριτον*). `[T]` on the sequence in both scenes and on the shared pair *Ῥωμαῖος* + *ἀκατάκριτος*; `[I]` on the significance — but the change is real and the preacher may make of it what the text allows: he had learned something, or Luke wants the reader to notice the difference.

**ἀνατρέφω / the three-verb résumé (22:3).** See Headline 3. *ἀνατρέφω* stands in **three New Testament verses, all in Acts: 7:20, 7:21, 22:3** `[T]`.

**παρὰ τοὺς πόδας (22:3).** Five Acts verses: 4:35, 4:37, 5:2, 7:58, 22:3 `[T]`. See Headline 3. **NASB95 drops the phrase at 22:3** ("educated under Gamaliel") and so loses the link; ESV keeps it. Tool 8.

**ἀκρίβεια (22:3) — a New Testament hapax, and it reaches back into the preface.** `[T]` The cognate adverb *ἀκριβῶς* stands five times in the NT, and in Luke–Acts **twice: Luke 1:3** (*ἔδοξε κἀμοὶ … **ἀκριβῶς** καθεξῆς σοι γράψαι*) **and Acts 18:25** (Apollos teaching *ἀκριβῶς* the things concerning Jesus). The comparative *ἀκριβέστερον* occurs four times, **all in Acts** (18:26; 23:15; 23:20; 24:22), and the superlative *ἀκριβεστάτην* once, at 26:5, of the Pharisaic sect. **So Luke's own word for the quality of his research is the word Paul uses for the quality of his Pharisaic training, and the word the conspirators use twice for the enquiry they are pretending to want (23:15, 20).** `[T]` on every distribution; `[I]`, moderate, on the irony being designed. This extends the *κατηχέω*–*ἀσφαλής* thread that the 21:1–36 run identified and that was checked in Logos on 11 October: 18:25 already held **both** of the preface's process words (*κατηχημένος* and *ἀκριβῶς*), and 22:3 now supplies the noun. **The preface promised accurate instruction leading to certainty; Acts gives us instruction that is accurate but incomplete (18:25), instruction that is false (21:21, 24), accuracy in the service of an ambush (23:15, 20), and certainty that no official can obtain (21:34; 22:30; 25:26).**

**πατρῷος (22:3).** Three New Testament verses, **all in Acts**: 22:3 (*τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου*), 24:14 (*τῷ πατρῴῳ θεῷ*), 28:17 (*τοῖς ἔθεσι τοῖς πατρῴοις*) `[T]`. **All three are Paul's defence vocabulary, and 22:3 is the first.** The ancestral law, the ancestral God, the ancestral customs: three defences in three settings, and the last is the second-to-last thing Paul says in the book.

**πρεσβυτέριον (22:5).** Three New Testament verses: **Luke 22:66**, Acts 22:5, 1 Tim 4:14 `[T]`. Luke 22:66 is the convening of the body that tried Jesus — *συνήχθη τὸ **πρεσβυτέριον** τοῦ λαοῦ*. **Paul's character reference for having persecuted Jesus' followers is the council that condemned Jesus**, and Luke uses the word for that body exactly twice in the two volumes. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, high, on the irony. Both English versions render it adequately ("Council of the elders" / "whole council of elders"), so the preacher can make the point from the English with the cross-reference supplied.

**ὁ δίκαιος (22:14).** Used absolutely of Jesus three times in Acts: 3:14, 7:52, 22:14 `[T]`. (The adjective is also used ordinarily at 4:19, 10:22 and 24:15 — a collision to keep apart.) See Headline 3.

**προχειρίζομαι (22:14).** Three New Testament verses, **all in Acts**: **3:20** (*τὸν **προκεχειρισμένον** ὑμῖν χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν* — the appointed Messiah), **22:14** (God *προεχειρίσατό σε* — Paul), **26:16** (*προχειρίσασθαί σε ὑπηρέτην καὶ μάρτυρα*) `[T]`. **The verb by which God appointed the Christ is the verb by which God appointed Paul**, and Luke uses it of nobody else. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate, on the weight — the verb is ordinary Hellenistic Greek for designating someone to an office, so the finding is the *restriction* of its Acts use to two appointees, not an exalted sense. **Note the cognate trap:** *προχειροτονέω* at 10:41 is a different lemma (the witnesses "previously chosen"), and a chain must not be built across the two.

**ἀπολούομαι (22:16).** Two New Testament verses: Acts 22:16 and 1 Cor 6:11 `[T]`. **The only place in Acts where baptism is described as washing away sins**, and the only other occurrence in the NT is Paul's own letter. Preachers who want Acts' theology of baptism must reckon with the fact that this clause stands in a **reported command from Ananias inside a defence speech**, not in Luke's narration.

**ἐπικαλέω with ὄνομα (22:16).** Five Acts verses `[T]`: 2:21 (the Joel citation, *πᾶς ὃς ἐὰν ἐπικαλέσηται τὸ ὄνομα κυρίου*), 9:14 and 9:21 (**Saul hunting *τοὺς ἐπικαλουμένους τὸ ὄνομά σου***), 15:17 (the Amos citation), 22:16 (*ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ*). **The man whose commission was to arrest the name-callers is commanded to become one.** The two Damascus occurrences (9:14, 9:21) are in the mouths of Ananias and of the astonished Damascene Jews respectively, so the irony is already planted at the first telling and detonated at the second. `[T]`; `[I]`, high.

**ἔκστασις (22:17).** Four Acts verses: 3:10, 10:10, 11:5, 22:17 `[T]`. **Two of the four are Peter's vision and his retelling of it**, and the Cornelius trance is what opens the Gentile mission in the first half of the book. **Paul's trance opens it in the second.** `[I]`, moderate–high: the parallel is structural rather than verbal beyond the one noun, but both men receive the Gentile commission in a trance and both then have to defend it to Jerusalem.

**μάρτυς / μαρτυρέω / διαμαρτύρομαι / μαρτυρία (22:5, 12, 15, 18, 20; 23:11).** The witness-family saturates the unit, and the senses slide `[T]`:

| Ref | Word | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| 22:5 | *μαρτυρεῖ μοι* | legal attestation (the high priest will vouch for me) |
| 22:12 | *μαρτυρούμενος ὑπὸ πάντων* | reputation (Ananias is well attested) |
| 22:15 | *ἔσῃ μάρτυς αὐτῷ* | commissioned gospel witness |
| 22:18 | *οὐ παραδέξονταί σου **μαρτυρίαν*** | testimony that is refused |
| 22:20 | *Στεφάνου τοῦ **μάρτυρός** σου* | witness unto death |
| 23:11 | *διεμαρτύρω … μαρτυρῆσαι* | the Lord's own naming of the task |

**Six occurrences of the family in forty-five verses, running from courtroom attestation to martyrdom and back to commission.** *μάρτυς* itself occurs in thirteen Acts verses, and 22:15 and 22:20 are five verses apart — the commission and the martyr in one speech. The manuscript tradition saw it: part of the tradition reads *πρωτομάρτυρος* at 22:20 (Textual Variants (b)).

**συνευδοκέω (22:20).** Two Acts verses, 8:1 and 22:20 `[T]`; with *μάρτυς*, two New Testament verses, Luke 11:48 and Acts 22:20 `[T]`. See Headline 3.

**ἀκούω and φωνή — the unit's Leitwort pair.** *ἀκούω* stands **eight times** and *φωνή* **four times** in 21:37–23:11 `[T]`: *ἀκούσατέ μου* (22:1), *Ἀκούσαντες δέ* (22:2), *ἤκουσα φωνῆς* (22:7), *τὴν φωνὴν οὐκ ἤκουσαν* (22:9), *ἀκοῦσαι φωνὴν* (22:14), *ὧν … ἤκουσας* (22:15), *Ἤκουον δὲ αὐτοῦ ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* (22:22), *ἀκούσας δὲ ὁ ἑκατοντάρχης* (22:26). **The pericope is organised around who hears and how far.** And the frame is two failures: 21:34's tribune who could not *γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλές* **because of the noise**, and 22:22's crowd who heard **up to one word**. The only person in the unit who hears and acts on it is a Roman centurion (22:26). `[T]` on the counts; `[I]`, high, on the design — the density is three times the book's rate for *ἀκούω* (89 occurrences across 1,002 verses).

**λαός against ὄχλος — the switch of 21:35–36 is maintained, and then the word is handed to the Law.** In 21:37–23:11, *λαός* occurs at **21:39, 21:40 and 23:5 only**, and ***ὄχλος* does not occur at all** `[T]` — its 22 Acts occurrences resume at 24:12. The 21:1–36 run found that Luke switched from *ὄχλος* to *λαός* in consecutive clauses at 21:35–36 so that the cry *Αἶρε αὐτόν* was raised by **the people**. This unit shows the switch was not a one-verse effect: **once Paul begins to speak, Luke drops *ὄχλος* entirely.** Paul asks to speak *πρὸς τὸν λαόν* (21:39) and signals *τῷ λαῷ* (21:40) — he addresses as "the people" the body that has just demanded his death — and the word's third and last appearance is inside the quotation of Exodus: *ἄρχοντα **τοῦ λαοῦ σου*** (23:5). **Three *λαός*: Paul asking to address the people, Paul signalling to the people, and Paul citing the Law about a ruler of the people — in the mouth of the man accused of teaching *κατὰ τοῦ λαοῦ* (21:28).** `[T]` on the distribution (positive control run: *ὄχλος* returns 22 Acts verses, *λαός* 48, so the search is sound); `[I]`, high, on the design.

**ἀτενίζω (23:1).** Ten Acts verses of fourteen in the New Testament, and **23:1 is the last** `[T]`. Three of the ten belong to the Stephen complex: **6:15** (the Sanhedrin *ἀτενίσαντες* at Stephen), **7:55** (Stephen *ἀτενίσας* into heaven), **23:1** (Paul *ἀτενίσας* at the Sanhedrin). **The council stared at Stephen; Paul stares at the council.** `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate.

**συνείδησις (23:1).** Two Acts verses: 23:1 and 24:16 `[T]` — and both are Paul's self-description under accusation. **Thirty occurrences in the NT, twenty-six of them in Paul's letters and Hebrews**, which is worth a sentence to a congregation: the word is Paul's, and Luke only ever puts it in his mouth.

**τύπτω (23:2, 3 ×2).** Five Acts verses, and **four of them are in chapters 18–23** `[T]`: 18:17 (Sosthenes beaten in front of Gallio), 21:32 (the soldiers stop the crowd *τύπτοντες* Paul), 23:2 (the high priest orders Paul's mouth struck), 23:3 (twice — *Τύπτειν σε μέλλει ὁ θεός* … *κελεύεις με τύπτεσθαι*). **The striking verb passes from a mob to a high priest to God inside three chapters.** Note the related *δέρω*, three Acts verses: **5:40** (the Sanhedrin beat the apostles), **16:37** (Rome beat Paul), **22:19** (Paul beat the believers) `[T]`. **Acts' three beatings are by the council, by Rome, and by Paul** — and Paul reports the third himself.

**κονιάω / κονιορτός (23:3; 22:23).** *κονιάω* stands in **two New Testament verses: Matt 23:27 and Acts 23:3** `[T]` — and **Luke's Gospel does not have the word**, so this is *not* a Luke–Acts echo and must not be reported as one. *κονιορτός* ("dust") stands in five NT verses and **two in Acts: 13:51 and 22:23** `[T]` — **13:51 is Paul and Barnabas shaking the dust off their feet against Pisidian Antioch, the gesture Jesus prescribed (Luke 9:5; 10:11); 22:23 is the Jerusalem crowd flinging dust into the air against Paul.** Same noun, different gesture, opposite direction. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate, on the inversion — the honest caveat is that throwing dust in the air and shaking it off the feet are different acts, and the shared element is dust as the sign of repudiation. The two *κονι-* words standing eleven verses apart is noted as a root-level observation at **low** confidence under the paronomasia ceiling, not as a finding.

**παρανομέω (23:3) — a New Testament hapax** `[T]`. **The only person in the New Testament described as acting *παρανομῶν* — in breach of law — is the serving high priest, and the word is used by the man on trial for teaching apostasy from the law** (*ἀποστασίαν … ἀπὸ Μωϋσέως*, 21:21, itself a near-hapax: two NT occurrences, one in Acts). The charge is answered by being returned.

**Ἁνανίας — three men of that name in Acts, and two of them are in this pericope** `[T]`. Ten Acts verses: 5:1, 5:3, 5:5 (the Jerusalem disciple who lies to the Spirit and dies); 9:10, 9:12, 9:13, 9:17, 22:12 (the Damascus disciple); 23:2, 24:1 (the high priest). **The name means "the LORD has been gracious."** Within eleven verses Luke gives us the Ananias who is *εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον* and attested by every Jew in Damascus, and the Ananias who holds the highest office in the law and is accused of *παρανομῶν*. `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the juxtaposition being usable. **Caution for the preacher:** nothing in the text comments on the name-sharing, and three men with a common name in one book is not by itself a literary device. What is a finding is the **functional** contrast, which the text does mark, at 22:12 and 23:3.

**ἀναστατόω (21:38) and ἐξάγω εἰς τὴν ἔρημον (21:38).** *ἀναστατόω* stands in three NT verses and **two in Acts: 17:6 and 21:38** `[T]` — the Thessalonian charge against the missionaries (*οἱ τὴν οἰκουμένην ἀναστατώσαντες*) and the tribune's guess about the Egyptian. More striking: ***ἐξάγω* with *ἔρημος* occurs in two Acts verses — 7:36 and 21:38** `[T]`. **7:36 is Stephen on Moses** (*οὗτος **ἐξήγαγεν** αὐτούς … ἐν τῇ **ἐρήμῳ** ἔτη τεσσεράκοντα*); **21:38 is the tribune on the Egyptian** (*ἀναστατώσας καὶ **ἐξαγαγὼν εἰς τὴν ἔρημον***). **The tribune's mistaken identification describes Paul in the vocabulary Stephen used of Moses — and Paul is about to be accused of teaching apostasy from Moses.** `[T]` on the collocation; `[I]`, moderate, on the design, since a wilderness-leader is a stock figure and the collocation is natural Greek for it. But the restriction to two Acts verses is a fact, and the juxtaposition is Luke's.

**σικάριος (21:38), ἔδαφος (22:7), διαμάχομαι (23:9) — three New Testament hapaxes in one pericope** `[T]`, with *ἀκρίβεια* and *παρανομέω* making five. **ἀνετάζω (22:24, 29)** is a sixth item of the same kind: two NT occurrences, **both here** `[T]`.

**θαρσέω (23:11) and θάρσος (28:15).** *θαρσέω* occurs in seven New Testament verses and **Acts 23:11 is its only occurrence in Luke–Acts — Luke's Gospel never uses it** `[T]` (Matthew ×3, Mark ×2, John ×1). The cognate noun *θάρσος* is a New Testament hapax at **28:15**, where Paul sees the brothers at the Forum of Appius and *ἔλαβε θάρσος* `[S: sweep]`, confirmed here by lemma `[T]`. **Two courage-words in Luke–Acts, one at each end of the road to Rome, and the first is the Lord's.** This is earned agreement with the sweep, which had the pair; what this run adds is that *θαρσέω* is absent from Luke's Gospel, so the Lord's *Θάρσει* to Paul is the only time in two volumes that Luke writes the word.

**ἡσυχία / ἡσυχάζω.** *ἡσυχία* is four NT verses, **one in Acts (22:2)** `[T]`; *ἡσυχάζω* is two Acts verses, **11:18 and 21:14** `[T]`. **Acts has three quietings and all three are objections ceasing**: the Jerusalem circumcision party falls silent and glorifies God (11:18), Paul's companions stop arguing and pray Gethsemane's prayer (21:14), and the Jerusalem crowd goes quieter at the sound of Hebrew (22:2). **The third is the only one that does not hold.** `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate.

**ἁρπάζω (23:10).** Two Acts verses: **8:39** (*πνεῦμα κυρίου **ἥρπασεν** τὸν Φίλιππον*) and **23:10** (*ἁρπάσαι αὐτὸν ἐκ μέσου αὐτῶν*) `[T]`. **The Spirit snatches Philip away; the Roman army snatches Paul away.** `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate — the verb is common enough outside Acts that the pairing is suggestive rather than demonstrative, and it should be offered as such.

**Two lemma collisions to avoid.** (a) *ἀναιρέω* at **7:21** means "took up, adopted" (Pharaoh's daughter *ἀνείλατο* the infant Moses), not "killed" — its nineteen other Acts occurrences, including 22:20's *τῶν ἀναιρούντων αὐτόν*, are the killing sense. **A chain built across 7:21 and 22:20 would be a mistranslation dressed as a thread.** (b) *μεσημβρία* at **8:26** most probably means "south" (the road to Gaza) and at 22:6 means "noon"; the two are the word's only NT occurrences, which makes the pair look like a chain and is not one.

**Ancient versions check.** The one rendering on which NASB95, ESV and KJV all agree and which carries real exegetical weight is **23:5**'s *γέγραπται γὰρ ὅτι* — all three treat it as introducing a scriptural quotation of Exodus, and so does the Vulgate (*scriptum est enim*). **No ancient-versions split here**; the Greek is unambiguous and the quotation is verbatim from the LXX apart from number and word order (Tool 11). **One item worth naming rather than a split:** the LXX of Exod 22:28 has *Θεοὺς οὐ κακολογήσεις* as its first clause, and the Vulgate renders it *diis non detrahes*; Paul quotes only the second clause. That omission is a feature of the citation, not of any version (Tool 11, Move 1).

**Pulpit divergence note.** N/A — no pulpit text declared. **But record this for the engagement if it comes:** the standing caution carried across the 20:17–38 and 21:1–36 runs was that **the ESV varies its rendering for English readability at precisely the points where Luke's verbal repetition carries the structure.** This pericope **cuts the other way twice** — at 22:3 (*παρὰ τοὺς πόδας*) and 22:14 (*φωνήν*) it is **NASB95** that breaks the chain and the ESV that keeps it, while at 21:40/22:2 (*διάλεκτος*) the usual pattern holds. **The caution should therefore be restated as a habit to check rather than a property of one version**, which is a correction to the way the previous two runs framed it.

### 9. Tone and Feel

**Three registers, and the transitions are abrupt.**

**21:37–22:2 is almost comic, and then suddenly not.** A man being carried into a barracks by soldiers asks, with formal courtesy, *Εἰ ἔξεστίν μοι εἰπεῖν τι πρὸς σέ;* The tribune's reply is not an answer but a surprised question — *Ἑλληνιστὶ γινώσκεις;* — followed by a wildly wrong guess involving four thousand assassins. **The scene's humour is the officer's, and it is at his own expense.** Then the crowd falls silent and the register changes inside one verse.

**22:3–21 is the warmest passage of Pauline speech in Acts.** The vocabulary is kinship and locality: *ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες*, *ἐν τῇ πόλει ταύτῃ*, *τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου*, *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν*, *Σαοὺλ ἀδελφέ*. **Paul addresses the men trying to kill him as family four times, and twice has others address him that way inside his own narrative.** The speech's one moment of resistance is Paul arguing with the Lord (22:19–20) — and the argument is that his own notoriety as a persecutor makes him *useful* in Jerusalem. **It is the most personally revealing sentence Paul speaks in Acts**, and it is the sentence immediately before the one that gets him silenced.

**22:22–23:10 is violent and claustrophobic.** Shouting, cloaks thrown off, dust in the air, thongs, a blow to the mouth, a *κραυγὴ μεγάλη*, a sentence that breaks off mid-clause, and the fear that Paul will be *διασπασθῇ* — torn apart. **The spatial movement is relentlessly inward and downward**: from the open steps, into the barracks, out again to a council chamber, and back into the barracks.

**23:11 is quiet, and it is the only moment of relief in the unit.** One sentence, at night, to one man, with no audience and no narration of Paul's response. Luke does not say that Paul was comforted. **He reports the word and stops** — exactly as he reported the Gethsemane prayer at 21:14 and stopped.

**Soundtrack.** For 22:3–21, a single voice unaccompanied. For 22:22–23:10, percussion with no melody. For 23:11, one sustained low note and silence. **The pericope's emotional architecture is a diminuendo interrupted twice by noise**, and a sermon that rises steadily through it is fighting the text.

**One note the preacher will feel tempted to soften.** The speech fails. Twenty-one verses of patient, courteous, autobiographical self-defence to his own people, and the result is a demand for his execution. **Luke lets that stand without consolation for sixteen verses.** The consolation, when it comes, is not that the speech worked.

### 10. Repetition

- ***ἀκούω* ×8, *φωνή* ×4** — the Leitwort pair; see Tool 7. **Three times a voice is heard and once it is not.**
- ***παρεμβολή* ×3 within the unit** (21:37; 22:24; 23:10), six times in Acts and all six between 21:34 and 23:32 `[T]`. The barracks is the unit's gravitational centre.
- ***Ῥωμαῖος* ×4 in five verses** (22:25, 26, 27, 29), out of ten Acts occurrences `[T]`. The word is repeated until it works. **Each repetition is in a different mouth**: Paul's, the centurion's, the tribune's, and the narrator's.
- ***ἐφίστημι* ×3** (22:13, 20; 23:11) — three standers; see Tool 3.
- ***τύπτω* ×3 in two verses** (23:2, 3 ×2) `[T]`.
- ***Ἑβραΐς διάλεκτος* ×2 in three verses** (21:40; 22:2) — Luke says it, then says the crowd heard it. **The only time in Acts a speech's language is named twice**; 26:14 names it a third time, of the voice on the road.
- ***ἀδελφοί* ×5** (22:1, 5, 13; 23:1, 5) `[T]` — and the distribution is the point: Paul calls the crowd *ἀδελφοί*, says he carried letters *πρὸς τοὺς ἀδελφούς* to arrest them, is called *ἀδελφέ* by Ananias, calls the Sanhedrin *ἀδελφοί*, and calls them *ἀδελφοί* again while apologising. **Five brotherhoods, and two of them are to men who have just struck or shouted at him.**
- ***γίνομαι* + *ἐγένετο* as scene-openers** — *Ἐγένετο δέ μοι* at 22:6 and 22:17, the two visions; *ἐγένετο στάσις* 23:7 and *ἐγένετο κραυγή* 23:9, the two eruptions. **The same verb opens the unit's two revelations and its two riots.** `[T]`; `[I]`, moderate.
- ***ἀναστάς / ἀνάστηθι*** (22:10, 16) — twice in the reported commands, and the matching imperative at 9:6 and 26:16 `[T]`. The rising is what Paul is told to do at every telling.
- **Near-repetition worth flagging:** *ἐπιγνῷ* (22:24) and *ἐπιγνούς* (22:29) and *γνῶναι τὸ ἀσφαλές* (22:30) — **three knowledge-verbs for the tribune in seven verses, and the one he gets is the wrong fact.** He learns Paul is a Roman; he never learns what Paul is accused of.

**Exodus 22:28 LXX → Acts 23:5** *(high confidence — direct quotation with the formula *γέγραπται γάρ*)* `[T]`

**Numbering note first, because it is a trap.** The verse is **Exod 22:28 in the LXX and the English versions, 22:27 in the Hebrew**. Swete's Exodus numbers it 22:28, matching the English, so no Joel-style offset applies here — but a Hebrew citation of this verse must be given as 22:27 `[T]`, and the overview's map (which says "Exodus 22:28") is following the Greek, correctly for a Greek citation.

**The wording, side by side** (Swete against SBLGNT):

| | |
|---|---|
| **Exod 22:28 LXX** | *Θεοὺς οὐ κακολογήσεις, καὶ **ἄρχοντας τοῦ λαοῦ σου οὐ κακῶς ἐρεῖς**.* |
| **Acts 23:5** | ***Ἄρχοντα τοῦ λαοῦ σου οὐκ ἐρεῖς κακῶς**.* |

Three differences, all small and all worth noticing `[T]`: Paul **singularises** *ἄρχοντας* to *ἄρχοντα*, to fit the one man in front of him; he **inverts** *οὐ κακῶς ἐρεῖς* to *οὐκ ἐρεῖς κακῶς*; and **he quotes only the second clause.** The first clause — *Θεοὺς οὐ κακολογήσεις*, "you shall not revile gods/God" — he omits.

*Source context (Move 1).* Exodus 22:28 sits in the civil ordinances of the Book of the Covenant (Exod 21–23), and its immediate neighbours are not about rulers at all. Immediately before (22:25–27) is the law of the poor man's cloak taken in pledge: it must be returned before sunset, *ἔστιν γάρ τοῦτο περιβόλαιον αὐτοῦ … ἐν τίνι κοιμηθήσεται;* — and the clause that closes it is God's own: *ἐὰν οὖν καταβοήσῃ πρὸς μέ, εἰσακούσομαι αὐτοῦ· ἐλεήμων γάρ εἰμι*. Immediately after (22:29–31) are the firstfruits and the firstborn, and then *ἄνδρες ἅγιοι ἔσεσθέ μοι*. **So the verse Paul quotes stands between a law about hearing the cry of a man who has been stripped and a law about being a holy people.** `[T]` on the context; `[I]`, moderate, on whether Paul intends any of it. **He is quoting a one-clause rule, and nothing in 23:5 invites the frame.** The honest report is that the frame exists and the preacher should not lean on it: this is the one place in the unit where a triad's Move 1 yields atmosphere rather than argument, and saying so is part of the method.

*Book usage (Move 2).* **Exodus is a live source in Acts but not a citational one outside this verse.** Its four significant uses — 6:7, 7:17, 12:24, 19:20 — are the seam-notices built on Exod 1:7 LXX, and they carry **no citation formula at all**. Stephen's speech works extensively from Exodus narrative (7:17–44) but quotes it only at 7:33–34 (Exod 3:5–10) and 7:40 (Exod 32:1), both inside reported speech. **Acts 23:5 is therefore the only place in the book where Exodus is quoted with a formula, and it is the last *γέγραπται* in Acts** — the formula occurs at 1:20, 7:42, 13:33, 15:15 and 23:5 `[T]`. **The book's final appeal to a written text is Paul submitting to a law about speech.** `[T]` on the distribution; `[I]`, moderate–high, on the significance.

*OT-to-OT (Move 3).* No second OT citation stands in this passage, so there is no pairing to assess inside it. But one OT-to-OT relation inside the cited verse is worth recording: **Exod 22:28's two clauses yoke God and the ruler of the people in a single prohibition**, and that yoking is what the bystanders' objection at 23:4 appeals to — *Τὸν ἀρχιερέα **τοῦ θεοῦ** λοιδορεῖς;* **They cite the connection; Paul cites the clause.** `[I]`, moderate.

*Internal (Move 4) — and this is where the citation pays.* ***ἄρχων* with *λαός* occurs in two Acts verses: 4:8 and 23:5** `[T]`. At **4:8** Peter, filled with the Spirit, addresses this same body as *Ἄρχοντες **τοῦ λαοῦ** καὶ πρεσβύτεροι*. At **23:5** Paul quotes the Law's *ἄρχοντα **τοῦ λαοῦ** σου*. **The phrase by which Peter named the Sanhedrin in Acts 4 is the phrase Scripture uses to protect it in Acts 23** — and Luke 22:66 and Luke 23:13 have the same cluster (*τὸ πρεσβυτέριον τοῦ λαοῦ*; *τοὺς ἄρχοντας καὶ τὸν λαόν*) in the trial of Jesus. `[T]` on the distributions; `[I]`, moderate–high.

*What the full triad adds.* Not a theological argument but a **forensic** one, and that is the point. The quotation's function is to demonstrate, in the hearing of the body judging him, that **the man accused of teaching apostasy from Moses corrects himself out of Moses** — and he does it in the book's last *γέγραπται*, about the very institution that is striking him. **The citation is Paul's third answer to the threefold charge of 21:28** (people, law, place): the law leg, discharged by quotation.

**Isaiah 53:8 LXX → Acts 22:22** *(moderate–high confidence — verbal allusion by a two-occurrence phrase)* — phrase `[T]`; allusion `[I]`

See Headline 1. *αἴρω* + *ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* stands in **two New Testament verses, Acts 8:33 and Acts 22:22** `[T]`, verified by phrase search over all 27 books.

*Source context (Move 1).* Isaiah 53:7–8 LXX is the passage the eunuch is reading aloud when Philip reaches the chariot: the lamb led to slaughter, the one who opens not his mouth, *ἐν τῇ ταπεινώσει ἡ κρίσις αὐτοῦ ἤρθη* — his judgement was taken away in his humiliation — *τὴν γενεὰν αὐτοῦ τίς διηγήσεται; ὅτι αἴρεται ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς ἡ ζωὴ αὐτοῦ*. **The clause is about a judicial killing in which due process is the thing denied.** Philip's exposition (8:35) begins from that verse and preaches Jesus. **The source context is therefore not merely "the suffering servant" but specifically *a trial that is not a trial*** — which is precisely what 22:22–24 is: a verdict shouted by a crowd and a flogging ordered to supply the charge afterwards.

*Book usage (Move 2).* Isaiah 53:7–8 is used once in Acts, at 8:32–33, and the overview marks it live and high-confidence. **It is the only Servant Song quoted in Acts with its text given**; the other two Servant uses (Isa 49:6 at 13:47, Isa 42:6–7 behind 26:18) are transferred commissions rather than quotations. The overview's stated pattern is that **the Servant's role moves outward — to Jesus, to the missionaries, to Paul.** `[I]`, moderate–high: if the pattern holds, then 22:22's reuse of the Servant's own removal-phrase of **Paul** is the pattern's next step, and it arrives not as a commission but as a cry for his death. **The role has moved outward one stage further than the overview's map records, and it has moved into the Servant's suffering rather than his commission.**

*OT-to-OT (Move 3).* No second citation in the passage. Noting one relation inside the source: Isaiah 53:8's *κρίσις* ("judgement") is the word the LXX uses for the process denied the Servant, and Acts' forensic vocabulary in this unit (*κρίνω* 23:3, 6; *ἀκατάκριτος* 22:25) runs on the same root `[T]`. `[I]`, low–moderate — the root is too common to carry weight on its own, and it is offered as corroboration, not evidence.

*What it adds.* Three things. **(a)** The crowd's cry is not generic abuse; in Luke's Greek it is the Servant Song's phrase, and Luke has already told the reader what that phrase means and who it is about (8:32–35). **(b)** It explains why the unit's injustice is narrated so exactly — the illegality of the flogging (22:25), the illegality of the blow (23:3), the breaking-off of the verdict (23:9) — because the source text is about denied *κρίσις*. **(c)** It tells against the sweep's framing of this unit as Luke's apologetic alone: **the apologetic is real, and it is being conducted in the vocabulary of a man whose innocence was never recognised either.**

*Honest counterweight, stated rather than buried.* **This allusion was not previously identified in the Acts work and no secondary source has been consulted on it.** *αἴρω ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς* is idiomatic Greek for judicial removal, so a single shared prepositional phrase is thinner evidence than the Numbers 6 register-use or the Luke 23:27 pair that the 21:1–36 run established. What raises it above coincidence is (i) that there is **no third occurrence anywhere in the New Testament**, (ii) that both occurrences are in **one author**, and (iii) that the 8:33 occurrence is a **quotation Luke has expounded**, so the phrase is already loaded in his own book. **Confidence is moderate–high, not high, and it is flagged for a Logos cross-reference check.**

### 12. Genre

**Historical narrative containing two embedded forensic speeches**, which is the combination the overview's Trap 4 warns about. Three reading rules follow.

**(a) The speeches are *apologia*, and Luke says so.** *ἀπολογία* at 22:1 is the first of its two Acts occurrences `[T]`; the other is **25:16**, where Festus explains Roman procedure — *πρὶν ἢ ὁ κατηγορούμενος … τόπον τε **ἀπολογίας** λάβοι*. **The word Paul uses of his speech to a Jerusalem mob is the word Rome uses for the right of reply.** So the genre is not testimony-as-evangelism but defence-as-testimony, and the content follows the genre: credentials, narrative of the facts, and the legal point last.

**(b) It is autobiography inside a trial, which means selection is governed by audience.** This is where the three-accounts question becomes a genre question rather than a harmonisation problem. See Tool 13 and Difficult Verses.

**(c) 23:11 is a different sub-genre again** — a dominical appearance-and-commission, in the form Acts uses four times (16:9; 18:9; 23:11; 27:23) and always to redirect the mission `[T]`. **Luke does not describe it as a vision or a dream**; he writes *ἐπιστὰς αὐτῷ ὁ κύριος εἶπεν*, which is the same construction he uses of the angel at 12:7. **The sub-genre's reading rule is that it settles things the narrative has left open**, and here it settles two: whether the Jerusalem testimony counted (it did — *διεμαρτύρω*), and where Paul goes next.

### 13. Copycat

**The tribune** — descriptive. His errors are the narrative's engine: he guesses wrong about the Egyptian, orders an illegal flogging, and convenes a council he cannot control. **Luke does not condemn him**, and at 23:10 his intervention saves Paul's life for the second time in two chapters. *Rome in this unit is incompetent and indispensable at once*, which is the book's settled view of it.

**The centurion (22:26)** — descriptive, but the one figure in the unit who **hears and acts**. He is also the only person who obeys a law in the pericope. Worth a sentence; not a model.

**The crowd** — **negative example**, and the text supplies the verdict: they listen *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* (22:22). **The thing they could not hear was that God sent a Jew to the Gentiles.** That is not a general lesson about closed minds; it is the specific refusal the whole book is written to answer.

**The Sanhedrin** — negative, and more sharply than the crowd. The high priest's order at 23:2 is the only act in the pericope the text explicitly calls lawless.

**The Pharisaic scribes (23:9)** — **ambiguous, and Luke leaves them so.** They acquit Paul and they do it for a bad reason (party advantage) and they do it in Pilate's words. **Luke counts their verdict** — the overview's four acquittals begin here — without endorsing their motive.

**Paul** — the hard case, and three decisions need labelling separately:

| Act | Label | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Speaking in Hebrew to win a hearing (21:40) | **normative pattern**, narrowly | meeting hearers in their own language, at cost to himself; the NT commends the underlying principle elsewhere (1 Cor 9:19–23), so the virtue, not the tactic, transfers |
| Giving his testimony as *apologia* (22:3–21) | **normative pattern** | testimony under pressure, structured as before-and-after with the risen Christ at the centre, is the one thing Acts shows every witness doing |
| Invoking Roman citizenship (22:25) | **descriptive** | and note 16:37 for the variation. Using a legal protection is neither commanded nor forbidden; it is reported |
| Calling the high priest a whitewashed wall and then quoting the law (23:3–5) | **descriptive, and contested** | see Difficult Verses. Luke neither commends nor criticises |
| Dividing the council on the resurrection (23:6) | **descriptive, and the most contested act in the unit** | see Difficult Verses |

**The unique-event category applies to nothing in this unit except the Damascus road itself** — which is reported, not re-enacted. **That is worth saying because the pericope's temptation is the opposite of the usual Acts temptation.** The usual danger is making a one-off into a template (the overview's Trap 1). Here the danger is making a *pattern* into a one-off: concluding that because Paul's conversion was unique, his *manner of giving account of it* is also unique and therefore inapplicable. **The manner is the one thing here that does transfer.**

### 14. Bible Timeline

Spring, within days of Pentecost (20:16), roughly twenty-five years after the ascension in Acts' internal reckoning. **In narrative terms this unit is the last time Paul stands in a public space as a free man**: from 23:12 he is moved under guard, and the remaining five chapters are custody, hearings, a voyage and a rented room.

In redemptive-historical terms the unit sits at a joint the whole book has been approaching and that the 21:1–36 run named: **the point at which the temple and the Gentile mission become, in practice, incompatible.** This unit adds the reason. **The incompatibility is not doctrinal — Paul is in the temple praying when he is commissioned (22:17) — it is that the commission cannot be heard there.** The doors shut at 21:30; the word *ἔθνη* at 22:21 is where the hearing stops.

**Where the reader stands.** This side of the cross and of Pentecost, with the mission to the nations an accomplished fact and the temple gone. **The thing the crowd could not hear is the thing the reader takes for granted**, and that gap is the single most useful timeline observation for preaching this text: a modern congregation hears 22:21 as unremarkable and 22:22 as inexplicable, and the original proportions were the reverse.

### 15. Who Am I?

**Paul** is not the reader. He is, in this unit, **a witness under examination** — and the three roles the text assigns him are *μάρτυς* (22:15), *Ῥωμαῖος* (22:25–29) and *Φαρισαῖος* (23:6), of which only the first is one any reader shares.

**The crowd is the most dangerous identification to get wrong.** They are devout, zealous for the law, in the right city at the right festival, and they listen attentively to a twenty-one-verse speech. **They are not a mob of the irreligious; they are the congregation.** Luke's own verdict on their state is 22:3's *ζηλωτὴς ὑπάρχων τοῦ θεοῦ **καθὼς πάντες ὑμεῖς ἐστε σήμερον*** — Paul says their zeal is the thing he had, and the thing that made him a persecutor. **The identification the text offers the hearer is with the crowd, by way of Paul's own past**, and it is offered as a warning.

**Stephen is present throughout and never as an example to imitate.** He appears as **the one who was right**, named by Paul as *ὁ μάρτυς σου* and invoked in a sentence of self-accusation. Note what Paul does *not* do: he does not hold Stephen up, draw a lesson from him, or compare himself to him. **He confesses him.**

**Ananias of Damascus** is the unit's quiet model, and the one figure a congregation can be pointed to without distortion: a man of no office, *εὐλαβὴς κατὰ τὸν νόμον*, who went to a known persecutor, called him *ἀδελφέ*, and told him what God had appointed him to do. **In 22:14–16 the whole commission is delivered by a layman.** Luke's 9:13–16 shows what that cost him.

**The tribune and the centurion** are foils — they exist to show what the religious bodies cannot do. **The council cannot reach a verdict and the crowd cannot hear a sentence; a Roman junior officer hears four words and stops an illegal flogging.**

**God's part, and it is the answer to the moralism trap.** The acting subjects of the main verbs are worth listing, because the overview's Trap 2 corrective is exactly this question. **God appoints** (*ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν προεχειρίσατό σε*, 22:14). **God will strike** (*Τύπτειν σε μέλλει ὁ θεός*, 23:3 — and note this is a prediction Luke never reports fulfilled). **The Lord sends** (*ἐγὼ … ἐξαποστελῶ σε*, 22:21). **The Lord stands over him and says what must be** (23:11). **Paul's own main verbs in the unit are overwhelmingly verbs of speech and of being acted upon**, and the three times he acts decisively — speaking in Hebrew, naming his citizenship, naming the resurrection — the outcome is not deliverance but another room.

### 16. So What?

**Stage 1 — the response Luke seeks.** Confidence that the Gentile mission is God's own act and not the church's policy; and composure in the face of the fact that saying so publicly cost Paul his liberty and did not persuade his hearers. **Luke is not asking the reader to admire Paul. He is asking the reader to believe 22:21 and 23:11** — that the sending was God's word and that the outcome was God's necessity.

**Stage 2 — worldview before behaviour.** Three shifts the passage presses:

**First: certainty is not the same thing as vindication.** The unit's vocabulary makes the point. The reader has *ἀσφάλεια* (Luke 1:4); the tribune twice tries to get *τὸ ἀσφαλές* and cannot (21:34; 22:30); and the one verdict actually spoken is *οὐδὲν κακὸν εὑρίσκομεν* — by men who then start a riot. **A church that measures the truth of its message by whether tribunals and crowds concede it has mistaken which kind of certainty the gospel offers.**

**Second: zeal is not a safe possession.** *ζηλωτής* in Acts runs Simon the Zealot (1:13), the myriads zealous for the law (21:20), Paul zealous for God (22:3) — and Paul's own account of what his zeal produced is *ἐδίωξα ἄχρι θανάτου* (22:4). **The passage does not warn against indifference; it warns against the sincerity of the devout.** This is the hardest thing in the unit to preach to a congregation that thinks of itself as committed.

**Third: a hearing is a gift and not a right, and it may be withdrawn at a specific word.** *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* (22:22). **Luke tells us exactly which word ended the conversation**, and it was the word about outsiders.

**Behaviour — stop and start.** *Stop* assuming that a faithful account of Christ will be received if it is courteous, informed and in the hearer's own language; Paul's was all three. *Start* giving the account anyway, and in their language — the Hebrew of 21:40 is not a failed tactic, it is the thing that bought twenty-one verses. **The motive is not duty: it is that the sending is the Lord's (22:21) and the necessity is the Lord's (23:11).** Without those two sentences the application is stoicism.

**Four audiences.**

- **For me.** Which word, if I said it plainly, would end the conversation I am currently managing? And is my reluctance to say it prudence or cowardice? Paul's answer to "they will not accept your testimony" (22:18) was to argue with the Lord about his own usefulness — which is a more honest prayer than most.
- **For a Christian friend.** The one who has given a careful, loving, well-prepared account of Christ to family or colleagues and been met with anger needs 22:22 named rather than explained away: **the best speech in Acts to a Jewish audience fails in one word, and Luke does not treat that as a failure of the speaker.**
- **For the church community.** The unit's acquittal (23:9) comes from men acting for party advantage, and Luke counts it anyway. **A congregation under suspicion may be defended by people who do not share its faith and do not mean it kindly, and that is still God's providence** — as the tribune's letter (23:29) shows, where a Roman repeats a Pharisee's verdict to a governor.
- **For an unbeliever.** The claim on the table is narrow and testable in principle: a man who had made a career of destroying this movement says he was stopped on a road at noon by a voice that named itself, and spent the rest of his life saying so in courtrooms at the cost of his freedom. **Acts tells that story three times, and the differences between the tellings are the sort a witness's three statements actually show.** That is an argument worth putting.

**Prayer.** Lord, you appointed him to know your will, to see the Righteous One, and to hear a voice from your mouth; and you sent him far away, and they would not hear it. **Give us the courage you gave him in the barracks, and not the courage we would choose** — and where we are zealous for you and wrong, stop us on the road.

**Grammatical and syntactical features**

- **22:3's three perfect passive participles** — *γεγεννημένος, ἀνατεθραμμένος, πεπαιδευμένος* — are the clause's whole architecture `[T]`. **All three are passive: Paul does not claim to have made himself anything.** The one active element is the participle that follows, *ζηλωτὴς **ὑπάρχων*** — the zeal is his own. *(moderate–high: the voice pattern is a fact; its rhetorical force is inference.)*
- **22:9's accusative *τὴν φωνήν* against 9:7's genitive *τῆς φωνῆς*** `[T]`. The classical distinction (genitive = hear the sound of, accusative = hear and apprehend) is the standard harmonising explanation and it is **genuinely contested**, because Luke uses both cases of the same act elsewhere without apparent distinction. **Report the data and the dispute, not the resolution.** *(moderate on the data; uncertain on whether the case distinction is doing the work — flagged for Logos.)*
- **22:22's *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* is a temporal-extent phrase doing structural work** `[T]`: it tells the reader the exact point at which attention stopped, which is a narratorial precision Luke uses nowhere else about a speech. *(high)*
- **23:9's aposiopesis** — *εἰ δὲ πνεῦμα ἐλάλησεν αὐτῷ ἢ ἄγγελος—* `[T]`. The protasis stands with no apodosis. **Greek rhetoric knows the figure; so did the scribes, who supplied one** (Textual Variants (c)). *(high on the figure; moderate on its being intentional rather than a textual accident, though the manuscript evidence for scribal discomfort supports intention.)*
- **23:11's *οὕτω … δεῖ*** `[T]` — the impersonal *δεῖ* with the comparative *ὡς … οὕτω* makes Rome the *corresponding term* to Jerusalem, not merely the next stop. **The grammar says the Roman testimony is the same act as the Jerusalem one, performed again.** *(high)*
- **The *δεῖ*/*δέω* collision, again.** MorphGNT files *δεῖ* under *δέω*, as the 20:17–38 run established; 23:11's *δεῖ* must be separated from the binding-*δέω* of 22:29 by parse code, or a count of the chain will conflate the necessity with the chain. **The two stand nine verses apart in this unit, which makes the collision live rather than theoretical** `[T]`.

**Wordplay and sound-features**

- ***κονιορτόν* (22:23) and *κεκονιαμένε* (23:3)*** share the *κονι-* root eleven verses apart `[T]`: dust thrown into the air, and a dust-plastered wall. **Recorded at low confidence under the paronomasia ceiling**, since the two words belong to different semantic domains and no canonical precedent pairs them. It is noted so that a later pass does not upgrade it.
- ***Ἑλληνιστί … Ἑβραΐδι*** (21:37, 40) — the two adverbial/adjectival language-terms in adjacent verses, which is the scene's joke and its pivot at once `[T]`.

### Textual Variants

The NA28 apparatus for Acts is in hand (`_texts/logos-exports/02-New-Testament/05-Acts-NA28-apparatus.txt`). **This pericope is unusually rich, and the richness is itself a finding: the variants cluster precisely where the three conversion accounts differ from each other.** Five are worth the preacher's attention.

**(a) 22:7 — the Western and Byzantine traditions import a line from Acts 26.** NA28 text: *ἤκουσα φωνῆς λεγούσης μοι· Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις;* **E and gig (with some Vulgate manuscripts and the Harklean margin) add *σκληρόν σοι πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν*** — "it is hard for you to kick against the goads" — which stands in NA28 only at **26:14**. D\* rewrites 22:6–7 wholesale, nearer to 9:3. **This is direct manuscript evidence that ancient readers noticed the three accounts differ and tried to level them** `[T]`. *Most likely original: the shorter text, with 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B. Preaching implication: say that the harmonising impulse is ancient — it makes the differences a datum rather than a problem.* *(high confidence)*

**(b) 22:20 — part of the tradition upgrades *μάρτυς* to *πρωτομάρτυς*.** NA28: *Στεφάνου τοῦ **μάρτυρός** σου*. **L, 614, 945, 1505, 1739^mg, 1891 and the Harklean read *πρωτομάρτυρος*** — "your **first martyr** Stephen." And separately, **L Ψ 33 323 614 945 1175 1241 1505 1739 𝔪 add *τῇ ἀναιρέσει αὐτοῦ*** ("consenting **to his killing**"), which is the phrase Luke uses at **8:1**, where *ἀναίρεσις* is an NT hapax `[T]`. *Most likely original: the short text, with א A B E latt co.* **Preaching implication, and it is the project's own standing rule paying out: where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied.** The scribes read 22:20's *μάρτυς* as "martyr" and made it explicit, and they read 22:20 against 8:1 and supplied the link. **Both are external evidence for exactly the two observations Tool 7 and Headline 3 make from the text alone** — that *μάρτυς* here is at the pivot between witness and martyr, and that 22:20 is 8:1 in the first person. *(high confidence)*

**(c) 23:9 — the Byzantine tradition completes the broken sentence with Gamaliel's word.** NA28 leaves the Pharisees' protasis hanging: *εἰ δὲ πνεῦμα ἐλάλησεν αὐτῷ ἢ ἄγγελος—*. **L, 323, 614, 945^c, 1241, 1505, 𝔪 and the Sahidic add *μὴ θεομαχῶμεν*** — "let us not fight against God!" *θεομάχος* is a **New Testament hapax at 5:39**, in Gamaliel's speech to this same council `[T]`. *Most likely original: the aposiopesis, with 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C E Ψ 33 81 945\* 1175 1739 latt sy.* **Preaching implication, and this is the run's most interesting variant.** The overview's echo table already links **Gamaliel 5:34 → 22:3**. The apparatus shows that ancient readers extended the link to **23:9** — they heard the Pharisees half-defending Paul and finished their sentence with their teacher's own argument. **The variant is not original and it is a good reading of the text**; a preacher may legitimately say that the scribes saw what the Pharisees were about to say. *(high confidence on the attestation; the inference about what the scribes heard is `[I]`, moderate–high)*

**(d) 23:6 — "son of a Pharisee" or "son of Pharisees".** NA28 prints the plural *Φαρισαίων* with 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C Ψ 33 945^c 1175 2818 lat sy^p; **E L 323 614 945\* 1241 1505 𝔪 sy^h read the singular *Φαρισαίου*.** *Most likely original: the plural.* **Preaching implication: minor but real** — the plural makes the claim ancestral ("I come of Pharisees") rather than filial ("my father was one"), which is what Paul needs in a council. Both English versions follow the plural. *(moderate–high)*

**(e) 23:11 — the scribes supply the vocative.** NA28: *Θάρσει, ὡς γὰρ διεμαρτύρω*. **C³, L, 81, 323, 945, 1241, 𝔪, h and some Vulgate manuscripts add *Παῦλε*** — "Take courage, **Paul**." *Most likely original: the unadorned imperative, with 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C\* E Ψ 33 614 1175 1505 1739 1891 2818 lat sy co.* **Preaching implication: do not quote it as "Take courage, Paul."** The text has one word, and the absence of the name is part of its weight — compare 9:4's *Σαοὺλ Σαούλ* and 26:14's, where the name is doubled. **The Lord names him on the road and does not name him in the barracks.** *(high confidence on the text; the observation about the effect is `[I]`, moderate.)*

**Minor, recorded for completeness and not for the pulpit:** 21:39's D-text imports *ἐν Ταρσῷ δὲ τῆς Κιλικίας γεγεννημένος* from 22:3 (**the same harmonising hand as (a)**); 22:9's D E L Ψ 𝔪 add *καὶ ἔμφοβοι ἐγένοντο*; 22:12 varies on *κατοικούντων* with or without *ἐν Δαμασκῷ*; 22:29 has 614 and the Harklean add *καὶ παραχρῆμα ἔλυσεν αὐτόν*, anticipating 22:30; 23:1 shows a three-way word-order variation with no sense change; 23:8's B and the Latins omit the Sadducees' denial clause. **None affects a finding above.**

**The pattern across this pericope, stated as a method result.** Of the five significant variants, **three are harmonisations toward another part of Acts** (22:7 from 26:14; 22:20 from 8:1; 21:39 from 22:3) and **one completes an echo the text leaves implicit** (23:9 from 5:39). **In this unit the manuscript tradition behaves like a very early commentary on Acts' internal echoes**, and three of the four places it intervenes are places this run identified independently from the lemma index. That is the strongest corroboration available short of a library pass, and it should be recorded in the overview's method note as a fourth and fifth instance of "where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied" `[T]` on the attestations; `[I]`, high, on the pattern.

### Historical and Cultural Background

**Setting.** Jerusalem, spring, within a few days of Pentecost; the Antonia fortress at the north-west corner of the temple platform, connected to the outer court by stairs — which is why Paul can stand on the steps and address a crowd in the court below. *(The identification of the barracks with the Antonia is standard and well attested, though the archaeology of the fortress is contested in detail — *uncertain on the architectural specifics; high on the general arrangement*.)*

**What the original audience knew without being told.**

1. **A tribune commanding a cohort had summary powers of interrogation over provincials and none over citizens.** The *ἀνετάζω* of 22:24 is examination under the lash to extract a charge — a standard procedure applied to those without status. **The illegality at 22:25 is not the flogging but the flogging of a *Ῥωμαῖος* who is *ἀκατάκριτος***, and the tribune's fear at 22:29 is professional: he has already bound a citizen. *(high)*
2. **Citizenship was provable and falsely claiming it was capital.** The tribune's *Ἐγὼ πολλοῦ κεφαλαίου* (22:28) is a boast that doubles as a test — purchase was the common route under Claudius, and a man claiming birth-citizenship in a provincial city was claiming something rarer and older. **Paul's *Ἐγὼ δὲ καὶ γεγέννημαι* is a two-word answer that ends the matter**, and the fact that it ends it is evidence the claim was checkable. *(moderate–high; the specifics of Claudian venality are reported by Dio and are a commonplace — uncertain on how routine purchase actually was.)*
3. **"The Egyptian" and "the Assassins" were current events.** The tribune's guess (21:38) refers to a known messianic pretender and to the *σικάριοι*, the dagger-men of the nationalist resistance; *σικάριος* is a Latin loan and a NT hapax. **The point for the reader is the category the tribune reaches for: Paul looks to a Roman officer like a rural insurgent leader**, and Luke's apologetic depends on that being a mistake. *(high on the category; moderate on the identification of the individual, which rests on Josephus — *consider verifying*.)*
4. **Hebrew (or Aramaic) from the steps was a political act as well as a courtesy.** Greek was the lingua franca of the eastern empire and of the diaspora; addressing a festival crowd in the sacred vernacular placed the speaker inside Israel rather than beside it. **The crowd's increased quiet (22:2) is the measure of how much that mattered.** *(high)*
5. **Gamaliel's name was a credential and a checkable one.** The leading Pharisaic teacher of the generation, and Luke has already given him a speech (5:34–39) in which he counselled this council to let the movement alone. **A Jerusalem crowd in the fifties would know the name; many would know men who had studied with him.** *(high on the standing; the relationship itself is Luke's claim and is `[T]` as a report, `[S]` as history.)*
6. **The Sadducee/Pharisee division was institutional, not academic.** The high priesthood was Sadducean; the scribal class largely Pharisaic; they sat on the same council and disagreed about resurrection, angels and spirits (23:8). **Paul's sentence at 23:6 does not create a split, it names an existing fault line** — and Luke explains it at 23:8 because his readers may not know. *(high)*
7. **A blow to the mouth was a shaming act with a legal character.** It silences and degrades at once, and ordering it before any charge is heard is what Paul calls *παρανομῶν*. In an honour-shame setting the public striking of a man under examination is the insult the response at 23:3 answers. *(moderate–high)*
8. **Rome's interest in *στάσις* was absolute.** 23:7 and 23:10 use the word that describes Barabbas's offence (Luke 23:19, 25) and that will be the charge against Paul at 24:5. **A tribune whose council meeting turns into a *στάσις* has a career problem**, which is why the troops come down. *(high)*

**What this changes about how we read.** Two things. **The citizenship scene is not a lucky escape but a legal trap closing on the tribune** — Luke is showing Rome bound by its own rules, which is the apologetic's quiet claim. And **the language note at 21:40/22:2 is not local colour**: it is the reason the speech gets twenty-one verses, and it means the hearing ends not because Paul failed to connect but at the precise moment he named the nations.

### Original Audience Reception

**Canonical audience.** New Testament, Luke–Acts as a two-volume work. **The canonical reader of Acts 22 has read Luke's Gospel**, and therefore knows Pilate's *οὐδὲν εὑρίσκω αἴτιον ἐν τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ τούτῳ* (Luke 23:4, 14), the *πρεσβυτέριον τοῦ λαοῦ* that convened against Jesus (Luke 22:66), and Jesus' word to the lawyers that they were *μάρτυρες* who *συνευδοκεῖτε* to their fathers' killings (Luke 11:48). **Three of this unit's internal echoes are cross-volume and depend on that reader.** Within Acts, the canonical reader has read Stephen's speech and death (6:8–8:1), the first Damascus account (9:1–19), and Peter's trance (10:9–16; 11:5–10) — and those three passages supply eleven of the unit's Move-4 rows.

**The first hearers.** A mixed Jewish-and-Gentile Christian body, under suspicion in the synagogue and periodically before Roman courts, for whom the legality and the Jewishness of the movement were both live questions `[I]`, moderate on the reconstruction and high on the assurance motive, which Luke states.

**What they brought to the passage.** An expectation that God's purposes run through Israel and the temple; a knowledge that Paul was a contested figure; and — for the Jewish members — the memory or report of exactly the kind of festival crowd Luke describes.

**Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances.**

- **Shocking:** that the speech fails, and fails at the word *ἔθνη*. **For a Gentile believer sitting in that congregation, the sentence the crowd could not bear is the sentence that accounts for his presence there.** `[I]`, high — this is the reception effect the whole unit is built for.
- **Shocking, second:** that a serving high priest orders an illegal blow and is rebuked out of the Law by the accused. **For a Jewish reader this is not anticlericalism; it is the Law being honoured by the wrong man in the room.**
- **Surprising:** that Paul's conversion is presented as *continuity*. *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν* appointed him (22:14); the man who baptised him kept the law (22:12); the commission came in the temple (22:17). **A hearer expecting a story of defection gets a story of appointment by the ancestral God.**
- **Surprising, second:** that Rome behaves well. The centurion stops the flogging; the tribune, having blundered twice, saves his life. **To a congregation that had reason to fear Roman courts, this is a deliberate reassurance** — and the overview's presenting situation is where it lands.
- **Comforting:** 23:11, and specifically its grammar. *ὡς … διεμαρτύρω … οὕτω σε δεῖ* — **the Jerusalem testimony that nobody accepted is counted by the Lord as testimony given.** For hearers whose witness had met refusal, that single comparative is the pastoral centre of the unit.
- **Disturbing:** 22:3's *καθὼς πάντες ὑμεῖς ἐστε σήμερον*. Paul tells a hostile crowd that their zeal is the zeal he had — and he has just described what it made him do. **The devout hearer is given his own portrait.**

**What they would have heard that we do not.** The forensic register. **A first-century hearer knew that *ἀπολογία*, *ἀνετάζω*, *ἀκατάκριτος*, *κατηγορέω* and *στάσις* were the vocabulary of a legal process with real stakes**, and heard the unit as a trial report. A modern congregation hears it as a testimony with interruptions. **The preacher's remedy is simple: say at the start that this is a courtroom, and that nobody in it manages to conduct a trial.**

**What we bring that they did not.** Three distortions worth naming. **(a) We read 22:21 as unremarkable** and therefore cannot feel 22:22. **(b) We read "Pharisee" as a synonym for hypocrite**, which makes 23:6 unintelligible as a sincere claim and 23:9 unintelligible as a genuine acquittal. **(c) We read conversion testimony as a devotional genre** and so hear 22:3–21 as a personal story rather than as a legal defence organised to the shape of an indictment.

| **What they felt** | That their account of Christ — careful, Scripture-shaped, given in the hearers' own language, at real cost — might be refused anyway; and that the refusal would come from the devout rather than the indifferent `[I]`, anchored in 22:18's *οὐ παραδέξονταί σου μαρτυρίαν* and 22:22's *ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου* |
| **Candidate FCF (shared concern)** | **We want our witness to be received, and we cannot make it so; and when it is refused we are tempted to conclude either that we failed or that the refusal is the last word.** |
| **Shared / differs** | *Shared:* the experience of a faithful account meeting anger, and the specific sting of refusal by people who share our Scriptures. *Differs:* we are not on trial for our lives, and we stand on the far side of the Gentile mission's success, so the word that stopped the crowd no longer costs us anything to say. **That asymmetry must be named from the pulpit or the application becomes self-pity.** |
| **Confidence** | anchored — derived from 22:18, 22:22 and 23:11, not from contemporary felt-need |

**Implication for the sermon.** Recovering the reception changes the emotional target. **The passage does not comfort by saying the witness will eventually be received; it comforts by saying the Lord counts testimony that was refused as testimony given (23:11), and sends the witness onward.** A sermon aimed at persuasion-techniques, or at courage-in-general, will miss both the disturbance and the comfort.

### Biblical-Theological Themes

Two themes, and they converge.

**1. Witness — from programme to martyrdom and back.**

- **Where this passage sits:** at the point where *μάρτυς* has acquired its second sense without losing its first. 1:8 gave the programme (*ἔσεσθέ μου μάρτυρες … ἕως ἐσχάτου τῆς γῆς*); 1:22 and 2:32 made it a legal-historical function (witnesses of the resurrection); 6:13 and 7:58 used it of false witnesses and of men at an execution; **22:20 uses it of a man killed for testifying, and 22:15 of a man appointed to testify — five verses apart, in one speech.** 23:11 then has the Lord use the verb twice, for Jerusalem and for Rome.
- **What comes before:** the whole chain above, and Stephen as its hinge.
- **What comes after:** 26:16 (*ὑπηρέτην καὶ μάρτυρα*) and the book's end, where the witness is still testifying, in chains, *ἀκωλύτως* (28:31).
- **What it contributes:** **the theme's turn happens in this unit.** Luke does not define *μάρτυς* as martyr; he lets the two senses stand in one breath, and the manuscript tradition's *πρωτομάρτυς* at 22:20 shows how quickly readers collapsed them. **Preaching the unit as "witness" requires holding both: a commission to speak, and a precedent in a man who died speaking.** *Confidence: high.*

**2. Temple and presence — the commission given in the place it cannot be heard.**

- **Where this passage sits:** at the end of Acts' temple thread as a place of *access*. The apostles pray and preach in the temple (2:46; 3:1; 5:20–21, 42); Stephen is charged over it and answers from Isaiah 66 (6:13–14; 7:49–50); Paul purifies himself in it and is dragged out and the doors shut (21:26–30). **22:17 is the thread's last constructive occurrence: the temple as the place where the Lord spoke.** Every subsequent *ἱερόν* in Acts (24:6, 12, 18; 25:8; 26:21) is inside a legal dispute about what Paul did there `[T]`.
- **What comes before:** Solomon's temple, the departure of the glory (Ezek 10), the second temple without the cloud, and Stephen's argument that the Most High does not dwell in houses made with hands.
- **What comes after:** Christ as the temple, the church as temple, and no temple in the city (Rev 21:22).
- **What it contributes:** the unit supplies the **mechanism** of the transition that Acts' temple thread implies. **The Gentile commission was not given against the temple; it was given inside it, in prayer, in a trance — and the temple crowd would not hear it.** `[I]`, high. *Confidence: high on the distribution, moderate–high on the reading.*

**Confluence — and it is climactic, not parallel.** The two themes meet in one clause: *γενέσθαι με ἐν ἐκστάσει … Πορεύου, ὅτι ἐγὼ εἰς ἔθνη μακρὰν ἐξαποστελῶ σε* (22:17, 21). **The witness-commission is issued from the place of presence, to the nations, and the hearers in that place refuse it in the next verse.** That is not two themes running side by side; it is the temple theme producing the witness theme and being closed by it. **Relation: (c) climactic convergence.**

### Christological Reading

**Type of connection.** This is a NT passage, so Christ is named rather than figured. But **two typological operations are live and both run through the OT**, and one of them is the unit's hardest preaching question.

**How the passage presents Christ.**

**Ground — he is the one who is persecuted.** *Ἐγώ εἰμι Ἰησοῦς ὁ Ναζωραῖος ὃν σὺ διώκεις* (22:8). The overview calls the union of Christ with his people the book's sharpest Christological datum, and it is **spoken, not narrated** — all three times (9:5; 22:8; 26:15) `[T]`. **Note the addition here**: of the three accounts, only 22:8 has *ὁ Ναζωραῖος*. **Paul, defending himself before a Jerusalem crowd, reports that the voice identified itself with a town-name** — the most locally Jewish form of the title available, and Luke's standing designation in the sermons (2:22; 3:6; 4:10; 6:14; 26:9) `[T]`. *(high on the distribution; moderate on why 22:8 adds it, though the audience is the obvious answer.)*

**He is the Righteous One, and the title comes with a history.** *ἰδεῖν **τὸν δίκαιον*** (22:14) — see Headline 3. **Paul's appointed privilege is named with the title Stephen used in the sentence that killed him.** `[T]` on the three occurrences; `[I]`, high, on the significance.

**He is the appointer, and the sender.** *ὁ θεὸς τῶν πατέρων ἡμῶν **προεχειρίσατό** σε* (22:14) — the verb used of the Christ at 3:20. *ἐγὼ … **ἐξαποστελῶ** σε* (22:21) — first person, emphatic pronoun, future. **The mission's agent is Christ himself, and the sentence is the last of the speech.**

**He is the one who stands by.** *ἐπιστὰς αὐτῷ ὁ κύριος* (23:11). See Tool 3 for the triple.

**Typology, carefully, and the four tests.** The real question is whether Luke is presenting Paul as figured on Christ, and the answer is more restrained than the material first suggests.

| Element | Reading |
|---|---|
| The Isaiah 53:8 phrase at 22:22 | **Trajectory, not typology.** The Servant's removal-language is applied to Paul by **his enemies**, not by Luke and not by Paul. It tells us how the crowd acted, not what Paul is. *Category test: passes (the Servant is a theological category). NT-precedent test: partial — Acts applies Servant texts to Jesus (8:35) and Servant commissions to the missionaries (13:47) and to Paul (26:18), so applying Servant **suffering** to Paul has a precedent of kind. Escalation test: **fails** — Paul does not exceed the Servant. Authorial-pattern test: partial.* **Two and a half of four: report as trajectory and contrast, not as typology.** |
| Pilate's formula at 23:9 | **Parallel narration, deliberately unexplained.** Luke supplies the wording and no comment. **The servant's road runs where the master's ran — the 21:1–36 run's formula, and it holds here too.** |
| The Sanhedrin scene | **Parallel with inversion.** Jesus before this council was asked who he was and answered; Paul is struck before he can be asked, and the council never reaches a question. **Paul is not a second Christ; he is a man on the same road getting less of a hearing.** |

**Moralism check — and the temptation in this unit is specific.** The pull is not "be bold like Paul" (though it is available at 23:3) but **"give your testimony like Paul"** — treating 22:3–21 as a model to imitate in structure and tone. Three guards the text supplies:

**(a) The speech fails.** Any application built on its effectiveness is building on something Luke withholds.
**(b) Every active verb in the commission belongs to God or to Christ.** *προεχειρίσατο*, *ἐξαποστελῶ*, *δεῖ*. Paul's verbs are *ἤκουσα*, *ἔπεσα*, *οὐκ ἐνέβλεπον*, *ἦλθον*, *εἶπον* — and his one piece of initiative is the objection at 22:19–20, which the Lord overrules.
**(c) The one figure held up for anything like imitation is Ananias**, a man with no office who called a persecutor *brother*.

**The gospel grounding, in one sentence.** **The good news in this unit is not that Paul testified well but that the Lord who was himself removed from the earth counts a refused testimony as testimony given, and sends the witness on.** That sentence is 23:11, and it is where a Christ-centred sermon on this passage lands.

### Difficult / Contested Verses

**22:9 — "they did not hear the voice", against 9:7's "hearing the voice".**

- **Category:** apologetic.
- **The difficulty:** 9:7 says the companions stood *ἐνεοί, ἀκούοντες μὲν τῆς φωνῆς, μηδένα δὲ θεωροῦντες*. 22:9 says *τὸ μὲν φῶς ἐθεάσαντο, τὴν δὲ φωνὴν οὐκ ἤκουσαν*. **Read flatly: in one they hear and see nothing, in the other they see and hear nothing.** It is the classic Acts contradiction and a sharp enquirer will find it.
- **Common mishandling:** quoting the English and not noticing that **both NASB95 and ESV have already translated the problem away** by rendering *ἤκουσαν* as "understand" (Tool 8). A hearer with a KJV will see the difficulty that the preacher's text has hidden.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** in 9:7 the detail establishes that **something objective happened** — there were witnesses. In 22:9 the detail establishes that **the message was for Paul alone** — which is what a defence speech needs, since the commission he is reporting cannot be corroborated by anyone else. **The two accounts use the same event for opposite evidential purposes**, and that observation does more work than any grammatical solution.
- **The main options:** (i) the case distinction — genitive *φωνῆς* (9:7) = hear the sound, accusative *φωνήν* (22:9) = hear with comprehension; (ii) *ἀκούω* in 22:9 means "understand", which is what the versions assume; (iii) *φωνή* means "sound" in 9:7 and "the words spoken" in 22:9; (iv) Luke has let two sources stand and did not harmonise them.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** (i) is the traditional solution and **is contested**, because Luke does not observe the distinction consistently (9:4 has the accusative *φωνήν* of Paul's own hearing, where comprehension is certainly in view, so the correlation is not clean) `[T]`. (iii) is probably the strongest, and (ii) is (iii) in English clothing. **(iv) should not be dismissed:** the apparatus shows the ancient tradition itself harmonising across the accounts at 22:7 and 21:39, which is evidence that the differences were felt and were **not** smoothed by Luke `[T]`. *Confidence: moderate that a real distinction of sense is intended; uncertain that the case distinction carries it — flagged for Logos with a grammar.*
- **Suggested handling:** **name it, briefly and without anxiety.** "Two accounts of the same event differ about what the bystanders took in; the Greek allows that in one they heard a sound and in the other they did not make out words; and the oldest copyists tried to smooth it, which tells us the difference is original." That is both honest and strengthening.

**23:3–5 — did Paul lose his temper, and did he lie?**

- **Category:** ethical and pastoral.
- **The difficulty:** Paul calls the high priest a whitewashed wall and predicts God will strike him, then says *Οὐκ ᾔδειν … ὅτι ἐστὶν ἀρχιερεύς*. Either he did not know (hard to credit of a man who had carried letters from the high priest, 22:5) or he did and is being evasive. **And 1 Pet 2:23 says Christ, *λοιδορούμενος*, did not revile in return — the same verb the bystanders use at 23:4** `[T]`.
- **Common mishandlings:** (a) treating it as straightforward prophetic rebuke with no problem in it; (b) treating it as a confessed sin and preaching Paul's repentance, which the text does not report; (c) using it to license sharp speech to church officers.
- **Rhetorical function in context:** **23:5 is Paul's third answer to the charge of 21:28** (Phase 0.6), and it is the law leg. Whatever else it is, functionally it is **submission to Scripture in the hearing of the body that struck him** — and it is the last *γέγραπται* in Acts.
- **The main options:** (i) genuine ignorance — he had been away for years, Ananias may not have been robed or presiding visibly; (ii) irony — "I did not recognise him as high priest, because no high priest acts like that"; (iii) a deliberate withdrawal of the personal remark while keeping the charge, which is what the quotation actually accomplishes; (iv) a frank retraction of a hasty word.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **the text does not adjudicate, and the preacher should not either.** Two data constrain the options. **First, *παρανομῶν* is a New Testament hapax and it is Paul's word for what the high priest is doing** `[T]` — so the substance of the charge is not withdrawn at 23:5; only the address is. **Second, the LXX background of *τοῖχε κεκονιαμένε* is judicial rather than abusive** (Tool 11): in the Greek OT a plastered wall is a thing God tears down (Ezek 13) or writes doom upon (Dan 5). **So option (iii) fits the Greek best**: Paul retracts the insult to the office and leaves the indictment of the man standing, by quoting the law the man has just broken. *Confidence: moderate.* `[I]`
- **Suggested handling:** **address it briefly and let the ambiguity stand.** The pastorally useful observation is that the one thing Paul unambiguously does is **submit to a written text in a room where he has just been assaulted** — and that a congregation's officers, and its critics, are both inside the scope of that.

**23:6 — was the Pharisee claim a tactic?**

- **Category:** ethical, and the unit's live preaching trap.
- **The difficulty:** Paul announces his party allegiance to a divided council and the council fractures; the rescue follows. It looks like, and may be, a manoeuvre.
- **Common mishandling:** preaching it as a lesson in shrewdness — "find the fault line and use it." The sweep flags exactly this.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces, and this run adds a datum.** The sweep's defence is that Paul says the same thing four more times with no Sadducee in the room (24:15, 21; 26:6–8; 28:20), which tells strongly against pure tactics. **Add this:** *ἀνάστασις* occurs in eleven Acts verses, and **4:2 records that the Sadducees' original grievance against the apostles was precisely the proclamation of *τὴν ἀνάστασιν*** `[T]`. **So the issue Paul names in 23:6 is the issue on which the Sanhedrin first moved against the church, nineteen chapters earlier.** He is not inventing a wedge; he is naming the original charge. *Confidence: moderate–high* that the claim is substantive rather than tactical — though **the two are not exclusive, and the text leaves room for both.**
- **Suggested handling:** preach the substance (he is on trial for the resurrection, and he says so five times) and name the tactical question honestly rather than suppressing it.

**23:5 — "I did not know": see above. 22:16 — baptism and the washing away of sins.**

- **Category:** doctrinal.
- **The difficulty:** *βάπτισαι καὶ ἀπόλουσαι τὰς ἁμαρτίας σου* reads, flatly, as baptismal remission, and the verse is used that way in debate.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** three constraints, all textual. **(a)** Both verbs are **middle imperatives** and the participle that governs the whole is *ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ* — **calling on the name is the clause's instrumental element** `[T]`. **(b)** The clause stands in **reported speech inside a defence speech** — it is Ananias quoted by Paul, not Luke's narration; the parallel at 9:18 narrates the baptism and says nothing of washing. **(c)** *ἀπολούομαι*'s only other NT occurrence is **1 Cor 6:11**, *ἀπελούσασθε … ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ* `[T]` — Paul's own usage, and there the washing is tied to the name, as here.
- **Suggested handling:** **do not make this verse the sermon's doctrinal load-bearing wall in either direction.** Note the middle voice and the *ἐπικαλεσάμενος*, note that Acts' four Spirit-frontiers have four different sequences (the overview's Trap 1), and move on.

**22:22 — "away with such a fellow from the earth."**

- **Category:** pastoral, and easily mishandled in the other direction.
- **The difficulty:** the verse invites a sermon about Jewish hostility, and in a congregation with no Jewish members it can slide into something ugly.
- **What an honest exegesis surfaces:** **Luke's own framing cuts against that use.** The crowd is *ζηλωτὴς τοῦ θεοῦ* in exactly the way Paul was (22:3); the acquittal that follows is spoken by Pharisees (23:9); the overview's Trap 5 notes that the turn to the Gentiles happens three times and is each time local, and that 21:20 reports *μυριάδες* of law-zealous Jewish believers. **And the phrase the crowd uses is the Servant's own** (Headline 1), which puts them in the position of those who removed the Righteous One — a charge Acts lays on *Jerusalem's rulers* (3:17; 13:27) while insisting on their ignorance.
- **Suggested handling:** preach the refusal as **the devout refusing God's generosity**, with the hearer in the crowd (Tool 15), and never as an ethnic verdict.

## Preaching Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Preaching 22:3–21 as a model testimony

**How it sounds:** "Here is how to give your testimony — note Paul's structure: before, the encounter, after." Possibly with a handout.
**Why it is wrong:** it is not a testimony, it is an *ἀπολογία* (22:1), and Luke's other use of that word is Festus explaining Roman trial procedure (25:16). It is organised not as a conversion story but as a **point-by-point rebuttal of the three-part charge of 21:28**. And **it fails** — twenty-one verses of it produce a demand for his execution. A sermon that commends the method has commended something the text shows not working.
**Instead:** preach it as a defence that answers the accusation exactly, and preach the failure. Then preach 23:11, where the Lord calls the failed defence *testimony given*.

### Pitfall 2: Making 23:6 a lesson in shrewdness

**How it sounds:** "Paul knew his audience — find the fault line and use it."
**Why it is wrong:** Paul says the same thing four more times with no Sadducee in the room (24:15, 21; 26:6–8; 28:20), and *ἀνάστασις* is what the Sadducees first moved against the church over, nineteen chapters earlier (4:2). **He is naming the original charge, not inventing a wedge.**
**Instead:** preach the substance — he is on trial for the resurrection, and the Pharisees are half-right. Then name the tactical question honestly rather than suppressing it; Luke leaves it open and so may the preacher.

### Pitfall 3: Reading 22:22 as an ethnic verdict

**How it sounds:** a sermon in which the crowd's cry becomes evidence about a people.
**Why it is wrong:** the crowd is zealous for God in exactly the way Paul was (22:3); the acquittal eleven verses later is spoken by Pharisees (23:9); the man who baptised Paul is attested by every Jew in Damascus (22:12); and the overview's Trap 5 records that the turn to the Gentiles happens three times and is each time local, with *μυριάδες* of law-zealous Jewish believers at 21:20. **The crowd's own phrase is the Servant's** (Headline 1), which puts them among those who removed the Righteous One — a charge Acts lays while insisting on their ignorance (3:17; 13:27).
**Instead:** preach the devout refusing God's generosity, with the hearer placed in the crowd by way of Paul's own past — which is where 22:3 puts them.

### Pitfall 4: Resolving 22:9 from the English

**How it sounds:** "They heard the sound but didn't understand it — no contradiction." Delivered as settled.
**Why it is wrong:** **both NASB95 and ESV have already made that decision in the translation**, rendering *οὐκ ἤκουσαν* as "did not understand". The reading may well be right; it is not what the Greek says, and a hearer with a KJV will see the difficulty the sermon has concealed. The grammatical solution usually offered — genitive at 9:7 against accusative at 22:9 — is contested, because 9:4 uses the accusative of Paul's own comprehending hearing.
**Instead:** name the difference in one sentence, say what the Greek allows, and say that the earliest copyists tried to smooth across the three accounts (22:7 imports a line from 26:14) — which is evidence that the differences are original. **That strengthens a congregation rather than unsettling it.**

### Pitfall 5: Making the whitewashed wall a point about hypocrisy

**How it sounds:** "Paul exposes the high priest's hypocrisy — all show and no substance, like Jesus' whitewashed tombs."
**Why it is wrong:** *κονιάω* occurs twice in the New Testament, at Matt 23:27 and here — **and Luke's Gospel does not contain the word**, so Luke's reader has no whitewashed tombs. In the Greek Old Testament a plastered wall is a thing God tears down (Ezek 13:10–15) or writes a king's doom upon (Dan 5:5), and the only Pentateuchal use of the verb is the plastering of the stones that carry the Law (Deut 27:2, 4). **Paul is not calling the man a fake; he is calling him a structure about to come down** — which is why the next clause is *Τύπτειν σε μέλλει ὁ θεός*.
**Instead:** preach the image as judicial. And note that *παρανομέω* is a New Testament hapax: **the only man in the NT described as acting against the law is the serving high priest, and the word is used by the man on trial for teaching apostasy from it.**

### Pitfall 6: Building a doctrine of baptism on 22:16

**How it sounds:** "'Be baptised and wash away your sins' — Acts is clear."
**Why it is wrong:** the clause is **reported speech inside a defence speech** (Ananias, quoted by Paul), not Luke's narration; the parallel at 9:18 narrates the baptism and says nothing of washing; both verbs are **middle imperatives** governed by the participle *ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ*; and the overview's Trap 1 records that Acts' four Spirit-frontiers have four different sequences.
**Instead:** note the middle voice and the calling on the name, say that Acts does not give a single order of operations, and do not let this verse carry a doctrinal wall in either direction.

### Pitfall 7: Quoting 23:11 as "Take courage, Paul"

**How it sounds:** harmless, and almost everyone does it.
**Why it is wrong:** the vocative *Παῦλε* is a later addition (C³ L 81 323 945 1241 𝔪), absent from 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C\* E Ψ 33 614 1175 1505 1739 1891 2818. **The Lord names him on the road, twice, and does not name him in the barracks.**
**Instead:** quote the one word. And note that *θαρσέω* occurs nowhere else in Luke–Acts — **this is the only time in two volumes that Luke writes the word**, and its cognate noun appears once, at 28:15, when Paul sees the brothers near Rome and takes courage. Two courage-words, one at each end of the road.

### Pitfall 8: Mining the barracks

**How it sounds:** a devotional on the Antonia stairs, or on the four thousand Assassins, or on how much the tribune paid for his citizenship.
**Why it is wrong:** the overview's Trap 6 names this for Acts generally. Luke's own interest in 21:38 is that a Roman officer mistook Paul for an insurgent; his interest in 22:28 is the contrast with *γεγέννημαι*.
**Instead:** if the tribune is preached, preach him as the man who cannot find out the facts — which is the thread he belongs to (21:34; 22:24, 29, 30; 23:29; 25:26).

**2. The 9:7 / 22:9 case question is answered, and the answer is better than expected: the grammar of record abandons the solution.**

- **Wallace**, *Greek Grammar beyond the Basics* (Zondervan, 1996), 133–134, lists New Testament instances of *ἀκούω* + genitive **with** understanding (Matthew 2:9; John 5:25; 18:37; **Acts 3:23; 11:7**; Revelation 3:20; 6:3, 5; 8:13; 11:12; 14:13; 16:1, 5, 7; 21:3) and of *ἀκούω* + accusative **without** (Matthew 13:19; Mark 13:7; **Acts 5:24**; 1 Corinthians 11:18; Ephesians 3:2; Colossians 1:4; Philemon 5; James 5:11; Revelation 14:2), observes that **the exceptions outnumber the rule**, and concludes that **"an appeal to different cases probably ought not form any part of the solution to the apparent contradiction"** `[S: Wallace]`.
- **Robertson**, *Grammar* (506), who defends the distinction, **concedes the same counter-instance this run found independently**: the genitive can be used where understanding is meant though not stressed, **citing Acts 22:7 against 9:4 and 26:14** `[S: Robertson]`. **BDF** (95): the New Testament "wavers between genitive and accusative" in phrases meaning "to hear a sound", where classical Greek uses the genitive `[S: BDF]`.
- **Wallace's list tested against the corpus.** His strongest item is exact — **Acts 11:7** is *ἤκουσα … φωνῆς λεγούσης μοι*, genitive, of hearing understood and obeyed `[T]`. But the list is **mixed in kind**: Acts 3:23 is *ἀκούσῃ τοῦ προφήτου*, a genitive of **person**, not of sound; and Acts 5:24 (*ἤκουσαν τοὺς λόγους*) is a weak example, since the hearers plainly understood and were perplexed about the meaning. **Luke's own *φωνή* set makes the case more cleanly than Wallace's mixed list does.**
- **The governed set, re-verified, is seven verses**: genitive at **9:7, 11:7, 22:7**; accusative at **9:4, 22:9, 22:14, 26:14** `[T]`. Three verse-level hits are **not** governed by *ἀκούω* and must be excluded: **Acts 2:6**, whose *τῆς φωνῆς* is a genitive absolute (*γενομένης δὲ τῆς φωνῆς ταύτης*, with *ἤκουον* governing *λαλούντων αὐτῶν*); Acts 4:24 and 22:22, where *φωνήν* is the object of *αἴρω* / *ἐπαίρω*; and Luke 9:35, where *φωνή* is nominative. (`acts-logos-questions-round-1.md` gave the set as eight verses, including 2:6; corrected there.)
- **It also answers Blomberg's objection before he makes it.** **Blomberg**, *Can We Still Believe in God?* (Brazos, 2020), 121, concedes the classical distinction was eroding and does not hold consistently in Luke, but argues it is hard to explain why Luke would vary the case if he intended no distinction `[S: Blomberg]`. **The corpus answers: Luke varies it for the same moment** — 9:4 accusative and 22:7 genitive both report Paul's own comprehending hearing `[T]`.
- **A live alternative that is semantic, not grammatical.** **Witherington**, *Acts* (Eerdmans, 1998), 312–313, notes **Bratcher's** prior objection that Luke does not observe the distinction, and retreats to **Polhill's** distinction between a voice "which was speaking" (22:9) and the hearing of a sound (9:7) — stressing that 22:9 does not deny they heard the sound, only that they did not hear the voice of the one speaking to Paul `[S: Witherington]`. **Lenski** (905) holds the case line in a softer form `[S: Lenski]`.
- **Consequence for Difficult Verses above:** option (i) should no longer be reported as the standard solution tout court. **It is the traditional solution, and it is rejected by the standard reference grammar on the strength of New Testament usage, with Luke's own *φωνή* set as the sharpest case.** The entry can now conclude. Options (ii) and (iii) are unaffected.
- **And one library fact: BDAG is not in the library.** The Exegetical Guide at 22:9 returned morphology — *φωνήν*, accusative singular feminine; *ἤκουσαν*, aorist active indicative third plural — and **no lexicon entry**. The guidelines sheet should record this, since "do it in the Exegetical Guide, it is free" cannot promise a lexicon.

**3. Acts 23:3 — the literature is unanimous for Ezekiel 13, and a follow-up found a fifth candidate this run had missed.**
