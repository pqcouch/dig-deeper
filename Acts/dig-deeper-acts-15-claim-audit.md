# Claim Audit: Acts 15:1–35 with 21:25 — the Apostolic Decree and the Sojourner Laws of Leviticus 17–18

**Passages audited:** Acts 15:1–35 (the decree at 15:20 and 15:29), with 16:4 and 21:25; Leviticus 17:1–18:30, with Gen 9:4, Deut 14:21, Jer 12:15–16, Zech 2:14–15 and Amos 9:11–12 as controls.
**Date:** 10 October 2026 · **Version:** 1.0
**Purpose:** to test one claim from `Torah/tripartite-law-paper.md` §5.4 before the paper relies on it. The claim is that **the four abstentions correspond to the laws Leviticus 17–18 imposes on the גֵּר ("sojourner")**. The audit tests it in five parts: order, mapping, the προσήλυτος ("proselyte") link, the Western-text history, and the rival explanations.
**Documents under test:** `tripartite-law-paper.md` §5.4 and its footnote on πνικτοῦ ("strangled"). Secondary documents read at Phase 0.5: `Acts/dig-deeper-acts-sweep.md` (unit 20, Acts 15:1–35) and `Acts/book-overview-acts.md` (v2.3). `Acts/dig-deeper-acts-21-1to36.md` and the project note on the Acts apparatus were read at Phase 5.5, because they make claims about the decree's text.
**Primary texts:** SBLGNT (Greek NT); NA28 text and apparatus for Acts (Logos exports); WLC Hebrew; Swete LXX; BHS apparatus for Leviticus (Logos export). **Study text:** NASB95. **Pulpit text:** none. No sermon is in view.

## Positional Necessity Check (Phase 0.6)

**Q1 — what has the preceding movement set up that this passage provides?** Peter's house-visit (10:1–11:18) settled *that* Gentiles may be received: 11:18 says God has given the Gentiles repentance. It did not settle *on what terms* they may share life with Jewish believers. The Antioch mission (11:19–26; 13–14) turned one household into whole congregations, and 15:1 states the terms one party wants: Ἐὰν μὴ περιτμηθῆτε … οὐ δύνασθε σωθῆναι ("unless you are circumcised … you cannot be saved") `[T]`. The decree is the Council's answer to the question of terms. It is not an answer to the question of salvation, which 15:11 has already settled.

**Q2 — why here?** The decree is placed so that it is both **refused and qualified** in the same chapter. 15:19 refuses to "trouble" the Gentiles (μὴ παρενοχλεῖν), and 15:28 refuses to lay on them "any greater burden" (μηδὲν πλέον … βάρος). Between and after those refusals stand four items, grounded with a γάρ ("for") in a sentence about Moses: Μωϋσῆς γὰρ … ἐν ταῖς συναγωγαῖς κατὰ πᾶν σάββατον ἀναγινωσκόμενος ("for Moses … is read in the synagogues every Sabbath", 15:21) `[T]`. **Whatever 15:21 means precisely, Luke grounds the four abstentions in Moses being read.** That is the text-internal licence for looking in the Torah for their source, and §5.4 does not cite it.

**The positional finding that matters most is at 21:20–25, not in chapter 15.** James sets two groups side by side in one speech `[T]`:

- *Jewish* believers, μυριάδες ("myriads") who are all ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου ("zealous for the law", 21:20). They are rumoured to be taught not to circumcise their children (21:21), and Paul is to show that he walks φυλάσσων τὸν νόμον ("keeping the law", 21:24).
- *Gentile* believers, περὶ δὲ τῶν πεπιστευκότων ἐθνῶν ("but concerning the Gentiles who have believed", 21:25), for whom the four items are restated.

The δέ ("but") divides the two, and the verb is the same on both sides: φυλάσσων τὸν νόμον (21:24), φυλάσσεσθαι … ("to keep themselves from …", 21:25). **Acts itself therefore lays out the structure §5.4 infers.** Israel keeps the whole law. The non-Israelite living among Israel keeps a defined subset. That is the Torah's Israel/gēr distinction in narrative form, whether or not Luke had Leviticus 17–18 in view `[I]`, high. It is the strongest single support for the paper's conclusion, and it should be cited in the paper.

## Phase 0.5 — the Acts overview and sweep

**What the two documents already say.**

- **The sweep (unit 20)** treats the four abstentions as **occasional**. They are addressed to mixed table-fellowship, with 15:21 as the rationale. It warns that how far they bind is "a genuinely contested question". **It does not mention Leviticus 17–18.** Its Pitfall says 15:21 supplies the rationale: "Gentile believers are asked to do the minimum that makes a shared table possible."
- **The overview (v2.3)** has no row for Leviticus 17–18 in its Intertextual Map. The only Torah row near the Council is the Amos citation and the σκηνή ("tent") thread. The 21:1–36 solo dig lists "15:20/29 (the decree, echoing Lev 17–18 `[S]`)" in its source-distribution table. **The link has never been tested in the Acts library. This audit is the first test.**
- **Active threads.** *Presenting situation:* a mixed church needs ἀσφάλεια ("certainty", Luke 1:4) that the Gentile mission is God's doing. *Preaching trap 5:* supersessionism, and its opposite. Both bear on the result here. If the decree places Gentiles in the gēr's position, it is neither a supersessionist decree nor a Judaising one.

## Claims Audited

### Part 1 — Order

**Claim tested:** that the decree's sequence follows the sequence of the gēr laws in Leviticus 17–18. The paper does not state this claim. It is the first part of the brief, and it is the strongest form of the case if it holds.

**Finding.** Leviticus runs: sacrifice (17:8–9) → blood (17:10–12) → game blood (17:13) → the abominations (18:26). Number the decree's items in that sequence:

- **15:29** — idol-sacrifices · blood · strangled · fornication = **1 · 2 · 3 · 4** `[T]`
- **21:25** — the same = **1 · 2 · 3 · 4** `[T]`
- **15:20** — idol-pollutions · fornication · strangled · blood = **1 · 4 · 3 · 2** `[T]`

So **the two formal statements of the decree follow Leviticus exactly. The spoken proposal does not.** Four items can stand in twenty-four orders, and the letter and its later citation share the Levitical one. That is worth having, but not decisive. *Idolatry, then blood, then the ritual item, then the moral item* is also a natural order for anyone listing these things, and only the placing of πορνεία last is a real constraint. *Moderate confidence.*

**15:20 has a sense of its own** `[I]`, moderate. James pairs the idol item with πορνεία, then pairs strangled with blood. The pairing reflects the two kinds of item: two that concern the cult and the body, two that concern meat. It is also the order in which Lev 17:7 itself already links idolatry with "whoring" (ἐκπορνεύουσιν in the Greek, זֹנִים in the Hebrew). Either way, a speech can group items for argument while a letter sets them out in their source order.

**What §5.4 does.** It quotes the decree in **15:20's order** — "from what is polluted by idols, from πορνεία, from what is strangled, and from blood" — and then lists Leviticus in Leviticus's order. **The paper prints the one form of the decree that does not match, beside the source it claims to match.** That is easily fixed: cite 15:29 (and 21:25) as the form of the decree, and give 15:20's order as James's own grouping.

**Verdict: Confirmed with nuance.** Moderate.

### Part 2 — Mapping

**Claim (§5.4):** the four abstentions correspond to (a) sacrifice only at the tent, 17:8–9; (b) no eating of blood, 17:10–12; (c) draining the blood of game, 17:13; (d) the sexual prohibitions, 18:26.

**The distributional test — the audit's main finding.** Why these four laws and not the sojourner's other obligations? The Torah binds the gēr to Sabbath rest (Exod 20:10), to the Day of Atonement (Lev 16:29), against Molech (Lev 20:2) and against blasphemy (Lev 24:16). A mapping that chose four of these laws and ignored the rest would be selection, not correspondence. So the audit searched the WLC for verses containing both גֵּר ("sojourner") and תָּוֶךְ ("midst"), by lemma, across the whole Hebrew Bible:

- **Hebrew Bible: 15 verses** — Exod 12:49; Lev 16:29; 17:8, 10, 12, 13; 18:26; Num 15:14, 26, 29; 19:10; 35:15; Josh 20:9; Ezek 22:7; 47:22 (WLC). *Positive control:* Lev 17:8, read beforehand and known to contain both words, is returned.
- **Leviticus: 6 of the 18 verses that mention the gēr.** גֵּר occurs 21 times in 18 verses of Leviticus (WLC). The six are 16:29 and **17:8, 10, 12, 13; 18:26**.
- **Leviticus 17–26: exactly five verses — 17:8, 10, 12, 13 and 18:26 — and they carry precisely the four laws §5.4 names** (17:12 restates 17:10's blood law). Every other gēr verse in chapters 17–26 lacks the phrase: 17:15; 19:10, 33, 34; 20:2 (בְּיִשְׂרָאֵל, "in Israel"); 22:18; 23:22; 24:16, 22; 25:23, 35, 47 `[T]`.

**So the selection is not arbitrary. Within the Holiness Code, "the sojourner in your midst" is the formula of Leviticus 17–18 alone, and the four laws so marked are the four the decree lists.** The paper's choice of 17:8–9, 17:10–12, 17:13 and 18:26 is exactly the set the Hebrew marks out. This is the evidence §5.4 needs. It is also why 17:15, which binds the sojourner, does not belong in the list: it lacks the formula and imposes a purification rather than a prohibition `[T]` on both points. *High confidence on the distribution; the inference that the decree was built on it is* `[I]`, *moderate.* The distributional argument is associated in the literature with Richard Bauckham `[S]`, recalled and not yet checked — see Open Questions.

**Two qualifications to the distribution.**

1. Lev 16:29 also has "the sojourner in your midst", for the Day of Atonement, and lies just outside the Holiness Code. The pattern holds for chapters 17–26 and not for Leviticus as a whole. A reader who counts from 16:29 gets five laws, not four. The answer that 16:29 is a fast-day rest and not an abstention is reasonable but is `[I]`.
2. The paper quotes the gēr clause as וּמִן־הַגֵּר הַגָּר בְּתוֹכָם. In that exact form it stands only at 17:10 and 17:13. 17:8 has אֲשֶׁר־יָגוּר ("who sojourns"), and 17:12 and 18:26 have the second-person בְּתוֹכְכֶם ("in your midst") `[T]`. The shared element is גֵּר + בְּתוֹךְ, and the paper should quote it that way.

**Pair by pair.**

| Decree | Leviticus | Fit | Warrant |
|---|---|---|---|
| εἰδωλόθυτα / ἀλισγήματα τῶν εἰδώλων ("idol-sacrifices / pollutions of idols") | 17:8–9 — offer burnt offering or sacrifice only at the tent | **Partial.** Leviticus regulates where the sojourner *offers*; the decree regulates what the believer *eats*. The bridge is 17:7 (no sacrifice to the שְׂעִירִם, "goat demons"), which gives the reason for 17:3–9. But 17:7 is addressed to Israel, not the sojourner. | `[T]` on the texts; `[I]` on the bridge, moderate |
| αἷμα ("blood") | 17:10–12 — no eating of any blood | **Direct.** The only item where Acts and the Greek Leviticus share a word: αἷμα | `[T]`, high |
| πνικτόν ("strangled") | 17:13 — pour out the blood of game | **Conceptual.** Leviticus does not mention strangling. A strangled animal is one whose blood has not been poured out, so the item is a case of 17:13–14. **πνικτός does not occur anywhere in the Septuagint** (Swete, nil; positive control πνιγ- returns 1 Sam 16:15). It came from later Jewish usage, not from the Greek Bible | `[T]` on the absence; `[I]` on the pairing, moderate |
| πορνεία ("fornication") | 18:26 — "none of these abominations" | **Good, with one reservation.** The πορν- root occurs in Leviticus only at 17:7, 19:29, 20:5–6 and 21:7, 9, 14 (Swete), and **never in Leviticus 18**, which speaks of ἀσχημοσύνη ("nakedness"). The Hebrew chapter's catalogue (incest, adultery, male intercourse, bestiality) is what Second-Temple Greek would call πορνεία. 18:26's "these abominations" also covers **Molech at 18:21**, so the Levitical item is not purely sexual | `[T]` on the vocabulary; `[I]` on the equivalence, moderate–high |

**Verbal volume is very low, and that is itself a finding.** Of the decree's five key words, only αἷμα occurs in the Greek of Leviticus 17–18. εἰδωλόθυτον occurs once in the Septuagint (4 Macc 5:2, beside pork). ἀλίσγημα has only its verb, at Dan 1:8 (both Greek texts) and Sir 40:29, both about defiling food at a foreign table. πνικτός is absent. πορνεία is absent from Leviticus 18 (all Swete) `[T]`. **The decree does not quote Leviticus.** It summarises it in Luke's own vocabulary and in the vocabulary of Jewish food scruple (ἀλισγέω is Daniel's word for the king's table). This is not an allusion in the Hays sense of a verbal echo. It is a **legal correspondence**, and it has to be judged as one.

**Hays criteria (applied to the correspondence, not to a verbal echo):**

| Criterion | Score | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Strong | Leviticus was read in every synagogue, and **15:21 says so, as the ground of the decree** `[T]` |
| Volume | Weak | One shared word (αἷμα). The correspondence is in the content of the laws and their order, not in their wording `[T]` |
| Recurrence | Possible | Leviticus 17–18 is not cited elsewhere in Acts. But the decree itself recurs three times (15:20, 29; 21:25), and 16:4 calls it τὰ δόγματα ("the decrees") `[T]` |
| Thematic coherence | Strong | The question of 15:1–5 is on what terms non-Israelites belong among Israel, and Leviticus 17–18 is the Torah's answer for the non-Israelite who lives among Israel. 21:20–25 displays the two-tier structure in narrative `[I]`, high |
| Historical plausibility | Strong | James is Jerusalem-based and Torah-observant (21:20–24). An exegetical argument from the Hebrew of Leviticus fits him; the gēr question was live in Second-Temple Judaism `[I]`/`[S]` |
| History of interpretation | Strong | The standard modern explanation; held among others by Haenchen, Fitzmyer and Bauckham `[S]`, recalled, to be checked. Ancient reception went another way (see Part 4) |
| Satisfaction | Strong | It explains why there are *these four* items, why the letter has *this order*, why the ritual items stand beside a moral one, and why 15:21 appeals to Moses. No rival explains all four |

**Verdict: Confirmed with nuance.** Moderate, and the best single explanation on the evidence. §5.4's caution ("an inference, not a certainty") is right, but it understates the case. Recommended reframing: the four abstentions correspond to the four laws that Leviticus 17–26 marks with the formula "the sojourner in your midst". The correspondence is in substance and order, not in wording, and two of the four pairs (idols ↔ 17:8–9; strangled ↔ 17:13) are conceptual.

### Part 3 — The προσήλυτος link

**Claim tested:** that the Greek Leviticus's rendering of גֵּר as προσήλυτος makes the decree a ruling that Gentile believers stand where the Torah's proselytes stand.

**What the Greek does with the gēr.**

- **Swete renders every gēr in Lev 16:29–24:22 as προσήλυτος**, and in Leviticus 17–18 always with προσκείμενος / προσγενόμενος ἐν ὑμῖν ("attached / added among you"). It even **adds** the proselyte to the slaughter law at 17:3, where the Hebrew has no gēr. BHS records the plus `[T]`.
- **But at Deut 14:21, where the gēr is *permitted* to eat the carcass Israel may not, the Greek has πάροικος ("resident alien"),** not προσήλυτος: τῷ παροίκῳ τῷ ἐν ταῖς πόλεσίν σου δοθήσεται καὶ φάγεται ("it shall be given to the resident alien in your towns, and he shall eat it"; Swete numbers the verse 14:20) `[T]`. In Lev 17:15 the gēr who eats the same food is a προσήλυτος and must purify himself `[T]`.

**So the Greek translators were already sorting gērim.** The sojourner who is bound by Israel's purity law is a προσήλυτος; the sojourner who is not is a πάροικος. *Moderate–high confidence* on the pattern from these two verses. A full count of every gēr in the Torah against its Greek rendering has not been run, and the confidence should not be raised until it has. This sharpens the point the paper's own §4.1 makes from Deut 14:21 and Lev 17:15, that "the 'sojourner' category is not uniform across the Torah". **The Septuagint shows the same non-uniformity in its vocabulary.** It belongs in §4.1, not only §5.4.

**What Acts does with the word.** προσήλυτος occurs four times in the NT, all in Matthew and Acts: Matt 23:15; Acts 2:11; 6:5; 13:43 (SBLGNT). **It does not occur in Acts 15 or 21** `[T]`. There Luke's terms for the Gentile believers are τοῖς ἀπὸ τῶν ἐθνῶν ἐπιστρέφουσιν ἐπὶ τὸν θεόν ("those from the Gentiles who turn to God", 15:19), ἀδελφοῖς τοῖς ἐξ ἐθνῶν ("brothers from the Gentiles", 15:23) and τῶν πεπιστευκότων ἐθνῶν ("the Gentiles who have believed", 21:25) `[T]`. Where Luke does use προσήλυτος (Nicolaus, 6:5; the σεβόμενοι προσήλυτοι, "devout proselytes", 13:43), it names people who have *become Jews* `[I]`, high.

**The consequence.** If the decree is read through the Greek Leviticus, it makes Gentile believers "proselytes", and becoming a proselyte is exactly what 15:1 and 15:5 demand: περιτέμνειν αὐτοὺς παραγγέλλειν τε τηρεῖν τὸν νόμον Μωϋσέως ("to circumcise them and to direct them to observe the law of Moses") `[T]`. The Council's answer is the opposite of that. **The decree gives Gentile believers the gēr's obligations without the proselyte's conversion.** In Hebrew terms the category fits. In Greek terms it is a deliberate redefinition, which is why Luke does not use the word `[I]`, moderate.

**The link runs through the Hebrew, and that bears on the citation in 15:16–18.** The gēr laws' formula is גֵּר + בְּתוֹךְ ("in the midst"). The Greek of Leviticus renders the phrase ἐν ὑμῖν ("among you") every time, not ἐν μέσῳ ("in the midst") `[T]`. Two texts said to stand behind James's composite citation have the Hebrew word:

- **Jer 12:16** — וְנִבְנוּ בְּתוֹךְ עַמִּי ("they will be built up in the midst of my people"), of the nations who learn the ways of Israel; Greek οἰκοδομηθήσεται ἐν μέσῳ τοῦ λαοῦ μου `[T]`. Jer 12:15, the verse before, gives Acts 15:16 its opening (μετὰ … ἐπιστρέψω, "after … I will return", against Luke's Μετὰ ταῦτα ἀναστρέψω, "after these things I will return"). Swete's Amos 9:11 opens differently: Ἐν τῇ ἡμέρᾳ ἐκείνῃ ἀναστήσω ("in that day I will raise up") `[T]`. **Codex Bezae reads ἐπιστρέψω at Acts 15:16**, which is Jeremiah's verb (NA28 apparatus) `[T]`. At least one early copyist heard Jer 12:15 there. The sweep's own note that 15:15 introduces "the words of the prophets" in the *plural* points the same way.
- **Zech 2:15** (2:11 in the Greek) — וְנִלְווּ גוֹיִם רַבִּים … וְהָיוּ לִי לְעָם וְשָׁכַנְתִּי בְתוֹכֵךְ ("many nations shall join themselves … and shall be my people, and I will dwell in your midst"). Note לְעָם ("for a people") beside Acts 15:14's λαόν ("a people") `[T]`. The Greek makes the *nations* the ones who dwell: κατασκηνώσουσιν ἐν μέσῳ σου ("they shall dwell in your midst") `[T]`.

The argument — attributed to Bauckham `[S]`, to be checked — is that James connects the nations who will be in the midst of God's people (Jer 12:16; Zech 2:15) with the laws for the sojourner in the midst of Israel, and that the connecting word is בְּתוֹךְ. **The corpus confirms that the connecting word exists in the Hebrew of all three texts and is lost in the Greek of Leviticus** `[T]`. On this reading the decree is an argument a reader of the Hebrew would make and a reader of the Septuagint could not see unaided. That suits James at Jerusalem `[I]`, moderate. For Luke's Greek readers 15:21 does the work: Moses is read, and the hearer is pointed there. *Confidence: moderate.* The Jer 12:15–16 contact at 15:16 is a separate claim, and it belongs with the outstanding Amos audit.

**Verdict: Needs reframing.** The link does not run through the Greek προσήλυτος. Read through the Greek, it would undo the Council's decision. It runs through the Hebrew גֵּר and its formula בְּתוֹךְ. **§5.4 already works from the Hebrew and is right to.** It should say why the Greek cannot carry the link, and it should notice that Luke keeps the word "proselyte" out of the chapter.

### Part 4 — The Western-text history

**Claim tested (§5.4 n.):** "τοῦ πνικτοῦ / πνικτῶν carries a textual variant (omitted in the Western text). Confirm in the NA28 apparatus."

**The apparatus (NA28, Acts 15:20, 29; 21:25), read in full** `[T]`:

| | 15:20 | 15:29 | 21:25 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **πνικτ- omitted** | D gig; Irenaeus (Latin and the 1739 margin) | D l; Irenaeus (Latin and the 1739 margin); Tertullian; some manuscripts of Jerome | D gig |
| **πνικτ- read** (selected) | 𝔓⁴⁵ א C E L 323. 614. 945. 1175. 1241. 1505. 1739 𝔪 lat sy; 𝔓⁷⁴ A B Ψ 33. 81 (with the article) | א* A* B C 81. 614. 1175 (co); Clement; 𝔓⁷⁴ א² Aᶜ E L Ψ 33. 323. 945. 1241. 1505. 1739 𝔪 (lat) sy (without the article) | all other witnesses |
| **πορνεία omitted** | **𝔓⁴⁵** | — | — |
| **negative Golden Rule added** ("and whatever they do not wish to happen to themselves, not to do to others") | D 323. 945. 1739. 1891 sa; Irenaeus; Eusebius (1739 margin) | D 323. 614. 945. 1739. 1891 l p w sy^h** sa; Irenaeus; Eusebius; Cyprian | — |
| **other** | — | D: πράξατε φερόμενοι ἐν τῷ ἁγίῳ πνεύματι ("you will do well, being borne along by the Holy Spirit") | D L 323. 614. 1241 𝔪 gig and others: μηδὲν τοιοῦτον τηρεῖν αὐτοὺς εἰ μή ("that they observe no such thing, except …") |

**Three results.**

**(a) The footnote is right in substance and too broad in its wording.** The omission of πνικτ- is attested by **Codex Bezae, two Old Latin witnesses (gig, l), Irenaeus and Tertullian**, with some manuscripts of Jerome at 15:29. The family that most often travels with D in Acts, 323. 945. 1739. 1891, **adds the Golden Rule and keeps πνικτοῦ** at 15:20 `[T]`. So "omitted in the Western text" should be "omitted by D and some Latin witnesses, with Irenaeus and Tertullian". The ethical addition is the wider Western reading; the omission is narrower `[T]`, high.

**(b) There is a second short form, and it points the other way.** **𝔓⁴⁵ (third century) omits καὶ τῆς πορνείας at 15:20** and keeps idols, strangled and blood `[T]`. It is a single witness at a single place. Even so, the decree's text history now contains **two three-item forms that delete opposite members**:

- D / Irenaeus / Tertullian: **idolatry · πορνεία · blood**. A moral list. With the Golden Rule added, "blood" naturally means *bloodshed*, and the list matches the three sins later Jewish tradition held to be unpardonable `[S]`, recalled.
- 𝔓⁴⁵ (at 15:20 only): **idols · strangled · blood**. A food list.

**Each short form is explained as a simplification of the four-item text, towards ethics in one case and towards food in the other. Neither explains the other, and neither explains the four-item text.** That is the classic shape in which the longer reading is the source `[I]`, moderate–high. External attestation agrees: 𝔓⁷⁴ א A B C and the mass of witnesses have four items at all three places `[T]`.

**(c) A correction to the Acts reports.** `dig-deeper-acts-21-1to36.md` (Textual Variants (c)) and the project note on the Acts apparatus closures both say that the Western text applies "one editorial instinct at all three appearances": drop the ritual item, add an ethical maxim. **That holds for Codex Bezae and Irenaeus alone.** The two moves are spread over different witnesses: 323. 945. 1739. 1891 add the maxim and keep the ritual item. At 21:25 there is no Golden Rule, and the expansion there (μηδὲν τοιοῦτον …) is in the Majority text, not distinctively Western. **Neither report mentions 𝔓⁴⁵.** The finding should be restated as "Codex Bezae and Irenaeus consistently convert the decree into a moral rule; other witnesses take one step or the other." The method note those reports cite — *where a tradition tidies, look at what it tidied* — is strengthened, not weakened. Two traditions tidied the decree, in opposite directions.

**What the history means for the Leviticus claim.** The correspondence with Leviticus 17–18 **depends on the four-item text.** On D's text the Levitical link disappears, and the ethical reading is the natural one. On 𝔓⁴⁵'s text 18:26 disappears. The four-item text is secure, so the dependence does no damage. **But the reception is evidence in its own right.** By the second century, at least one major stream of the church no longer read the decree as Levitical. It treated the ritual items as either redundant or embarrassing, and it rewrote them `[I]`, moderate–high. That sits well with the paper's argument: the ancient church felt the very problem the paper discusses, a list that mixes moral and ritual items, and some copyists solved it by deleting one kind.

**Verdict: Confirmed with nuance** (the footnote), **with a correction to two Acts documents.** High on the data.

**Synthesis.** Only Leviticus 17–18 explains all three: the items, the order and the appeal to Moses. (c) and (d) explain the circumstances the decree addressed. (a) explains why one item (blood) could be presented as more than Mosaic. (b) explains how the decree was later read. **The rivals are mostly answers to different questions.** The one real competitor for the *source* is (c), and its best evidence (Revelation 2) concerns two items, not four. *Moderate confidence.*

**The paper's conclusion, independent of the source question.** §5.4 concludes that the apostles answered by "neither the tripartite scheme nor the 'only what is repeated' rule". **That holds on every one of the five explanations.** On all of them, πορνεία (a "moral" law) and blood / strangled meat (a "ceremonial" or food law) stand in one list, with one verb (ἀπέχεσθαι, "abstain"), one rationale (15:21) and one classification, ἐπάναγκες ("necessary", 15:28) `[T]`. The decree does not sort its items into moral and ceremonial. The paper can make this point first and without qualification. The Levitical source then explains *why* the decree's line falls where it does.

## What the Audit Adds

1. **The decisive evidence is distributional, and it is in the Hebrew.** Within Leviticus 17–26, "the sojourner in your/their midst" (גֵּר + בְּתוֹךְ) stands in exactly the five verses that carry the four laws (WLC). This turns §5.4's "often proposed" into a checkable claim.
2. **Acts 21:20–25 is the narrative proof of the paper's conclusion.** Jewish believers keep the law, Gentile believers keep the four, and the δέ ("but") at 21:25 divides them. The paper should cite it.
3. **15:21 is the textual licence.** The decree is grounded, with γάρ ("for"), in Moses being read.
4. **The Septuagint already distinguishes two kinds of sojourner** (προσήλυτος at Lev 17:15; πάροικος at Deut 14:21). This strengthens §4.1.
5. **𝔓⁴⁵ omits πορνεία at 15:20.** With D's omission of πνικτόν, the text history holds two short forms that delete opposite items. This is good evidence for the four-item text, and it is new to the library.
6. **Revelation 2:14, 20, 24 is a second NT witness to a decree-like pair and a "no other burden" formula.** It is new to the library and bears on rival (c).
7. **πνικτός is absent from the whole Septuagint** (Swete). The decree's third item is not Greek-Bible vocabulary.
