# Luke's Purpose and Plan in the Telling and Retelling of Cornelius

**Acts 10:1–48 · Acts 11:1–18**

*A summary statement. The evidence, the counts, the warrant tags and the contested points are in `acts-cornelius-authorial-intent-10-11` and in the solo dig `dig-deeper-acts-10-1to11-18`; this document states the conclusions.*

**Date:** 20 November 2026

---

## The governing principle

One event, two texts, one author. The event happened once and does not vary, so **every difference between the two accounts is located in the text and nowhere else** — in what Luke selects, what he omits, who he lets speak, and where he puts it.

With Saul that principle had to work across thirteen chapters. Here it works across a single page, and that makes it sharper. **The reader has just read the thing being retold.** There is no possibility of the second account serving as a reminder. Luke spends eighteen verses re-narrating what he has narrated in the preceding forty-eight, to a reader who cannot have forgotten it — which is an extravagant use of narrative space unless the retelling is doing work the telling could not do.

And the repetition is not confined to the two tellings. The angel's instruction to Cornelius is reported four times by four speakers in sixty-six verses — the narrator at 10:3–6, the messengers at 10:22, Cornelius himself at 10:30–32, and Peter reporting Cornelius at 11:13–14, which is at third hand. Peter's vision is told twice. **This is not a narrative with a retelling attached; it is built out of repetition throughout.**

So the question is not *what happened at Caesarea?* but *why has he written it twice, and in these two shapes?*

---

## 1 · Acts 10:1–48 — the evidence, entered first

### Purpose

To put on the reader's own record, at full length and on the narrator's authority, that **God admitted the Gentiles before the church had any view on the matter** — so that when the objection is raised at 11:2, the reader is already able to judge it, and to judge it as the Jerusalem church cannot, because the reader has *seen* what Jerusalem has only been *told*.

### Plan

**He narrates it himself, and at length.** Chapter 10 runs to 836 words of Greek, and nothing that can be shown directly is reported at second hand: the reader sees the angel, sees the sheet, hears the voice, hears the sermon, and sees the Spirit fall. **Everything Peter will later assert in Jerusalem, the reader has already watched.** That is the precondition of the retelling working at all, and it is why the order cannot be reversed.

**He places it where only one question is left untested.** By chapter 10 a Gentile has already been baptised (8:38) and the apostle to the Gentiles has already been commissioned (9:15). What the book has not tested is whether a Jewish believer may share a table with Gentiles and survive the report — and that is the question actually put at 11:3: *εἰσῆλθες πρὸς ἄνδρας ἀκροβυστίαν ἔχοντας καὶ συνέφαγες αὐτοῖς*. It is a question about the table, not about salvation.

**He pairs it with Saul's call, and shelves Saul in order to do so.** 9:30 puts Saul on a boat to Tarsus and he does not return until 11:25. In between, Peter goes to Caesarea and the Spirit falls on the uncircumcised. **The apostle to the Gentiles is commissioned and removed from the stage; the apostle to the circumcision is sent to the Gentile.** Acts 9 and Acts 10 are a single piece of construction, and neither makes its full sense without the other.

**He lodges the apostle with a tanner before he shows him the sheet.** 9:43 leaves Peter staying with Simon a *βυρσεύς*, a word standing in three New Testament verses, all in this stretch (9:43; 10:6; 10:32). Before the vision about clean and unclean creatures, Luke has put the apostle in a house that handled their carcasses.

**He puts the Gentile first and gives him the better part.** The narrative opens in Caesarea, not Joppa, and God addresses Cornelius a full day before he addresses Peter. The dossier at 10:1–2 is the narrator's own verdict and not any character's — *εὐσεβής*, *φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν*, *ἐλεημοσύνας πολλάς*, *δεόμενος διὰ παντός* — and the vocabulary is loaded: *δεκτός* with *μνημόσυνον* stands in exactly one verse of the whole Greek Bible, Sirach 32:9, and *δεκτός* elsewhere is the altar's word for an offering declared fit. **The asymmetry of response is Luke's too: the Gentile obeys immediately, and the apostle refuses three times.**

**He makes God the subject of every decisive verb.** An angel comes in (10:3), a voice comes (10:13), the Spirit says (10:19), *ἐγὼ ἀπέσταλκα* (10:20), God showed me (10:28), the Spirit fell (10:44), God gave (11:17), God granted (11:18). Peter's verbs are reactive — perplexed, going down, catching up. **His one unprompted act in the whole chapter is to refuse.**

### What this telling will not bear

It is not a commendation of Cornelius's piety as the ground of his acceptance, and the sheet is not an allegory to be decoded creature by creature. Peter supplies the single interpretation himself at 10:28, and it is about **people**.

## 2 · Acts 11:1–18 — the hearing, answered by narrative

### Purpose

To convert a private act into a **public precedent**, and to do it by evidence rather than by argument — so that what goes on the record is what God did, and not what Peter concluded. Luke could have had Peter win the debate. He has him decline to debate.

### Plan

**Peter answers the charge that was actually brought, and nothing else.** Measured against chapter 10, the retelling keeps three words in ten overall — but it keeps **nearly nine in ten of the vision and three in a hundred of the sermon**. That is not compression; a summary shortens everything roughly in proportion. This shortens almost nothing and deletes almost everything, and the thing it selects by is the accusation. The charge at 11:3 is about going in and eating; the vision is the evidence that bears on it, and the sermon bears on nothing that was alleged.

**He keeps the detail that does him no credit.** 11:8 reproduces his own refusal of the Lord's command. He could have left it out. The vision comes through nearly whole, blemish included, because the vision is the evidence.

**He drops everything that could make the act look like his own.** The entire piety dossier goes: *εὐσεβής*, *ἐλεημοσύνη*, *μνημόσυνον*, *δίκαιος*, *δεκτός* — every one of them stands in chapter 10 and **not one appears anywhere in 11:1–18**. *προσωπολήμπτης* goes, a New Testament hapax, and with it the thesis of 10:34–35 that God shows no partiality, so that **the chapter's theological centre is not repeated**. The 185 words of christological proclamation go — the resurrection, the appointed Judge of living and dead, forgiveness through the name. Simon the tanner goes, and so does Peter's hunger, so that the first mention of eating in the retelling is God's command rather than Peter's appetite.

**The result of all that dropping is the point.** In Jerusalem Peter does not argue that Cornelius deserved it. He does not argue that God is impartial. He does not preach. **He narrates.** Every ground on which a man might be thought to have made his own case is removed, and what is left is a sequence of things done to him.

**What he adds is warrants, not doctrine** — three authorities outside himself. At 11:14, the angel's promise that Peter's words will save the household, *ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ σὺ καὶ πᾶς ὁ οἶκός σου* — **a clause standing nowhere in chapter 10**, where the angel's speech has no soteriological content at all. At 11:16, *ἐμνήσθην τοῦ ῥήματος τοῦ κυρίου*: he does not reason his way to a conclusion, he remembers a dominical saying and finds the event has fulfilled it. At 11:17, *ἐγὼ τίς ἤμην δυνατὸς κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν;* — which escalates 10:47's *κωλύω* from the water to God himself.

**He insists he had barely started.** The narrator says the Spirit fell while Peter was still speaking (10:44); Peter says it fell *as he began* to speak (11:15). The second is the stronger claim, and Peter is the one who makes it about himself. **The preaching cannot be credited with the result, and the preacher is the one who says so.**

**Luke marks the act of telling with his own preface vocabulary.** *ἀρξάμενος δὲ Πέτρος ἐξετίθετο αὐτοῖς καθεξῆς* (11:4). *καθεξῆς* stands in five New Testament verses, all in Luke–Acts, and **11:4 is the only place in either volume where it describes a character giving an account of events**. The verb it governs, *ἐκτίθημι*, occurs as a verb of knowledge-transfer three times, all in Acts, and all three are orderly or sustained exposition. So at 11:4 Luke shows an eyewitness doing what his own preface claims to do, and shows the method working. One qualification, from the library: the orderliness in view is **persuasive arrangement, not chronological sequence** — which is exactly what selection-by-charge requires.

**And the verdict matches the method.** *ἀκούσαντες δὲ ταῦτα ἡσύχασαν καὶ ἐδόξασαν τὸν θεόν* (11:18). **They do not concede to Peter. They fall silent and glorify God** — which is the only response a narrative of this kind leaves available.

### What this telling will not bear

It is not a model for apologetics and not a template for testimony. It is a defendant answering one charge by refusing to argue, and its force depends on the hearer having already watched chapter 10 — which no hearer of a testimony ever has.

## The two together

**One telling and one retelling, and the retelling is by the one man who was there.** That is the whole architecture, and it is the reason the unit is shaped as it is: the evidence is entered by the narrator, and then the witness is put up.

**The retelling is the hinge the Council later swings on.** *διακρίνω* stands four times in Acts and all four are in this single argument: the Spirit tells Peter to go *μηδὲν διακρινόμενος* (10:20), the circumcision party *διεκρίνοντο* with him (11:2), Peter reports that he went *μηδὲν διακρίναντα* (11:12), and at the Council the subject becomes God — *οὐθὲν διέκρινεν μεταξὺ ἡμῶν τε καὶ αὐτῶν* (15:9). Command, objection, compliance, divine precedent. **Without 11:12 on the record, 15:9 has nothing to quote.**

**Set against Saul, the two retellings share an instinct and apply it in opposite directions.** Saul's conversion and Cornelius are the only events in Acts that Luke narrates more than once. Both are moments on which the legitimacy of the Gentile mission rests; both commissions come in a trance (*ἔκστασις*, 10:10, 11:5, 22:17); both men afterwards have to defend what they received to Jerusalem; and in both, Luke marks the act of retelling with preface vocabulary. But in Paul's accounts the mediator shrinks to nothing while the mandate grows and its speaker rises — **weight transferred from the mediator to the mandate**. In Peter's retelling the human contribution is stripped out and **nothing grows in its place**.

**The reason is that the shape of each retelling is dictated by the accusation.** Paul is charged with apostasy and must show continuity, so his tellings accumulate authority. Peter is charged with an act and must show he did not initiate it, so his telling sheds everything that could look like initiative. That the two shapes answer their two charges so exactly is the strongest single indication that the shapes are designed.

**And read in order, the two endings are one argument:**

| | Telling | Ends with |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | the narrator, to the reader | the Spirit poured out on Gentiles, and no one able to withhold the water |
| **11** | the witness, to Jerusalem | silence, and glory given to God |

**God did it; Peter could only report it; Jerusalem could only fall silent.** The chapter establishes the fact and the hearing establishes the precedent, and Luke needed both because a thing seen by one apostle in a Gentile house is not yet something the church can be held to.
