The Passage

NA28:

5 Νεκρώσατε οὖν τὰ μέλη τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, πορνείαν ἀκαθαρσίαν πάθος ἐπιθυμίαν κακήν, καὶ τὴν πλεονεξίαν, ἥτις ἐστὶν εἰδωλολατρία, 6 δι᾽ ἃ ἔρχεται ἡ ὀργὴ τοῦ θεοῦ [ἐπὶ τοὺς υἱοὺς τῆς ἀπειθείας]. 7 ἐν οἷς καὶ ὑμεῖς περιεπατήσατέ ποτε, ὅτε ἐζῆτε ἐν τούτοις· 8 νυνὶ δὲ ἀπόθεσθε καὶ ὑμεῖς τὰ πάντα, ὀργήν, θυμόν, κακίαν, βλασφημίαν, αἰσχρολογίαν ἐκ τοῦ στόματος ὑμῶν· 9 μὴ ψεύδεσθε εἰς ἀλλήλους, ἀπεκδυσάμενοι τὸν παλαιὸν ἄνθρωπον σὺν ταῖς πράξεσιν αὐτοῦ 10 καὶ ἐνδυσάμενοι τὸν νέον τὸν ἀνακαινούμενον εἰς ἐπίγνωσιν κατ᾽ εἰκόνα τοῦ κτίσαντος αὐτόν, 11 ὅπου οὐκ ἔνι Ἕλλην καὶ Ἰουδαῖος, περιτομὴ καὶ ἀκροβυστία, βάρβαρος, Σκύθης, δοῦλος, ἐλεύθερος, ἀλλὰ [τὰ] πάντα καὶ ἐν πᾶσιν Χριστός.

Where the SBLGNT differs: it prints 3:6’s “upon the sons of disobedience” without brackets, with a note of the omission, and prints τὰ πάντα (3:11) without brackets; NA28 brackets both, marking its doubt [T: as each edition prints it]. See Textual Variants.

ESV (pulpit text):

5 Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry. 6 On account of these the wrath of God is coming. 7 In these you too once walked, when you were living in them. 8 But now you must put them all away: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk from your mouth. 9 Do not lie to one another, seeing that you have put off the old self with its practices 10 and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge after the image of its creator. 11 Here there is not Greek and Jew, circumcised and uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ is all, and in all.

Headline Findings

  1. “Put to death therefore” is the letter’s answer to the regime it has just rejected — and the “members” to be killed are named by the list that follows, not by the limbs of the body. [T]/[I] Surfaced by: Positional Necessity, Linking Words, Translations, Move 4. οὖν (“therefore”, 3:5) draws on “you died” (3:3) and repeats 3:2’s “on earth” (τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς) [T]. Two verses earlier the rules were “of no value against fleshly indulgence” (2:23); now the flesh is dealt with — by death, not regulation [T]/[I]. “The members which are on earth” are set in apposition to five accusatives — immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, greed — so the “members” are those practices [T]. The 2:16–23 dig reached the same reading [S: dig] (reached independently). The NASB95 restructures the verse (“consider the members of your earthly body as dead to immorality …”), softening the imperative into a reckoning and turning the list into what one is dead to (Tool 8). High.

  2. Two lists of five, moving from the body and possessions to the tongue — and the second list begins with the word for God’s wrath. [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Repetition, Vocabulary. 3:5: πορνεία, ἀκαθαρσία, πάθος, ἐπιθυμία κακή, πλεονεξία (sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, greed); 3:8: ὀργή, θυμός, κακία, βλασφημία, αἰσχρολογία (anger, wrath, malice, slander, foul speech), “from your mouth”, followed by “do not lie to one another” (3:9) [T]. 3:12 will answer with five virtues [T]. ὀργή (“wrath, anger”) stands in Colossians only at 3:6 (“the wrath of God is coming”) and 3:8 (“put away … anger”) (SBLGNT) [T]: the anger believers are to put off is the very word for the wrath that comes on these things [I]. High on the words.

  3. Greed is called idolatry — and the first list ends where idolatry begins. [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Tool 11 (Eph 5:5; Wisdom 14). πλεονεξία (“greed”) is defined: ἥτις ἐστὶν εἰδωλολατρία, “which is idolatry” [T]. Ephesians makes the same identification of the greedy person (πλεονέκτης, ὅ ἐστιν εἰδωλολάτρης, Eph 5:5); the two nouns stand together within a verse only at Col 3:5, and the agent nouns at 1 Cor 5:10, 11 and Eph 5:5 (SBLGNT) [T]. The Greek Bible’s Wisdom of Solomon traces sexual immorality to idolatry (“the devising of idols was the beginning of fornication”, Wis 14:12) — the same field, approached from the other end [T]/[I]. High on the identification; moderate on the field.

  4. The old self has been stripped off and the new put on — done, as Christ stripped off the powers — and the new self is being renewed after the image of the Creator. [T] Surfaced by: Vocabulary, Original Languages, Tool 11 (Gen 1:26–27), Move 4. ἀπεκδυσάμενοι … ἐνδυσάμενοι (“having stripped off … having put on”, aorist participles, 3:9–10) — ἀπεκδύομαι stands in the NT only at 2:15 (Christ “stripped off” the rulers and authorities) and 3:9 (SBLGNT) [T]; the 2:6–15 dig recorded the pair [S: dig]. The new self is “being renewed” (present passive) “into knowledge, according to the image of the One who created him” (κατ᾽ εἰκόνα τοῦ κτίσαντος αὐτόν) — Gen 1:27, κατ᾽ εἰκόνα θεοῦ ἐποίησεν αὐτόν (“according to the image of God he made him”), rated high by the overview and confirmed high at 3:10 by claim audit 1 [S: audit 1]. εἰκών (“image”) and κτίζω (“to create”) stand in Colossians only at 1:15–16 and 3:10 (SBLGNT) [T]: the image in which the new humanity is renewed is the Son who is “the image of the invisible God” [I], moderate–high. High on the words.

  5. In the renewed humanity the letter’s own divisions are named and dissolved, and “Christ is all” takes up the confession’s “all things”. [T] Surfaced by: Structure, Vocabulary, Tool 11 (Gal 3:28; 1 Cor 12:13), Move 4. The list begins “Greek and Jew” — the reverse of Gal 3:28 and 1 Cor 12:13, which begin with the Jew [T]. It adds “circumcision and uncircumcision” (the letter’s own words at 2:11, 13), “barbarian, Scythian” (Σκύθης, its only NT occurrence; a byword for savagery in 2 Macc 4:47 and 3 Macc 7:5), and “slave, free” (who will be addressed at 3:22–4:1 and embodied in Onesimus, 4:9) [T]. It has no “male and female”; some Western witnesses add it from Gal 3:28 [T]. And it ends ἀλλὰ [τὰ] πάντα καὶ ἐν πᾶσιν Χριστός — the τὰ πάντα (“all things”) created, held and reconciled in him (1:16, 17, 20) [T]. High on the words; moderate–high that the ordering is deliberate.

The Sixteen Tools

1. Author’s Purpose

Book-level: to keep the church walking in Christ, persuaded it lacks nothing, and to show what that sufficiency looks like in speech, household and work (overview) [I].

Passage-level: to show the first practical shape of the risen life: what must die (the desires and greed of 3:5), what must be put off (the anger and speech of 3:8–9), on what ground (the old self already stripped off, the new already put on), and with what result (a renewed humanity in which Christ is all) [T]/[I]. It does this as the alternative to the rival’s ascetic rules (2:20–23): the remedy for the flesh is union with Christ worked out in death and renewal, not a regime [I], high.

2. Context

Positional Necessity Check

Preceding movement: 3:1–4 said “you died … your life is hidden with Christ”, and “set your minds … not on the things on earth” [T]. Before that, 2:23 judged the rival’s regime “of no value against fleshly indulgence” [T]. And 2:11 said that in Christ they had been circumcised “in the stripping off (ἀπέκδυσις) of the body of the flesh” [T].

Necessity answer — why the passage exists here.

  1. 3:3 requires 3:5. “You died” (indicative) must be lived: “put to death therefore” (imperative) [T]. The first command of the new life follows the first statement of the old life’s end [I].
  2. 2:23 requires it. Having denied that rules restrain the flesh, the letter must say what does [I]. The 2:16–23 dig recorded the link 2:23 → 3:5 [S: dig] (reached independently).
  3. 2:11–15 is cashed. The stripping off of 2:11 and 2:15 becomes the believers’ stripping off of the old self (3:9); the circumcision “made without hands” (2:11) becomes a people where “circumcised and uncircumcised” no longer divide (3:11) [T].
  4. The confession reaches the church. The “image” (1:15) and the “all things” (1:16–20) arrive in the life of the congregation (3:10–11) [T].

Implication for this passage: it is not a free-standing moral list. Its commands rest on four earlier passages, and preached without them it becomes exactly the “do not handle, do not taste” religion the letter has just rejected [I], high.

Revisit after Tool 11: Move 2 (Gen 1) adds that the renewal is creation’s purpose restored: the humanity made “according to the image” is being remade according to it [I].

Immediately before: 3:1–4 (above). Immediately after: 3:12, “So, as those who have been chosen of God, holy and beloved, put on …” — the clothing image continued positively; οὖν (“therefore”) again [T].

Section: 3:5–17 is the overview’s “put off, put on”; 3:5–11 is its first half. The break at 3:11/3:12 is real (a new imperative, Ἐνδύσασθε οὖν, “put on therefore”) [T], but 3:9–10 has already announced the putting on [T].

3. Structure

Verses Section Function
3:5–7 Put to death: the first list Command, five vices, greed defined as idolatry; the wrath coming; “you once walked” in them
3:8–9a Put away: the second list “But now” — five sins of temper and tongue; “do not lie to one another”
3:9b–10 The ground: old self off, new self on Two aorist participles; the new self renewed after the Creator’s image
3:11 The new humanity No Greek and Jew … slave, free; “Christ is all, and in all”

Device 1 — “once … but now”. ποτε (“once”, 3:7) … νυνὶ δέ (“but now”, 3:8) — the second and last occurrence of each word in the letter; the first was 1:21–22 (overview Echo 8) [T]. At 1:21–22 the turn was God’s act (reconciled); here it is the believers’ response (put away) [T]/[I].

Device 2 — balanced lists. Five and five (3:5; 3:8), answered by five virtues at 3:12 [T]. The first list moves inward and down — from act (immorality) to impurity, passion, desire, and greed — to the root [I], moderate. The second moves outward — from inner anger to speech [I].

Device 3 — clothing. ἀποτίθημι (“put away”, 3:8), ἀπεκδύομαι (“strip off”, 3:9), ἐνδύω (“put on”, 3:10, then 3:12) [T]. The clothing language runs on into 3:12–14 [T].

Central assertions, one line each: - 3:5 — command: put to death what belongs to the earthly life. - 3:8–9a — command: put away anger and evil speech; do not lie to one another. - 3:9b–10 — truth (ground): you have stripped off the old self and put on the new, which is being renewed after the image of its Creator. - 3:11 — truth: in that renewal the human divisions are gone; Christ is all.

Three imperatives, one long ground, one statement of the new reality [T]. The commands rest on the participles, not the reverse [I], high — the overview’s moralism check in grammatical form.

4. Linking Words

  • οὖν, 3:5 — “therefore” →therefore→ from 3:1–4, especially “you died” (3:3) [T]. In the letter οὖν stands at 2:6, 2:16, 3:1, 3:5 and 3:12; the last three launch the new life (the 2:16–23 dig’s count [S: dig]).
  • ἥτις ἐστίν, 3:5 — “which is”: definition — greed is idolatry [T].
  • δι᾽ ἅ, 3:6 — “on account of which” ←for←: the reason the members must die — God’s wrath comes because of them [T].
  • ἐν οἷς … ὅτε … ἐν τούτοις, 3:7 — “in which … when … in these”: the past life. If “upon the sons of disobedience” is read at 3:6, ἐν οἷς can mean “among whom” (people), and ἐν τούτοις “in these things” (vices); without it, both mean the vices (Textual Variants) [T]/[I].
  • νυνὶ δέ, 3:8 — “but now”: the turn (Tool 3) [T].
  • καὶ ὑμεῖς, 3:7 and 3:8 — “you too”: twice — you too walked in them; you too put them away [T]. The repetition binds the past and the present to the same people [I].
  • ἀπεκδυσάμενοι … ἐνδυσάμενοι, 3:9–10: participles grounding “do not lie” — “since you have stripped off … and put on” [I], high. An imperatival reading (“strip off … put on”, as in Eph 4:22–24’s infinitives) is possible [S], but the aorists and the parallel with 2:11, 15 favour a completed fact [I], moderate–high (Original Languages).
  • ὅπου, 3:11 — “where”: the sphere of the new self — “in which renewal” (NASB95 supplies the noun) [T]/[I].
  • ἀλλά, 3:11 — “but”: the strong contrast: not these categories, but Christ [T].

5. Parallels

Prose, with three parallel constructions [T]: - The two five-item lists (3:5; 3:8), each in apposition to a collective object (“the members”; “all things”) [T]. - ἀπεκδυσάμενοι / ἐνδυσάμενοι (3:9–10) — antithetic: off / on; old / new [T]. - 3:11’s pairs — Greek and Jew; circumcision and uncircumcision (each joined by καί); then four unjoined terms, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free [T]. The first two pairs are religious-ethnic opposites; “barbarian, Scythian” is not an opposite but an intensification (the outsider, and the outsider’s outsider) [I], moderate; “slave, free” a social opposite [T].

7. Vocabulary

Proper-noun and group inventory: Ἕλλην (“Greek”), Ἰουδαῖος (“Jew”), βάρβαρος (“barbarian”), Σκύθης (“Scythian”); Χριστός (“Christ”) [T]. Triage: Greek and Jew — routine in Paul; Scythian — a candidate: its only NT occurrence (SBLGNT) [T]. In Swete, Σκύθης as a people stands in five verses; two use it as a byword for barbarity: men who “would have been released as innocent even if they had pleaded before Scythians” (2 Macc 4:47) and men “filled with a cruelty more savage than the law of the Scythians” (3 Macc 7:5) [T] (the remaining hits on the stem are σκυθρωπός, “sullen”, a false friend, dropped, and the place-name Scythopolis). So a hearer of the Greek Bible would hear “the most barbarous of barbarians” [I], moderate–high. βάρβαρος is relative: in Ps 113:1 LXX Egypt is “a barbarian people”; in 2 Macc 2:21 the Greek armies are “the barbarian hordes” [T]. A list beginning “Greek” and including “barbarian” looks at humanity through Greek eyes and then dissolves the view [I], moderate.

νεκρόω (“to put to death”), 3:5 [T], high. Three NT verses: Rom 4:19 and Heb 11:12 (Abraham’s body “as good as dead”) and here (SBLGNT) [T]. Here an aorist imperative: a decisive act, not a state of reckoning [T]/[I]. The NASB95’s “consider … as dead” borrows the sense of Rom 6:11 (“consider yourselves to be dead to sin”, λογίζεσθε), which is a different verb [T]/[I].

τὰ μέλη τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς (“the members which are on earth”), 3:5 [T], moderate–high on the reading. μέλος (“member, limb”) stands only here in the letter (SBLGNT) [T]. The phrase “on earth” repeats 3:2 [T]. The members are identified by the following list, in apposition [T]; the limbs of the body are not literally to be killed (cf. 2:23’s rejection of severity to the body) [I], high. Rom 6:13, 19 uses μέλη for the bodily members presented to sin or righteousness [T] — Colossians stretches the word to the practices themselves [I].

The first list (3:5) [T] for the words; confidence flags as stated: - πορνεία — sexual immorality of every kind [S], high. - ἀκαθαρσία — impurity; in Paul often sexual (Rom 1:24; 1 Thess 4:7) [T]. - πάθος — passion; three NT verses: Rom 1:26 (“degrading passions”), 1 Thess 4:5 (“lustful passion”, πάθει ἐπιθυμίας), here (SBLGNT) [T]. - ἐπιθυμία κακή — evil desire [T]. - πλεονεξία — greed, the desire to have more; ten NT verses, often beside sexual sins (Rom 1:29; Eph 4:19; 5:3; 1 Thess 2:5) [T]. Defined as idolatry (Headline 3).

1 Thess 4:3–7 — the closest parallel in substance [T], high on the words. “This is the will of God, your sanctification: that you abstain from sexual immorality (πορνείας) … not in lustful passion (πάθει ἐπιθυμίας), like the Gentiles who do not know God; and that no man transgress and defraud (πλεονεκτεῖν) his brother … because the Lord is the avenger in all these things … For God has not called us for the purpose of impurity (ἀκαθαρσίᾳ)” [T]. All five items of Col 3:5 stand there in the same or cognate form, with judgment (“the Lord is the avenger”) as Col 3:6 has the wrath [T]/[I]. πάθος with ἐπιθυμία in one verse: only Col 3:5 and 1 Thess 4:5 (SBLGNT) [T].

ὀργή (“wrath”), 3:6, and (“anger”), 3:8 [T], high. Headline 2. ἔρχεται (“is coming”) is present: the wrath is already on its way [T]; the NASB95’s “will come” makes it future (Tool 8). ὀργή with θυμός (“anger and rage”) stands within a verse in six NT verses, among them Eph 4:31 and Rom 2:8 (of God) (SBLGNT) [T].

The second list (3:8) [T]: - ὀργή, θυμός — anger, rage [T]. - κακία — malice [T]. - βλασφημία — slander, abusive speech against others [S], high. - αἰσχρολογία — its only NT occurrence (SBLGNT) [T]. “Foul talk”: obscene or abusive speech; the word can cover both [S], moderate. The ESV chooses “obscene talk”, the NASB95 “abusive speech” (Tool 8). - ἐκ τοῦ στόματος ὑμῶν — “from your mouth”: the second list is about speech [T].

ὁ παλαιὸς ἄνθρωπος (“the old self”), 3:9 [T], high. παλαιός with ἄνθρωπος stands in Rom 6:6 (“our old self was crucified with him”), Eph 4:22 and Col 3:9 (and Matt 13:52 in another sense) (SBLGNT) [T]. In Romans the old self was crucified; here it has been stripped off [T]. Both describe a past event in union with Christ [I], high.

ἀπεκδύομαι (“to strip off”), 3:9 [T], high. Headline 4. The noun ἀπέκδυσις stands once in the NT, at 2:11 (“the stripping off of the body of the flesh”) (SBLGNT) [T]. The three uses form a chain: stripping off in Christ’s circumcision (2:11); Christ stripping off the powers (2:15); believers having stripped off the old self (3:9) [T]. The 2:6–15 dig found a slight lean to “stripped off from himself” at 2:15, on the analogy of this verse’s middle [S: dig].

ὁ νέος (“the new”), 3:10 [T], moderate on the nuance. νέος stands only here in Colossians; Eph 4:24 has καινός (“new”) for the new self (SBLGNT) [T]. Whether νέος (“young, recent”) and καινός (“new in kind”) differ in force here is debated [S]; the participle that follows — “being renewed” (ἀνακαινούμενον, from the καιν- root) — supplies the “new in kind” sense [T]/[I].

ἀνακαινόω (“to renew”), 3:10 [T], high. Two NT verses: 2 Cor 4:16 (“our inner man is being renewed day by day”) and here (SBLGNT) [T]. Both present passive: renewal as a continuing work of God [T]/[I].

εἰς ἐπίγνωσιν (“into knowledge”), 3:10 [T], high. The letter’s fifth ἐπίγνωσις — after 1:9, 1:10, 2:2 [T]. The knowledge Paul prayed for (1:9–10) is the goal of the renewal (3:10) [T]/[I].

ὅπου οὐκ ἔνι (“where there is not”), 3:11 [T], high. ἔνι stands in four NT verses: 1 Cor 6:5, Gal 3:28, Col 3:11, Jas 1:17 (SBLGNT) [T]. Gal 3:28 uses it three times (“there is neither Jew nor Greek … slave nor free … male and female”) [T].

[τὰ] πάντα καὶ ἐν πᾶσιν Χριστός (“Christ is all, and in all”), 3:11 [T], high. τὰ πάντα: the confession’s “all things” (1:16 twice, 1:17, 1:20) [T]. 8. Translations

  1. 3:5 — Νεκρώσατε οὖν τὰ μέλη τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς. The pulpit text is closer here than the study text. The overview’s Pulpit Note makes the same point [S].

  2. 3:5 — πλεονεξία. ESV “covetousness”; NASB95 “greed” [T]. No difference in sense; “covetousness” connects to the tenth commandment for an English hearer [I].

  4. 3:7 — ἐν οἷς. ESV “In these you too once walked” (the vices); NASB95 “and in them you also once walked” (ambiguous: the vices or the sons of disobedience) [T]. The difference follows from 3:6 (Textual Variants).

  6. 3:8 — αἰσχρολογία. ESV “obscene talk”; NASB95 “abusive speech”; KJV “filthy communication” [T]. The word can cover both; the ESV narrows to the sexual, the NASB95 to the aggressive [I].

9. Tone and Feel

Urgent and severe, then expansive. Three aorist imperatives and one present — “put to death”, “put away”, “do not lie” [T]. Lists hammered out without connectives (asyndeton) [T]. A note of judgment: “the wrath of God is coming” [T]. Then the tone opens: the new self, renewal, the image of the Creator, and a human family without walls, ending on “Christ is all, and in all” [T]/[I].

Soundtrack: a percussive passage — drumbeats on each vice — giving way at 3:10–11 to a broad chorale. The preacher should not let the severity swallow the end, nor the end soften the severity [I].

Pauline epistle; paraenesis with vice lists, a common form in Hellenistic moral writing and in Jewish ethics [S]. Reading rule: a vice list is representative, not exhaustive; its order and grouping (body and greed; temper and tongue) carry meaning [I]. The clothing image is metaphor for a real change of identity, not a technique [I].

14. Bible Timeline

This side of the cross; the old self put off at conversion and baptism (aorists), the new self being renewed now (present), the wrath coming (present/future) [T]. 15. Who Am I?

  • “You” (the Colossians) — once walking in these vices, now commanded to kill and put them off, having already put on the new self [T]. The modern church stands with them [I].
  • “The sons of disobedience” (if read) — those still under the wrath; the readers’ former company [T]/[I].

16. So What?

Stage 1 — the response the author seeks: decisive renunciation of sexual sin, greed, anger and deceit, grounded in a new identity already put on and a renewal already under way; and a church in which no ethnic, religious or social category ranks anyone, because Christ is all [I].

Stage 2: - Worldview. My old self is not my true self; it has been stripped off (3:9). My new self is being made like the image of the Creator — like Christ (3:10; 1:15). Greed is worship of a false god (3:5). God’s wrath is real (3:6). - Stop: treating sexual sin and greed as private matters; managing anger and lies as personality traits; ranking fellow believers by background, class or status. - Start: taking decisive action against particular sins (put to death); guarding speech as the community’s life (3:8–9; 3:16); receiving every believer as “in Christ” first. - Motive: not fear of the wrath alone (3:6), but the reality of the new self already put on and the image being restored (3:9–10) [T]/[I].

Four audiences. For me: which of the ten names my sin? For a Christian friend: the believer in despair over a persistent sin — the old self has been stripped off; renewal is God’s ongoing work (passive). For the church: the lies and slander are “to one another” (3:9); the unity is “in all” (3:11). For an unbeliever: greed as idolatry; the wrath of God coming; and a new humanity open to Greek, barbarian and Scythian alike.

Prayer. Lord Jesus, you are all and in all. We have put off the old self and put on the new; give us grace to put to death what is earthly in us, to put away anger and lies, and to be renewed in knowledge after your image, one people in you.

Historical and Cultural Background

Setting

As for the letter (an imprisonment of Paul; Colossae in Phrygia; Roman Asia) [T]/[S].

What the original audience knew

  1. Vice lists were a familiar form in Greek moral philosophy and in Hellenistic Jewish writing [S], high. Paul’s lists would sound familiar; their grounding in death and resurrection with Christ would not [I].
  2. Greek and barbarian; Scythians. Greek-speaking people divided humanity into Greeks and barbarians; Scythians, from north of the Black Sea, were the proverbial extreme of barbarity in Greek writers and in the Greek Bible (2 Macc 4:47; 3 Macc 7:5) [T]/[S].
  3. Phrygians were themselves often looked down on as servile or barbarous by Greeks [S], uncertain — consider verifying. If so, the list would have touched the Colossians’ own standing [I], low–moderate.
  4. Slaves and free. A household church included both (3:22–4:1; Philemon) [T].
  5. Clothing at baptism. Later practice stripped candidates and clothed them anew; whether that practice already existed in the 60s is uncertain [S]. The image needs no rite to be understood [I].
  6. Greed as idolatry has Jewish roots (the tenth commandment; Wis 14) [S]/[T]; for Gentiles leaving temples, the claim that greed is itself worship would have been pointed [I].

What this changes about how we read

“Barbarian, Scythian” is not a random list of foreigners; it reaches to the bottom of the ancient world’s ranking of peoples, and declares that in Christ the ranking has no place [I], moderate–high.

Original Audience Reception

Canonical audience

A reader coming from Romans, Galatians and Ephesians knows Rom 6:6, Gal 3:27–28 and Eph 4:22–5:6 [T]. Colossians sounds familiar; its distinctive marks are the aorist participles (already put off and on), the Creator’s image, and the expanded list of 3:11 [T]/[I].

The first hearers

  • Who: Gentile believers who “once walked” in these sins (3:7) [T]; Jews among them likely few (4:11) [I].
  • Situation: offered ascetic rules as the cure for the flesh (2:21–23) [T].
  • What they brought: a culture in which sexual licence, greed and abusive speech were normal; a ranking of peoples in which Phrygians were not high [I]/[S].

Surprises, shocks, comforts, disturbances

  • Shocking: “greed, which is idolatry” — to people who had left their idols but not their wants [I]. “Scythian” in the family of God [I].
  • Surprising: that the cure for the flesh is death and new creation, not rules [I].
  • Comforting: “you have put off the old self … and put on the new” — done [T].
  • Disturbing: “the wrath of God is coming” [T].

What we bring that they didn’t

A therapeutic view of anger and desire as needs to be met; a privatised sexual ethic; a national, class or racial hierarchy that we do not see as a hierarchy; and a habit of reading “old self / new self” as two inner selves at war [I].

Implication for the sermon

Preach the commands with their ground, and end where the passage ends — not with a sin, but with Christ who is all [I].

Biblical-Theological Themes

Image of God / new humanity

  • Where: humanity renewed according to the Creator’s image (3:10) [T].
  • Before: Gen 1:26–27; 5:1; 9:6 [T]; in the letter, Christ the image (1:15) [T].
  • After: Rom 8:29; 2 Cor 3:18; 1 Cor 15:49 [S].
  • Contributes: the ethic is creation restored, not a new law.
  • Confidence: high.

Idolatry

  • Where: greed identified as idolatry (3:5) [T].
  • Before: the first and tenth commandments; Wis 14 [T] as texts.
  • After: 1 John 5:21 [S].
  • Confidence: high on the identification.

One people from the nations

  • Where: “there is not Greek and Jew … Scythian … but Christ is all” (3:11) [T].
  • Before: the promise to Abraham for the nations (Gen 12:3) [S]; in the letter, “among the Gentiles … Christ in you” (1:27) [T].
  • After: Eph 2:15 (“one new man”); Rev 7:9 [S].
  • Confidence: high.

Confluence: climactic convergence — the restored image (theme 1) is Christ, and the one people (theme 3) is the humanity renewed in him; idolatry (theme 2) is what the image was replaced with [I].

Christological Reading

Type of connection: NT; Christ is the image after which the new self is renewed (3:10 with 1:15, moderate–high) and the “all and in all” (3:11) [T]/[I]. Gen 1’s image-bearer finds its original and its goal in him: typology passes the theological-category test (image, creation), the NT-precedent test (Rom 8:29; 2 Cor 4:4), the escalation test (Adam bore the image; Christ is it) and partly the authorial-pattern test (Genesis’s image language returns in 5:1 and 9:6) — moderate–high.

Moralism check: - The “be like X” temptation: “kill these ten sins” as self-improvement. - The gospel grounding: “you died” (3:3); “having stripped off … having put on” (3:9–10); “being renewed” (passive) [T]. - Christ as hero: he is the image, the one in whom the new humanity is made, and “all” [T].

Confidence: high on the Christ-centredness.

Difficult / Contested Verses

3:5–8 — sexual ethics and anger

  • Category: pastoral (and contested in culture).
  • The difficulty: the list names sexual immorality and impurity without qualification, and calls greed idolatry.
  • Rhetorical function: the first application of death and resurrection with Christ (3:1–4) [T].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the commands are absolute; their ground is a new identity, not shame; the wrath is real [T]/[I].
  • Suggested handling: preach plainly, with pastoral awareness of those in the room carrying sexual sin or harm; keep the ground (3:9–10) and the end (3:11) in view.

3:9–10 — “you have put off the old self”

  • Category: doctrinal (sanctification).
  • The difficulty: if the old self is gone, why the commands?
  • Options: (a) completed in principle at conversion, worked out in practice (causal participles) [S]; (b) commands to put off (imperatival participles) [S].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: (a) fits the aorists and the letter’s pattern of indicative then imperative (3:3 → 3:5; 3:10 → 3:12) [I], moderate–high.
  • Suggested handling: address; this is the passage’s engine.

3:11 — “no Greek and Jew … slave, free”

  • Category: weaponised (in two directions).
  • The difficulty: read as abolishing all distinctions (including the household roles of 3:18–4:1), or as purely “spiritual” with no social effect.
  • Rhetorical function: the scope of the new self — where renewal happens [T].
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: the categories still exist (the letter addresses slaves and masters at once, 3:22–4:1) but no longer rank or divide in Christ; the effect is social within the church (Onesimus “brother”, 4:9) [T]/[I]. The omission of “male and female” should not be pressed either way [I].
  • Suggested handling: address briefly; link to 3:22–4:1 and Philemon.

3:6 — “the wrath of God is coming”

  • Category: doctrinal / pastoral.
  • What an honest exegesis surfaces: present tense; eschatological and already active (cf. Rom 1:18) [S]; directed at the practices and, if the phrase is read, at those who persist in them [T]/[I].
  • Suggested handling: do not omit; frame by 1:13 (rescued from the domain of darkness) and 3:4 (revealed with him).

Preaching Pitfalls

Pitfall: A list without a ground

  • What it looks like: a sermon on ten sins to avoid.
  • Why it’s wrong: the commands rest on “you died” (3:3) and “having put off … having put on” (3:9–10). Without them the sermon becomes the rule-keeping the letter has just rejected (2:20–23; overview moralism check).
  • The corrective: preach the ground at least as long as the commands.

Pitfall: “Consider yourself dead” as the whole response

  • What it looks like: following the NASB95, “reckon yourself dead to these things” as an act of the mind.
  • Why it’s wrong: the Greek is an aorist imperative, “put to death”; the members are the practices themselves.
  • The corrective: call for decisive action — the ESV’s “put to death” is closer.

Pitfall: “Old self / new self” as a civil war inside the believer

  • What it looks like: “Your old nature and your new nature are fighting; feed the right one.”
  • Why it’s wrong: the old self has been stripped off (aorist); the passage describes two humanities, the old and the renewed, not two selves within.
  • The corrective: preach identity and renewal: you are the new; become what you are.

Pitfall: Ending at 3:9

  • What it looks like: a sermon that stops at the sins and the lying.
  • Why it’s wrong: the passage ends on the renewed image and “Christ is all, and in all”.
  • The corrective: make 3:10–11 the climax.

Pitfall: 3:11 as a slogan for erasing every distinction — or as a merely inward truth

  • What it looks like: using 3:11 against the household instructions; or reducing it to “we’re all equal before God” with no practical effect.
  • Why it’s wrong: the letter keeps the categories and addresses them (3:18–4:1), while denying them any power to rank in Christ, and acts on it (4:9).
  • The corrective: preach the social reality within the church: who sits where, who is listened to, who is called brother.
