Colossians: Logos Answers, Round 4 — Assessment

Source: Patrick’s round-4 session with the Logos Study Assistant (28–29 October 2026), pasted into colossians-logos-questions-round-4.odt. The raw answers are kept as Colossians/colossians-logos-answers-round-4.md. For 1.2 (Pao), 1.3 (Lange), 2.1 (five commentators) and the Philo check, Patrick pasted the commentary text itself; the other answers are the Assistant’s summaries.
Checked against: SBLGNT; Swete’s LXX; the NA28 text and apparatus export; the NASB95, ESV and NIV84 exports of Colossians; the Clementine Vulgate of 3:22–4:1 (filed this round). Every count or chain names its edition. Commentary statements are tagged [S: Logos 4]; where the corpus disagrees, the corpus wins.
Applied in: 1:15–20 dig v1.6; 2:16–23 dig v1.4; 3:18–4:1 dig v1.2; 2:6–15 dig v1.6; 1:24–2:5 dig v1.6 (library status only). The book overview is untouched: its changes wait for the Finalise pass. No claim-audit rating is changed; the history-of-interpretation findings are recorded for the audits.
Date: 29 October 2026

Summary

  • 1:19 — Round 3 misfiled Pao. In the English ZECNT (pp. 101–2), Pao sets out the case for “God” as the implied subject and then chooses the third option: “all the fullness” is itself the subject, a periphrasis for God, so “to insert God as the implied subject is thus redundant”. Round 3 had him as the main holder of “God implied”. With Dunn (as summarised) also taking “the fullness” as subject, the three owned technical commentators who were returned (Dunn, Pao, Wright) now agree. The lean moves from slight, moderate to moderate–high. This is the ESV’s reading.
  • 1:20 — εἰς αὐτόν refers to Christ in Pao (English p. 104) and in Dunn. Dunn’s wording is still reached only through the Assistant, but it now includes two phrases quoted from him. The lean to the Son moves from slight to a clear lean, moderate. All three English versions read “to Himself”, meaning God, so this is a note for the preacher, not a correction of the pulpit text.
  • 2:23 — reading 2 now has an owned holder. Brown (LRC) says nothing on the clause; Patrick checked. Lange (p. 57) takes πρός as “tends to”: the regimen serves “only to the satisfying of the flesh”. He follows Braune and Meyer and puts Ellicott nearest. The one lexical entry returned (TDNT, abridged, 942–43) also reads πρός here in a consecutive sense, “leading to the satisfaction of the flesh”. No owned work illustrates πρός = “against” in the sense of a remedy. Reading 2 moves from low–moderate to moderate, level with reading 1. The dig’s shared point stands at high.
  • The Sabbath — the commentators at last. Wright says explicitly that the triad runs “in descending order” from annual festivals to “the weekly Sabbaths”. Witherington takes 2:16 as ending Sabbath regulations for believers. Dunn treats it as the regular Jewish triad and lists eight OT and Maccabean instances. Pao’s view is that the target is the practices attached to the days, not the calendar as such, and it rests on an Adventist article (Cole, AUSS). Bird and the ESV Expository Commentary say nothing. The dig’s “includes the weekly Sabbath”, moderate–high, stands, and now has owned holders.
  • 3:24 — the Vulgate is verified: Domino Christo servite, an imperative, with no “for” at 3:24 and enim (“for”) at 3:25. That is the shape of the NA28 text. Dunn and Pao take δουλεύετε as an indicative. Corpus: Rom 16:18 is the only other NT verse that joins δουλεύω with “the [our] Lord Christ”, and it is a statement (SBLGNT). The apparatus also shows a Western reading (F G) with a relative pronoun, “whom you serve”, which is indicative. There is now a slight lean to the indicative, moderate.
  • Patrick’s ticks (in Wingdings, recovered from the file) settle which reference works he owns. Not owned: Beale’s BECNT, Beale–Carson’s CNTUOT, Harris’s Prepositions and Theology, Comfort’s Text and Translation Commentary, Campbell, and one line that holds both Hidden but Now Revealed and DPL (see §7). Owned: Beale’s Temple, EDNT, Vincent, Robertson (Word Pictures and Grammar), Deenick. Citations of the works not owned in the digs are second-hand, and each dig now says so.
  • Philo — the numbering is settled; the Greek is not. Both Colson (LCL) and Yonge print §§146–147. Evans’s “Confusion 28 §146” shows that round 2’s “section 28” was Yonge’s chapter number. The Assistant’s claim that Philo’s word is πρωτότοκος is not backed by any resource that prints the Greek, so it is discarded.
  • Not returned: 2.2 (authorship, place and date); Chrysostom on 2:18 and on the sense of 2:23; Wright and Witherington at 3:24; the commentators on 3:25 apart from the Ephesians commentaries and theologies.

1. The commentator’s own words

1.1 Colossians 1:20, εἰς αὐτόν — Dunn

What came back. This is a summary again (NIGTC 102, 104, 107), but it carries two phrases in quotation marks:

  • 1:20 “echoes the ‘all things through him and to him’ of 1:16”;
  • at 1:22 the language, “freed from the poetic constraints of the hymn, reverts to the more typical Pauline thought of reconciliation through Christ to God”.

The summary also says that Dunn keeps “the fullness” as subject through 1:20.

Watch-for met. This time the summary keeps apart the subject of reconciling (the fullness) and the one reconciled to (Christ). Dunn’s position is now clear: εἰς αὐτόν = Christ, against Paul’s usual direction, which he explains as the hymn’s own constraint.

Corpus check of Dunn’s 1:22 contrast. 1:22 does not say “to God”.

  • NA28 reads the active ἀποκατήλλαξεν (“he reconciled”) with no subject expressed.
  • 𝔓⁴⁶ and B read the passive ἀποκατηλλάγητε (“you were reconciled”), which the SBLGNT prints. Other readings are αποκαταλλαγεντες (D* F G) and αποκατηλλακται (33) (NA28 apparatus) [T].
  • What follows is “to present you … before him” (κατενώπιον αὐτοῦ).

So Dunn’s “reverts to … through Christ to God” is his reading of an unexpressed subject [I]. On the SBLGNT’s passive text no agent is named at all. The contrast he draws is fair as theology, but 1:22 does not state it.

Verdict. Dunn can be cited for “to Christ”, with his two quoted phrases, as [S: Logos 4, summary with quotations]. The page should be confirmed (104 or 107) before the reference is printed.

1.2 Colossians 1:19–20 — Pao, in English

Patrick pasted the text (ZECNT, pp. 101–2, 104).

On 1:19. Pao lists three options.

  1. Christ as subject of the verb. Pao rejects it: it would turn αὐτῷ into a reflexive (his n. 2, citing Porter).
  2. God implied, with the fullness as subject of the infinitive. For it: εὐδοκέω usually has a personal subject; in the LXX that subject is often God; the masculine εἰρηνοποιήσας; God is the reconciler of 1:20.
  3. “All the fullness” as subject, meaning “God in all his fullness”, with the participle “a construction according to sense” (Moule).

He chooses 3. “The factors that favor ‘God’ as the implied subject … also support this reading”, and two more are added:

  • at 2:9, “all the fullness” is clearly the subject;
  • in the LXX, πληρόω and πλήρης point to God’s presence (Isa 6:1; Ezek 43:5; 44:4; Hag 2:7). Ps 67:17 LXX supports this.

His conclusion: “It seems best, therefore, to see ‘all the fullness’ as a periphrasis for God; to insert God as the implied subject is thus redundant.”

Correction to round 3. Round 3’s assessment §1.8 and the 1:15–20 dig (v1.5) listed Pao as the main holder of “God implied”. That is wrong. Pao states option 2’s arguments but adopts option 3. The round-3 summary appears to have reported the case he sets out rather than the conclusion he reaches.

Corpus checks on Pao’s evidence (Swete):

  • His LXX list for a divine subject of εὐδοκέω uses English verse numbers. Ps 40:13; 51:16; 85:1 are Swete 39:14; 50:18; 84:2. All three are verified, as are Jer 2:19; 14:12; Jdt 15:10; 2 Macc 14:35; Sir 45:19.
  • Jer 14:10 in Swete reads εὐόδωσεν (“prospered”), not εὐδόκησεν. Pao is presumably using Rahlfs; the Rahlfs reading was not checked.
  • Sir 34:19 (Rahlfs numbering) is Swete 31:23, “the Most High is not pleased (οὐκ εὐδοκεῖ) with the offerings of the ungodly”.
  • His πλήρης verses are verified: Isa 6:1; Ezek 43:5; 44:4. Hag 2:7 has πλήσω (πίμπλημι), a different verb from πληρόω. The point stands, but it rests on two word families, not one.
  • Ps 67:17 is verified: τὸ ὄρος ὃ εὐδόκησεν ὁ θεὸς κατοικεῖν ἐν αὐτῷ. Pao takes it as a “possible allusion”, which is C1’s history.
  • His appeal to 2:9 is the same as Moo’s in round 1. The dig’s check (a) still applies: at 2:9 the fullness is the subject of κατοικεῖ, and on the NASB95’s construction it is also the subject of the infinitive κατοικῆσαι at 1:19. So 2:9 fits both readings [T]. Pao’s decisive argument is the one he calls simple: no subject has to be supplied. That agrees with the dig’s v1.4 finding that every divine εὐδοκέω in the NT has its subject expressed.

On 1:20. Pao finds “to him” referring to Christ, in English, on p. 104:

“In light of the consistent usage of the pronoun ‘him’ (αὐτός) in reference to Christ in this hymn, ‘to him’ (εἰς αὐτόν) here should best be taken as also referring to Christ.”

He notes that “most versions” prefer the reflexive because elsewhere reconciliation is always to God (Lightfoot, 160). His reply: “the strength of other Pauline usages fails to supersede the clear context where Christ is the agent and the goal of both the original and new creation.”

The Spanish edition (p. 113) said the same.

Verdicts:

Item Before (v1.5) Now Holders
1:19, subject of εὐδόκησεν slight lean to “all the fullness” (= God in all his fullness), moderate lean to “all the fullness” (= God in all his fullness), moderate–high Wright (TNTC 80); Pao (101–2); Dunn (as summarised); Moo and Harris (second-hand). God implied: Robertson; Vincent (owned); the NASB95 and NIV84
1:20, εἰς αὐτόν slight lean to the Son, moderate lean to the Son, moderate Pao (104); Dunn (as summarised, with quotations); the syntax of 1:16. God: Lightfoot (via Pao); Harris (not owned); Robertson; every English version

What the congregation will hear. At 1:19 the ESV, “in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell”, gives the reading now preferred. At 1:20 the ESV’s “to reconcile to himself” follows “the fullness of God” as subject, so the hearer takes “himself” to be God. On the dig’s reading it is the Son: “to him”. This is a gap between the pulpit text and the finding. The preacher can name it in a sentence (“Paul’s Greek points the reconciliation to Christ himself, as the goal of all things in v. 16”), or leave it, since on either reading the reconciliation is through the Son.

1.3 Colossians 2:23 — Brown, and Lange instead

Brown (LRC): no comment on the clause. Patrick checked it himself. This closes round 3’s unreturned question.

Lange (A Commentary on the Holy Scriptures: Colossians, p. 57; Braune’s section, with the American editor’s bracketed note) is owned.

  • He supplies ἐστίν from ἅτινα: the regimen’s repute is “without honorable grounds”. οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι is “a sharp negation” of will-worship, humility and severity to the body.
  • He then reads πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός as a positive statement: “[only] to the satisfying of the flesh”. “The false doctrine tends (πρός) to a satisfying (in contrast with ‘unsparingness’) of the fleshly nature.”
  • He rejects Luther’s “not giving to the flesh the honour due to its necessities”. With Bengel (fere excessum denotat, “it nearly always denotes excess”), he holds that πλησμονή implies blame.
  • He insists on the contrast of σῶμα and σάρξ.
  • The editor’s note prefers Braune’s (= Meyer’s) view on four grounds: least ungrammatical; the best correlate to μέν; the σῶμα / σάρξ distinction; the bad sense of πλησμονή. It names Eadie, Alford and Ellicott for other views, with Ellicott nearest to Braune.

Corpus test of Bengel’s premise (Swete). πλησμονή occurs in 28 verses. Most denote plenty or eating “to the full” without blame: Gen 41:30; Exod 16:3, 8; Lev 25:19; 26:5; Ps 77:25; Prov 3:10; Sir 1:16 (“fullness of wisdom”); Isa 30:23; 55:2 and others. Only a few carry blame: Hos 13:6 (“filled … and their heart was lifted up”); Ezek 16:49 (Sodom’s “fullness of bread”); Hab 2:16 (“a fill of dishonour”); Isa 1:14 (God sated with Israel’s feasts); perhaps Isa 65:15 [T] for the list, [I] for the classification. “Nearly always excess” does not describe the Greek Bible’s usage. Lange’s reading needs the context, not the word, to supply the blame. The context can do so: 2:18 has “puffed up … by the mind of his flesh” [I].

Library note: Lange is owned. Meyer, Braune, Eadie, Alford and Ellicott reach this assessment only through Lange.

1.4 πρός = “against”?

What came back: one entry, TDNT (abridged, 942–43). It classes πρός with the accusative in Col 2:23 as consecutive: “leading ‘to the satisfaction of the flesh’”. No owned lexicon or grammar gave an example of πρός meaning “against” in the sense of a remedy.

Two cautions:

  • The abridged TDNT is not an independent voice. As far as I know, the full TDNT article on πρός is by Bo Reicke, who is the first holder of reading 2 in Dunn’s list (round 3). I am fairly confident of the attribution but have not checked it. If it is right, the TDNT entry is Reicke’s view in dictionary form.
  • An absence in the Assistant’s search is weak evidence. Lightfoot (pp. 204–5, via Dunn) is said to supply “to check, prevent”, and BDAG is not owned.

As far as I recall, LSJ gives πρός + accusative for a remedy “against” a disease (φάρμακον πρός …). I cannot give a reference, and I do not know whether LSJ is in Patrick’s library.

Verdict on 2:23 (1.3 + 1.4):

Reading Before (v1.3) Now Owned holders
1. “of no value against the indulgence of the flesh” moderate moderate — the commentators’ consensus, but its key sense of πρός is still unillustrated in the library Dunn (“in regard to”); Pao (“against”, asserted); Wright (“in relation to”)
2. “[the regimen serves] only for the satisfaction of the flesh” low–moderate moderate Lange (Braune; Meyer, Ellicott via Lange); TDNT abridged (probably Reicke); Reicke, Hollenbach, O’Brien, Pokorný and Fowl remain second-hand
3. “not in any honour [to the body]” low low — (Lange explicitly rejects Luther’s form of it)

The dig’s shared point stands, high: on every reading, severity to the body is no remedy for the flesh. It either fails to check the flesh (1) or feeds it (2). Lange’s σῶμα / σάρξ contrast is the same one Dunn uses (round 3), turned to the opposite reading. So the contrast supports Headline 2 on both readings.

Corpus checks (Swete):

The “practices, not days” view has an owned holder (Pao) whose source is Adventist. The overview’s Trap 3 note can cite these four. 2.3 Colossians 1:15–20 as a “hymn”

All four commentators were returned as summaries.

  • Dunn (84–85):
    • Preformed material “can never be definitively proven”; Paul may have “struck a purple passage”.
    • The second strophe is less rhythmic than the first, and a cosmos sustained “in him” sits in tension with one alienated. That is presumably why NA26 sets only 1:15–18a as verse.
    • He entertains two hypotheses: a one-strophe creation hymn supplemented by a second hand; or a pre-Christian hymn to Wisdom or Logos, taken over by “Paul and Timothy”.
    • He reports the “most commonly agreed glosses”: the lines on the powers, “the church” (1:18a) and “through the blood of his cross” (1:20). The last would explain the awkwardness of the second “through him”.
  • Wright (TNTC 68–69): Paul’s own composition. His structure has four sections: the outer two open “who is”, the inner two “and he”.
  • Pao (90–91): sceptical of the pre-Pauline theory. The arguments “are not decisive”, and Paul can compose structured pieces. He reports that most reconstructions treat “the church” and “through the blood of his cross” as obvious Pauline insertions, but holds that nothing in the passage contradicts Paul and all of it serves his argument.
  • Witherington (131–32): either Paul transformed an earlier hymn into Asiatic style or composed it himself for the occasion. If there was an earlier hymn, Paul may have moved “making peace through the blood of the cross” so that “the body” could stand at the nadir of a V-pattern, and “the church” may be his addition.

Corpus checks:

  • Wright’s frame reproduces (SBLGNT): ὅς ἐστιν (1:15) · καὶ αὐτός ἐστιν (1:17) · καὶ αὐτός ἐστιν (1:18a) · ὅς ἐστιν (1:18b) [T]. On that frame “the church” stands inside the third member, not outside the structure. This agrees with the dig’s finding that both phrases are bound into the strophes.
  • Dunn’s “awkwardness of the second ‘through him’” rests partly on a variant. NA28 brackets the second [δι᾽ αὐτοῦ] in 1:20.
    • It is omitted by B D* F G I L 075 0278 81 104 1175 1241ˢ 1739 1881 2464 latt sa; Origen.
    • It is read by 𝔓⁴⁶ א A C D¹ K P Ψ 048ᵛⁱᵈ 33 365 630 1505 𝔪 sy bo; Hilary (NA28 apparatus) [T: as the apparatus prints it].
    • Scribes felt the awkwardness too, and some removed it. An argument for a gloss from a phrase whose text is itself uncertain is weaker than it looks [I].

Watch-for met: the “additions” are reported by Dunn and Pao as the consensus of reconstructions. Neither commits to them, and Wright and Witherington give structural reasons for keeping both phrases in place.

Verdict: the overview’s [S] tag (“a form-critical reconstruction”) stands, now with owned holders on each side: Paul’s own — Wright, Pao (in effect); open — Witherington; preformed material possible, no certainty — Dunn. Nothing here needs the sermon to reconstruct an “original hymn”. The dig’s reading rule is unchanged.

4. History of interpretation for the claim audits

Claim audit 3 ran on 28 October, after these questions were written, so its ratings are already set. What follows supplies its history-of-interpretation criterion. No rating changes.

  • C42 — “fearing the Lord”, 3:22 (audit 3: confirmed with nuance, moderate–high).
    • Found: Pao (273): manner (“reverence for the Lord”), contrasting the earthly master with the Lord in heaven, with no OT reference. Robertson (Word Pictures, owned): the contrast with the “lords according to the flesh”. Pope (a theology): fearing = pleasing God. None links the phrase to the OT “fear of the LORD”, and none discusses the variant.
    • Bearing: history: not noticed by the owned commentators returned. An absence in a search; it neither supports nor weakens the textual case.
    • Verified from Patrick’s paste: the Clementine Vulgate reads timentes Deum, the θεόν reading that NA28’s apparatus assigns to vgᶜˡ; the Stuttgart text (vgˢᵗ·ʷʷ) supports κύριον. The KJV’s “fearing God” and the Clementine agree with the majority text.
  • C40 / C43 / C46 — inheritance, 1:12 → 3:24; the Joshua field (audit 3: C40 moderate; C43 low; C46 moderate).
    • Found: Dunn (via Brown LRC; page not given): Paul offers Gentile slaves “a share in the inheritance of Abraham”. No one addresses whether slaves could inherit in law.
    • Bearing: C40: one holder for the inheritance-as-Abraham’s link (Dunn, second-hand). C43: none, which supports audit 3’s low.
    • Corpus (Swete): Num 18:20 and Deut 32:9 both join μερίς with κληρονομία, the word of 3:24, not κλῆρος. It is a standing OT doublet, so 1:12 → 3:24 moves within a familiar field. That supports C40 as an internal link carried by an idiom, not as an echo of any one verse. Record only.
  • C35 / C36 — 2 Chr 19:6–7; Deut 10:17; Lev 25 (audit 3: C35 low–moderate as the topos; C36 low).

Bruce (TNTC Romans), Brown–Twist (LRC Romans) and Bock (TNTC Ephesians) cite the two OT texts at Rom 2:11 and Eph 6:9, not at Colossians. A paragraph on “the great leveller” came back with no source; disregard it until one is found.
    • Bearing: C35: a reference list names both texts, which meets the history criterion as a listing, not as an argument. C36: audit 3’s “occasionally cited … never as a source [S]” can now name Jones. The audit’s own finding stands: Lev 25:43’s Greek is “with toil”, not “harshness”, although the verse does end “and you shall fear the Lord your God” (Swete).
  • C32 — Isa 1:13–14 at 2:16, 23 (audit 3: low–moderate).
    • Found: Dunn lists Isa 1:13–14 among the triad passages (2.1 above); the Assistant missed this in its own answer to 4.4. EDNT (2:462, owned): Barnabas cites Isa 1:13, and the Colossian series matches the LXX. Bacchiocchi: Isa 1:13–14 puts the new moon first. No one notes God’s rejection of the feasts, or πλησμονή.
    • Bearing: history: Isa 1:13–14 is noticed as an instance of the triad, not as an allusion. This supports audit 3’s low–moderate. The πλησμονή contact remains the audits’ own observation, and audit 3 found it fails on sense.

5. Colossians 3:18–4:1 — the three cruxes

5.1 3:24, δουλεύετε

  • Dunn (256, summary): a “motivational statement”, grounded in serving “the same Lord from whom they will receive the inheritance”. Assistant error: its second paragraph says that “the grammatical form itself — a present tense indicative rather than an imperative — supports reading it as declarative”. The form is identical for both moods (second plural present active), and the SBLGNT word index even tags it imperative [T]. The form decides nothing.
  • Pao (275): indicative, for three reasons: the code’s imperatives otherwise address reciprocal household relations; “the Lord Christ” explains “from the Lord” as an identification; and “the Lord Christ” occurs elsewhere in Paul only at Rom 16:18. He reports that “a majority of recent commentators” take it as an imperative. Their reasons: the asyndeton, the parallel with 3:23, and the γάρ of 3:25. He does not name them in the text returned.
  • The NET notes: both readings set out, with Rom 12:11 (τῷ κυρίῳ δουλεύοντες) for the imperative.
  • Wright, Witherington: nothing returned.

Corpus and text:

  • Rom 16:18 reads οἱ γὰρ τοιοῦτοι τῷ κυρίῳ ἡμῶν Χριστῷ οὐ δουλεύουσιν (“such men do not serve our Lord Christ”). Col 3:24 is the only NT verse with the exact τῷ κυρίῳ Χριστῷ, but Rom 16:18 is the one other verse that joins δουλεύω with the dative “Lord … Christ”, and it is a statement of whom people serve (SBLGNT) [T]. Pao’s third reason is stronger than he puts it.
  • The Vulgate is verified: scientes quod a Domino accipietis retributionem hæreditatis. Domino Christo servite. Qui enim injuriam facit … (Clementine). That is an imperative; no “for” at 3:24; enim at 3:25. It is the NA28 text’s shape, read as a command.
  • The apparatus shows a third ancient reading. F G (with ar d m* boᵖᵗ; Ambrosiaster) have “the inheritance τοῦ κυρίου ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, ᾧ (‘of our Lord Jesus Christ, whom’) you serve”. A relative clause makes the verb indicative. The Byzantine γάρ (D¹ K L Ψ 075 104 630 1175 1505 𝔪 sy; Clement) also makes it a statement. So the early Greek and Latin tradition is divided: the Western Greek and the Byzantine text read a statement; the Vulgate reads a command [T: as the apparatus prints it].
  • The English versions all read the indicative: NASB95 “It is the Lord Christ whom you serve”; ESV “You are serving the Lord Christ”; NIV84 “It is the Lord Christ you are serving”.

Verdict: a slight lean to the indicative, moderate. For it: Dunn, Pao, Rom 16:18, and two ancient readings that make it explicit. For the imperative: the Vulgate, the unnamed recent majority, the asyndeton and the γάρ of 3:25. The dig’s handling stands: preach the identity, and mention the command. The congregation hears a statement in all three versions.

Verdict: this supports the dig’s reading (c), that the verse faces both ways, moderate, unchanged. The owned commentators are still to be asked.

5.3 4:1, ἰσότης

  • Lange (78, owned): τὸ δίκαιον is what slaves are owed as God’s creatures; τὴν ἰσότητα is the equality given in redemption, by which the redeemed are brothers. He rejects “equity” or “impartial treatment”.
  • Osborne (Verse by Verse, 127, owned): “a double meaning, not only indicating ‘fair’ treatment but also directing owners to consider slaves ‘equal’ to themselves before God”, with 3:11.
  • Stott (on Eph 6:9): unequal in law, equal before the one Lord and Judge.

Watch-for met: no Philo or Stoic usage is cited.

Text and corpus:

  • The Clementine Vulgate reads quod justum est et æquum. Aequus covers both “level, equal” and “fair”, so the Latin keeps the ambiguity rather than settling it [I].
  • In Swete, ἰσότης occurs at Job 36:29, Zech 4:7 and Pss Sol 17:46 (see §4) [T]. The Psalms of Solomon verse, a lord’s ἰσότης that leaves no one oppressed, fits “fair” as well as “equal”. It shows the word at home in the language of how a ruler treats his subjects [I].

Verdict: both owned holders take the fuller sense, Lange strongly and Osborne as a double sense. Range “fair ↔︎ equal”, moderate, leaning to the double sense: fairness grounded in equality before the one Master. The dig’s handling stands: tell the congregation the word. The ESV’s “justly and fairly” keeps only the first half.

What the English suggests, held lightly. The two translations differ in ways that point to the Greek. These are inferences from English, not readings of the Greek [I].

  • At §146 both have “first-born” for the Word. That is consistent with πρωτόγονος, the word recalled in round 3 [I].
  • At §147 Colson’s “the eldest-born image of God” and Yonge’s “his most ancient word” both suggest a superlative of “old”, probably πρεσβύτατος, rather than a “-born” compound [I].

My round-3 recollection (τῆς ἀιδίου εἰκόνος) followed Yonge. If Colson’s text reads ἀειδοῦς, Philo calls the Logos God’s invisible image. That matters for Col 1:15 (“the image of the invisible God”), where it is God who is invisible and the Son who is the image. I am uncertain here.

I do not know whether the Logos edition includes the Greek. If it does, the Philo question closes without another round, and the memory note that he has no Philo Greek would need correcting.

Verdict: the numbering is closed. Philo’s Greek words are still unverified. The 1:15–20 dig keeps “πρωτόγονος (recalled)” and adds the “invisible / eternal” question.
