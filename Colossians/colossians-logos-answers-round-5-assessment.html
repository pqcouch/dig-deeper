Summary

  • Authorship, place and date now have holders from Patrick’s own commentaries (§1). Paul is the author for Wright, Pao and Osborne. Witherington and Bird have Paul writing with others (Timothy as secretary or co-author). Dunn leaves it open: Paul, or a disciple shortly after his death, with the letter as a “bridge”. On place, Dunn, Pao and Osborne have Rome; Wright and Bird have Ephesus. Witherington gives no view. On date, Pao and Osborne have AD 60–62, and Wright the early 50s. Bird’s c. 55–57 is stated for Philemon. Follow-up (§14.1): Dunn’s own pages (35–39) now confirm round 2. He judges the letter “probably” composed by Timothy as Paul’s secretary, at about the same time as Philemon, with Paul adding 4:18. If Paul approved it, he says, it is “Pauline in the full sense”.
  • Philo’s Greek is settled (§7). The word for “firstborn” is πρωτόγονος (“first-born”) at Conf. 146 and Agr. 51, not Colossians’ πρωτότοκος (“firstborn”). Colson’s “invisible image” translates ἀειδοῦς (“unseen”), not Colossians’ ἀοράτου (“invisible”). So two of the three key words differ, and only εἰκών (“image”) and ἀρχή (“beginning”) are shared. The Philo parallel is one of concept, not of wording.
  • Chrysostom answers all three questions round 4 left open (§6). At 2:15 Christ is the subject, the middle is true (“having put off from Himself”), and the triumph is at the cross. At 2:18 he takes the genitive as objective: worship directed to angels as go-betweens. His text has the negative (“which he hath not seen”). At 2:23 he takes “not in any honour” with the body: the regimen dishonours the flesh that “God hath honored”. That is the dig’s reading 3, so reading 3 now has a patristic holder.
  • The Vulgate and KJV of the whole letter are filed (§8). Every “from recall” wording in the digs is checked. All of them stand except one: the Vulgate at 3:3 reads vita vestra est abscondita, not vita vestra abscondita est. Two points matter for the preacher:
    • the KJV hears no “you → us” shift at either 1:12 (“made us meet”) or 2:13 (“you … you”);
    • the Vulgate and the KJV both follow the Byzantine “that he might know your estate” at 4:8.
  • The Assistant twice said “none” where the paste itself shows a holder. At 3:24 Dunn cites Ps 19:11 for ἀνταπόδοσις (“repayment”) as a good outcome (C45). At 3:12 Gurtner (BKBC, owned) lists Ps 144:9 LXX, which is Ps 145:9 in the Hebrew and English numbering (C54). Both are corrected here from the pasted text and the corpus.
  • One misfiling and one false textual claim. At 4.8 the Assistant credits Wright and Bird with what its footnotes take from Wilson (ESV Expository Commentary) and Gaffin. At 4.13 it calls ἵνα γνῶτε τὰ περὶ ἡμῶν (“that you may know about us”) the reading of “the earliest and most reliable manuscripts”. But 𝔓⁴⁶ has the other reading (NA28 apparatus).
  • History of interpretation for claim audit 4: ten of the eleven claims now have at least one named holder, often several. Only Job 31 (C44) has none. No verdict moves, because history is one row of the Hays table (§2).
  • For claim audit 5:
    • the personal frame (1:24–2:5 with 4:2–18) has an owned holder, Pao, who names the verses. The corpus confirms his verbal links (§3.4);
    • the dwelling chain still has no holder for 3:16. The Assistant’s claim that Pao links “fullness” across 1:19, 2:9 and 3:16 cannot be right as stated, because πλήρωμα (“fullness”) is not at 3:16 (§3.5);
    • Ps 107 (C61) and Luke 1:68–79 (C63) have no holder;
    • Deut 9:26–29 (C62) is cited only for “redemption”, and only in the Ephesians commentary.
  • Ownership settled:

3. For claim audit 5

3.1 Psalm 107 (106 LXX) behind 1:13–14 (C61)

No holder. Pao names Isa 42:7, 16 (darkness and the new exodus), the alternative the question allowed. Swete’s Isa 42:7 has καθημένους ἐν σκότει (“sitting in darkness”), and Ps 106:10, 14 LXX has the same phrase with ἐξήγαγεν αὐτοὺς ἐκ σκότους (“he brought them out of darkness”) [T]. So Isaiah and the psalm share the image. The auditors should test Isa 42:7 as a rival to Ps 106.

3.2 Deuteronomy 9:26–29 behind 1:12–14 (C62)

  • Deut 32:9 is cited at 1:12 by Pao, Osborne and Brown (and Wall, round 4).
  • Deut 9:26 is cited only by Merkle at Eph 1:7, for “redemption”. That is another letter. The Assistant’s sentence that this “establishes the theological framework Paul employs” is its own and is discarded.
  • Corpus: Swete’s Deut 9:26 has τὴν μερίδα σου ἣν ἐλυτρώσω (“your portion, which you redeemed”), and 9:29 has κλῆρός σου (“your lot”) [T]. So μερίς (“portion”), κλῆρος (“lot”) and λυτρόω (“to redeem”) all stand within four verses. Col 1:12–14 has μερίς, κλῆρος and ἀπολύτρωσις (“redemption”).
  • By comparison, Deut 32:9 has μερίς and κληρονομία (“inheritance”) but not κλῆρος, while Josh 19:9 has κλῆρος, κληρονομία and μερίς [T].
  • The text case for C62 is better than its history row. It is for the audit to weigh.

3.3 Luke 1:68–79 behind 1:13–14 (C63)

None. No liturgical theory was offered either.

3.4 The personal frame: 1:24–2:5 and 4:2–18

Pao (pp. 315–16) meets the criterion with verses named.

  • Epaphras’s “striving” in prayer (4:12) uses Paul’s own verb from 1:29.
  • His prayer for maturity (4:12) answers 1:28.
  • “The will of God” (4:12) answers 1:9.

Corpus check (SBLGNT lemma index, Colossians only) [T]:

  • ἀγωνίζομαι (“to strive”): 1:29 and 4:12 only, with ἀγών (“struggle”) at 2:1;
  • τέλειος (“mature, perfect”): 1:28 and 4:12 only;
  • πληροφορία (“full assurance”) at 2:2, and πληροφορέω (“to assure fully”) at 4:12, each once.

Pao’s links are exact. This is a holder for the Arc Map’s frame (moderate), and the audit can test it.

3.5 The dwelling chain: 1:19, 2:9 and 3:16

Not yet a holder for the three-passage chain.

  • Corpus [T]: κατοικέω (“to dwell”) at 1:19 and 2:9; ἐνοικέω (“to dwell in”) at 3:16, a different compound; πλήρωμα (“fullness”) at 1:19 and 2:9 only. So the summary’s “Pao identifies ‘fullness’ and ‘to dwell’ … in 1:19, 2:9 and 3:16” cannot be right as it stands. Pao’s p. 84 should be pasted.
  • Garland and Moo are reached only through Brown (LRC). They read 1:19 and 2:9 against God’s choosing a place to dwell. The progression to 3:16 (“living temples”) is the Assistant’s synthesis and has no named author.
  • Beale’s BECNT notes (2.7) come nearest. He reads 3:16 against Ps 75:3 LXX (God’s dwelling in Zion) and links it to “the earlier references to the temple” at Col 1:19 and 2:9–10. But he gets there through the psalm titles, not through the verb.

Verdict: the thread stays near the gate, not macro.

3.6 “You” → “us” at 1:12–13 and 2:13

  • Osborne (p. 28): “you” for the Gentile Colossians, “we” for the whole church of Jews and Gentiles.
  • Wilson (ESV Expository Commentary, 11:215–16, 237; owned): at 1:13 “us” takes in Paul, Timothy and the wider church, a community “that includes both Jews and Gentiles”. At 2:13 the shift widens the address to all believers.
  • Neither discusses the 1:12 variant, and neither offers a liturgical or scribal explanation.
  • The older versions level the shift [T]:
    • the KJV has “made us meet” (1:12) and “delivered us” (1:13), following ἡμᾶς (“us”) at 1:12 (A C D F G K L P Ψ 𝔪 lat). It has “you … forgiven you” at 2:13 (ὑμῖν, “you”, in א² K* L P …);
    • the Vulgate has dignos nos fecit (“made us worthy”, 1:12).
  • A KJV reader hears no shift at either place. That is a note for the preacher, and it does not count against the dig’s reading of the NA28 text.

6. Chrysostom, Homilies VI and VII (NPNF¹ 13)

Patrick pasted both homilies whole. They are filed in English only, with the translators’ notes. The lemmas are the translators’ KJV-style English. Only Chrysostom’s comments count as his.

  • 2:15.
    • Christ is the subject, and the middle is a true middle. “‘Having put off from himself the principalities and the powers.’ … because human nature had arrayed itself in these, or because they had, as it were, a hold, when He became Man He put away from Himself that hold.”
    • The triumph is at the cross. “When That Body was nailed to the Cross, the dead arose. There death received his wound”. “To die with confidence is the devil’s shame”, and the translators’ note 112 shows that he reads ἐν παρρησίᾳ (“openly, boldly”) as “through His confidence in dying”.
    • What is not shown: he never parses ἐν αὐτῷ (“in him / in it”) explicitly in the English. “In it” in the NPNF is the lemma.
    • Verdict: Chrysostom holds the dig’s option 2 (Christ, true middle, the cross). Jamieson–Fausset–Brown’s “the Greek fathers read ‘in it’” is consistent with him, not proved by him. The dig’s slight lean to option 2 now has a patristic holder; it stays moderate.
  • 2:14, the bond. He gives three options for the χειρόγραφον (“handwritten bond”):
    1. Israel’s “All that God hath said will we do” (Exod 24:3);
    2. the obedience we owe God;
    3. the bond made for Adam (Gen 2:17), “which the devil held”.
    He takes τοῖς δόγμασιν (“by the decrees”) as “doctrines”, that is, faith. The translators’ note 113 says Theodoret did the same.
  • 2:8, the στοιχεῖα (“elements”). “Meaning by elements of the world the sun and moon”, because they govern the observance of days (with Gal 4:9). This is an ancient holder for the calendar sense.
  • 2:16–17. He presses ἐν μέρει (“in respect of”) as “in part”, which the NPNF editor calls “exegetically wrong”. He reports two punctuations of 2:17: “the body is of Christ”, or “the Body of Christ let no man adjudge away from you”, which joins 2:17b to 2:18.
  • 2:18.
    • Objective genitive: “There are some who maintain that we must be brought near by Angels, not by Christ, that were too great a thing for us … he said not ‘introduction by,’ but ‘worshiping of Angels.’”
    • The humility is a pretence (“puts forward a show of humility”).
    • καταβραβεύω (“to disqualify”) is the umpire’s verdict that gives the prize to another.
    • His text has the negative: “intruding into things he hath not seen … For he hath not seen Angels, and yet is affected as though he had”. The ἃ μή (“which not”) reading is the one in א² C D¹ K L Ψ 𝔪, against 𝔓⁴⁶ א* A B D* I 6 33 1739 [T].
    • The Vulgate (quæ non vidit) and the KJV (“which he hath not seen”) agree with him [T].
    • So the dig’s “uncertain” on the genitive gains a patristic objective-genitive holder. It stays uncertain, with the handling unchanged.
  • 2:23. “‘Not of any value against the indulgence of the flesh.’ For God hath given it honor, but they use it not with honor … They dishonor the flesh, he says, depriving it, and stripping it of its liberty … God hath honored the flesh.”
    • That is reading 3: the regimen denies the body the honour God gave it.
    • The dig set reading 3 at low in v1.2 after Wright’s rejection. A Greek-speaking reader of the fourth century takes it, so reading 3 moves to low–moderate.
    • Two cautions. First, this rests on the NPNF English, and Chrysostom uses “flesh” where the verse sets σῶμα (“body”) against σάρξ (“flesh”). Second, the Vulgate’s non ad parcendum corpori, non in honore aliquo ad saturitatem carnis (“not sparing the body, not in any honour for the satisfying of the flesh”) has the shape of reading 2 or 3, not of reading 1.
    • Readings 1 and 2 stay level at moderate.

14.1 Dunn on authorship, place and date (NIGTC, pp. 35–39)

What Dunn adopts.

  • On style and theology he confirms “the strong likelihood that the letter comes from a hand other than Paul’s”. The differences are “at the authorial level”. The Christology (1:15–20; 2:9–10, 15) and the household rules (3:18–4:1) look “further along the trajectory”.
  • But a full post-Pauline origin fails on three points: 4:7–17, the overlap with Philemon (the same senders and nearly the same greeters), and the earthquake of 60 or 61 (with Schweizer).
  • His conclusion: “the most plausible solution is probably that the letter was written at about the same time as Philemon but actually composed by someone other than Paul himself”. He pictures Paul outlining his concerns to “a secretary (Timothy)” who formulates the letter “with a fair degree of license”, and Paul adding only 4:18. If Paul approved what was written, “we have to call the letter ‘Pauline’ in the full sense of the word”.
  • His n. 47 places himself with Schweizer, Ollrog and Wedderburn, and names the post-Pauline majority (Lohse, Gnilka, Pokorný, Wolter and others).

So round 2’s summary was right, and §1’s table should read, for Dunn:

  • author: probably Timothy as Paul’s secretary, with Paul’s approval and Paul’s own 4:18;
  • date: in Paul’s lifetime, at about the same time as Philemon.

Place: p. 40 was not pasted. These pages mention Ephesus only hypothetically (“if Colossians was written from Ephesus [see p. 40 below]”). Round 5’s “inclined toward Rome” stays as Patrick reported it, pending p. 40.

Colossians and Ephesians. Dunn holds that Ephesians was “written using Colossians as a kind of template (so most)”, with Mitton, and against Coutts. This is an owned holder for the direction of dependence that round 1 found divided. It is recorded here; nothing in the digs turns on it.

For the overview’s Presenting Situation (Finalise): Dunn’s position is Pauline through a secretary, not post-Pauline. It belongs with Witherington and Bird, not apart from them. The owned commentators then divide only on how much of the composition is Paul’s own, and all six put the letter in Paul’s lifetime.

14.2 1:22 — the subject, and the passive

Dunn (p. 107): the thought “reverts to the more typical Pauline thought of reconciliation through Christ to God … but here with Christ as subject (though see n. 1), as in Eph. 2:16”. His n. 1 says that the passive ἀποκατηλλάγητε (“you were reconciled”) “meets all the criteria to be counted as original”: it is well supported (𝔓⁴⁶ B), it explains the other readings, and it is the most difficult. Yet “the early correction/improvement was wholly justified”, because the second-person passive fits so badly.

Pao (pp. 106–7): “the implied subject of an unspecified third person singular verb should be Christ himself”, because the focus is “consistently on Christ” and “all the fullness” (1:19) was a periphrasis for God chosen to keep the focus on Christ. The ambiguity “may be intentional”. His n. 120 rejects the passive, since it cannot take the following infinitive (“you were reconciled … to present you”). He notes that “many see this as the original reading” (Metzger, Moo, Sumney).

Wright (TNTC): “God has now reconciled you by Christ’s physical body”. That is the NIV’s wording, which he follows.

Corpus [T]:

  • ἀποκαταλλάσσω (“to reconcile”) occurs only at Col 1:20, Col 1:22 and Eph 2:16 (SBLGNT).
  • At 1:20 the fullness (God) reconciles “through him”.
  • At Eph 2:16 Christ reconciles “both to God in one body through the cross”.
  • So the verb’s two other uses divide exactly as the commentators do.

English versions [T]: all three have “he”. The NIV84’s “by Christ’s physical body” implies God as subject. The ESV (“in his body of flesh”) and the NASB95 (“in His fleshly body”) leave it open.

Verdict. The 1:21–23 dig rated God moderate–high. With three of the four owned technical commentators (Dunn, Pao, Bird) taking Christ, and the Eph 2:16 parallel on their side, that rating is too high. It moves to “open: God or Christ, moderate either way”. This is a change of verdict on a crux, not on a claim audit. The preaching point holds on either reading: the reconciliation happened “in the body of his flesh through death”. Note for the NIV84 pulpit: the NIV has decided for God.

On the passive: both Dunn and Pao print the active and concede the passive’s text-critical claim. The dig’s “divided” stands.

14.3 1:22 — present or final presentation

  • Dunn (pp. 109–10): final. “The presentation has not yet taken place and … it will be the final climax of God’s saving purpose”. This is “borne out by the immediately following note of caution (1:23)” and by 1:24. Against him he names Lightfoot, Lohse and Aletti.
  • Wright: final, by way of a process. God “has done [it] in principle … is doing in practice … will do in the future”. The process “will result in Christians being presented … before God, as glad subjects before their king”.
  • Pao (pp. 108–9): present. “As in Rom 12:1, this presentation may point to the present status of believers”; this “progressive reading … is confirmed by the use of the same verb in v. 28”. He also takes “before him” as Christ, and says “a future reference is not necessary”.
  • Corpus [T]: κατενώπιον (“before”) occurs in Paul only at Eph 1:4 and Col 1:22, and elsewhere in the NT only at Jude 24, where it is plainly final (“to make you stand in the presence of his glory blameless”).

Verdict: eschatological, moderate–high; unchanged. Dunn, Wright and Bird hold it. Pao holds the present reading, with Lightfoot, Lohse, Aletti and Moule.

14.4 1:23 — the condition

Wright: “This promise, like most, has a condition. The hope holds good, if Christians hold on to it … genuine faith is seen in patient and steadfast day-to-day Christian living.” He also sees “almost certainly a deliberate echo of 1:4–5”. This fits Trap 6’s qualified form (§4, 4.5).

14.5 Dwelling: Pao at 1:19 and 3:16; Beale’s Temple

  • Pao at 1:19 (pp. 101–2): the κατοικέω (“to dwell”) word group “evokes the presence of God in both his heavenly (1 Kgs 8:39, 43, 49; 2 Chr 6:30, 33, 39) and earthly (2 Chr 6:18, 21) abodes. Christ fulfills the role of the temple in which one finds the full presence of God.” His n. 92 (with Beetham) reads Ps 68 as a transfer of God’s presence from Sinai to Zion, “now from Zion to Christ himself”.
    • Corpus [T]: Swete has κατοικητήριον (“dwelling place”) at 1 Kgs 8:39, 43, 49 and 2 Chr 6:30, 33, 39; κατοικήσει θεὸς μετὰ ἀνθρώπων (“will God dwell with men?”) at 2 Chr 6:18; and κατοικήσεως (“dwelling”) at 6:21.
    • His references are exact, and they are the same heavenly-dwelling verses found in §2 (C56).
  • Pao at 3:16 (pp. 247–48): he does not join 3:16 to 1:19 or 2:9, so the summary in §3.5 was wrong. He compares ἐνοικείτω (“let it dwell”) with the indwelling of the Spirit (Rom 8:11) and of God (2 Cor 6:16), and with οἰκέω (“to dwell”) at Rom 8:9, 11 and 1 Cor 3:16.
    • Corpus [T]: ἐνοικέω occurs in the NT only at Rom 8:11, 2 Cor 6:16, Col 3:16 and 2 Tim 1:5, 14.
    • At 2 Cor 6:16 it stands inside a temple text: ἡμεῖς γὰρ ναὸς θεοῦ ἐσμεν ζῶντος (“for we are the temple of the living God”), with God saying “I will dwell in them”.
    • 1 Cor 3:16 is likewise “you are God’s temple”.
  • So two of Pao’s own parallels at 3:16 are temple texts, though he does not say so [I].
  • Beale’s Temple (owned): no reference to Col 3:16 (Patrick’s check).

Verdict: the chain is closer than it was. Pao makes 1:19 a temple statement, and the verb at 3:16 has its nearest Pauline use in a temple text. But no one reads 3:16 as a temple statement in its own right, and the link at 3:16 is an inference from Pao’s cross-references. Still near the gate, not macro. Claim audit 5 should test it as one claim: “the dwelling verbs at 1:19, 2:9 and 3:16 carry a temple thread”, with 2 Cor 6:16 as the test case for ἐνοικέω.

14.6 3:16 — the punctuation, the songs, and ἐν [τῇ] χάριτι

Pao’s pp. 247–50, pasted, confirm §4’s summary and add more.

  • “In all wisdom” goes with the teaching: “as in most versions … supported by 1:28”. In his n. 49 the KJV, NKJV and NLT take it with the dwelling.
  • The songs are the means of teaching, on four grounds:
    • the parallel at Eph 5:19;
    • 1 Cor 14:26;
    • the instrumental datives (with “singing” one would expect accusatives);
    • a parallel ἐν … participle structure (BDF §491).
    He also allows that, in parallel phrases, “items that appear in the first one are often also relevant for the second … To God, they are to sing these songs.”
  • Holders (his nn. 50–51): Harris, Lohse, Schweizer and Sumney join the songs to the singing; Lightfoot, O’Brien, Barth–Blanke, MacDonald, Callow and Moo join them to the teaching.
  • ἐν [τῇ] χάριτι — “with gratitude”. This is the 3:12–17 dig’s Open Question 3, not asked this round, and now answered. The grounds are the thanksgiving frame (vv. 15, 17) and Paul’s use of χάρις for thanks (Rom 6:17; 7:25; 1 Cor 10:30; 15:57; 2 Cor 2:14; 8:16; 9:15; 2 Tim 1:3). Against him are “grace” (KJV; Lightfoot), especially if the article is read. He grants that the article “has strong external support: 𝔓⁴⁶ א² B D* F G Ψ 6 1505 1739”. Apparatus [T]: that is exactly NA28’s “txt” list; the omission is in א* A C D² K L … 𝔪.
  • “The word of Christ”: objective genitive, “the message about Christ” (the gospel of 1:5); not sayings of Jesus, and not the hymn alone.
  • Hymns: 1:15–20 “comfortably qualifies” as a hymn to Christ as God (Pliny, Ep. 10.96).

Verdicts.

  • “In all wisdom” with the teaching: moderate–high (unchanged from §4).
  • Songs as the means: slight lean, moderate, with the songs also sung (unchanged).
  • ἐν [τῇ] χάριτι: slight lean to “with thankfulness”, moderate. It was open; it now has one owned holder and the context. The ESV (“with thankfulness in your hearts”) and the NASB95 (“with thankfulness”) are both on this side [T: as the exports print]. The KJV (“with grace”) is the other.

14.8 1:7, 4:8, 4:10–14, 1:11–12 and ἐξαγοράζω

  • 1:7 (Lexham Textual Notes). Their text is ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν (“on our behalf”), “attested by many early manuscripts”, against NA28’s ὑμῶν (“your”). They give no argument beyond that. So an owned edition-level work prints the other reading from NA28. Uncertain; unchanged.
  • 4:8 (Lexham Textual Notes). Both readings are set out. They add that the unaccented ΓΝΩ can be read as γνῷ (“he may know”) or γνῶ (“I may know”). The apparatus prints the lemma as γνω, unaccented [T]. Moderate; unchanged.
  • 4:10 (Wright). The term may be metaphorical, “but the literal meaning (‘fellow-prisoner-of-war’) suggests that he, too, is literally imprisoned”. Wright leans literal, with Dunn. Pao remains figurative. Wright also reads Mark’s possible visit, with Phlm 22, as showing a place of writing “not too far distant from Colosse”, which is consistent with his Ephesus.   • 1:11–12 (Wright). “It is impossible to tell” where “joyfully” goes; “it makes little overall difference”, because verse 12 continues the same sentence. Slight lean to the thanksgiving, low–moderate; unchanged (Pao for it; Wright neutral).
  • ἐξαγοράζω (LSJ, p. 580). The senses are:
    • “buy from” (Polybius 3.42.2);
    • “buy up” (Plutarch, Crassus 2);
    • “buy off” (Dicaearchus);
    • “redeem” (Diodorus 36.2; Gal 3:13).
    The middle ἐξαγοράζεσθαι τὸν καιρόν is cited only from Col 4:5 and Eph 5:16, “but -άζειν τὸν κ.” (the active) at Dan 2:8 LXX. Corpus [T]: both Greek texts of Dan 2:8 have the active ἐξαγοράζετε (“you are buying”), and Col 4:5 the middle ἐξαγοραζόμενοι (“buying up for yourselves”). Two results:
    1. “Buy up” is an attested sense outside the Bible, so the dig’s preferred reading does not depend on Daniel.
    2. LSJ knows no non-biblical use of the phrase with καιρός, and it marks the difference of voice between Daniel and Paul.
    For C57 this means Dan 2:8 is the only Greek precedent for the phrase, but in another voice and another sense. The audit’s low–moderate for the verb contact stands. Moderate–high for “buying up”; unchanged, now with lexical support.
  • πρός at 2:23 (Patrick’s check, same day). Two entries came back.
    • The abridged Liddell (the “Middle Liddell”, p. 684), not the full LSJ: under πρός with the accusative, “in hostile sense, against”: fighting against the Trojans (Homer), striving against a god (Pindar), going against someone (Sophocles); and in speeches “in reply to” (Demosthenes). This is opposition to a person, not a remedy against a condition.
    • The full LSJ, s.v. ἀλέξημα (“remedy, medicine”, p. 62): “ἀ. πρός τι” (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 7.13; Pausanias 10.18.3), “a remedy / protection against something”, beside the genitive construction (“remedy for pain”).
    • What this shows. It is the first owned illustration of πρός τι (“towards something”) in the sense “against” after a word for a remedy. But the “against” is carried by the head noun: ἀλέξημα means a defence, and πρός marks what it is aimed at. At 2:23 the head noun is τιμή (“value, honour”), which is not a word for a remedy. So the parallel supports reading 1 only if τιμή can mean “value” in the sense of “efficacy”, and then πρός is better rendered “with regard to, in dealing with” (Dunn’s “in regard to”; Wright’s “in relation to”) than “against” proper.
    • The classical references are LSJ’s; neither Dionysius nor Pausanias is in the corpus, so they are unchecked.
    • Verdict: readings 1 and 2 stay level, moderate each. Reading 1’s πρός is now illustrated in kind (πρός τι after a remedy word), no longer merely asserted. But no owned work illustrates τιμή … πρός in that sense, and that would be the decisive parallel. Superseded by the τιμή check below.
  • τιμή at 2:23 (Patrick’s paste of the whole LSJ entry). LSJ s.v. τιμή (whole entry, Patrick’s paste): sense I is honour, esteem; sense II, “of things”, is worth, value, price (buying, selling, assessment); sense III is compensation, penalty. There is no sense “efficacy, usefulness”, and no τιμή … πρός with the accusative. The idiom ἐν τιμῇ is listed under “honour” (ἐν τ. ἄγεσθαι Hdt. 1.134; ἐν μεγάλῃ τιμῇ εἶναι X. An. 2.5.38; ἐν τιμαῖς ἔχειν). Corpus [T]: ἐν τιμῇ / ἐν τῇ τιμῇ occurs in five other verses of Swete and the SBLGNT (Ps 44:10; 48:13, 21; Isa 14:18; 1 En. 14:16), and every one means “in honour”; 1 Thess 4:4 has the body possessed ἐν ἁγιασμῷ καὶ τιμῇ (“in holiness and honour”).
    • What follows. Reading 1 (“of no value against”) needs two things LSJ does not supply: τιμή as “efficacy”, and πρός as “against” without a remedy word before it. Readings 2 and 3 (“not in any honour”) need only the ordinary sense of both words.
    • Verdict: readings 1 and 2, level until now, become a lean to the “honour” readings (2, with 3 behind it), moderate. Reading 1 falls to low–moderate. This moves against Dunn, Pao and Wright and every modern English version, so it should be stated as a lexical lean, not a settled result. BDAG, not owned, may list a “value” sense that includes this verse; that is the one check that could restore reading 1.

15. BDAG on τιμή and πλησμονή (9 November 2026)

Patrick now has BDAG as a PDF and pasted both entries; they are filed in _texts/logos-exports/04-Reference-Works/. This supersedes the 2:23 verdict in §14.8.

  • τιμή: Col 2:23 under sense 1, “price, value”; “are of no value” (NRSV); οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι “may be a Latinism” (in pretio esse: Ovid, Fasti 5.316; Livy 39.6.9; Seneca, Ep. 75.11; Lohmeyer); Bornkamm: “nothing to do with ‘honor’”. ἐν τιμῇ εἶναι is granted as “honour” in ordinary Greek (Xenophon, Anabasis 2.5.38).
  • πλησμονή: σάρξ pejorative “acc. to vs. 18”; πρός “has the force ‘against’”; “of no value in checking self-indulgence” (Hall; Hedley; Bornkamm). Conceded: “the Gk. exegetes” read “for the gratification of physical needs”. The one parallel given, Ps.-Clem., Hom. 8.15, πρὸς τὴν ἑαυτῶν πλησμονήν, has πρός “for”.

Verdict: readings 1 and 2 level, moderate each; reading 3 low–moderate. The §14.8 lean to the “honour” readings is withdrawn.

Critical note. BDAG answers the question §14.8 left open (is there a lexicon behind “value” here?) but not the one it raised (where is πρός “against” illustrated?). The Latinism is a hypothesis BDAG marks with “may be”. And the pejorative-flesh argument suits reading 2 as well as reading 1. So BDAG restores parity; it does not reverse the lean into a lean the other way.
