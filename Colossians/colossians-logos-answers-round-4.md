## 1. The Commentator's Own Words

**1.1 Colossians 1:20, **"**to Himself**"** --- Dunn**

-   **Ask:** How does Dunn (NIGTC) take εἰς αὐτόν in Colossians 1:20
    ("to reconcile all things to him/himself")? Does he refer it to God
    or to Christ, and how does he relate it to εἰς αὐτόν in 1:16? Please
    quote his discussion.

-   **Why:** round 3's summary had Dunn reading "to him", meaning
    Christ, but in a garbled form ("Christ serves as the subject"). The
    1:15--20 dig (v1.5) now leans slightly to the Son, partly on Dunn.
    His own words decide whether he can be cited.

-   **Watch for:** the summary's confusion between the *subject* of
    reconciling and the one reconciled *to*. Paste Dunn's text if the
    answer is a summary again.

Dunn notes that unlike 1:20, where the poetic structure of the hymn
maintains the pattern of "in, through, to" resulting in reconciliation
"to him" (that is, to Christ), verse 1:22 reverts to the more typical
Pauline thought of reconciliation through Christ to God. This represents
a deliberate shift in referent between the two passages.

Regarding the relationship between the two instances of εἰς αὐτόν, Dunn
observes that verse 1:20 "echoes the 'all things through him and to him'
of 1:16"---noting the parallel structure where both verses employ this
preposition to connect "all things" to their destination. However, the
referent differs: the divine purpose in reconciliation was to restore
the harmony of original creation, bringing into renewed oneness "all
things," "whether things on the earth or things in the heavens".

The key interpretive move Dunn makes is that within the hymnic context
of 1:20, **the εἰς αὐτόν refers to Christ**, maintaining the poetic
parallelism with 1:16. The clause continues the explanation of why the
risen Christ is preeminent in all things, with the subject still being
"the fullness" (πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα), further emphasizing the personal
character of "the fullness" as the completeness of God's presence
throughout creation. Yet Dunn recognizes this as a distinctive feature
of the hymn's rhetoric, since the language in 1:22, "freed from the
poetic constraints of the hymn, reverts to the more typical Pauline
thought of reconciliation through Christ to God".

A Commentary on the Greek Text*, New International Greek Testament
Commentary (Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B. Eerdmans Publishing;
Paternoster Press, 1996), 102, 104, 107.

Here are Pao's comments in full:

**1:19** **For in him all the fullness was pleased to dwell** (ὅτι ἐν
αὐτῷ εὐδόκησεν πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα κατοικῆσαι). Paul now points to the full
deity of Christ, while in v. 20 he explains the function of this fact.
Just as v. 16 provides the basis for the assertion in v. 15, so vv.
19--20 provide the basis for v. 18b.

The difficulties in understanding this verse lie in the ambiguity of
subject of the verb "was pleased" (εὐδόκησεν), as well as the presence
of the neuter nominative/accusative phrase, "all the fullness" (πᾶν τὸ
πλήρωμα). This leads to a number of proposed readings.

\(1\) Christ or the Son is the subject of the verb, and the verb
introduces an indirect discourse: "For Christ was pleased that all the
fullness dwells in himself."[^1] This option is the least probable
because this would have to turn the dative pronoun "him" (αὐτῷ) into a
reflexive pronoun.[^2] Moreover, it is best to take "God" as the subject
of the verb "to reconcile" in v. 20.

\(2\) God as the implied subject of the verb, with the accusative phrase
as the subject of the indirect discourse: "For God was pleased to have
all his fullness dwell in the Son". In favor of this view is that "was
pleased" often takes a personal subject, and in the LXX the subject is
often God himself (cf. Pss 40:13; 51:16; 85:1; Jer 2:19; 14:10, 12; Jdt
15:10; 2 Macc 14:35; Sir 34:19; 45:19). The masculine participle "by
making peace" (εἰρηνοποιήσας, v. 20) also suggests a masculine personal
subject. Moreover, God is likely the implied subject of the act of
reconciliation in v. 20.

\(3\) A simple solution that alleviates the need to supply a subject is
one that takes "all the fullness" (πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα) as the subject of the
verb: "For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell". This
reading assumes that "all the fullness" refers to "God in all his
fullness" or "all the fullness of God", and the masculine participle
that follows in v. 20 "may be explained as a construction according to
sense."[^3]

The factors that favor "God" as the implied subject of the verb also
support this reading, but a number of additional considerations add
credence to this third option. First, in the conceptually parallel
statement in 2:9, "all the fullness" is clearly the subject of the verb:
"For in him all the fullness of deity dwells bodily." Second, in the
LXX, the verbal (πληρόω) and adjectival forms (πλήρης) of "fullness" (τὸ
πλήρωμα) often point to the presence of God (Isa 6:1; Ezek 43:5; 44:4;
Hag 2:7).[^4] The understanding of "all the fullness" as referring to
God is further supported by the possible allusion to Ps 68:16 (LXX Ps
67:17): "at the mountain which God was pleased \[ὃ ευδόκησεν ὁ θεός\] to
dwell in it."[^5] It seems best, therefore, to see "all the fullness" as
a periphrasis for God; to insert God as the implied subject is thus
redundant.

By using the phrase "all the fullness" as referring to God, Paul keeps
the emphasis on the Son, to whom the phrase "in him" refers.[^6] In the
wisdom tradition, God's act of filling the earth signifies his dominion
and authority (cf. Wis 1:7; Philo, *Alleg. Interp.* 1.44).[^7] Paul's
use of "in him" therefore emphasizes again the supreme authority of
Christ. This point is reinforced by the "dwell" (κατοικέω) word group,
one that evokes the presence of God in both his heavenly (1 Kgs 8:39,
43, 49; 2 Chr 6:30, 33, 39) and earthly (2 Chr 6:18, 21) abodes. Christ
fulfills the role of the temple in which one finds the full presence of
God. This points to the reality of the incarnation.[^8] "Fullness"
becomes a technical term in later Gnostic writings, and it might have
been used in a similar way in certain circles in the first century
AD.[^9]

This note on incarnation reminds the readers that the christological
point acquires a soteriological function here.[^10] All fullness dwells
in Christ, so that through him universal reconciliation can be
accomplished (v. 20). What is striking, however, is the focus on Christ
as the embodiment of full deity in a section that begins with a
reference to his own death (v. 18c). Instead of resting his arguments on
the abstract assurance of Christ's exalted status, Paul shows how
Christ's exalted status is relevant for those who are alienated from
God, particularly in his work on the cross.

Like the first section of the hymn, this one also contains the three
prepositional phrases: "in him" (ἐν αὐτῷ, vv. 16, 19), "through him"
(διʼ αὐτοῦ, vv. 16, 20), and "for/to him" (εἰς αὐτόν, vv. 16, 20). In
light of the consistent usage of the pronoun "him" (αὐτός) in reference
to Christ in this hymn, "to him" (εἰς αὐτόν) here should best be taken
as also referring to Christ. Most versions prefer to translate this
personal pronoun as a reflexive ("to himself") because elsewhere the one
to whom the created order is reconciled is always God himself (Rom 5:10;
2 Cor 5:18--19).[^11] In context, however, the strength of other Pauline
usages fails to supersede the clear context where Christ is the agent
and the goal of both the original and new creation.[^12] The striking
departure from these other usages achieves its intended purpose to
highlight the close identification between God and Christ. This high
Christology provides the critical foundation for Paul's subsequent
arguments in this letter.

> Pao, D.W. (2012) *Colossians and Philemon*. Grand Rapids, MI:
> Zondervan (Zondervan Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament:), pp.
> 101--102, 104.

**not in any honour**, οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι. Here belongs έστίν, which
follows ἄτινα, in order to contrast with "the repute of wisdom among the
people," the Apostle's judgment, *viz.:* the repute is without honorable
grounds, without true honor. This is strongly affirmed; there is nothing
at all in it which is really honorable; hence "in any honor" is a sharp
negation (οὐκ) of will-worship, humility and unsparingness of the body.

To this negative Paul adds a positive statement: \[**only**\] **to the
satisfying of the flesh**, πρὸς πλησμοὴν τῆς σαρκός.---The former clause
denies "the repute of wisdom" as a just repute; this gives a motive for
the negation, in connexion with "unsparingness of the body." The false
doctrine tends (πρός) to a satisfying (in contrast with "unsparingness")
of the fleshly nature (τῆς σαρκός opposed to σώματος). It is incorrect
to render: "not giving to the flesh the honor due to its necessities"
(Luther and others). Πλησμονή implies blame (Bengel: *fere excessum
denotat*) and cannot=πρόνοια (Rom. 13:14). The distinction between τοῦ
σώματος and τῆς σαρκός, and the omission of τοῦ σώματος after ἑν τιμῇ
τινι must not be overlooked. Grotius singularly deduces praise from
this: *habent ista rationem non stultam, si adsint cautiones, si sponte
ista suscipiantur non abominando ea, quæ deus creavit*,---*cum ea
modestia animi, quæ alios aliter viventes non damnet*,---*si hoc sibt
propositum habeant, dure tractare corpus neque carni obsequi ad
saturitalem.*---\[Braune's view is that of Meyer, and is to be
preferred, 1) as least un-grammatical; 2) as giving the best correlate
to μέν; 3) preserving the distinction between σε͂μα and σάρξ; 4) bringing
out the bad sense of πλησμονήν and thus conveying the sharp
condemnation, that asceticism, while it appears to subdue the body,
serves only to gratify the flesh and its evil nature. For other
interpretations see Eadie, Alford, Ellicott. The latter agrees most
nearly with Braune.

> Lange, J.P. *et al.* (2008) *A commentary on the Holy Scriptures:
> Colossians*. Bellingham, WA: Logos Bible Software, p. 57.

Bird: no comment specific to Sabbath.

**Dunn**: Characteristically Jewish ... is the practice of referring to
the "sabbath" in the plural... But if sabbath is so clearly a
distinctively Jewish festival, then the probability is that the
"festival" and "new moon" also refer to the Jewish versions of these
celebrations. The point is put beyond dispute when we note that the
three terms together, "sabbaths, new moons, and feasts," was in fact a
regular Jewish way of speaking of the main festivals of Jewish religion
(1 Chron. 23:31; 2 Chron. 2:3; 31:3; Neh. 10:33; Isa. 1:13--14; 1 Macc.
10:34; **Ezek. 45:17**, and **Hos. 2:11** in reverse order, as here;
see, e.g., Sappington 163; Aletti, *Épître aux Colossiens* 193 n. 112).

**Dunn**, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon:
a commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), p. 174-5.

**Pao**: "Festival" (ἐορτῆς), "new moon" (νεομηνίας), and "sabbaths"
(σαββάτων) point clearly to a Jewish context. These three terms appear
together in a number of OT passages (e.g., 1 Chr 23:31; 2 Chr 2:4; 31:3;
**Ezek 45:17**; cf. 2 Kgs 4:23; Neh 10:33; Isa 66:23; Ezek 46:1; Amos
8:5). Some consider these terms here simply as the imposition of a
"religious calendar" on the Colossian believers.[^13] It should be
noted, however, that when these terms are listed together in the OT, it
often refers to cultic rituals linked with these festal days. If so,
Paul is not opposed to the Jewish calendar per se but to the imposition
of practices related to these feasts.[^14]

Pao, D.W. (2012) Colossians and Philemon. Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan
(Zondervan Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament:), p. 185.

**Witherington**: Paul says in v. 16, believers should let no one judge
them with food rules, festivals, or Sabbath requirements.[^15]
Christians are not under such OT rules. Rather they are creatures of the
new covenant. It is evading the point to say that Paul meant that
believers no longer keep the Sabbath in the way that the false teachers
were suggesting. Rather, Paul says, "let no one cause you to submit to
food laws or Sabbath regulations."[^16] It would appear that he believed
that Christ fulfilled or brought to an end (or both) all such rules and
paid the price so that believers are no longer in their debt. We owe the
rules nothing.

Witherington, B., III (2007) The letters to Philemon, the Colossians,
and the Ephesians : a socio-rhetorical commentary on the captivity
Epistles. Grand Rapids, MI: Wm. B. Eerdmans Publishing Co., p. 160.

**Wright**: *Or with regard to a religious festival, a new moon
celebration or a Sabbath day* is a fairly typical list of Jewish holy
days (e.g. Ezek. 45:17; Hos. 2:11), referring in descending order to the
great annual festivals, the monthly celebrations and the weekly
Sabbaths.

**2.3 Colossians 1:15--20 as a **"**hymn**"

-   **Ask:** In my Colossians commentaries, do Dunn, Wright, Pao and
    Witherington treat Colossians 1:15--20 as a pre-Pauline hymn that
    the author adapted, or as written for this letter? What evidence
    does each give, and does anyone say that "the church" (1:18) or
    "through the blood of His cross" (1:20) was added?
-   **Why:** round 3 found holders only outside your library (MacDonald;
    the *Dictionary of Paul and His Letters*, Bevere and Frederick). The
    overview tags the hymn as a form-critical reconstruction *\[S\]*.
-   **Watch for:** "additions" that rest only on the reconstruction. The
    1:15--20 dig finds both phrases bound into the strophe structure.

**Dunn** acknowledges that it can never be definitively proven that
preformed material was incorporated here, noting it remains possible
that Paul himself became lyrical or simply struck a purple
passage.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F375457)
However, he observes that the second strophe does not fall into as
natural a rhythmic pattern as the first, and there is tension between a
cosmos sustained "in him" versus one disrupted and alienated, which is
presumably why NA 26 prints only 1:15--18a in poetic lines.

**Dunn** proposes an alternative hypothesis: a one-verse hymn about
Christ's role in creation was supplemented by a second hand to elaborate
on Christ's redemptive work. He further entertains whether 1:15--18a was
a pre-Christian hymn in praise of Wisdom or Logos (nothing in these
verses need refer to Christ apart from the last two words), which Paul
and Timothy took over and elaborated to indicate both Christ's
"takeover" of Wisdom's role and the completion of that role through
redemption.

Regarding interpolations, Dunn identifies the most commonly agreed
glosses as: lines about the visible and invisible authorities
(introduced because of their importance to the letter's argument), "the
church" (1:18a), by which a pre-Christian hymn to Wisdom could have been
"christianized," and "through the blood of his cross" (1:20), which
would explain the awkwardness of the second "through him."

**Wright's approach** differs markedly. Wright analyzes the poetic
structure by highlighting connecting words, identifying four sections
where the outer two begin with "who is" and the inner two with "and he,"
demonstrating impressive balance in detailed content. Wright does not
argue for pre-Pauline composition; rather, he treats the passage as
Paul's own poetic expression.

**Pao's position** is more skeptical of pre-Pauline theories. Pao argues
that the arguments for Paul's use of an existing hymn are not decisive,
noting that Paul's ability to provide well-crafted arguments elsewhere
suggests he could create structured pieces even in difficult
circumstances, and the content is not sufficient evidence against
Pauline authorship. While scholars cannot agree on the form of the
"original hymn," most agree that vv. 15--16a and 19--20a should be part
of it, with "the church" (v. 18a) and "through the blood of his cross"
(v. 20b) as obvious Pauline insertions. However, Pao notes that nothing
in the hymn contradicts Paul's teaching elsewhere, and everything should
be considered significant parts of Paul's wider argument.

**Witherington's view** is nuanced. He suggests it seems quite possible
that Paul either transformed an earlier hymn into Asiatic style or
composed the hymn himself for this specific occasion, as it fits
perfectly in the discourse and immediate context. He proposes that if
the hymn did come from someone before Paul, Paul may have transferred
the phrase about "making peace through the blood of the cross" so that
"the body" could be the nadir of the V-pattern, and "the church" may be
a Pauline addition in v. 18. Even if Paul used a pre-existing pattern,
he made the material his own and adapted it to his rhetorical purposes.

4.3 2 Chronicles 19:6--7 and Deuteronomy 10:17 behind 3:22--4:1 (C35)

-   **Ask:** Do any commentators on Colossians 3:22--4:1 refer to 2
    Chronicles 19:6--7 (Jehoshaphat's judges: "you do not judge for man
    but for the LORD ... no injustice or partiality") or Deuteronomy
    10:17 ("who shows no partiality") behind "no partiality" (3:25) and
    "a Master in heaven" (4:1)?
-   **Why:** C35, low--moderate. 2 Chr 19:7 is the one Swete verse with
    fear + Lord + ἀδικ- + πρόσωπον.
-   **Watch for:** Rom 2:11 and Eph 6:9 offered instead. Record them,
    but they are not the OT claim.

Commentators do make these connections. Colossians 3:25 is linked with
Deuteronomy 10:17 and 2 Chronicles 19:7,[^18] establishing the Old
Testament foundation for the principle of divine impartiality.

Deuteronomy 10:17 declares that "Yahweh your God, he is God of the gods
and Lord of the lords, the great and mighty God, the awesome one who is
not partial, and he does not take bribes."[^19] Additional references to
God's impartiality can be found in various OT books, including 2
Chronicles 19:7, which addresses judicial impartiality in the context of
Jehoshaphat's judges.

In Ephesians 6:9, Paul commands masters not to be harsh to their slaves
because they share the same master in heaven with whom "there is no
partiality" (compare Colossians 3:25). This parallel passage
demonstrates how Paul applies the Old Testament principle of divine
impartiality to the master-slave relationship---precisely the context of
Colossians 3:22--4:1.

A reference work on Old Testament quotations and allusions explicitly
lists Colossians 3:25 as alluding to both 2 Chronicles 19:7 and
Deuteronomy 10:17, and Colossians 4:1 as alluding to Leviticus 25:43 and
25:53.[^20]

The principle of God's impartiality appears throughout the canon, with
its primary expressions found in the Torah. Leviticus 19:15 prohibits
showing partiality in judgment, and Deuteronomy 10:17 declares that God
"is not partial, and he does not take bribes." References to God's
impartiality also appear in 2 Chronicles 19:7, among other Old Testament
and Jewish writings.[^21]

Regarding Colossians 3:22--4:1 specifically, Ephesians 6:9 commands
masters not to threaten their slaves because they share the same master
in heaven with whom "there is no partiality"---a passage that parallels
Colossians 3:25. In Ephesians 6:9, masters are called to do the same as
slaves, giving up threats because both master and slave have the Lord in
heaven and there is no favoritism with him---an awareness that matches
what slaves are to have as they serve their masters.[^22]

The presence of the Lord functions as "the great leveller," since he has
no favorites and will treat everyone the same, with all being equal
before him according to Deuteronomy 10:17 and 2 Chronicles 19:7. This
means that at the final judgment, justice will be done to correct any
injustice, for what a master might miss or leave unexpressed, the Lord
will see and reward.

## 5. Colossians 3:18--4:1 --- the Three Cruxes

5.1 3:24, δουλεύετε --- indicative or imperative?

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 3:24, is τῷ κυρίῳ Χριστῷ δουλεύετε ("you
    serve the Lord Christ") taken as a statement or a command by Dunn,
    Pao, Wright and Witherington, and what reasons do they give?
-   **Why:** an open crux in the 3:18--4:1 dig. The NA28 has no γάρ; the
    majority text's γάρ (KJV "for ye serve") makes it a statement. The
    dig recalls the Vulgate as *servite* (imperative), unchecked.
-   **Watch for:** an answer settled by the KJV's γάρ without mentioning
    that it is a variant.

Dunn treats the clause as a motivational statement rather than an
imperative command. The phrase provides "further motivation" for the
exhortation to serve masters wholeheartedly, grounded in the recognition
that believers ultimately "serve the same Lord from whom they will
receive the inheritance as a due return." Dunn notes that "the 'Lord' is
Christ" emerges clearly from the surrounding context, and he emphasizes
that this assignment of Christ's role as "final arbiter in the affairs
of humankind" reflects the letter's elevated christology while also
drawing on earlier Pauline themes.

The grammatical form itself---a present tense indicative rather than an
imperative---supports reading it as declarative: a statement of fact
about whom the slaves actually serve, which then motivates compliance
with earthly masters. This functions rhetorically as motivation grounded
in reality rather than as a new command layered onto the preceding
imperatives.

\[James D. G. Dunn, *The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: A
Commentary on the Greek Text*, New International Greek Testament

Pao treats δουλεύετε as an indicative verb ("you serve the Lord
Christ"), and he presents multiple arguments supporting this reading.
First, all the imperatives in the household code thus far address
reciprocal relationships between household members, and introducing an
imperative with "the Lord" would break this structural pattern. Second,
the identification of "the Lord" as "Christ" appears to explain the
preceding phrase "from the Lord," functioning as an indicative
explanatory clause: "it is the Lord Christ whom you serve." Third, the
phrase "the Lord Christ" appears elsewhere in Paul only in [Romans
16:18](https://ref.ly/Ro16.18), where it identifies Christ as Lord amid
competing claims of lordship, suggesting it functions primarily as an
identification statement.

However, Pao notes that a majority of recent commentators take the verb
as an imperative: "Serve the Lord Christ." This reading is supported by
the lack of a conjunction, which is consistent with other imperatival
clauses throughout the code. As an imperative, the clause parallels the
imperative in verse 23 ("do it wholeheartedly") while explicitly
identifying Christ as Lord. Additionally, the "for" clause in verse 25
provides a basis for this imperative reading.

5.2 3:25 --- whose wrongdoing?

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 3:25 ("he who does wrong will receive the
    consequences of the wrong"), do the commentators apply the warning
    to slaves, to masters, or to both, and why?
-   **Why:** the dig finds that "no partiality" faces both ways in
    Colossians, while Eph 6:9 places it with masters (Headline 4). The
    commentators' reading tests that finding.
-   **Watch for:** Philemon 18 ("if he has wronged you", ἀδικέω) brought
    in to make it Onesimus. Note who does this.

Commentators apply the warning in Colossians 3:25 to **both slaves and
masters**, though the immediate context addresses slaves directly.

The warning operates under the principle that Christ "will judge without
favoritism...in full justice, repaying wrongdoers and rewarding those
who serve Him."\[1\] Slaves, like their masters, "will be duly requited
for wrongdoing," and "in the court of the heavenly Master no favouritism
is shown in dispensing rewards and punishments or in treating masters
and slaves."\[2\]

The commentators emphasize that this principle applies universally. The
principle "applies to all alike, rich and poor, slave and free, servant
and master."\[3\] Paul does not "glamorize slavery, as though, however a
slave acted, he could do no wrong," and slaves could be "guilty of
pilfering...and...disrespectful."\[2\]

However, the warning carries particular force for masters in the
following verse. Paul "warns masters against abusing their authority,"
knowing "that those who possess authority are prone to use their social
position to oppress those beneath them," and therefore masters "must
desist from threatening in a domineering and imperious way," for
"masters too have the same Lord in heaven, and he is impartial and will
judge them if they mistreat their slaves."\[4\]

The dual application reflects Paul's strategy: the warning to slaves
motivates faithful service despite injustice, while the warning to
masters restrains their abuse of power---both accountable to the same
impartial heavenly judge.

**5.3 4:1, ἰσότης --- **"**fairness**"** or **"**equality**"**?**

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 4:1, how do the commentators take τὴν ἰσότητα
    ("what is fair" / "equality") --- fair treatment within the
    relationship, or a sense of equality between master and slave? What
    usage do they cite?
-   **Why:** an open crux in the dig; the ESV's "justly and fairly"
    loses the noun. Paul's only other use is 2 Cor 8:13--14, where it
    means "equality".
-   **Watch for:** Philo's or the Stoics' use of ἰσότης cited without a
    reference.

Commentators interpret τὴν ἰσότητα ("equality") in Colossians 4:1 along
two distinct lines, though they differ on whether this represents merely
fair treatment or a deeper theological equality.

One interpretive tradition distinguishes between τὸ δίκαιον (what
belongs to slaves as God's creatures according to creation's
regulations) and τὴν ἰσότητα (equality ordained within redemption, by
which the redeemed are brethren, a parity masters should demonstrate in
their treatment of slaves). This view rejects understanding ἰσότητα as
merely "equity" or "impartial treatment", instead grounding it in the
theological reality of Christian brotherhood.[^28]

However, another commentator notes that ἰσότητα carries "a double
meaning, not only indicating 'fair' treatment but also directing owners
to consider slaves 'equal' to themselves before God". This dual semantic
range reflects the term's flexibility. The connection to Colossians
3:11---where Paul affirms that in Christ there is "neither slave nor
free"---suggests that masters must reflect this new relational reality
in their treatment of slaves[^29].

From a broader theological perspective, while masters and slaves were
patently unequal in culture and law (since one owned the other), they
were equal before God because they had the same Lord and judge who
showed no partiality between them. Their shared knowledge of Christ's
lordship and future judgment made them equal, and remembering that Jesus
was their common Lord and judge would transform their entire attitude
toward one another[^30].\

\(146\) And even if there be not as yet any one who is worthy to be
