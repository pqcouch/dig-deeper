## 1. A Named Commentator's View

1.1 Colossians 2:23, the last clause

-   **Ask:** How does Dunn (NIGTC) translate and explain the last clause
    of Colossians 2:23, "not of any value against the indulgence of the
    flesh" (οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός)? What does he
    take πρός, τιμή and πλησμονή to mean, and what other readings does
    he mention?
-   **Why:** this is the hardest clause in the letter and the 2:16--23
    dig leaves it open (Open Question 1). The modern versions read πρός
    as "against" and πλησμονή as "indulgence"; the older Latin shape
    reads "not in any honour, for the satisfaction of the flesh", which
    makes the regimen *serve* the flesh. Both readings support the dig's
    Headline 2 (severity to the body cannot touch the flesh); which one
    a sermon uses depends on the answer.
-   **Watch for:** whether "against" for πρός is supported with examples
    or simply asserted; and whether πλησμονή is given its usual neutral
    sense ("satisfaction") or a negative one ("gratification").
-   **Then ask the same question of:** Pao (ZECNT), Wright (TNTC) and
    Brown (LRC).

Dunn:

The phrase ἐν τιμῇ τινι is highly unusual, but **τιμή** in the sense of
"value, worth" is well enough known (LSJ s.v. II), and the phrase itself
may be a Latinism (BAGD s.v. 1).

For **πρός** in the sense of "for," that is, to check or prevent or
cure, see again Lightfoot 204--5. If the phrase should be taken
together, a translation "not of any value to anyone in regard to" seems
best.

**πλησμονή** clearly means "being filled, satiety," particularly with
regard to food and drink, and so "gratification" (BAGD). And the most
obvious implication here is that "gratification of the flesh" is
something undesirable. If we compare its use in the LXX, a neutral sense
predominates (satisfaction of hunger, usually in the equivalent
prepositional phrase, εἰς πλησμονήν, e.g., Exod. 16:3, 8; Lev. 25:19;
Prov. 3:10; Lam. 5:6); but a negative note is probably present in Ezek.
16:49 ("surfeit of food") and Hos. 13:6 (εἰς πλησμονήν).[^1]

How then should the pieces be put together into a single sentence? The
most common interpretation in effect makes the understandable assumption
that the sentence is structured around the contrast provided by the two
ἐν phrases: "Such things have a reputation of wisdom in (ἐν) ... but are
not in value (ἐν τιμῇ, i.e., "of worth") to anyone...."[^2] The
difficulty here is the absence of an adversative before the οὐκ ("but,"
ἀλλά, or δέ to balance the earlier μέν). It is true that the omission of
an adversative is not exceptional (BDF §447.2, 3; Moule, *Colossians and
Philemon* 108, suggests the rather different 2 Tim. 2:14 as a parallel);
but the absence of an adversative here is so striking (it would leave
Paul's conclusion critically ambiguous) that the attempt should be made
to make sense of the text without reading one in. A well-argued
alternative takes everything from λόγον μέν ... ἐν τιμῇ τινι as a
parenthesis, leaving "which things are for gratification of the flesh"
as the main clause, itself a rebuke to the attitude expressed in
2:21;[^3] but the lack of an adversative at the same point is still a
problem and the whole remains awkward.

Perhaps a better key to structure is to note the further play on the
concepts σῶμα and σάρξ, since this has been such a feature of the letter
so far (1:22, 24; 2:11, 17--19). That is to say, the contrast between
ἀφειδίᾳ σώματος and πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός needs to be given more weight.
When we add in the likelihood that the original text did not have an
"and" linking ἀφειδίᾳ σώματος to what went before (see n. 2 above), we
are left with the possibility that ἀφειδίᾳ σώματος ... is attached to
the preceding context in apposition to the antecedent datives. This
would diminish the problem of the lack of an adversative, since the
contrast between the two main phrases would be sufficient of itself to
make the point. The following sense is the result: "which things have a
reputation of wisdom in self-chosen worship and humility---severe
treatment of the body, not of value to anyone in regard to the
gratification of the flesh."

(Dunn, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: a
commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), p. 195-7.)

Pao:

The translation adopted here demands a discussion of the structure of
the entire verse: "These rules have no value in restraining the
gratification of the flesh, even though they have an appearance of
wisdom with their self-imposed worship, self-humiliation, and harsh
treatment of the body." Considered "almost impossible to translate,"[^4]
the difficulties of this verse lie in the possible meanings of the
various parts of the verse and the lack of clarity concerning the
relationship between these parts. The basic difference among the
proposals centers on the decision concerning the main clause and
therefore what is to be taken together with the first two words (lit.,
"which are"; ἅτινά ἐστιν).

\(1\) In light of its close proximity, some consider the participle
"having" (ἔχοντα) to be linked with "are" (ἐστιν), thus forming a
periphrastic: "Such rules indeed appear wise...."[^5] The placement of
the contrastive marker (μέν) between the finite verb and the participle
is unusual, however, especially when it is expected to appear as the
second word of a clause.

\(2\) Some take "are" (ἐστιν) with the final prepositional phrase,
"result in the indulgence of the flesh" (πρὸς πλησμονὴν τῆς σαρκός),[^6]
with the two clauses in between, the second subordinating to the first:
"Even though they have the appearance of wisdom with their self-imposed
worship and false humility achieved by an unsparing treatment of the
body---​a wisdom with no true value---​they in reality result in fleshly
indulgence" (NET). While this does take into account of the postpositive
position of the particle μέν, it seems difficult to have two subordinate
clauses interrupting the flow of the sentence, especially when the
second subordinate clause modifies the first.

\(3\) Although not without difficulties, the best reading is to consider
"no value in restraining the gratification of the flesh"[^7] as
completing the thought introduced by the finite verb "are" (ἐστιν), with
the intervening clause as a concessive clause, as in NAB: "While they
have a semblance of wisdom in rigor of devotion and self-abasement
\[and\] severity to the body, they are of no value against gratification
of the flesh." This reading takes advantage of reading (2) in treating
the particle μέν in the postpositive position, but without assuming the
presence of two complex subordinating clauses in between. Moreover, the
negative particle "not" (οὐκ) can function as a particle that provides
the contrast to the statement introduced by the particle μέν.[^8] The
contrast set up by the phrases "the appearance of wisdom" and "no value"
also fits well within the flow of Paul's argument here.[^9] Finally,
this reading also recognizes the contrast between the two clauses that
contain the preposition "by means of/in" (ἐν).

"Have no value" (οὐκ ἐν τιμῇ τινι) literally reads "not in any honour"
(KJV), and those who take this word in the sense of "honor" would
suggest that "the 'honor' (τιμή), which they claim for themselves,
cannot be conferred on them."[^10] In this context the word commonly
rendered as "honor" can acquire the sense of "value" or "price" (Matt
27:9; Acts 4:34; 1 Cor 6:20; 7:23).[^11] Most versions therefore
translate this phrase as "are of no value" (ASV, NASB, NRSV, NKJV, ESV;
cf. NET, TNIV, NIV). Taking the preposition πρός in the sense of
"against," Paul is affirming that the ascetic rules and regulations have
no value in combating the desires of "the flesh." This reference to the
failure of their practices recalls Paul's critique of the false teachers
as "conceited without reason by his carnal mind" (v. 18). Paul's
rhetoric here points to his sustained attempt in this section to unmask
the claims of the false teachers as their seemingly spiritual practices
simply reflect their denial of the sufficiency of Christ.

(Pao, D.W. (2012) Colossians and Philemon. Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan
(Zondervan Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament:), pp. 196--198.)

Wright:

The Greek of the last verse of chapter 2 is sufficiently odd for several
sober-minded commentators to suggest that, despite the absence of
manuscript variations, scribal corruption has occurred at an early stage
in transmission. Against this it must be said that any manuscript which
offered an easy way out of the problems of verse 23 would instantly
become suspect for just that reason; scribes are more likely to smooth
out oddities than to introduce them. The first half of the verse is not
too difficult, though a little awkward in expression. NIV translates,
probably rightly, such regulations indeed have an appearance of
(possibly 'a reputation for') wisdom, with their self-imposed worship
(the most likely meaning of a word which occurs only here: it could also
indicate a self-imposed discipline heavily enforced), their false
humility (as in v. 18, the idea 'false' has to be supplied from the
context) and their harsh treatment of the body. It is not difficult to
picture the sort of religion Paul is opposing here. Its elaborate
liturgies and seemingly rigorous self-abasing asceticism give it a name
for serious piety: but it is a sham.

Why it is a sham Paul apparently says in the second half of the verse.
But what exactly his reason may be has long baffled commentators.[^12]
One suggested alternative (e.g. RSV margin) gives a strong charge
against these regulations: they 'are of no value, serving only to
indulge the flesh'. What looks like rigorous discipline is in fact a
subtle form of self-indulgence. This involves reading most of the verse
as a parenthesis: 'which are (though possessing a reputation for wisdom
with their self-imposed worship, abject grovellings and harsh physical
discipline, all to no value) merely a way of gratifying the flesh'. This
may ease the grammatical problem in the first part of the verse, where
the main verb 'are' (estin) is followed almost at once by an accusative
participle 'having' (echonta), but at the cost of straining the rest of
the sentence almost unbearably. Another alternative is to take the Greek
word time (here rendered 'value') in its more usual sense of 'honour',
and to suggest that perhaps these practices do not give proper honour to
the human body; but this imports too many extraneous ideas into the
clause. It is safest overall to follow NIV (and RSV): they (i.e. these
regulations) lack any value in restraining sensual indulgence. This
takes the clause as a single whole, understanding pros plēsmonēn tēs
sarkos, literally 'towards the gratifying of the flesh', as modifying
time, 'value'.[^13] The value these practices do not have is precisely
the one which might make them worthwhile, and which their supporters
claim for them: that they restrain fleshly indulgence (which includes
not only sensuality but also jealousy, anger and so forth: see Gal.
5:19--21). Genuine holiness, which is an anticipation of the life of the
age to come into which the risen Christ has already entered (1:18;
3:1--4), is not to be had by methods whose very nature, focusing as it
does on perishable material things, binds them to the present age.

(Wright, N.T. (1986) Colossians and Philemon: an introduction and
commentary. Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press (Tyndale New Testament
Commentaries), pp. 131--133.)

**1.2 Colossians 2:18, **"**the worship of the angels**"

-   **Ask:** What does Dunn say about "the worship of the angels"
    (θρησκείᾳ τῶν ἀγγέλων) in Colossians 2:18? Does he take the genitive
    as the angels being worshipped, or as the worship the angels offer,
    and what evidence does he give?
-   **Why:** the dig found that the grammar cannot decide --- the LXX
    has an objective genitive with θρησκεία at Wis 14:27 ("the worship
    of idols") and a subjective one at 4 Macc 5:6 ("the religion of the
    Jews") (Open Question 2). The answer decides how the rival teaching
    is described in a sermon (overview, Trap 2).
-   **Watch for:** a reconstruction ("the Colossian heresy was ...")
    doing the work instead of the Greek.
-   **Then ask:** "Which commentators hold each view of 'the worship of
    the angels' in Colossians 2:18, and what is the main argument on
    each side?" (Brown's survey is likely to answer this best.)

Dunn:

"Worship of angels" is more problematic, but a wholly natural rendering
would take it as worship given to angels (objective genitive).[^14] It
is true that there is no close parallel to the phrase, but popular
religion in the Greco-Roman world did reckon with ἄγγελοι, "messengers"
both from heaven and from the underworld (W. Grundmann, TDNT 1.75). And
there is some evidence for worship of angels in western Asia Minor,
first adduced by W. Ramsay (BAGD s.v. θρησκεία; Sheppard; Trebilco
132--33; DeMaris 62), though it may equally suggest pagan borrowing of
only half-understood Jewish concepts (NDIEC 5.72--73, 136; Sheppard
86--87; Trebilco 137; Mitchell 2.45--46; see also pp. 29ff. above).[^15]
A plausible picture can thus emerge, one which envisages the Colossian
"philosophy" as a syncretistic religious mix involving ascetic practices
and worship of angels. Linked with the talk of rulers and authorities
(1:16; 2:15), these angels could be seen within the "philosophy" as
either benevolent, and therefore to be worshiped to attain their
blessing, or malevolent, and therefore to be appeased.[^16]

How does this fit with the strongly Jewish character which has been
evident in earlier allusions to the Colossian "philosophy"? "Humility"
as fasting is certainly Jewish enough. But worship of angels is
something one would not expect in any of the forms of Judaism known to
us for this period. It is true that various second-century sources
describe (or accuse) Jews of worshiping angels: Kerygma Petri (in
Clement of Alexandria, Stromata 6.5.41.2); Apology of Aristides 14:4;
and Celsus, in Origen's Contra Celsum 1:26 and 5:6 (also Origen himself
in Comm. in Joann. 13:17); but none of these can be described as a
friendly witness.[^17] Pseudo-Philo 13.6 also speaks of "an offering for
your watchers (= guardian angels?)"; 1 Enoch 48:5 and 62:6, 9 envisage
worship given to the Son of Man; and later Tosefta Hullin 2:18 alludes
to angel worship within popular Judaism (GLAJJ 2.295).[^18]

More characteristic of Judaism, however, was warning against worship of
the host of heaven (Deut. 4:19; 17:3; Jer. 8:2; 19:13; Zeph. 1:5),
including the repeated warnings in first-century Judaism against the
worship of angels (Apocalypse of Zephaniah 6:15; Apocalypse of Abraham
17:2; Philo, De fuga et inventione 212; De somnis 1.232, 238; similarly
Rev. 19:10 and 22:9; Ascension of Isaiah 7:21);[^19] in Adam and Eve
13--15 angels are commanded by Michael to worship Adam as the image of
God; in pseudo-Philo 34:2 sacrifice to angels is linked with magic and
condemned; and when in the early second century Elisha ben Abuyah hailed
a second divine power in heaven, he was completely disowned as apostate
by his fellow rabbis (for details see, e.g., Rowland, Open Heaven
331--39). Were the Colossian "philosophy" Jewish in character, on this
hypothesis, we would have to envisage a very syncretistic form of
Judaism, unlike anything else we know of. This, however, hardly squares
well with the evidence of a Jewish character for the "philosophy" which
relished not simply odd bits and pieces abstracted from Judaism but the
identity markers which marked out ethnic Jews anxious to maintain their
ancestral traditions (circumcision, food laws, and sabbath in
particular; see on 2:11 and 2:16).[^20]

However, an alternative hypothesis has been put forward, particularly by
F. O. Francis, which has proved influential and has been helpfully
elaborated by others (see p. 29 n. 27[^21] above). This starts from the
neglected observation that θρησκεία τῶν ἀγγέλων can also be taken as a
subjective genitive (as in 4 Macc. 5:7; Josephus, Antiquities 12.253),
denoting worship offered by angels to God. Such worship is implicit
already in the great visions of Isa. 6:2--3; Dan. 7:10; and 1 Enoch
14:18--23 and explicit in 1 Enoch 36:4, 39--40; 61:10--12; 2 Enoch
20--21; Apocalypse of Abraham 17--18; Testament of Levi 3:3--8, not to
mention the Christian evidence of Luke 2:14; Phil. 2:10--11; Revelation
4--5; and Ascension of Isaiah 7--9.[^22] But more to the point here is
the evidence of a desire particularly within apocalyptic and mystical
circles of first-century Judaism to join in with the worship of angels
in heaven. It is implicit already in such Psalms as 29:1--2 and
148:1--2. But it is most strikingly attested in Testament of Job 48--50,
where the three daughters of Job speak in the language of angels,
praising and worshiping God. Similarly, in Apocalypse of Abraham 17
Abraham is taught a hymn of praise by the angel who joins with him in
reciting it. The same motif is a feature of Ascension of Isaiah
7:13--9:33 (particularly 8:17 and 9:28, 33). And in Apocalypse of
Zephaniah 8:3--4 the seer sees the angels praying and prays with them,
knowing their language (see further Sappington 90--94; Mach 239--40;
Morray-Jones, "Paradise Revisited" 182; cf. Attridge).

Most interesting of all is the evidence that such worship was coveted at
Qumran.[^23] According to 1QSa 2:8--9 the rules for the congregation of
the last days would have to be strict, "for the Angels of Holiness are
\[with\] their \[congregation\]." But the implication of other
references is that these rules were already in operation, indicating
that the Qumran community saw itself as a priestly community whose
holiness was defined by the presence of the angels (cf. particularly
4QCDb and 1QM 7:4--6 with Lev. 21:17--21). So explicitly in 1QH
3:21--22: "Thou hast cleansed a perverse spirit of great sin ... that it
may enter into community with the congregation of the Sons of Heaven"
(similarly 1QH 11:10--13). More to the immediate point, in 1QSb 4:25--26
one of the blessings of the priest is: "May you be as an Angel of the
Presence in the Abode of Holiness to the glory of the God of
\[hosts\].... May you attend upon the service in the Temple of the
Kingdom and decree destiny in company with the Angels of the Presence."
Most interesting of all are the recently published complete (but often
fragmentary) texts of the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice (4Q400--405),
which contain songs of praise to be offered to God by angels in the
heavenly temple during the first thirteen sabbaths of the year and in
which it is clear enough (since the Songs presumably belonged to the
community's liturgy) that the community itself (or at least its priests)
joined with the angels in reciting these songs of heavenly worship.[^24]

It is quite possible, therefore, to envisage a Jewish (or Christian
Jewish) synagogue in Colossae which was influenced by such ideas and
which delighted in their worship sabbath by sabbath as a participation
in the worship of the angels in heaven (cf. 1 Cor. 11:10). In this case
the "humility" associated with this worship could very well denote the
spiritual discipline and mortification (particularly, but not only,
fasting; see above) regarded as essential to maintain the holiness
required to participate with the holy ones and the holy angels (see also
1:12). The association of fasting and heavenly revelation is stressed in
such passages as Dan. 10:2--3; Apocalypse of Abraham 9:7--10; 12:1--2;
Testament of Isaac 4:1--6; 5:4; 4 Ezra 5:13; 6:35; and 2 Baruch 5:7; and
Philo notes that Moses heard the strains of heavenly worship as a result
of having fasted for forty days and nights (De Somnis 1.35--37; De vita
Mosis 2.67--69).[^25] But it would be equally possible to take both
nouns as referring to the angels---"delighting in the humility and
worship of the angels"---as may be implicit in the very fact that such
glorious beings also fall down in worship before God.[^26] Despite
Lohse, Colossians and Philemon 119 n. 36; Martin, Colossians and
Philemon 94; Schweizer, Colossians 159; also "Christ" 452; W. Radl, EDNT
2.155; and DeMaris 77--79, reference to 2:23 by no means rules out this
line of interpretation (see on 2:23). And (pace Bockmuehl, Revelation
180) it is not so much interest in angelic worship which Paul finds
"reprehensible" as the attitude of dismissive superiority which it
evidently engendered among its practitioners (μηδεὶς ὑμᾶς καταβραβεύετω
...).

The further clause, "which things[^27] he had seen on entering (ἃ
ἑόρακεν ἐμβατεύων)," if anything strengthens the above hypothesis. To be
sure, the most influential interpretation for most of the present
century has been that put forward by Dibelius, most fully in 1917.[^28]
He argued on the basis of inscriptions found at the site of the
sanctuary of Apollo at Klaros (a few miles northwest of Ephesus) that
ἐμβατεύων here is drawn from the language of the mysteries, a technical
term for initiation into a mystery cult, so that the phrase refers to
visions seen preparatory to initiation; hence the translation of Lohse,
Colossians and Philemon 114: "visions of them \[angels\] during the
mystery rites"---a catchword quoted from the Colossian cult (Lohse 120).
But since neither "the elemental forces" (see on 2:8) nor the talk of
burial with Christ (see on 2:12) need evoke thought of mystery cults,
this single reference (ἐμβατεύων) is a very limited base on which to
build such a theory.[^29] Nor, once again, does it make sense of the
strong ethnic character of the Jewish features of the Colossian
"philosophy" so far analyzed (2:11, 16).

(Dunn, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: a
commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), pp. 179--183.)

Logos:

The two main interpretations of "worship of the angels" in Colossians
2:18 rest on fundamentally different understandings of what the false
teachers promoted.

**The Mystical/Participatory View** holds that believers were seeking to
join angels in their worship of God. Scholars like Bird argue that Paul
refers to joining angels in worship rather than making angels the object
of worship, claiming this practice drew from "the great tradition" of
Jewish visionary revelations.[^30] Davids similarly dismisses the idea
that people honored angels themselves, maintaining instead that
believers were "joining or trying to join" angels worshiping in
heaven.[^31] O'Brien objects to the objective interpretation due to
insufficient evidence for Jewish angel worship, instead citing Jewish
literature to argue that Colossians denotes worship performed by angels,
with believers "enraptured and participates in the heavenly worship of
God performed by the angels".[^32] The linguistic argument here centers
on the genitive case: Francis contends that thrēskeia refers to worship
belonging to angels---worship performed by them---rather than worship
directed toward them\[2\].

**The Syncretistic/Veneration View** interprets the phrase as actual
worship or invocation of angels as spiritual intermediaries. Dunn argues
that angels should be understood as the object of worship, picturing a
syncretistic religious mix where the purpose was to attain the blessing
of angels or possibly to appease them\[1\]. Hendriksen points to the
definite article in the phrase as indicating actual angel-worship, with
parallels in Revelation\[1\]. This interpretation aligns with evidence
that Phrygian belief held angels could assist in repelling evil\[3\].

Rather than viewing these as mutually exclusive, Stuckenbruck suggests
there's no need to choose absolutely between objective and subjective
interpretations, since insisting on benefits from angelic worship also
attributes something special to the angels themselves, and gaining
access through ascetic practices while appeasing angels through law
observance are not mutually exclusive\[2\]. The debate ultimately hinges
on whether the false teachers promoted mystical participation in
heavenly worship or practical veneration of angels as protective powers.

**1.3 Colossians 2:18, **"**taking his stand on visions he has seen**"

-   **Ask:** What does Dunn say about the meaning of ἃ ἑόρακεν ἐμβατεύων
    in Colossians 2:18 ("taking his stand on visions he has seen" /
    "going on in detail about visions"), and does he discuss the
    inscriptions from the oracle of Apollo at Claros?
-   **Why:** the dig found two senses in the LXX --- entering and
    occupying land (Josh 19:49, 51; 1 Maccabees) and entering into a
    subject in detail (2 Macc 2:30), which is the ESV's sense --- and a
    third, initiatory sense from Claros that is recalled, not checked
    (Open Question 3). BDAG is not in the library, so a commentator's
    report of the lexical evidence is the route.
-   **Watch for:** the date of the Claros inscriptions (second century
    AD), and whether the answer says so.
-   **Then ask the same question of:** Pao and Beale.

Dunn:

It is dubious whether ἐμβατεύω was, strictly speaking, a technical term,
at least for entry into a mystery cult. Its basic meaning is simply
"enter," and the word was regularly used in legal papyri of entering
into possession of an inheritance.[^33] So in the two Old Testament
(LXX) occurrences (Josh. 19:49, 51), where the thought is of entering
into possession of the promised land. So, too, in the Klaros
inscriptions the basic thought seems to be of entering into the
sanctuary to consult the oracle (Francis, "Background" 199--204). And
the Fathers seem unaware that the term could have such sensitive
overtones (PGL s.v.). Moreover, when we set the usage here against the
background already sketched out above, we cannot but be aware that the
revelations described usually involved a visionary or mystical ascent to
(entry into) or through the heavens (e.g., 1 Enoch 14:8--13; 2 Enoch 3;
3 Baruch 2:2; 3:1--2; Testament of Levi 2:5--7; Rev. 4:1--2). It was
when Elisha ben Abuyah "entered a garden" (= paradise = heaven) that he
committed the heresy of recognizing a second divine power in
heaven.[^34] Indeed, ἐμβατεύειν itself is attested in the sense of
"enter heaven."[^35] Most interesting of all are the clear indication in
the Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice that heaven was seen as a temple
where the angelic worship took place and the prominence given to
doorways of the temple (probably on the basis of Ezekiel 40--41; see
Newsom 39--59) and to the theme of entering in 4Q405 14--15i:3--4
("their wonderful praise is for the God of gods ... and they sing ...
the vestibules by which they enter, the spirits of the most holy inner
Temple") and 4Q405 23i:8--10 ("When the gods of knowledge enter by the
doors of glory, and when the holy angels depart towards their realm, the
entrance doors and the gates of exit proclaim the glory of the King,
blessing and praising all the spirits of God when they depart and enter
by the gates").

When we add the echo of Josh. 19:49, 51 in Col. 1:12, the inference
becomes strong that there was a prominent strand among the Colossian
Jews who thought of heaven either (or both) as a promised land into
which they should aspire even now to enter, or particularly as the
temple of God into which they could now enter by means of appropriate
spiritual disciplines in order to share the worship of the angels in
heaven. It was their delight thus to "enter" sabbath by sabbath (note
the present tense). This would also make the best sense of the other
part of the phrase (ἃ ἑόρακεν), which Dibelius treated too lightly. For
it is precisely the characteristic of the apocalyptic and mystical
Jewish traditions documented above to give prominence to "the things
seen" (e.g., 1 Enoch 14; Testament of Abraham 10; Revelation 4--5); we
should recall that Paul himself enjoyed such a visionary or mystical
journey (2 Cor. 12:2--4). This no doubt was why the Colossian Jews so
"delighted in" these practices, so rich were the audiovisual experiences
which they had enjoyed at least once in the past (the force of the
perfect tense is not wholly clear).[^36] And if they were persuasive
word-spinners on the subject (2:4, 8), it is no wonder that Paul and
Timothy were concerned that they might well captivate the Colossian
believers by the descriptions they gave and fearful lest Epaphras's
converts feel that they were indeed in danger of being disqualified and
deprived of the prize of their new faith.

(Dunn, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: a
commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), pp. 183184.)

Dunn does not discuss them in the search results provided. The
inscriptions from Claros are mentioned by Stuckenbruck as evidence used
to support a pagan mystery cult background, but Stuckenbruck himself
questions their viability since they date from the middle of the second
century2. Dunn's approach focuses instead on Jewish apocalyptic and
mystical traditions as the primary interpretive framework for
understanding the false teaching at Colossae, rather than invoking pagan
mystery religion parallels.

Pao:

Paul continues to criticize the false teachers for their promotion of
visionary experiences in the worship of angels. The difficulties
involved in making sense of v. 18d are reflected by the insertion of a
negative particle in some manuscripts[^37] by early scribes, and this
emendation is reflected in some modern versions: "intruding into those
things which he has *not* seen" (NKJV, italics added). The various
interpretations of this difficult clause depend on the way the
participle "entering" (ἐμβατεύων) is understood.

\(1\) Many versions translate this participle as "entering into
details": "such a person also goes into great detail about what they
have seen" (NIV; cf. NET, TNIV). Others have made it explicit that "what
he has seen" are the visions: "dwelling on visions" (NRSV; cf.
ESV).[^38] While this meaning of the participle is not without precedent
(cf. 2 Macc 2:30), such a usage is rare. Moreover, this reading "is very
tame, where the context seems to require something more assertive."[^39]

\(2\) Understanding "entering" as a technical term for part of the entry
rites into mystery cults, some see this Hellenistic cultic background as
explaining the meaning of this clause: "as he has had visions of them
during the mystery rites."[^40] While this view may explain the
significance of the act of "entering," it is difficult to explain
"self-humiliation" and "the worship of angels" as that which the
initiated see when they enter the cult. Moreover, "entering" acquires a
technical sense only when it is used with other related
vocabularies.[^41] Finally, the references to Jewish regulations and
customs elsewhere in this section also question this connection even
when a syncretistic Judaism is assumed.

\(3\) The best reading takes the participle ἐμβατεύων in the sense of
entry into an inheritance to take possession of it. Together with the
legal term "written record" (χειρόγραφον) in v. 14, this term points to
the acquisition of property.[^42] The LXX usage is particularly relevant
here, where "entering" and "inheritance" appear together in reference to
taking possession of the Promised Land (Josh 19:49), and both terms
appear in Colossians (cf. Col 1:12). This reading retains the usual
meaning of the verb while allowing the additional significance to be
carried by this verb as demanded by the context in which it appears.

If we read it this way, this section refers to visionary experiences
while worshiping the angels, and "entering into these things that he has
seen" is an attempt to enter into the heavenly realm through visionary
experiences to gain possession of that which escapes mere mortals.
Instead of gaining possession of the heavenly realm, however, they are
only able to encounter what they see in their futile minds.[^43] The
Colossian believers are called to resist such practices because all
promises to them have already been provided through Christ, in whom "all
the fullness of deity dwells" (v. 9). Again, while the specific problem
may remain unclear to modern readers, the solution is clear and
unmistakable.

(Pao, D.W. (2012) Colossians and Philemon. Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan
(Zondervan Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament:), pp. 190--191.)

-   **Ask:** Does Dunn (or Pao) take "self-abasement" (ταπεινοφροσύνη)
    in Colossians 2:18 and 2:23 as a technical term for fasting, and

-   **Why:** the ESV's "asceticism" has decided for a practice; the
    NASB95's "self-abasement" leaves an attitude (dig, Tool 7 and Tool

    the church's virtue. If it means "fasting" at 2:18, the 3:12

-   **Watch for:** the date of Hermas (second century) being used as if

Dunn:

Ταπεινοφροσύνη usually means "humility," but most follow the observation
that the LXX uses the repeated phrase "to humble (ταπεινόω) one's soul"
in the sense of "to mortify oneself" (Lev. 16:29, 31; 23:27, 29, 32) or
more specifically "to fast" (Ps. 35:13; Isa. 58:3, 5; Jdt. 4:9; see also
Ps. 69:10; *Psalms of Solomon* 3:8); ταπεινοφροσύνη is clearly used in
this latter sense in Hermas, *Visions* 3.10.6 and *Similitudes* 5.3.7.
This suggests a fair degree of ascetic practice as part of the Colossian
"philosophy" (see also 2:21, 23; cf. 1 Tim. 4:3).[^44]

(Dunn, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: a
commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), pp. 178--179.)

Pao:

The verb "let ... condemn" (καταβραβευέτω) has often been understood in
a narrower meaning of "robbing of a prize" (cf. ASV, NKJV; NJB) or
"disqualifying" (cf. NAB, NRSV, REB, TNIV, NIV), which has been assumed
to signify the presence of an athletic metaphor. This reading is often
built on an etymological analysis of the word: "βραβεύω has the primary
meaning of 'award a prize' ... hence καταβραβεύω ... means 'decided
against' as an umpire."[^45] Although this verb appears only here in the
NT and rarely in extracanonical material, the few contemporary parallels
in both literary and nonliterary sources point to the use of this term
in a general sense without any allusion to the athletic context.[^46] In
this context, therefore, it is best to take this in the general sense of
"condemn" (NLT) or "pass judgment" (NET). If so, "let no one condemn
you" builds on the similar call in v. 16 with a greater sense of
urgency.

(Pao, D.W. (2012) Colossians and Philemon. Grand Rapids, MI: Zondervan
(Zondervan Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament:), pp. 187--188.)

Logos:

The interpretation that ταπεινοφροσύνη functioned as a quasi-technical
term for fasting rests on its close association with worship practices
in Colossians 2:18 and 2:23, suggesting it denoted a cultic practice
rather than a mere disposition of lowliness.\[1\] This reading gains
support from the use of the term as a technical designation for fasting
in Tertullian and The Shepherd of Hermas.\[1\]

Cognate forms appear throughout the Septuagint in contexts where fasting
expresses abasement before God (Leviticus 16:29, 31; 23:27, 29, 32;
Isaiah 58:3, 5; Psalm 34:13--14).\[1\] Beyond linguistic precedent, the
contextual fit is compelling: fasting frequently served as preparation
for visionary experience and reception of divine revelations in Jewish
literature (Daniel 10:2--9; 4 Ezra 5:13, 20; 9:23--25; 2 Baruch 5:7--9;
12:5--7; 43:3).\[1\] This convergence is highly relevant to Colossians
2:18, where the two elements associated with fasting---worship of angels
and visionary experience---appear together.\[1\]

However, this interpretation faces scholarly resistance. Some scholars
argue for maintaining the general New Testament usage of ταπεινοφροσύνη
as humility, rejecting identification with fasting exercises or cultic
practice, and contending instead that it refers to the selfless love the
heretics lacked.\[2\] Others note the Jewish-Christian currency of the
term as evidence of Jewish moral emphasis, while some link it with
abstinence rules and self-mortification.\[3\]

The debate ultimately hinges on whether the false teachers'
"self-abasement" was a \*practice\* (fasting) or a \*disposition\*
(perverted humility). The evidence from Tertullian and Hermas
demonstrates the term's technical usage in early Christian contexts, yet
the broader New Testament pattern of ταπεινοφροσύνη as virtue
complicates straightforward identification with ascetical discipline
alone.

\[2\] Horst Robert Balz and Gerhard Schneider, in Exegetical Dictionary
of the New Testament (Grand Rapids, Mich.: Eerdmans, 1990--), 3:334.

\[3\] John M. G. Barclay, Colossians and Philemon (London; New York: T&T

**1.5 Colossians 2:8 and 2:20, **"**the elements of the world**"

-   **Ask:** What does Dunn say about "the elements of the world" (τὰ
    στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου) in Colossians 2:8 and 2:20, and which of the
    three senses --- physical elements, elementary principles, or
    elemental spirits --- does he favour, and why?
-   **Why:** carried over from rounds 1 and 2 (2:6--15 dig, Open
    Question 1): only Dunn's note on 2:15 came back. The ESV's
    "elemental spirits" decides the question for the congregation. The
    2:16--23 dig adds that "you died ... *from* the elements" (2:20) is
    a construction found nowhere else in the NT, which fits a power or
    regime better than a substance.
-   **Watch for:** evidence for "spirits" that is evidence for spirits
    *associated with* the elements (Jubilees; 1 Enoch), not for the word
    στοιχεῖα used *of* spirits before the second century.

Dunn:

κατὰ τὰ στοιχεῖα τοῦ κόσμου ("according to the elemental forces of the
cosmos"), is enigmatic and has spawned a huge debate (see bibliography
in G. Delling, *TDNT* 7.670; Bandstra, *Law* 5--30; Schweizer,
"Elemente" 147--48; O'Brien, *Colossians, Philemon* 129--32; Sappington
164--68). The basic meaning of στοιχεῖον is "element," and here, where
the immediate context is dominated by cosmic categories (2:9--10, 15),
the most obvious reference in the full phrase is to the elemental
substances of which the cosmos was thought to be composed (earth, water,
air, and fire---as the term is used in Wis. 7:17 and 2 Pet. 3:10, 12),
by far the most common usage in literature prior to Paul.[^47] However,
as Philo knew well, these substances could be understood (mythologized
or personified) as spirits or given the names of deities (*De vita
contemplativa* 3; *De decalogo* 53). "The divinization of the elements
was a commonplace in the whole Graeco-Roman period" (Wink 74).[^48]

Whether στοιχεῖα was at this time actually being used of heavenly
bodies, the stars understood as heavenly powers that influence or
determine human destiny, is still unclear. The usage is not attested
until after the time of the New Testament; the earliest explicit
references are usually taken to be those in *Testament of Solomon*
8:2--4, where seven demonic spirits identify themselves as στοιχεῖα
("rulers of this world of darkness.... Our stars in heaven look small,
but we are named like gods"), and Diogenes Laertius 6.102, who calls the
twelve signs of the zodiac "the twelve στοιχεῖα" (cf. *Corpus
Hermeticum* 13.12). But it is a natural extension of the more
established use, since stars were understood to be composed of one of
the elements, fire. Thus, in condemnatory tone, Wis. 13:2: "they
(Gentiles in general) supposed that either fire or wind or swift air, or
the circle of the stars, or turbulent water, or the luminaries of heaven
were the gods that rule the world." Philo describes the στοιχεῖα as
"powers" (*De aeternitate Mundi* 107--9), and Hermas (*Visions* 3.13.3)
speaks of the world as controlled (κρατεῖται) through the four στοιχεῖα
(cited also by DeMaris 53--55). And both here and in Galatians there is
a clear implication that the στοιχεῖα were closely associated with
heavenly beings (Gal. 4:8--9---gods as popularly understood; Col.
2:10---rulers and authorities).[^49]

It is important here to note that the issue does not turn on any
distinction of personal versus impersonal: it was commonplace, not least
within Judaism, to think of the stars as living beings (Judg. 5:20; Job
38:7; Dan. 8:10; *1 Enoch* 86; Philo, *De opificio mundi* 73; *De
plantatione* 12; Rev. 1:20; 9:1); likewise "fate" could be personified
("the Fates") or attributed to a personal δαίμων (*OCD* s.v. "Fate"; LSJ
s.v. δαίμων). The allusion, in other words, is to the belief that was no
doubt then common (as still among not a few today) that human beings had
to live their lives under the influence or sway of primal and cosmic
forces, however precisely conceptualized (for an effective summary of
the case see Martin, *Colossians and Philemon* 10--14). Hence the
translation "elemental forces" or "elemental spirits of the universe"
(RSV/NRSV, NEB/REB); but "principles of this world" (JB/NJB; cf. NIV) is
too vague.

It is particularly interesting that precisely the same phrase is used in
Gal. 4:3 and 9 (as again in Col. 2:20). There it is clearly linked into
the Jewish law, understood as itself a kind of power set in charge over
Israel like a slave-custodian or guardian (Gal. 3:23--25; 4:1--3, 9--10)
and given "through angels" (3:19). The close association of the thought
here with talk of Jewish festivals (Col. 2:16; cf. Gal. 4:10) and
"worship of angels" (Col. 2:18) strongly suggests that we are moving in
the same realm of thought and association as with the same phrase in
Galatians. Here it needs to be remembered that Jewish apocalyptic also
spoke of spirits controlling the elements: for example, *Jubilees* 2:2:
"the angels of the spirit of fire, the angels of the spirit of the winds
..."; *1 Enoch* 75:1: "the leaders of the chiefs of thousands who are in
charge of all the stars"; *2 Enoch* 4:1: "the rulers of the stellar
orders ..., the angels who govern the stars"; *Testament of Abraham*
13:11: "the archangel Purouel, who has authority over fire" (Lohse,
*Colossians and Philemon* 98, cites *1 Enoch* 43:1--2 and *2 Enoch*
19:1--4). *1 Enoch* 18:14--16 also speaks of stars bound and imprisoned
for their transgression, and the Greek fragment of *Jubilees* 2:8 links
"the placings of the stars" with τὰ στοιχεῖα (καὶ τὰς τῶν ἄστρων θέσεις
καὶ τὰ στοιχεῖα). We might also note that some fragmentary horoscopes
have been discovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls (4Q186 and 4QMessAr),
that one of the ways in which Hellenistic Jewish apologists sought to
commend Judaism was by presenting the Jewish patriarchs as the
discoverers of astrology (Artapanus and pseudo-Eupolemus in Eusebius,
*Praeparatio Evangelica* 9.18.1 and 9.17.8--9), and that Josephus could
describe the Pharisees and the Essenes in particular as believers in
Fate (*Antiquities* 13.172) and could even claim that the Essenes prayed
to the sun (*War* 2.128). It is quite possible, then, to conceive of an
essentially Jewish "philosophy" in Colossae that drew on such traditions
as a way of commending their religious practices to their fellow
citizens (see further on 2:18).

The key test of such systems for the letter writer(s), however, was
whether they accorded with Christ (κατὰ Χριστόν). Here the credit built
up in 1:15--20 and 26--27 is now drawn on. The Christ who embodies the
secret of both cosmos and history must obviously serve as the yardstick
by which all other claims to religious insight (not least claims
regarding human relationships with and within the cosmos) should be
measured. It was precisely the failure of such speculation to grasp the
significance of Christ and of his death that demonstrated their
emptiness and deceitfulness.

(Dunn, J.D.G. (1996) The Epistles to the Colossians and to Philemon: a
commentary on the Greek text. Grand Rapids, MI; Carlisle: William B.
Eerdmans Publishing; Paternoster Press (New International Greek
Testament Commentary), pp. 148--151.)

1.6 Colossians 2:10, ὅς or ὅ

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 2:10, some early manuscripts (𝔓⁴⁶ B D F G)

-   **Watch for:** an answer about 1:27 again. Ask Dunn first, then

Logos:

Philip Comfort discusses this variant, noting that early manuscripts 𝔓⁴⁶
B D F G read the neuter pronoun ὅ ("which") rather than the masculine ὅς
("who")\[1\]. Comfort is the primary commentator in the search results
who directly defends the neuter reading and explains its significance\
Comfort argues that the masculine form was likely a scribal alteration
motivated by reverence for Christ's person\[1\], making the neuter the
more difficult reading and therefore more likely original. He contends
that the neuter pronoun "anticipates the identification of Christ as
being 'head' of the body"\[1\]---suggesting that the neuter
grammatically aligns with the predicate noun κεφαλή (head), which is
feminine in form but conceptually neuter when referring to an abstract
office or function.

Comfort identifies an inconsistency in manuscript evaluation: the
Nestle-Aland editors accepted the neuter in Colossians 1:27, supported
by several of the same manuscripts (𝔓⁴⁶ B F G), but rejected it in
2:10\[1\]. He attributes this to "atomized eclecticism,"\[1\] implying
the editors applied inconsistent criteria across parallel passages.

The grammatical principle underlying this debate involves relative
pronoun agreement. A relative pronoun can assimilate to the gender of
its predicate substantive rather than its antecedent---a perfectly
natural agreement, particularly when the predicate presents the main
idea\[2\]. In Colossians 2:10, if "head" (the predicate) carries the
interpretive weight, the neuter ὅ becomes defensible. However, most
English translations follow the masculine reading, reflecting the
textual preference of the majority manuscript tradition.

References:\
\[1\] Philip W. Comfort, New Testament Text and Translation Commentary:
Commentary on the Variant Readings of the Ancient New Testament
Manuscripts and How They Relate to the Major English Translations (Carol
Stream, IL: Tyndale House Publishers, Inc., 2008), 627.

\[2\] A. T. Robertson, A Grammar of the Greek New Testament in the Light
of Historical Research (Logos Bible Software, 2006), 712.

The final ἐν αὐτῷ (the fifteenth "in him" in the letter and the fifth

Brown:

**in it**---namely, His cross, or crucifixion: so the Greek fathers
translate. Many of the Latins, "In *Himself*" or "in Him." Eph 2:16
favors *English Version,* "reconcile ... by *the cross,* having slain

ascension are a public solemn triumph over the principalities and powers
of death. It is striking that the heathen oracles were silenced soon

Logos:

The commentators divide into two main camps on whether ἐν αὐτῷ refers to
Christ or the cross.

The "In Christ" Reading

Constantine Campbell argues that the disarmament of the rulers and
authorities has been achieved in Christ, noting that while ἐν αὐτῷ could
refer to the cross, it more likely refers to Christ since this phrase
functions as a standard Pauline idiom for God's actions in
Christ[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PAULHOPEGLORY?pos=res%2FLLS%3APAULHOPEGLORY%2F2020-10-30T20%3A10%3A04Z%2F338017).
G. K. Beale similarly suggests that if ἐν αὐτῷ refers to Christ, the
passage focuses on God stripping the evil powers in public display and
winning victory through
Christ[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BECNT72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABECNT72COL%2F2023-01-31T15%3A02%3A49Z%2F971720).
James Dunn takes the position that the final ἐν αὐτῷ "probably is simply
Paul's attempt to retain the focus on what has been done 'in Christ' on
the
cross"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F585360).

The "In the Cross" Reading

Beale argues that ἐν αὐτῷ refers to the cross since the nearest
preceding nominal antecedent is "the cross" at the end of verse 14, and
a reference to the cross would express the irony of victory---God or
Christ overcoming the opponent through the death and weakness of the
cross[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BECNT72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABECNT72COL%2F2023-01-31T15%3A02%3A49Z%2F972235).
David Pao notes that "in him" points to Christ's critical role in divine
warfare, but acknowledges that some consider the public display of honor
and shame to occur at Jesus' death on the cross, though Pao himself
argues it seems probable that this public display takes place during
Jesus'
exaltation[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F702565).

Interpretive Nuance

Campbell provides a synthesis: though the phrase refers to Christ, God
triumphs specifically by the cross of Christ, as the certificate of debt
nailed to the cross is the means by which God has disarmed the rulers
and
authorities[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PAULHOPEGLORY?pos=res%2FLLS%3APAULHOPEGLORY%2F2020-10-30T20%3A10%3A04Z%2F338375).
Beale concludes that "it is difficult to determine which is
best"[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BECNT72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABECNT72COL%2F2023-01-31T15%3A02%3A49Z%2F971676),
reflecting genuine scholarly uncertainty on this point.

**1.8 Colossians 1:19, the subject of **"**was pleased**"

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 1:19 (ἐν αὐτῷ εὐδόκησεν πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα
    κατοικῆσαι), what do Dunn and Pao take as the subject of εὐδόκησεν
    ("was pleased") --- "all the fullness", or God understood --- and on
    what grounds?
-   **Why:** still open (1:15--20 dig, Open Question 1): slight lean to
    "the fullness" (ESV), moderate. The corpus has answered Vincent's
    argument; BDAG is not in the library.
-   **Watch for:** an appeal to 2:9, which the dig found does not
    discriminate (the fullness "dwells" on either construction).

Logos:

Dunn argues that "the fullness" (πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα) serves as the subject
of εὐδόκησεν, maintaining this grammatical reading through verse
1:20[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F419741).
His reasoning rests on treating the neuter phrase as a personified
entity capable of performing the action of being pleased. For Dunn, this
reading emphasizes that divine completeness---God's comprehensive
self-revelation and interaction with creation---finds its focus and
expression through Christ's ministry, particularly his death and
resurrection[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F417542).

Pao presents a more nuanced analysis, laying out three competing
interpretations. He identifies God as the implied subject of εὐδόκησεν
as the most defensible reading, arguing that "was pleased" typically
requires a personal subject, that LXX usage frequently presents God as
the one pleased, that the masculine participle "by making peace" in
verse 1:20 suggests a masculine personal agent, and that God likely
functions as the implied subject of reconciliation in verse
1:20[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F499903).
Pao also acknowledges a third option---taking "all the fullness" as the
subject itself---which some translations adopt, though this requires
explaining the masculine participle in verse 1:20 as a "construction
according to
sense"[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F500574).

The fundamental disagreement centers on whether the neuter noun phrase
can function as a grammatical subject performing the action, or whether
a personal agent (God) must be supplied. Both commentators recognize the
ambiguity of the subject as a genuine interpretive
difficulty[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F499230).
Dunn's approach treats the fullness as capable of personified agency,
while Pao's preferred reading restores an explicit divine agent,
aligning the passage more closely with LXX patterns where God
consistently acts as the one pleased.

The commentators divide into three competing interpretations, with

"**All the Fullness" as Subject**

Jipp argues that "all the fullness" is likely the subject of "was
pleased to dwell," explaining both Christ's preeminence and providing
the ground for his reconciling work---Christ can achieve cosmic
reconciliation because God invested him with "all the
fullness."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:MSSNCTHNWTSTMNT?pos=res%2FLLS%3AMSSNCTHNWTSTMNT%2F2020-09-29T00%3A55%3A39Z%2F752704)
Moo similarly prefers this reading since it requires no supplied subject
and because "fullness" clearly functions as the subject of the same verb
in [Colossians
2:9](https://ref.ly/Col2.9).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBG72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBG72COL%2F2019-09-04T23%3A00%3A29Z%2F68547)
Harris notes this reading can be understood either impersonally as "all
the fullness" or personally as "God in all his
fullness."[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PREPTHEOGRKNT?pos=res%2FLLS%3APREPTHEOGRKNT%2F2013-08-15T17%3A14%3A38Z%2F279230)

God as Implied Subject

Pao identifies God as the implied subject, arguing that "was pleased"
typically requires a personal subject, and in the LXX the subject is
often God himself (citing [Psalms 40:13](https://ref.ly/Ps40.13),
[51:16](https://ref.ly/Ps51.16), [85:1](https://ref.ly/Ps85.1);
[Jeremiah 2:19](https://ref.ly/Je2.19), [14:10](https://ref.ly/Je14.10),
[14:12](https://ref.ly/Je14.12); and other
passages).[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F499903)
The masculine participle "by making peace" in verse 20 also suggests a
masculine personal subject, and God is likely the implied subject of
reconciliation in verse
20.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EXEGCOMM72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEXEGCOMM72COL%2F2024-10-16T21%3A39%3A33Z%2F500379)
Robertson notes that while no word for "the Father" appears in the
Greek, the verb demands either God or the Father as
subject.[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:46.50.2?pos=res%2FLLS%3A46.50.2%2F2019-09-25T15%3A44%3A04Z%2F3723497)

Hybrid Approaches

Moo acknowledges the tension: the masculine participle in verse 20 would
agree with "God," while "all the fullness" is neuter, creating a certain
"awkwardness." He proposes a hybrid translation: "God in all his
fullness was pleased," rooting the concept in temple imagery rather than
OT wisdom
traditions.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBG72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBG72COL%2F2019-09-04T23%3A00%3A29Z%2F68547)
Harris similarly supports construing "all the fullness" personally,
noting that both εὐδόκησεν and the reconciliation language suggest a
personal subject, and the masculine participle in verse 20 shows "all
the fullness" may be understood
personally.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PREPTHEOGRKNT?pos=res%2FLLS%3APREPTHEOGRKNT%2F2013-08-15T17%3A14%3A38Z%2F279419)

The fundamental tension remains unresolved: grammatically, "fullness" is
neuter, yet the context and parallel structures suggest a personal
divine agent.

**1.9 Colossians 1:20, **"**to Himself**"

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 1:20, "to reconcile all things to Himself"
    (εἰς αὐτόν), do Dunn, Pao or Beale discuss whether "Himself" is God
    or the Son, and do any compare the εἰς αὐτόν of 1:16?
-   **Why:** evenly balanced in the 1:15--20 dig (Open Question 2). Most
    commentators read God, but only Col 1:20 has εἰς + accusative with a
    reconciliation verb; every other NT instance has a dative of the
    party.
-   **Watch for:** an answer that assumes the accent (αὑτόν, reflexive)
    settles it; the early manuscripts are unaccented.

Logos:

Dunn explicitly addresses this question, noting that in 1:20 the pattern
of "in, through, to" results in reconciliation "to him" (that is, to
Christ), whereas the more typical Pauline thought elsewhere presents
reconciliation through Christ to
God[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F430846).
He observes that in [Romans 5:10](https://ref.ly/Ro5.10) and [2
Corinthians 5:18--20](https://ref.ly/2Co5.18-20), Paul presents
reconciliation through Christ to God, but here Christ serves as the
subject[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F430846).

Pao takes a different approach. He argues that given the consistent use
of the pronoun "he" (αὐτός) referring to Christ throughout the hymn, εἰς
αὐτόν should be understood as referring to Christ, though most
translations prefer rendering the pronoun reflexively ("to himself")
because elsewhere in Paul the one to whom all creation is reconciled is
always
God[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CLSNSSXGTCPRCTC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACLSNSSXGTCPRCTC%2F2024-12-05T14%3A46%3A36Z%2F525039).
However, Pao contends that in context, the force of other Pauline uses
cannot replace the clear context where Christ is both the agent and
objective of both original creation and new creation, and this striking
departure from other Pauline usage achieves the intended purpose of
highlighting the close identification between God and
Christ[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CLSNSSXGTCPRCTC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACLSNSSXGTCPRCTC%2F2024-12-05T14%3A46%3A36Z%2F525442).

Regarding comparison with 1:16, Dunn observes that verse 1:20 obviously
echoes the "all things through him and to him" language of 1:16,
establishing a deliberate parallel between creation and
reconciliation[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F423673).
Pao similarly notes that both sections of the hymn contain the three
prepositional phrases "in him," "through him," and "to him," and argues
that the consistent reference to Christ throughout suggests εἰς αὐτόν in
1:20 also refers to
Christ[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CLSNSSXGTCPRCTC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACLSNSSXGTCPRCTC%2F2024-12-05T14%3A46%3A36Z%2F524835).

Information as to whether or not Beale does directly address whether
"himself" refers to God or the Son in his discussion of 1:20 was not
available.

1.10 Colossians 1:24, Brown's survey

-   **Ask:** What does Brown (*Logos Research Commentaries*) say about
    "filling up what is lacking in Christ's afflictions" in Colossians
    1:24? Which readings does he list, and who holds each?
-   **Why:** carried over from round 2's "still open" list. The dig
    leans to messianic woes with Christ suffering in his body (options
    1 + 2), and the absence reading (option 4, Garland) is still live on
    the idiom (1:24--2:5 dig, Open Question 1). Brown surveys the field.
-   **Watch for:** Wright filed as a "cross" reading, which round 2
    corrected.

Logos:

Brown identifies three primary interpretations of "Christ's
afflictions." Hendriksen and Wright understand them as referring to
Christ's sufferings on the cross. Bruce interprets them as the
afflictions believers endure for Christ's sake and for one another.
O'Brien takes an eschatological approach, viewing them as suffering by
believers prior to Christ's return.

O'Brien delineates five explanations for the meaning of "Christ's
afflictions." First, some argue Christ's vicarious sufferings were
deficient and Paul's sufferings complete Christ's work. Second,
interpreters take the genitive as objective, meaning "for the sake of
Christ." Third, the genitive indicates quality---Paul's sufferings
resemble Christ's. Fourth, some scholars employ a mystical framework,
arguing Paul shares Christ's sufferings through mystical union. Fifth,
many now interpret "Christ's afflictions" as "messianic
woes"---suffering that must occur before the messianic kingdom's
inauguration.

Brown also presents individual commentators' positions: Abbott argues
the verse refers to Paul's own afflictions, noting that *thlipsis*
("affliction") never denotes Christ's sufferings in the New Testament.
He connects this to passages like [2 Corinthians
1:5](https://ref.ly/2Co1.5), [4:10](https://ref.ly/2Co4.10), and
[Philippians 3:10](https://ref.ly/Php3.10), viewing Christ's sufferings
as a "type" for Christians to
endure.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBG72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBG72COL%2F2019-09-04T23%3A00%3A29Z%2F82269)
Arnold interprets the verse through Jewish apocalyptic belief that "a
set amount of suffering" must be endured before eschatological events
unfold. He rejects any implication that Christ's sufferings were
deficient, arguing instead that they are only "lacking" because they "do
not fill the appointed measure to usher in the end of the age."

Regarding the verb *antanaplēroō* itself, Moo identifies five possible
meanings: suffering "in place of" the church; acting "on behalf of";
responding to what is lacking; reciprocal force (Paul suffers as Christ
suffered); or simple synonymy with the common verb meaning "fill up."

## 2. The State of a Debate

2.1 Colossians 2:16--17 and the weekly Sabbath

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 2:16, does "a Sabbath day" (σαββάτων, plural)
    include the weekly Sabbath, or only special sabbaths? What are the
    main views, who holds each, and what evidence do they give?
-   **Why:** the dig reads the triad as including the weekly Sabbath,
    since the same triad does in Hos 2:11 and Ezek 45:17
    (moderate--high), but advises against making the sermon a Sabbath
    debate (overview, Trap 3; dig, Preaching Pitfalls). The preacher
    should know the state of the question before the pulpit.
-   **Watch for:** confessional conclusions about the Lord's Day
    presented as exegesis of the verse.

Logos:

The scholarly debate centers on whether σαββάτων includes the weekly
Sabbath or refers only to ceremonial sabbaths, with the evidence
supporting the weekly Sabbath interpretation now commanding broader
consensus.

The Weekly Sabbath View (Mainstream)

The consensus of commentators holds that the three terms---festival, new
moon, and sabbath---represent a logical and progressive sequence
(annual, monthly, and weekly) as well as an exhaustive enumeration of
sacred
times.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SBBTHSNDNVSTGTN?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASBBTHSNDNVSTGTN%2F2014-10-04T08%3A33%3A22Z%2F881184)
This interpretation gains support from the intertextual connection to
[Numbers 28--29](https://ref.ly/Nu28-29), where the calendar of ritual
offerings includes special sacrifices on weekly seventh-day Sabbaths,
monthly new moons, and yearly
festivals.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PERPLEXDOCTQA?pos=res%2FLLS%3APERPLEXDOCTQA%2F2020-08-07T23%3A39%3A50Z%2F4803217)
Historically, ancient and modern expositors have understood the text as
referring to "weekly Sabbaths," with John Meredith (1729) noting that
"Sabbaths or Sabbath-Days" in this context "chiefly, if not always and
only signify the Seventh-Day
Sabbath."[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:JDGNGSBBTHDSCVR?pos=res%2FLLS%3AJDGNGSBBTHDSCVR%2F2014-10-07T21%3A04%3A28Z%2F114848)

The Ceremonial Sabbaths View (Seventh-day Adventist)

The Seventh-day Adventist Bible Commentary interprets "sabbath days" as
referring to annual ceremonial sabbaths rather than the weekly
Sabbath.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SBBTHSNDNVSTGTN?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASBBTHSNDNVSTGTN%2F2014-10-04T08%3A33%3A22Z%2F881750)
However, this interpretation faces linguistic difficulty: ceremonial
sabbaths in the Septuagint are designated by the compound expression
"sabbata sabbaton," which differs from the simple "sabbaton" found in
[Colossians 2:16](https://ref.ly/Col2.16), making it linguistically
impossible to interpret the latter as ceremonial
sabbaths.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SBBTHSNDNVSTGTN?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASBBTHSNDNVSTGTN%2F2014-10-04T08%3A33%3A22Z%2F882171)

A Mediating Position

A nuanced approach distinguishes between Sabbath rest and rituals
performed on the Sabbath, arguing that ceremonies carried out on Sabbath
days---not the days themselves---functioned as typological
"shadows."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PERPLEXDOCTQA?pos=res%2FLLS%3APERPLEXDOCTQA%2F2020-08-07T23%3A39%3A50Z%2F4803690)
This interpretation agrees that the passage refers to the obsolescence
of ceremonial practices rather than Sabbath rest itself, but includes
the seventh-day Sabbath (referring to rituals performed on it) rather
than restricting "sabbaths" exclusively to yearly ceremonial
sabbaths.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:PERPLEXDOCTQA?pos=res%2FLLS%3APERPLEXDOCTQA%2F2020-08-07T23%3A39%3A50Z%2F4805978)

The evidence overwhelmingly favors the weekly Sabbath interpretation,
though scholars increasingly distinguish between the day itself and the
ceremonial practices associated with it.

2.2 The rival teaching at Colossae

-   **Ask:** What are the main reconstructions of the teaching opposed
    in Colossians 2:8--23 (for example, Jewish mysticism, folk religion,
    a syncretistic mix, Hellenistic philosophy), who holds each, and
    what is the main evidence for each?
-   **Why:** the overview and every dig work from the features, not a
    label (overview, Presenting Situation and Trap 2). A short, sourced
    map of the reconstructions lets the Finalise pass tag them properly
    rather than from recall.
-   **Watch for:** later evidence (the Council of Laodicea; Theodoret;
    the Michael shrine at Chonae) used as if it were first-century.

Logos:

Scholarly attempts to reconstruct the teaching opposed in Colossians
have produced no consensus, with an ever-increasing diversity of
proposals emerging over the past 170
years.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHEFFCL72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHEFFCL72COL%2F2014-10-04T08%3A51%3A01Z%2F71273)
In 1973, J. J. Gunther catalogued forty-four different reconstructions,
and dozens more have been proposed
since.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2489935)
However, several major schools of interpretation have emerged.

Jewish Mysticism and Essenism

J. B. Lightfoot argued for a line of development from Judaism to
second-century Gnosticism, locating the Colossian philosophy within that
stream. Lightfoot saw the Colossian emphasis on mysticism and rigid
asceticism as stemming from Essene Judaism, while Gnosticism contributed
the focus on wisdom, intermediate beings, and cosmological
speculation.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2490973)
The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls led more scholars to conclude that
the Colossian philosophy shared links with Essene
Judaism.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2491379)

Hellenistic Philosophy and Mystery Cults

Hellenistic reconstructions are variegated and include Hellenistic
mystery cults (M. Dibelius), Jewish Gnosticism and paganism (G.
Bornkamm), Middle Platonism (R. E. DeMaris), and Cynic philosophy (T.
Martin).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2491511)
Dibelius saw syncretism between Christianity and the Isis Mystery cult
with initiation into a cosmic mystery associated with "the
elements."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2491788)
Bornkamm theorized syncretism of Jewish Gnosticism and paganism, with
the Colossian philosophy promising redemption and deification along
gnostic lines found in the Mithras Liturgy and Isis
Mystery.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2491948)

Internal Temptation Rather Than External Heresy

M. D. Hooker argued that the situation in Colossae involved temptation
to conform to traditional ideas and practices or the attractiveness of
teachings from other groups in Colossae, rather than a "false teaching"
already winning support among church
members.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F234841)
However, this proposal has been criticized for ignoring key pronouns and
Paul's use of the term "philosophy," and for minimizing clear catchwords
of the
philosophy.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2490411)

The Fundamental Problem

Although elements of the practices warned against are common to
reconstructions of Jewish practice, Stoicism, mystery religions, and
Gnosticism, no known position in the first century lines up with all the
elements mentioned in the
polemic.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WISDOMCOMM71PHP?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWISDOMCOMM71PHP%2F2019-06-05T22%3A50%3A53Z%2F424627)
Many reconstructions blend themes from two or more categories, making
the categories not
watertight.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:9781433684012?pos=res%2FLLS%3A9781433684012%2F2024-11-05T21%3A41%3A05Z%2F2490819)

2.3 Authorship, place and date

-   **Ask:** What are the main positions on the authorship of
    Colossians, who holds each in the commentaries in my library, and
    where does each place Paul's imprisonment (Rome, Ephesus or
    Caesarea)?
-   **Why:** the overview tags these *\[S\]* and lists them as "the
    first things for a Logos round to test" (Colophon). The Finalise
    pass needs them sourced, not recalled. No exegetical finding depends
    on them.
-   **Watch for:** stylistic arguments presented as settled.

Logos:

The commentaries in the library present three major positions on
Colossian authorship, each with distinct implications for the
imprisonment location.

Pauline Authorship with Roman Imprisonment

Bartel argues that evidence within the letters credits Paul with
authorship, and absent convincing contrary evidence, the clear
statements regarding authorship should
stand.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BSBPRSNEPST?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABSBPRSNEPST%2F2016-07-07T17%3A32%3A43Z%2F71314)
The traditional view places these letters in Rome, supported by
allusions to the praetorian guard and Caesar's household, making Roman
imprisonment the preferred
location.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BSBPRSNEPST?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABSBPRSNEPST%2F2016-07-07T17%3A32%3A43Z%2F71727)
A slight majority of scholars favoring Pauline authorship also favor
Roman imprisonment, dating Colossians between 60--62
C.E.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTRONTDESILVA?pos=res%2FLLS%3AINTRONTDESILVA%2F2018-02-23T00%3A50%3A52Z%2F2348676)
Gurtner concludes the letter was written by the Apostle
Paul.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BKBC65AC?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABKBC65AC%2F2016-02-11T02%3A21%3A14Z%2F2118491)

Pauline Authorship with Ephesian or Caesarean Imprisonment

Some defenders of Pauline authorship locate the imprisonment elsewhere.
The Marcionite prologue claims Paul wrote from Ephesus, and this
location would facilitate the movement of persons described in the
letter.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTRONTDESILVA?pos=res%2FLLS%3AINTRONTDESILVA%2F2018-02-23T00%3A50%3A52Z%2F2346283)
Comfort notes that considering the journeys and cast of characters
(particularly Aristarchus, arrested with Paul in Ephesus), Ephesus makes
the most
sense.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM70EPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM70EPH%2F2009-06-10T20%3A51%3A53Z%2F743940)
Others favor Caesarea, which is only 350 miles from Colossae compared to
Rome's 900 miles, allowing faster travel for Epaphras and
Onesimus.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:INTRONTDESILVA?pos=res%2FLLS%3AINTRONTDESILVA%2F2018-02-23T00%3A50%3A52Z%2F2347919)

Deutero-Pauline Authorship

MacDonald argues Colossians is deutero-Pauline, composed either at the
very end of Paul's career when imprisonment prevented writing, or
shortly after his death, possibly with Timothy as scribe in Rome, though
the location cannot be determined with
certainty.[\[5\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SP17COLEPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASP17COLEPH%2F2022-03-30T17%3A07%3A53Z%2F49052)
Some scholars placing Colossians after Paul's death date it to the
70s.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:CSTONECM70EPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACSTONECM70EPH%2F2009-06-10T20%3A51%3A53Z%2F740813)

The debate reflects genuine tension between theological development
(favoring later Roman composition) and logistical proximity (favoring
Ephesus or Caesarea).

**2.4 Colossians 1:15--20 as a **"**hymn**"

-   **Ask:** Which commentators in my library treat Colossians 1:15--20
    as a pre-Pauline hymn that Paul quotes and edits, which treat it as
    composed for the letter, and what evidence does each side give?
-   **Why:** the overview tags the "hymn" as a form-critical
    reconstruction *\[S\]* (Structural Arc Map). The Finalise pass
    should name the holders.
-   **Watch for:** the claim that Paul "added" words (for example "the
    church", 1:18; "through the blood of His cross", 1:20) resting only
    on the reconstruction.

Logos:

The commentaries in your library present two competing positions on
whether [Colossians 1:15--20](https://ref.ly/Col1.15-20) represents a
pre-Pauline hymn that Paul incorporated and edited, or a composition
Paul created for the letter.

Pre-Pauline Hymn with Pauline Editing

MacDonald notes that scholars identify the passage as a hymn based on
repeated terms, non-Pauline expressions, hapax legomena, concentrated
relative constructions, shifts in pronouns, and rhetorical devices like
chiasmus, with consensus treating it as an independent unit inserted
into the
letter.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SP17COLEPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASP17COLEPH%2F2022-03-30T17%3A07%3A53Z%2F211294)
Most scholars believe it represents traditional liturgical material
edited by Paul or a deutero-Pauline
author.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SP17COLEPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASP17COLEPH%2F2022-03-30T17%3A07%3A53Z%2F213229)
The author of Colossians made two interpretative additions: the phrase
"of the church" identifying Christ as head of the body, and the
expression "through the blood of his cross," both apparently added to
give historical reference to a hymn with cosmological
emphasis.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SP17COLEPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASP17COLEPH%2F2022-03-30T17%3A07%3A53Z%2F213357)

Pauline Composition

McKnight notes that recent scholarship leans toward interpreting the
section as "an on-the-spot poetic creation by the apostle Paul" rather
than an inherited hymn, arguing against speculation about pre-Pauline
provenance while maintaining that Paul may have composed it "perhaps on
the basis of preexisting confessional lines that he perhaps also had a
hand in
himself."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:DICTOPAULLET2ND?pos=res%2FLLS%3ADICTOPAULLET2ND%2F2023-04-11T20%3A34%3A27Z%2F807834)
Leschert observes that while many scholars speculate Paul adapted the
passage from an earlier hymn, it is equally possible Paul composed it
himself, noting that the subject matter elevates the language above
ordinary prose but the passage still lacks strict poetic
structure.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:FLOWNT?pos=res%2FLLS%3AFLOWNT%2F2014-10-04T00%3A11%3A21Z%2F559835)

The debate reflects genuine tension between linguistic and structural
features suggesting liturgical provenance versus the possibility of
Pauline composition shaped by elevated theological content.

Logos:

Scholars have proposed four main identifications for the letter from
Laodicea, each with different supporting arguments.

A Lost Letter

The oldest proposal holds that Paul wrote a separate letter to the
Laodiceans that has since been lost, a view that is not inherently
implausible given that Paul wrote other letters that were not
preserved.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FBBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_BBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1%2F2021-06-10T20%3A19%3A29Z%2F486410)

before Ephesians, which would make the Laodicean letter a distinct
composition, since [Colossians 4:16](https://ref.ly/Col4.16) references
a letter "supposed to already
exist."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:DRYNTTBRMWTHBBL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ADRYNTTBRMWTHBBL%2F2014-04-24T19%3A56%3A29Z%2F162222)

Ephesians

An imposing array of scholars identify the letter as Ephesians,
proceeding from the view that Ephesians is a circular letter intended
for various churches in Asia, making it the logical complement to
Colossians without requiring postulation of a fourth letter, provided
the encyclical theory is
accepted.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FBBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_BBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1%2F2021-06-10T20%3A19%3A29Z%2F486702)

One proposal rejects the standard identifications and proposes Philemon,
arguing that since Archippus (mentioned in [Colossians
4:17](https://ref.ly/Col4.17)) would not have needed direct address if
he worked in the Colossian church, he must have ministered in another
community---most likely the Laodicean church, which may have gathered in
Philemon's
house.[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:RELTHEOABSTRACTS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ARELTHEOABSTRACTS%2F2014-10-04T10%3A58%3A02Z%2F106818070)

letter obviously composed to supply the missing epistle mentioned in
[Colossians
4:16](https://ref.ly/Col4.16).[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FBBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_BBE6EC35642E4D76A5947F5B508A11D1%2F2021-06-10T20%3A19%3A29Z%2F487264)

3.2 The umpire pair, 2:18 and 3:15 (C4, C19)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators connect "let no one disqualify you"
    (καταβραβευέτω) in Colossians 2:18 with "let the peace of Christ
    rule" (βραβευέτω) in 3:15, and do any reject the connection as
    unintended?
-   **Why:** audited at moderate--high as intended wordplay; history of
    interpretation was scored "often noted, seldom pressed" from recall
    *\[S\]*. This would confirm or correct that line.
-   **Watch for:** "rule" glossed from the English without the umpire
    sense.

Logos:

Multiple commentators recognize the connection between καταβραβευέτω in
[Colossians 2:18](https://ref.ly/Col2.18) and βραβευέτω in 3:15, noting
that the latter verb appears only in 3:15 while the compound form occurs
uniquely in
2:18[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BLAB01?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABLAB01%2F2006-09-14T16%3A12%3A58Z%2F102077).
Arnold draws attention to the athletic background of both verbs,
comparing Paul's use of the compound in 2:18 and calling the Colossians
to allow "the wonderful gift of peace from the Messiah" to "adjudicate"
their fears, much as umpires function in athletic
competitions[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBG72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBG72COL%2F2019-09-04T23%3A00%3A29Z%2F149686).
Still similarly explains the meaning of brabeuō in 3:15 in relation to
the related verb katabrabeuō in 2:18, noting that whereas the
philosophers wanted to "disqualify" the Colossians, Paul urges them to
allow "the peace of Christ"---not the philosophers---to judge their
lives[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:LBG72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ALBG72COL%2F2019-09-04T23%3A00%3A29Z%2F152442).

Sumney identifies both terms as New Testament hapax legomena and argues
that the use of brabeuō in 3:15 deliberately echoes the earlier compound
term, containing an implicit rejection of the posture of condemnation it
pictures while inviting readers to embody a dramatically different way
of
being[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NTL72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANTL72COL%2F2015-03-03T22%3A44%3A57Z%2F704839).
Geisler also connects the terms, explaining that katabrabeuō means "to
decide against" and cross-references brabeuō in 3:15, comparing the
disqualification to a judge who disqualifies those running the wrong
direction in a
race[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.1.1?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.1.1%2F2024-11-06T22%3A12%3A00Z%2F9002775).

None of the commentaries in the search results reject this connection as
unintended. Rather, the consensus treats the verbal link as deliberate
wordplay that creates thematic continuity between Paul's warning against
false teachers and his exhortation toward Christ's peace as the proper
arbiter of Christian life.

**3.4 **"**The body is Christ's**"** (2:17) and **"**the body of
Christ**"** (C26)**

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 2:17, "the substance (τὸ σῶμα) belongs to
    Christ", do any commentators see a reference to the church as the
    body of Christ (as in 1:18, 24 and 2:19), or a deliberate play on
    σῶμα across 2:17, 2:19 and 2:23?
-   **Why:** the dig's Headline 2 rests on the three occurrences and the
    body/flesh contrast of 2:23, which are textual; the deliberate play
    is moderate--high and queued (C26). Elsewhere in Paul "the body of
    Christ" always means Christ's own body or the church (Rom 7:4; 1 Cor
    10:16; 12:27; Eph 4:12).
-   **Watch for:** the reading "let no one judge you ... but let the
    body of Christ (judge)", which some have proposed; note who holds
    it.

Logos:

Multiple commentators explicitly recognize that σῶμα in [Colossians
2:17](https://ref.ly/Col2.17) may reference the church as the body of
Christ, and at least one traces a deliberate pattern of wordplay across
the passage.

Gupta notes that given the repeated use of σῶμα in Colossians in
reference to the social "body of Christ" (1:18, 24; 2:19; 3:15) as well
as the personal, physical "body of Christ" (1:22), there may be multiple
layers of meaning
embedded[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHC72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHC72COL%2F2018-08-16T23%3A15%3A19Z%2F321524).
He suggests one might read 2:17 as Paul's way of saying, "these things
are a shadow of what is to come, but---the body of Christ!" as a
syntactically awkward
interjection[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHC72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHC72COL%2F2018-08-16T23%3A15%3A19Z%2F321782).
Gupta identifies three possible meanings of σῶμα in 2:17: substance
(contrasted with shadow), the personal human body of Christ, and the
communal body of Christ as the
church[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SHC72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASHC72COL%2F2018-08-16T23%3A15%3A19Z%2F322078).

Deenick develops this observation more systematically, tracing σῶμα
throughout the Colossian argument. He notes that Paul's comments are
part of a larger discussion about the "body" that has begun in chapter
1---Christ is the head of the "body," he has reconciled us in the "body
of his flesh," and Paul suffers for the sake of Christ's
"body"[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NSBT45?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANSBT45%2F2018-07-26T21%3A14%3A48Z%2F351578).
In 2:17, Paul makes the enigmatic statement that Old Testament practices
are "a shadow of the things that are coming, but the body is Christ,"
and given Paul has been consistently emphasizing the "body," it seems
more likely that he means to do the same here
too[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NSBT45?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANSBT45%2F2018-07-26T21%3A14%3A48Z%2F352034).
Paul is saying that the Old Testament regulations were merely the
shadows created by the body of Christ, and the Old Testament practices
are only shadows; it is in Christ that the body
grows[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:NSBT45?pos=res%2FLLS%3ANSBT45%2F2018-07-26T21%3A14%3A48Z%2F352916).

This interpretation suggests Paul deliberately recycles σῶμα to
establish continuity between Christ's physical body, his ecclesial body,
and the substance that fulfills Old Testament shadows.

3.5 Psalm 68:16 behind Colossians 1:19 (C1)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators connect "in Him all the fullness was
    pleased to dwell" (Colossians 1:19) with Psalm 68:16 (LXX 67:17),
    "the mountain which God has desired for His abode" (NASB95), and do

Logos:

Multiple commentators connect [Colossians 1:19](https://ref.ly/Col1.19)

Regarding opposition to the link, Wall sees a reference to the OT temple
but does not adduce convincing
parallels.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BECNT72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABECNT72COL%2F2023-01-31T15%3A02%3A49Z%2F761296)
Additionally, O'Brien says only that the combination of the two verbs in
both [Psalm 67:17](https://ref.ly/Ps68.16) LXX and [Colossians
1:19](https://ref.ly/Col1.19) "is of special significance," while Hay is
more
tentative.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:COMNTUSEOT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACOMNTUSEOT%2F2016-09-19T17%3A03%3A27Z%2F4031926)
However, these scholars do not explicitly reject the connection; rather,
they express greater caution about its certainty. The consensus among
commentators who engage the question favors recognizing the allusion,
though with varying degrees of confidence about its intentionality.

3.7 The Jesus tradition behind 2:8 and 2:16--22 (C15)

-   **Ask:** Which commentators connect Colossians 2:8, 2:16 and
    2:20--22 with Jesus' teaching in Mark 7:1--23 and Matthew 15:1--20
    ("the tradition of men"; Isaiah 29:13; food entering the stomach),
    and do they argue for dependence on the Gospel tradition or only a
    shared theme?
-   **Why:** reframed by the audit to "shared dominical tradition,
    moderate; Mark 7 specifically, low--moderate"; history of
    interpretation not checked.
-   **Watch for:** the observation that Colossians keeps the LXX order
    of Isa 29:13 while the Gospels do not --- if a commentator notices
    it, record it.

Logos:

Multiple commentators recognize parallels between [Colossians
2:8](https://ref.ly/Col2.8), [2:16](https://ref.ly/Col2.16), and
2:20--22 with Jesus' teaching in [Mark 7:1--23](https://ref.ly/Mk7.1-23)
and [Matthew 15:1--20](https://ref.ly/Mt15.1-20), though they differ on
whether this represents direct dependence on Gospel tradition or merely
a shared theological theme.

Hooker observes that "the argument in 2:22 is reminiscent of Jesus'
argument with the Pharisees on this very subject ([Mark
7:1--23](https://ref.ly/Mk7.1-23) par. [Matt
15:1--20](https://ref.ly/Mt15.1-20)), where [Isa
29:13](https://ref.ly/Is29.13), echoed in Colossians, is quoted in
full."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:EERDMANSCOMMBBL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AEERDMANSCOMMBBL%2F2014-10-03T22%3A55%3A27Z%2F8772496)
Wright notes that "the two arguments here advanced against regulations
of this sort are so close to those found on the lips of Jesus in
[Matthew 15:1--20](https://ref.ly/Mt15.1-20) and [Mark
7:1--23](https://ref.ly/Mk7.1-23) that many scholars have suggested a
conscious echo on Paul's part. This may be judged probable whether or
not the Colossians would have picked up such a
reference."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:TNTC72COLUS?pos=res%2FLLS%3ATNTC72COLUS%2F2016-10-18T14%3A38%3A33Z%2F273370)

MacDonald emphasizes the connection: the language in [Colossians
2:22](https://ref.ly/Col2.22) is "very similar to that in LXX [Isa
29:13](https://ref.ly/Is29.13), which is cited by Jesus in response to
the ritual requirements of the tradition of the elders," and "the notion
of perishable things calls to mind Jesus' strong statement concerning
unclean foods: 'Do you not see that whatever goes into a person from
outside cannot defile, since it enters, not the heart but the stomach,
and goes out into the sewer?' (NRSV [Mark
7:18--19](https://ref.ly/Mk7.18-19)
par.)"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:SP17COLEPH?pos=res%2FLLS%3ASP17COLEPH%2F2022-03-30T17%3A07%3A53Z%2F377077)

However, the search results do not explicitly argue whether Paul
directly depends on Gospel tradition or merely shares Jesus' theological
critique. Beale lists numerous scholars who see the [Isaiah
29:13](https://ref.ly/Is29.13) allusion in [Colossians
2:8](https://ref.ly/Col2.8) and 2:22, including Dunn, who notes "an
allusion to [Mark 7:7](https://ref.ly/Mk7.7)/[Matt.
15:9](https://ref.ly/Mt15.9),"[\[4\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:COMNTUSEOT?pos=res%2FLLS%3ACOMNTUSEOT%2F2016-09-19T17%3A03%3A27Z%2F4052737)
suggesting some scholars recognize the Gospel connection, though the
commentaries stop short of definitively establishing literary dependence
versus shared polemical strategy.

3.8 Exodus 19:12 behind Colossians 2:21 (C27) --- low priority

-   **Ask:** Do any commentators connect "do not touch" (θίγῃς) in
    Colossians 2:21 with the Sinai prohibition in Exodus 19:12--13 or
    with the purity laws of Leviticus 11:8?
-   **Why:** θιγγάνω occurs once in the LXX, at Exod 19:12, beside ἅπτω
    --- the same pair as Col 2:21. The dig rates it low--uncertain and
    queues it only so that it is tested rather than preached.
-   **Watch for:** Hebrews 12:20, which cites the Sinai verse with the
    same verb; the Assistant may answer about Hebrews instead.

Logos:

Dunn identifies the purity concern behind "do not touch" (θίγῃς), noting
that it reflects fear of defilement through physical contact with
something forbidden, a concept rooted in [Leviticus
5:2--3](https://ref.ly/Le5.2-3); [7:19](https://ref.ly/Le7.19);
[11:8](https://ref.ly/Le11.8),
[24--28](https://ref.ly/Le11.24-28)[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F636528).
He explicitly connects the prohibition to purity laws rather than to the
Sinai theophany at [Exodus 19:12--13](https://ref.ly/Ex19.12-13).

Dunn emphasizes that "the echo of characteristically Jewish concerns is
strong, and particularly purity concerns, though that is missed by
almost all commentators," and notes that the distinction between clean
and unclean food within Jewish tradition is essentially a purity
distinction[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F638030).
According to Jewish law, one became impure by touching what was impure,
particularly a corpse ([Numbers 19:11--13](https://ref.ly/Nu19.11-13)),
but also through physical contact with a menstruant, someone with a
discharge of blood ([Leviticus 15](https://ref.ly/Le15)), or a leper
(implied by [Leviticus 13:45--46](https://ref.ly/Le13.45-46)), and
touching human impurity of whatever sort made one impure ([Leviticus
5:3](https://ref.ly/Le5.3))[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F638464).

Notably, at Qumran there is precisely the same combination of purity
concerns and heavenly worship as implied for the Colossian "philosophy,"
since the Dead Sea sect saw itself as a priestly community with
accentuated concern for purity, anticipating the eschatological
congregation in the presence of holy angels and encouraging mystical
entrance into the heavenly temple, making purity important for entry
into
both[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F639473).

The likelihood is that the Colossian regulations in view in 2:21 are
those of Colossian Jews anxious to maintain the purity they regarded as
necessary both to maintain their status as God's people and for entry
into the heavenly temple in their
worship[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:29.56.12?pos=res%2FLLS%3A29.56.12%2F2014-10-08T01%3A56%3A56Z%2F641939).

The search results do not explicitly connect "do not touch" with the
[Exodus 19:12--13](https://ref.ly/Ex19.12-13) prohibition against
touching Mount Sinai.

Text*](https://ref.ly/res/LLS:29.56.12/2014-10-08T01:56:56Z/636528?len=270),

## 4. Lexicon, Grammar and Text

**4.1 ἐθελοθρησκία (**"**self-made religion**"**), 2:23**

-   **Ask:** Is the word ἐθελοθρησκία ("self-made religion", Colossians
    2:23) found in any Greek text earlier than Colossians, and do the
    commentators think Paul coined it or took it from his opponents?
-   **Why:** the corpus shows it absent from the NT elsewhere and from
    the LXX; "not known before this verse" is recalled *\[S\]* (2:16--23
    dig, Open Question 6).
-   **Watch for:** EDNT and Dunn; an answer that cites only later
    Christian writers.

Logos:

ἐθελοθρησκία is a hapax legomenon and never appears in Greek literature
prior to
Colossians.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WBC72COL2NDED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWBC72COL2NDED%2F2025-03-20T14%3A47%3A27Z%2F1799219)
The term is not present in classical Greek or in the
Septuagint.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FCOMBIBLIBGK?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_COMBIBLIBGK%2F2021-05-05T16%3A59%3A10Z%2F4801425)

Commentators are divided on whether Paul coined the word or borrowed it
from his opponents. Moulton-Milligan suggest that it was coined by the
apostle Paul himself who used it once in the New Testament at
[Colossians
2:23](https://ref.ly/Col2.23).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FCOMBIBLIBGK?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_COMBIBLIBGK%2F2021-05-05T16%3A59%3A10Z%2F4801527)
However, O'Brien takes a different approach, viewing its origination in
the language of Paul's opponents and connecting it to their apparent
"worship of angels" in [Colossians 2:18](https://ref.ly/Col2.18),
arguing that Paul was using it
sarcastically.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WS$5FCOMBIBLIBGK?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWS_COMBIBLIBGK%2F2021-05-05T16%3A59%3A10Z%2F4801765)

Arnold offers a more nuanced assessment. It is difficult to know with
certainty if it is a catchword of the opponents or a polemical comment
that Paul has
constructed.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WBC72COL2NDED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWBC72COL2NDED%2F2025-03-20T14%3A47%3A27Z%2F1799366)
Yet he ultimately leans toward the opponents' origin: The term is most
likely a catchword of the opponents that the factional leader may have
brought with him from his own previous initiatory experience and cultic
involvement. As such, for him, it is a positive term suggesting the
alignment of some of the practices and experience with the local
mysteries.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WBC72COL2NDED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWBC72COL2NDED%2F2025-03-20T14%3A47%3A27Z%2F1801058)

The ambiguity reflects the term's construction. The ἐθελο- prefix
denotes something that is "freely chosen" or "voluntary," as in
ἐθελοδουλεία, "voluntary
subjection."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:WBC72COL2NDED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AWBC72COL2NDED%2F2025-03-20T14%3A47%3A27Z%2F1799613)
Whether Paul deployed existing opposition language polemically or
created the term himself to characterize their practices remains
uncertain from the textual evidence alone.

4.2 θέλων ἐν, 2:18

-   **Ask:** In Colossians 2:18, how do the commentators take θέλων ἐν
    ταπεινοφροσύνῃ --- "delighting in" (a Septuagintal idiom),
    "insisting on", or adverbially ("wilfully")?
-   **Why:** the dig found θέλω + ἐν + dative in the LXX in the sense
    "delight in" (1 Sam 18:22; 2 Sam 15:26; 1 Kgs 10:9; Ps 146:10) and
    favours the NASB95's "delighting in" against the ESV's "insisting
    on" (moderate--high). A commentator's view would confirm or correct.
-   **Watch for:** the KJV's "voluntary humility", which takes θέλων
    adverbially.

Logos:

Commentators interpret θέλων ἐν ταπεινοφροσύνῃ in three main ways, with
significant disagreement about which reading best fits the context.

**The "delighting in" interpretation** draws on Septuagintal usage.
Barth and Blanke note that the expression reflects the Hebrew ḥapaṣ bĕ,
used several times in the LXX, meaning "to find pleasure
in."[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ANCHOR72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AANCHOR72COL%2F2022-03-11T17%3A22%3A30Z%2F1211327)
Kent similarly observes that the phrase may be taken to mean "delighting
in," describing the individual as taking pleasure in his
humility.[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:KENT72COLREVED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AKENT72COLREVED%2F2014-10-03T00%3A12%3A36Z%2F168035)

**The adverbial "willfully" interpretation** treats θέλων as a modal
participle. Robertson notes that some take it as an adverb meaning
"wilfully," somewhat like θελοντας in [2 Peter
3:5](https://ref.ly/2Pe3.5).[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:46.50.2?pos=res%2FLLS%3A46.50.2%2F2019-09-25T15%3A44%3A04Z%2F3765958)
Kent argues this explanation "may be better," treating θέλων as a modal
participle, "acting by his mere will," which is similar to its use in [2
Peter 3:5](https://ref.ly/2Pe3.5) and agrees well with the thought in
Colossians where these people were accused of "self-made religion"
(2:23).[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:KENT72COLREVED?pos=res%2FLLS%3AKENT72COLREVED%2F2014-10-03T00%3A12%3A36Z%2F168157)

**The "insisting on" interpretation** appears less prominent in the
search results, though Robertson's Revised Version margin offers "of his
own mere will, by
humility,"[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:46.50.2?pos=res%2FLLS%3A46.50.2%2F2019-09-25T15%3A44%3A04Z%2F3766133)
which captures a sense of self-directed insistence.

The commentators remain divided. Barth and Blanke acknowledge that while
it is grammatically possible to interpret θέλων adverbially, "the
argument for such a closer connection is not compelling," and note that
verses 16--17 do not specify a certain manner of judging but rather
prohibit judging in its basic
manner.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:ANCHOR72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3AANCHOR72COL%2F2022-03-11T17%3A22%3A30Z%2F1211586)
The tension reflects genuine ambiguity in the Greek construction, with
the Septuagintal idiom and the modal participle reading each offering
plausible but distinct theological emphases.

## 5. Background

**5.1 Food, calendar and **"**philosophy**"** in Jewish Greek (C28)**

-   **Ask:** Do any commentators on Colossians 2:8 and 2:16--23 compare
    4 Maccabees, where Jewish food laws are called both a "religion"
    (θρησκεία) and a "philosophy" (4 Macc 5:6--11), or the Maccabean
    martyrs' refusal to taste forbidden food (2 Macc 6--7)?
-   **Why:** the dig's systematic scan of the LXX found this field as
    the passage's closest vocabulary partner, and 4 Macc 5:6 is the only
    LXX verse with both θρησκεία (2:18) and the "philosophy" word of
    2:8. It supports a Jewish component in the rival teaching without
    choosing a reconstruction. Moderate, background, queued.
-   **Watch for:** Josephus' description of the Jewish schools as
    "philosophies" (*Antiquities* 18.11) --- record it if cited.

Logos:

The search results do not contain any commentary that explicitly
compares the Colossian opponents' practices to 4 Maccabees or to the
Maccabean martyrs' refusal of forbidden food in [2 Maccabees
6--7](https://ref.ly/2Mac6-7).

However, the search results do provide relevant context. In the LXX,
"philosophy" occurs only in 4 Maccabees, where the author defends
obedience to the Law as sound philosophy because it enables one to
achieve the cardinal Greek philosophical virtues, countering the charge
that the religion of the Jews is foolish
philosophy.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:14.0.4?pos=res%2FLLS%3A14.0.4%2F2025-02-03T18%3A21%3A57Z%2F31224327)
This demonstrates that Jewish writers of the Second Temple period did
indeed characterize Torah observance as both "religion" (θρησκεία) and
"philosophy"---precisely the conceptual framework evident in [Colossians
2:8](https://ref.ly/Col2.8) and 2:16--23.

Since contemporary Jewish writers describe Judaism as a philosophy, and
both dietary regulations and Sabbath observances belong to the practices
of the Colossian philosophers (2:16), the Colossian philosophy may have
been a form of
Judaism.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:14.0.4?pos=res%2FLLS%3A14.0.4%2F2025-02-03T18%3A21%3A57Z%2F31225764)
Yet the commentators in these search results stop short of invoking 4
Maccabees as a specific parallel text or drawing on the Maccabean
martyrs' resistance to dietary violation as a comparative framework for
understanding the Colossian opponents' commitment to food laws.

The absence of this comparison in the provided commentaries suggests it
remains an underdeveloped avenue in recent Colossian scholarship,
despite the conceptual resonance between how 4 Maccabees frames Jewish
practice and how Paul's opponents appear to have framed theirs.

5.2 Angels in Phrygia, and its date

-   **Ask:** What evidence do the commentators give for the veneration
    of angels in Phrygia --- for example canon 35 of the Council of
    Laodicea, Theodoret's comment on Colossians 2:18, or the shrine of
    Michael at Chonae --- and how much weight do they give it for the
    first century?
-   **Why:** the dig reports all three from recall *\[S\]* and notes
    they are centuries later (Historical Background, item 5). The answer
    should give the exact wording of the canon and of Theodoret, with
    dates.
-   **Watch for:** later evidence offered as proof of a first-century
    angel cult.

Logos:

Commentators cite three main types of evidence for angel veneration in
Phrygia, though they assess its relevance to the first century
differently.

Theodoret reports in his exposition of [Colossians
2:18](https://ref.ly/Col2.18) that the notion of angel worship
"continued long in Phrygia and Pisidia," and that the synod meeting at
Laodicea (the metropolis of Phrygia) forbade prayer to angels by law. He
adds that in his own time, oratories of St. Michael could be seen among
the people of those countries and their
neighbors.[\[1\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:GILLSERMTRAC02?pos=res%2FLLS%3AGILLSERMTRAC02%2F2023-08-17T15%3A29%3A14Z%2F1642556)

Theodoret also explains that the worshipers "were leading to worship
angels such as were defending the Law; for, said they, the Law was given
through angels. And this vice lasted for a long time in Phrygia and
Pisidia. Therefore it was that the synod which met at Laodicea in
Phrygia, prohibited by a canon, that prayer should be offered to angels,
and even to-day an oratory of St. Michael can be seen among them, and
their
neighbours."[\[2\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:6.60.28?pos=res%2FLLS%3A6.60.28%2F2017-05-22T20%3A39%3A15Z%2F658515)

Beyond Theodoret's testimony, inscriptional evidence from Phrygia and
surrounding regions shows dedications to "the Good Angel," "the Divine
Angel," and "the Angelic Divinity," with angelic beings given elevated
titles and venerated for blessings
granted.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BLACK72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABLACK72COL%2F2019-04-05T20%3A42%3A24Z%2F706239)
This contextual evidence from the wider area of Phrygia makes it
plausible that those whom the Colossians were told to resist saw angel
veneration as one of their superior religious
practices.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BLACK72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABLACK72COL%2F2019-04-05T20%3A42%3A24Z%2F706788)

However, commentators exercise caution about projecting later evidence
backward. The precise nature of the practice remains unrecoverable, and
it may even be that the author of Colossians described it
polemically.[\[3\]](https://ref.ly/logosres/LLS:BLACK72COL?pos=res%2FLLS%3ABLACK72COL%2F2019-04-05T20%3A42%3A24Z%2F707131)
The Council of Laodicea's fourth-century prohibition and Theodoret's
later testimony provide important regional context, but the
inscriptional evidence---which may be earlier---offers more direct
support for first-century angel veneration in the region. Commentators
treat the post-apostolic sources as confirmatory of a persistent
regional practice rather than as direct evidence of first-century
